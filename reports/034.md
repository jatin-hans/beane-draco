The three processors all used origin procurement, local processing, farmer-group programmes and traceability to strengthen their positions with premium chocolate customers, but the available disclosures do not support a like-for-like overall ranking.

Barry Callebaut provides the clearest quantified example of direct-chain operational integration, with 83.0% of plots in its direct cocoa supply chain mapped in 2023/24, barcode bag traceability launched in Ghana, and three-year contracts with 90% of directly sourcing cooperatives in Côte d’Ivoire.[1][2] Cargill and Olam likewise combined West African sourcing and processing with farmer-facing sustainability systems, while Barry Callebaut explicitly connected its farm-service and labour-group investment to stable bean supply during shortages.[3][4][2] These capabilities can improve eligibility and credibility for premium manufacturing contracts with customers such as Lindt, Ferrero and Mondelēz, but a customer relationship or sustainability programme does not establish that it won a particular contract.[2][5]

## Vertical integration strategies

This section covers activities from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2024 and uses evidence available by 31 December 2024. Cocoa processing is vertically integrated when one company connects procurement at origin, bean grinding and ingredient manufacture, and—where applicable—finished-chocolate production for customers. In West Africa, that integration matters because Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana are the core supply base, while most cocoa is grown by independent smallholders who may sell through cooperatives, licensed buying companies, or local intermediaries. Barry Callebaut describes the two countries as producing almost two-thirds of world cocoa and notes that farmers may supply several companies through several cooperatives.[5]

**Claim —** From 2018 to 2024, Barry Callebaut, Cargill and Olam each deepened upstream links to West African production through origin procurement, farm-data systems, cooperative or farmer-group support, and sustainability programmes. Their approaches were not interchangeable: company programmes, independent certification, direct sourcing, and traceability each describe different parts of the chain.

A cooperative is a farmer-owned organisation that can aggregate beans, deliver services, receive certification and negotiate with buyers. A sustainability certification is an independently governed standard and assurance process, such as Rainforest Alliance or Fairtrade. A company sustainability programme is a buyer-designed system that may use certification but can also include premiums, training, farm services, mapping, and social programmes. Traceability is the ability to follow a product or its inputs through supply-chain stages; it is not automatically direct sourcing, certification, or proof of farmer-income improvement. Cargill distinguishes supply-chain mapping—identifying supply-chain actors and their relationships—from traceability, which follows a product or component through stages; it also distinguishes mass balance, an administrative chain-of-custody model, from segregation, which physically keeps certified cocoa separate.[6]

The three processors operated at global scale during the period. A 2022 Cocoa Barometer comparison listed 2021 used-cocoa volumes of 1,019 thousand metric tonnes for Olam, 988 thousand metric tonnes for Barry Callebaut and 875 thousand metric tonnes for Cargill; the table used ICCO conversion rates and included author estimates for some companies.[7] Barry Callebaut separately reported processing around 1 million tonnes of cocoa, approximately 20% of the world crop.[8] These figures describe processing scale, not distinct farm procurement, because cocoa traders and processors can buy from one another as well as from origin suppliers.

Origin-country processing is strategically important because it brings bean grinding—production of cocoa liquor, butter and powder—closer to cocoa supply. Barry Callebaut described its physical model as cocoa factories in countries of origin and chocolate factories near customers.[8] Cargill stated that it had sourcing and trading operations at origin in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana and processing facilities in both countries.[3][9] Olam held 100% of Olam Cocoa Processing Ghana Limited and Olam Cocoa Processing Côte d’Ivoire, whose stated activities included sourcing, processing, packaging and merchandising agricultural products and inputs.[4] This operating architecture gave all three companies a means to link upstream procurement to downstream industrial ingredient and chocolate supply.

### Barry Callebaut

Barry Callebaut described itself in 2023/24 as fully vertically integrated across the value chain, from sourcing through production of cocoa and chocolate products.[1] Its Global Cocoa segment centrally handled group-wide ingredient procurement and cocoa processing for regional chocolate businesses.[10] The company reported 62 factories directly operating in cocoa-bean processing or chocolate production.[1] In practical terms, this model connected farmer-facing cocoa sourcing, origin processing, industrial cocoa ingredients, and customer-facing chocolate manufacture under one group.

Barry Callebaut’s upstream capabilities also included Biolands alongside Farm Services and Cocoa Horizons.[99]

West Africa was central to that model. Barry Callebaut identified Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire as its largest cocoa-sourcing countries in 2023/24, when adverse weather and cocoa-tree disease most visibly damaged crops there.[11] Its 2020/21 presentation stated that about 70% of global cocoa beans originate in West Africa.[8] The company operated Nyonkopa, a Ghana-based licensed buying company, as a subsidiary.[12] A licensed buying company is an authorised buyer in Ghana’s regulated domestic cocoa market; it provides a route from farmgate purchases to the processor’s supply network.

Barry Callebaut’s sustainability umbrella was Forever Chocolate.[1] The framework had four pillars: Prospering Farmers, Human Rights, Thriving Nature and Sustainable Ingredients.[1] Its original 2025 targets were to lift more than 500,000 cocoa farmers in its supply chain out of poverty, eradicate child labour from its supply chain, become carbon and forest positive, and use 100% sustainable ingredients in its products.[5] These targets established the corporate strategy; they were not themselves certification standards.

Barry Callebaut launched Forever Chocolate in 2016 and said a third-party auditor verified its annual progress against the strategy’s time-bound targets.[20]

Barry Callebaut launched Forever Chocolate in 2016, and its annual progress reporting was subject to third-party audit verification.[20][26][5]

Cocoa Horizons was Barry Callebaut’s principal customer-linked implementation vehicle. The company described it as its preferred vehicle for delivering sustainability ambitions and addressing customer needs.[5] Cocoa Horizons was Barry Callebaut’s programme for improving farmer and community livelihoods through sustainable entrepreneurial farming, productivity support, and community development that protects nature and children.[2] It therefore sat beneath the Forever Chocolate umbrella: Forever Chocolate set company-wide strategic objectives, while Cocoa Horizons channelled customer premiums and programme activities into cocoa-growing communities.[2]

Barry Callebaut described HORIZONS cocoa and chocolate products as traceable from its warehouse back to the individual farmer.[100][99]

Barry Callebaut reported that Cocoa Horizons accounted for 37.5% of its sustainably sourced cocoa in 2021/22.[5] This is a share of Barry Callebaut’s sustainably sourced cocoa, not a share of total company cocoa procurement and not a direct-sourcing percentage.

The company reported that 223,235 farmers participated in Cocoa Horizons programmes focused on productivity and income in that year.[5] In 2023/24 Cocoa Horizons supported 356,011 cocoa producers across seven countries and generated CHF 43.5 million in funds from premiums on HORIZONS cocoa-product purchases.[2]

In 2019/20, Cocoa Horizons operated in Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Cameroon, Indonesia, Brazil and Ecuador; premiums generated CHF17.7 million and enabled more than 150,000 farmers to participate in productivity- and income-oriented activities.[100][99]

The programme’s premium total shows the scale of customer-funded sustainability finance. It does not mean CHF 43.5 million was paid as cash income to individual farmers: programme funds can support premiums, farm services, community projects and other interventions. Barry Callebaut itself described its living-income approach as a mix of yield improvement, non-cocoa income, cash premiums, conditional and unconditional transfers, village savings and loans associations, and support for farm-size consolidation.[2]

Cocoa Horizons premiums generated CHF37.4 million in 2021/22.[26][100][5][2]

Barry Callebaut used external certification routes, including UTZ Certified, Rainforest Alliance, Fairtrade and Organic, alongside Cocoa Horizons and customer programmes; Cocoa Horizons was its company programme rather than an independent certification.[13] In fiscal 2017/18, it said 44% of cocoa beans were sourced through sustainability programmes, including Cocoa Horizons, customer programmes, and external certifications such as UTZ Certified, Rainforest Alliance, Fairtrade and Organic.[13] UTZ later merged into Rainforest Alliance, but the 2017/18 disclosure retains the standards as then reported.

Barry Callebaut’s 2019 Sustainability Bond Framework listed Cocoa Horizons participation separately from third-party Rainforest Alliance, UTZ, Fairtrade and EU/USDA Organic certifications as eligible sustainable-cocoa purchasing categories.[99]

This combined measure is useful for showing the breadth of routes used to label cocoa sustainable; it should not be read as a single independent-certification rate.

The company reported a separate product metric: 43% of products sold in 2020/21 contained 100% sustainable cocoa or chocolate, rising to 49.4% in 2021/22 and 55.5% in 2023/24.[14][5][2] The 2023/24 figure covers products sold, not cocoa-bean volumes procured.

In the same 2023/24 reporting, Barry Callebaut reported that 56.2% of agricultural raw materials sourced were certified or verified, a measure covering agricultural raw materials generally rather than cocoa only.[1]

Barry Callebaut’s certification and verification architecture increasingly linked customer demand to ingredient offerings. Its Gourmet brands used 100% sustainably sourced cocoa in 2020/21.[14] In April 2024 it relaunched Maleo cocoa powder, which it stated was certified sustainable under Cocoa Horizons.[15] The company’s 2030 target was for all cocoa and other ingredients in its products to be certified or verified and traceable to farm level.[1] That target made traceability a required companion to certification or verification in the company’s forward strategy.

The company’s upstream strategy relied on direct cooperative engagement wherever possible. Barry Callebaut stated that it works directly with farmers and cooperatives where it can, and uses Cocoa Horizons and customer programmes to improve livelihoods and farming practices.[16] It publicly disclosed direct cocoa suppliers in Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Cameroon, including cooperatives and buying stations, in 2019/20.[17] Its supplier map identified location, cooperative or district name, certification scheme and the number of farmers in the supply chain.[18]

In Côte d’Ivoire, Barry Callebaut’s 2023/24 purchasing practice included three-year contracts with 90% of the cooperatives from which it directly sourced.[2] This measure is a share of direct-supplier cooperatives, not a share of cocoa volume.

The company said these arrangements reflected multi-year commitments from customers.[2] Long-term cooperative contracts can provide a buyer with more predictable access to beans and give cooperatives a commercial basis for maintaining farmer data, delivering sustainability services and financing operational improvements.

Barry Callebaut’s direct network also supported customer programmes beyond Cocoa Horizons. Unilever reported that it worked with Barry Callebaut and Cargill in 35 cooperatives within Unilever’s dedicated Côte d’Ivoire cocoa supply chain, combining long-term supplier relationships and impact programmes with certification programmes.[19] Unilever also reported a joint Barry Callebaut initiative that introduced time-saving products for women, including cooking stoves, washing machines and solar pumps, to create more opportunities for income generation.[19] These examples show brand customers using processor-linked cooperative networks for targeted development interventions.

The company developed farm-level operational support through Farm Services and Farm Business Plans. Barry Callebaut’s Farm Services specialists assessed farm conditions, soil, tree age, other crops, livestock, farmer skills and resources, then developed multi-year Farm Business Plans intended to maximize farm income.[2] In 2018/19, more than 16,000 farmers had adopted Farm Business Plans.[18] In 2020/21, 125,593 cocoa farmers had access to farm services and 92,508 adopted an individualized plan.[20] In 2021/22, 171,710 cocoa farmers in six countries, including Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, received Farm Services support, while 120,107 adopted Farm Business Plans.[21]

Barry Callebaut’s IFC–IDH risk-sharing partnership, valued at USD 9 million, offered two credit-based productivity packages with in-kind credit for training, farm services and fertilizer for eligible Ivorian farmers, alongside individualized coaching by accredited agents; it was designed to reach up to 103,000 smallholders by 2020.[101]

The partnership’s programme page reported USD 24 million in benefits generated for smallholders and 4,000 savings accounts opened, with coverage of 103,000 farmers planned for the 2020/21 season.[101]

By 2023/24, Barry Callebaut reported 197,763 cocoa farmers receiving farm-service activities.[16] The company had shifted emphasis from training toward investment-oriented support: subsidised soil inputs and planting material, financial support for third-party labour services, and additional premiums.[16] It stated that the reason for this shift was that farmers often lacked capital to invest in raising yield and income.[16]

The labour-groups programme was a distinct operational intervention. Barry Callebaut launched it in the 2021/22 season with global customers including Mondelēz International, Nestlé, Ben & Jerry’s and Cocoa Horizons customers.[2][21] The model recruited and trained local community members and cocoa farmers into professional labour teams to provide pruning and other pre-harvest services.[21] The programme also created additional income opportunities for the workers delivering those services.[2]

For 2021/22, Barry Callebaut reported total Labour Groups coverage of 8,107 hectares and separately reported 5,620 hectares in Côte d’Ivoire and 2,490 hectares in Ghana.[21] By 2023/24 it covered 43,631 hectares across all cocoa-producing regions in West Africa, an increase of 125.8% from the previous year.[2] The progression from 8,107 hectares to 43,631 hectares reflects expansion in area served, not a directly comparable count of farmers or a measured income increase.

Barry Callebaut linked labour services to supply resilience. In 2023/24, it reported that the volume of cocoa beans supplied by its farmer groups remained stable despite bean shortages, alongside wider pre-harvest interventions and deeper farmer engagement.[2] **The key commercial implication is that Barry Callebaut used farm-service investment not only as a livelihood programme but as a means to support continuity and quality of supply in a constrained West African market.**[2][15]

Agroforestry was the company’s principal farm-level nature intervention. Agroforestry means deliberately integrating trees with crop production to provide shade, diversify output, improve soil and habitat conditions, and sometimes generate carbon-related payments. Barry Callebaut’s intensified agroforestry approach began in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire in 2022.[1] It combined training, extended monitoring, and payments for ecosystem services, or PES—annual payments linked to surviving planted trees and their carbon-removal contribution.[1]

Barry Callebaut reported that its intensified agroforestry programme covered 11,000 hectares in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire in FY2021/22, combining training, monitoring and payments for ecosystem services.[26][5]

The company described fruit and timber trees as both an income-diversification opportunity and an agroecological input.[2] In 2023/24, it added 33,795 hectares to the PES programme and made PES payments on 48,348 hectares already established.[2] It also reported 33,795 hectares of newly established agroforestry, up from 18,066 hectares in 2022/23.[2] These figures reflect programme area; they do not quantify household-level returns from fruit, timber or payments.

Barry Callebaut’s intensified agroforestry approach covered 11,000 hectares in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire in 2021/22 and combined training, monitoring and payments for ecosystem services.[5]

Barry Callebaut reported distributing nearly 3.3 million non-cocoa trees in 2023/24. It said the trees supported income diversification, cocoa shade, carbon removal, soil health and biodiversity.[2] In 2021/22 it had distributed 3.91 million cocoa seedlings and more than 5 million non-cocoa trees, while expanding nursery capacity in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana as well as other origins.[21] In 2023/24 it reported distributing almost 3.2 million cocoa seedlings.[16]

The company also funded productivity inputs. In 2021/22, it spent more than CHF 2 million on fertilizer subsidies for farmers in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, partly funded by Cocoa Horizons customers.[5] In 2023/24, it allocated almost CHF 1.1 million of Cocoa Horizons customer funding to fertilizer subsidies for West African farmers.[2] The differing annual amounts describe subsidy spending, not capital expenditure on processing facilities.

Land tenure formed another upstream investment theme. Barry Callebaut joined the Côte d’Ivoire Land Partnership in 2019 with industry partners, donors and local implementers to develop faster, more affordable land documentation for farmers.[2] In the 2021/22 pilot, 130 land-tenure documents were issued, while the programme set an ambition of more than 9,000 by 2023.[5] In Ghana, around 500 cocoa farmers—approximately 30% women—received land-registration documents in June 2022 through contributions from Cocoa Horizons customers and partner organisations.[5]

Land documentation is commercially relevant because farmers with more secure tenure can make longer-term decisions about replanting, tree planting, rehabilitation and diversification. Barry Callebaut explicitly stated that secure land rights improve tenure security, help resolve disputes and allow farmers to invest in their land.[5] The programme therefore supported the conditions for sustained farm investment rather than serving as a cocoa certification in itself.

Barry Callebaut reported substantial growth in programme participation. Farmers in its sustainability programmes increased from 343,023 in 2021/22 to 462,471 in 2022/23 and 594,608 in 2023/24.[1] Women represented 15.0% of farmers in sustainability programmes in 2023/24, compared with 14.7% a year earlier.[1] Programme participation captures reach across multiple activities and origins; it is neither a cooperative count nor a direct measure of the number of farmers receiving a specific payment.

The company reported a poverty-status outcome as part of Forever Chocolate. In 2020/21, it stated that 214,584 cocoa farmers in its supply chain were out of poverty, using the International Poverty Line threshold of USD 1.90 per day.[20] In 2022/23, it reported 269,762 cocoa farmers out of poverty.[22] In 2023/24, it reported 475,752 cocoa farmers in its supply chain no longer in poverty, measured against a USD 2.15-per-day International Poverty Line threshold, a reported 76.4% increase from the prior year.[2]

The threshold change matters. The 2020/21 measure used USD 1.90 per day, while the 2023/24 measure used USD 2.15 per day. Barry Callebaut’s reported counts therefore show its stated poverty-status assessment under the respective thresholds; they are not a consistent series of average farmer-income changes. The company’s stated 2025 target remained 500,000 cocoa farmers lifted out of poverty.[2]

In FY2023/24, Barry Callebaut also applied a USD 2.67-per-person-per-day benchmark after inflation and purchasing-power adjustments and revised its household calculation toward the OECD modified household scale.[2]

Barry Callebaut’s own income research in Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana identified production characteristics associated with living income. It reported that a typical farmer reaching a living income cultivated 5 hectares of cocoa and achieved yields above 600 kg per hectare, compared with 3.8 hectares and an average yield of 450 kg per hectare for the average farmer.[2] These are comparative farm characteristics, not a before-and-after evaluation of one programme.

Barry Callebaut reported improved productivity per hectare among Farm Services participants in FY2018/19 but expressly did not attribute the improvement causally to its activities.[26]

The company’s farmer-income strategy had several income channels: cocoa-yield improvement, diversification beyond cocoa, cash premiums and transfers, village savings and loans associations, land-size consolidation, fertilizer subsidy, agroforestry income, and paid labour work.[2] This broad mix reflects the fact that cocoa smallholder livelihoods depend on farm output, prices, household composition, access to finance, off-farm work, land tenure and the costs of labour and inputs.

A customer-linked Barry Callebaut Farm Services trial found that additional pre-harvest labour, particularly pruning, and suitable soil inputs could increase yields and farmer income; the company did not publish an income increment, baseline, comparison group or participant count for the trial.[5]

Barry Callebaut also used direct-chain engagement for human-rights systems. The share of direct-supply-chain farmer groups with systems to prevent, monitor and remediate child labour rose from 42.2% in 2019/20 to 61.4% in 2020/21, 80.6% in 2021/22, 76.5% in 2022/23 and 91.0% in 2023/24.[1] This is a coverage measure for direct-supply-chain farmer groups, not a cocoa volume share and not a certification rate.

Barry Callebaut reported that its child-labour monitoring and remediation system covered 237 farmer groups and 220,878 farmers across Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Cameroon in 2020/21; coverage reached 275 groups and 253,269 farmers in 2021/22.[20][5]

The company conducted human-rights impact assessments in Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Cameroon and Nigeria in 2022/23 and reported that country action plans were under implementation.[2] It also described 2025 human-rights due-diligence ambitions for its entire supply chain.[23] These processes reinforced the integration of social due diligence into supplier relationships, but they are separate from independent cocoa certification.

Barry Callebaut’s sourcing architecture included both direct and indirect channels. It reported that farm-level traceability methods apply to direct and indirect supply chains, using farm polygons, land-use and land-cover data, and tree-cover-loss information to classify source areas by risk.[1] For direct sourcing, it described GPS mapping and overlaps with protected-area or forest maps; for indirect sourcing, it described country-level risk assessment using Maplecroft data.[17] This distinction matters because direct cooperative procurement allows farmer-level intervention, while indirect procurement requires supplier protocols and risk controls.

A 2021 Trase analysis estimated that approximately 30% of Barry Callebaut’s Côte d’Ivoire sourcing was indirect.[24] That country- and year-specific estimate is not a company-wide percentage across 2018–2024.

Barry Callebaut stated that for volumes it did not source directly, suppliers were expected to follow the same traceability protocol.[1] The company also ran a 2021/22 pilot with three key indirect suppliers in Côte d’Ivoire, training them on data collection and planning wider onboarding of suppliers and their cooperatives.[5]

Its FY2021/22 indirect-supplier data-collection pilot in Côte d’Ivoire also involved the Conseil du Café-Cacao (CCC).[5]

Barry Callebaut’s farm mapping grew rapidly. It recorded 295,383 farms in its Katchilè database at the end of 2018/19 and conducted census interviews with 229,142 farmers.[18] It reported 399,413 mapped cocoa plots in 2021/22, 547,804 in 2022/23, and 669,174 in 2023/24.[1] Katchilè, in use since 2018, is Barry Callebaut’s geotraceability application for assessing farmers’ needs and tracking sustainability impact.[17]

In 2018/19, Barry Callebaut had completed both geographic mapping and census interviews for 176,984 farms in its Katchilè database.[100][26][20][5]

In fiscal 2018/19, Barry Callebaut mapped 47,182 farms in its direct supply chain within 25 kilometres of protected forest areas in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana and established traceability for cocoa volumes from those farms.[26]

By the end of FY2018/19, 176,984 farms had both mapping and socioeconomic census data in Barry Callebaut’s Katchilè database.[18][26]

The full EUDR comparison belongs in the following section, but the upstream relevance is direct: mapping made cooperative and farmer relationships operationally usable for deforestation screening, bag tracing, yield controls, sustainability services and customer reporting. Barry Callebaut reported that its mapping database covered 83.0% of plots in its direct cocoa supply chain in 2023/24.[1] It also launched barcode-based bag traceability in Ghana that year, recording movements from farmer to factory.[1]

Barry Callebaut’s upstream model thus combined company-designed programmes, external standards, customer funding and direct procurement. It did not rest on cooperative ownership: the evidence shows multi-year contracts, mapping, farmer services, premiums, land-tenure initiatives, labour groups and agroforestry interventions rather than ownership of farmer organisations. **Barry Callebaut’s distinctive vertical-integration strategy was to turn direct cooperative relationships into a managed supply-and-service system connecting beans, farmer support, traceability and customer sustainability propositions.**[2]

This model made several things possible: it enabled tailored customer programmes, continuity-oriented farm support, product claims using Cocoa Horizons and other verified or certified routes, and more granular origin data. It also created dependencies on customer co-funding and on the company’s ability to maintain credible direct and indirect sourcing controls. Barry Callebaut reported that rising cocoa prices temporarily led some customers to deprioritize sustainable solutions and explore alternatives, although it expected the trend to be temporary.[2]

Barry Callebaut increased its mapped cocoa-plot record by 373,791 plots between FY2018/19 and FY2023/24, from 295,383 to 669,174—a 126.5% increase. This quantifies the expansion of the farm-data base underpinning its direct-chain traceability and farmer-service model.[18][1]

From the 2021/22 launch year to FY2023/24, Barry Callebaut’s Labor Groups coverage increased from 8,107 to 43,631 hectares, a 438.2% increase. The growth shows a substantial scaling of its paid pre-harvest labour-service model across West Africa.[21][2]

Barry Callebaut also developed an Income Accelerator approach in Côte d’Ivoire that used cash payments conditional on specified practices and household measures rather than a uniform premium per tonne of cocoa.

In fiscal year 2018/19, Barry Callebaut sourced 47% of its cocoa beans through sustainability programmes, including Cocoa Horizons, customer programmes and external certification organisations.[25]

Barry Callebaut reported that 47% of its cocoa beans were sustainably sourced in 2018/19.[26]

### Cargill

Cargill’s cocoa vertical integration linked origin-country buying and trading operations, cooperative-facing programmes, digital traceability systems, local processing, and industrial cocoa-and-chocolate supply. Cargill stated that it had established its own sourcing and trading operations in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, as well as Brazil, Cameroon, Ecuador and Indonesia, to support sustainable supply of quality cocoa beans.[3] It described its cocoa and chocolate business as producing cocoa powder, cocoa butter, cocoa liquor, chocolate, coatings and fillings for industrial manufacturers.[27]

In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported operating 24 cocoa-processing facilities worldwide, including two in West Africa, and offering fully segregated cocoa ingredients traceable from farm to facility.[28]

Cargill’s central sustainability architecture was the Cargill Cocoa Promise, launched in 2012. The company described it as the cornerstone of its cocoa sustainability approach and stated that Promise Cocoa is supplied through the programme.[28] The Promise was implemented in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, alongside Brazil, Ecuador and Indonesia.[29] Its stated ambition was to increase transparency, improve the socioeconomic resilience of farmers and communities, and deliver more sustainable cocoa and chocolate from bean to end product.[29]

Cargill’s direct network in West Africa was explicitly cooperative-based in Côte d’Ivoire and buying-station based in Ghana. In 2019, it published a sourcing-partner map naming and locating 128 cooperative offices in Côte d’Ivoire and seven buying stations in Ghana.[30] Cargill stated that the organisations and farmer members in that direct network benefited from Cocoa Promise activities.[30] In its 2024 Impact Report, it reported 147 farmer organisations in the Côte d’Ivoire Cocoa Promise and 14 districts in Ghana; the company defined country organisational units differently, including farmer organisations in Côte d’Ivoire and districts in Ghana.[28]

Cargill added a Ghanaian licensed buying company in 2016, before the 2018–2024 period.[66]

This difference reflects market structure. Côte d’Ivoire’s cooperative system gives processors a channel to buy from organised farmer groups, while Ghana’s system relies heavily on licensed buying companies and district networks. Ferrero’s supplier disclosure similarly explained that Ghana has relatively few farmer associations and that licensed buying companies operate sourcing networks in districts.[31] Cargill’s use of 128 cooperative offices in Côte d’Ivoire and buying stations in Ghana therefore represented adaptation to origin-specific procurement structures, rather than inconsistent vertical integration.

Cargill supported cooperative capacity through the Coop Academy. By 2022, the programme had trained more than 700 participants from 70 cooperatives in Côte d’Ivoire and Cameroon since 2013.[32] In 2019, Cargill and the International Finance Corporation expanded Coop Academy in Côte d’Ivoire by adding 40 farmer organisations, bringing the total reached by training and tools to 120 organisations.[30] The expansion added support for women’s groups, aimed to coach 250 women leaders and support 3,000 women in establishing income-generating activities.[30]

Cargill described operational and strategic support for ECASO COOP-CA, a Côte d’Ivoire cooperative that had partnered with it since 2004.[102]

Cargill described its 2019 IFC-backed Coop Academy 2.0 initiative as intended to provide management, operational and digital training to leaders and women entrepreneurs in 140 partner cooperatives.[6][36][102]

Coop Academy also incorporated digital-traceability modules. Cargill stated that the expanded IFC partnership introduced modules on integrating digital tools into supply chains to improve traceability.[30] This placed cooperative business training within a wider procurement-control strategy: stronger cooperative administration, financial records, bean inventory management and farmer data make it easier to buy directly, pay premiums, collect traceability data and verify sustainability claims.

Cargill’s Cooperative Management System, or CMS, was central to this model. It began setting up the system in Côte d’Ivoire in 2018.[30] Powered by Farmforce, CMS centralised inventory information, payment flows and financial operations.[30] Barcodes linked beans managed by farmer organisations back to individual farms, allowing cooperatives to manage loans, collect beans and compare fixed and variable costs.[30]

Cargill also identified CocoaWise ProCoop as a tool supporting cooperative finance, inventory management and first-mile cocoa traceability.[6][102]

Cargill identified ECASO COOP-CA in Côte d’Ivoire as a cooperative partner since 2004 and said it piloted the Cooperative Management System during 2017–18. Its CocoaWise cooperative tools also included ProCoop, designed to support cooperative finance, inventory management and member-level first-mile traceability.[6]

The company said CMS could help farmer organisations make data-driven business decisions and prevent beans from deforested areas from entering its supply chain.[30] A later study described Cargill’s Côte d’Ivoire CMS as including 77,320 farmers and tracking 151,190 metric tonnes of cocoa beans; the digital interface let cooperatives track beans to farm level and manage inventory, costs and outstanding loans.[33] The study’s figures show the operational scale of the system but do not describe the entire Cargill cocoa supply chain.

Cargill added customer-facing data infrastructure through CocoaWise. It launched the CocoaWise Portal in 2020 to provide customers access to sustainability information about products from Cargill’s direct supply chain.[34] CocoaWise drew on CocoaWise 360, a central data platform combining information from Cargill’s digital tools and systems.[30] Cargill described the portal as offering customers access to supply-chain sustainability metrics and origin stories tailored to customer needs.[30]

CocoaWise also recorded financial flows. Cargill stated that the platform could track how customer premium payments for sustainable-verified cocoa and direct sustainability projects were distributed and invested, while also supporting electronic physical-bean tracking and impact transparency.[3] This mattered for vertical integration because it linked origin-side cooperative records and farm data to downstream customer reporting.

Cargill made identifiable sustainability and traceability commitments in the two countries. In December 2019, it announced USD 12.3 million over three years to expand sustainability and supply-chain traceability programmes in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana.[35] It stated country allocations of USD 7.7 million for Côte d’Ivoire and USD 3.4 million for Ghana; those two disclosed country figures total USD 11.1 million, while the announcement stated a USD 12.3 million overall commitment.[35] The announcement described the funding as supporting child and family well-being, community initiatives and a more transparent cocoa supply chain.

Following completion of the Yopougon expansion in November 2021, Cargill committed a further USD 13 million over the next year to Côte d’Ivoire programmes, including nearly USD 6 million for coaching and training and USD 3.5 million for traceability projects.[3]

Cargill announced an additional USD 1.2 million for Côte d’Ivoire in 2020 for scalable mapping and monitoring technologies, including GPS polygon mapping and CMS. A polygon is a digital boundary representing the actual shape and area of a farm, rather than a single coordinate point.[36] Polygon mapping is particularly useful for testing overlap with protected areas and monitoring forest loss. Cargill described it as part of the data needed to assess farmers against protected-area boundaries, historical forest loss and future deforestation risks.[30]

The company reported an additional USD 3 million invested in traceability initiatives in crop year 2019/20.[6] That investment related to data integration and farmer-support tools rather than processing-facility capital spending.

Cargill reported that 64% of farmers participating in the Cocoa Promise were mapped in 2019/20, using GPS farm polygons as its mapping standard.[6] In Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana specifically, its mapping discussion reported 76% of farmers mapped in each country, covering 73,186 farms and 215,000 hectares in Côte d’Ivoire and 33,886 farms and 34,000 hectares in Ghana.[6]

Cargill separately reported first-mile traceability registration and use. In 2019/20, 66% of farmers in its direct supply chain were registered in first-mile traceability systems and 53% delivered volumes through those systems, up from 50% and 38% respectively in 2018/19.[6] First-mile traceability means linking cocoa to the first purchase point, normally the cooperative, buying station or local buyer. Registration is not the same as actual delivery through the system, which is why Cargill reported both measures.

Cargill continued tightening entry requirements. It stated that as of crop year 2023/24, farmers had to have all cocoa plots mapped and pass its deforestation-risk-area assessment to enter the Cocoa Promise supply chain.[37] Its 2024 Impact Report stated that 100% of cocoa in Cargill’s direct supply chain was traceable to the first point of purchase.[28] It also reported that 117,898 tonnes, or 76.6% of sustainable volume in the Cocoa Promise supply chain, were managed through digital First Mile Traceability; the country values shown were 100% for Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana and 42% for Cameroon.[28]

Cargill’s Ghana system was described more strongly in a local processing-facility account. The Ghana Chocolate Hub reported that Cargill had achieved 100% traceability and accountability from farm to factory in its Ghanaian supply chain by using GPS polygon mapping and CMS through CocoaWise.[38] That statement describes Cargill’s Ghanaian supply chain, while the 2024 Impact Report’s direct-supply-chain metric describes traceability to first purchase. The measures are complementary rather than identical.

Cargill reported that barcodes could trace each cocoa bag bought by its Ghana cocoa-and-chocolate business to one of 27,000 individual cocoa farmers.[103]

In Côte d’Ivoire, Cargill reported farm-to-factory traceability for 50% of cocoa in its direct supply chain, compared with 35% in 2017/18; this was not a total-procurement traceability rate.[35]

Trase’s 2023 Ghana analysis estimated that Cargill had mapped production locations for 16% of its 125,000-tonne Ghana cocoa supply; Trase said its assessment of company-described direct supplies did not establish the quantity bought through additional intermediaries.[104]

Cargill’s farm and community programmes extended beyond mapping. Its 2018 Ghana roadmap included capacity-building support and facilitated input access through Farmer Field Schools for 80,000 farmers.[39] The roadmap also targeted 9,000 hectares of cocoa agroforestry, 1 million cocoa seedlings for rehabilitation, 200,000 shade-tree seedlings, and crop-protection access for 30,000 farmers.[39] Cargill later stated that 130,000 farmers in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana had been trained in Good Agricultural Practices through its farmer-coaching model.[37]

In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported one-on-one Good Agricultural Practice coaching for 78,426 farmers in Côte d’Ivoire and 7,960 in Ghana.[28]

In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported one-to-one Good Agricultural Practices coaching for 78,426 farmers in Côte d’Ivoire, or 55% of its stated country denominator, and 7,960 farmers in Ghana, or 24% of its stated country denominator.[3][28]

Good Agricultural Practices, or GAP, are farm-management methods intended to improve productivity, quality, safety, soil management and environmental performance. Cargill described its coaches as often being farmers themselves, helping other farmers adopt GAP, use inputs more efficiently and improve productivity.[37] This is a farm-service strategy similar in commercial purpose to Barry Callebaut’s Farm Services, but organised through Cargill’s cooperative and field network.

Cargill’s Cocoa Promise was tied to independent certification and its own verification route. In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported 143,054 certified farmers in Côte d’Ivoire, 32,556 in Ghana and 211,434 globally.[28] The company defined this indicator as farmers who were sustainability-certified and took part in at least one Cocoa Promise activity, such as training or coaching.[28] It reported that 54% of cocoa and chocolate products sold, measured in bean equivalent, were sustainable in January–June 2024; its definition included Rainforest Alliance, Fairtrade, Promise Verified and customers’ own programmes.[28]

Promise Verified is Cargill’s company-led sustainability route, while Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade are third-party certification systems. Cargill’s reported sustainable-product figure therefore combines independent certification, its own verified programme and customer programmes. This is useful for commercial portfolio analysis but should not be treated as a third-party certified-cocoa percentage.

Cargill introduced Promise Verified in 2021 as an alternative assurance route for Promise Cocoa, with field verification by Bureau Veritas and SGS and programme-performance assurance by KPMG.[6]

Cargill also identified UTZ Certified alongside Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade as an independently audited standard for farmer organisations.[105]

Cargill also participated in the Cocoa & Forests Initiative, or CFI, with the governments of Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana and other companies. Cargill stated that it had worked under the CFI since 2017 to end cocoa-related deforestation and restore forest areas.[40] Its Protect Our Planet plan, introduced in 2018, aimed to eliminate deforestation from Cargill’s cocoa supply chain by 2030 through supply-chain transparency, supplier engagement, collaboration, reporting and Cocoa Promise programmes.[41]

The company reported 0.01% forest loss since 2014 in its mapped cocoa supply chain.[37] This is a forest-loss measure for mapped supply, not an indicator for all cocoa purchased.

It also stated that, by the 2022/23 crop year, a majority of farmers in its Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire direct supply chains were polygon mapped.[40] These disclosures demonstrate the integration of farm mapping with sourcing rules and forest-risk management.

Cargill used cooperative and community finance structures to support livelihoods. Cargill and CARE reported establishing 376 village savings and loan associations, or VSLAs, in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, with 9,034 members, including 6,853 women.[30] The associations provided access to capital for cocoa-farm investment or other activities.[30] A Cargill–CARE retrospective reported that these members had saved USD 301,186 and distributed USD 189,014 in loans.[42]

Cargill reported connecting more than 20,000 West African farmers to banks and mobile-network operators and aimed to connect another 140,000 farmers to financial institutions for direct digital payments.[3][28]

Cargill later reported establishing 686 VSLAs in West Africa, helping more than 11,000 women start or expand small businesses.[32] It also stated in a 2023 forest-progress report that 20,000 members of women-led VSLAs were supported and that a second phase with CARE began in 2023.[37] These figures have different scopes and reporting dates; they show programme growth but should not be combined into one time series.

Cargill’s cooperative-facing social programmes included child-labour systems. Its 2019 Côte d’Ivoire announcement targeted expansion of the Child Labor Monitoring and Remediation System, or CLMRS, from 53 cooperatives and 46,800 farmers to 130 cooperatives and 120,000 farmers in 2022.[35] In Ghana, it planned CLMRS coverage of 8,000 farmers and their families in 56 communities by September 2020.[35] In its 2024 Impact Report, Cargill reported 101,100 farmers monitored through CLMRS or a comparable due-diligence system.[28]

Alongside its planned Ghana child-labour monitoring expansion, Cargill allocated USD 800,000 for six schools and related infrastructure, with coverage of 8,000 farmers and their families in 56 communities planned by September 2020.[36][35]

Cargill also supported agroforestry through partnerships. In Côte d’Ivoire, it reported reaching 1,683 farmers in nine farmer organisations with PUR Projet through community-based agroforestry, and planting more than 580,000 shade trees with IMPACTUM. It planned to reach 2,700 further farmers across 14 cooperatives in Côte d’Ivoire and expand ecosystem restoration to Ghana.[41] These programmes linked forest protection, on-farm resilience and cooperative engagement.

Cargill’s direct network also underpinned product offers. It stated that Promise Cocoa products use beans entirely sourced through its direct-sourcing networks in West Africa from known farmers and farmer organisations that benefit from the Cocoa Promise.[43] In North America, its Made With a Promise offer used sustainable cocoa from the directly sourced network for chocolate brands sold through distributors, with part of sales proceeds supporting a Côte d’Ivoire community project.[30] This shows how Cargill converted upstream programme participation into a downstream commercial proposition.

Cargill’s direct and indirect sourcing architecture remained broader than its direct network. In 2024, it stated that it directly sourced cocoa from six producing countries—Brazil, Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire, Ecuador, Ghana and Indonesia—and indirectly sourced from 19 additional origins.[28] It also depicted its supply chain as involving farmer organisations and/or middlemen before beans reached warehouses, shipping, grinding, pressing and product manufacture.[28] The company provided origin counts, not a direct-versus-broker volume split.

**Cargill’s defining integration mechanism was the combination of owned origin buying with a cooperative-management and customer-data system that tied bean purchases, farmer payments, mapping, certification-linked support and customer reporting together.** This approach enabled Cargill to offer directly sourced Cocoa Promise material with a documented first-mile data trail, while retaining a wider indirect sourcing footprint across additional origins.[30][43][3][28]

Cargill’s documented farmer-income outcome is stronger than a statement of programme intent. Its 2024 Impact Report cited a Royal Tropical Institute, or KIT, baseline report on the Income Accelerator Program that recorded a 32% increase in cocoa production and a 38% increase in net income from cocoa and non-cocoa among programme households.[28] Impact was measured in 2023 across all households in the programme, including approximately 2,000 households in cooperatives engaged through Cargill.[28] The result covers net income from cocoa and non-cocoa sources and programme households; it is not a company-wide average across all Cocoa Promise farmers.

Cargill also set a living-income target with IDH, aiming by the end of a 2025 project to enable 25,000 cocoa-farming households in Côte d’Ivoire to close or significantly reduce, by more than 50%, their living-income gap.[28] This was a forward target at the end of 2024. The company’s programme results therefore combined one reported Income Accelerator outcome with larger-scale training, mapping, certification and finance programmes designed to improve farm resilience.

The strategy makes Cargill commercially credible where customers require farm-to-factory information, cooperative-level programme visibility and certified or verified sustainability routes. It also requires disciplined differentiation between its direct Cocoa Promise network and its wider cocoa procurement footprint. The competitive value rests on the integrated capability—origin presence, CMS, CocoaWise, Cocoa Promise and processing—not on any one certification label.[30][28][3]

### Olam

Olam’s cocoa operations were organised within Olam Food Ingredients, or ofi, during the period. Olam described ofi as covering cocoa, coffee, dairy, nuts and spices.[4] It described Olam more broadly as vertically integrated across the agricultural value chain, including growing, sourcing, trading, processing, manufacturing and distribution, with cocoa among its processed products.[44] This group-wide statement covers more than cocoa, but Olam separately described Olam Cocoa as a fully integrated cocoa business.[45]

In 2019, Olam described its cocoa business as sourcing beans from a network of 650,000 cocoa farmers across Africa, Asia and South America.[45] It reported global cocoa-bean grinding capacity above 820,000 metric tonnes and more than 80,000 metric tonnes of refining and milling presence in or near primary consumption markets.[45] The capacity figures establish global processing scale; they do not allocate processing capacity between Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire.

Olam had wholly owned cocoa-processing entities in both countries. Its 2024 subsidiary table listed Olam Cocoa Processing Ghana Limited and Olam Cocoa Processing Côte d’Ivoire as 100%-owned in 2023 and 2024.[4] Its 2023 annual report also listed Outspan Ivoire SA, Olam Cocoa Processing Côte d’Ivoire and Olam Ivoire SA as 100%-owned Côte d’Ivoire entities under an activity classification covering sourcing, processing, packaging and merchandising of agricultural products and inputs.[46] These entities supplied the corporate structure for origin-side sourcing and processing.

ofi identified cocoa facilities in Abidjan and San Pedro, Côte d’Ivoire, and Kumasi, Ghana.[106][65][107]

Olam’s principal cocoa sustainability strategy was Cocoa Compass, launched in October 2019. The strategy was structured around the pillars Focused on Farmers, Empowered to Grow and Investing in Nature.[45] Its goals applied to the company’s global direct cocoa supply chain and included improving livelihoods, addressing child labour, education, deforestation and environmental performance.[45] Olam’s stated 2030 ambition was to enable 150,000 cocoa farmers to achieve a defined living-income level.[45]

Olam defined its direct cocoa supply chain as cocoa bought from a farm, Farmer Group or cooperative.[47] This is a clear functional definition of direct sourcing. It differs from a certification definition and differs from farm-level traceability: a company can buy directly from a cooperative while still needing farm polygons, farmer identities, transaction data and controls to establish full traceability.

AtSource was Olam’s customer-facing sustainable-sourcing platform. Olam said that AtSource would be the mechanism through which it delivered Cocoa Compass programmes in cocoa-growing regions and understood and reported improvements needed to meet goals.[45] A market study described AtSource as having three levels, ranging from reassurance of Olam Supplier Code compliance to footprint calculations, certification readiness, traceability and programmes co-created with communities.[33] These tiers were company service offerings rather than a single independent certification.

Olam reported that it implemented AtSource at its own cost for 270,000 metric tonnes of in-country sourced cocoa for which customers were not paying for sustainability standards.[33] It also reported 380,000 metric tonnes of cocoa under managed programmes, in which customers invested in meeting sustainability requirements.[33] The report identified UTZ/Rainforest Alliance as the most common third-party certification within those managed programmes, with Organic and Fairtrade remaining small.[33] This demonstrates that Olam combined customer-funded managed programmes, proprietary AtSource services and external standards.

The distinction is material. Cocoa Compass, AtSource and the Olam Farmer Information System are company systems and programmes. They can operate together—for example, a Rainforest Alliance-certified farmer group can be managed through OFIS and supported under Cocoa Compass—but they make different claims about assurance, service delivery and performance.

Olam’s farmer data infrastructure was OFIS, the Olam Farmer Information System. In 2022, Olam reported more than 580,000 farmers registered on OFIS, with data including farm location, nearest schools and yields.[47] It also reported that close to 700,000 farmers’ sustainability programmes were managed through OFIS. The broader figures are not cocoa-only, but they demonstrate the scale of the platform Olam used across its supply base.[47]

OFIS supported individualised farm interventions. Olam stated in 2019 that it had issued more than 64,000 tailored Farm Development Plans intended to increase farmer productivity and income.[45] Farm Development Plans are farm-specific improvement plans informed by data on yields, production costs, household income composition and land size. Olam said it was developing OFIS to analyse those variables and identify barriers to living income in its direct cocoa supply chain.[33]

Olam also reported sustainability premiums. In its 2019 Cocoa Compass announcement, it stated that it had paid USD 136 million in sustainability premiums to farmers and farming cooperatives in partnership with customers.[45] The announced total demonstrates substantial financial transfers through its cocoa programmes. The statement did not provide a period, per-farmer distribution or country allocation.

Olam linked those premiums to wider farmer services. It reported training 108,000 farmers in Good Agricultural Practices and 115,000 farmers in deforestation awareness in 2019/20.[33] It also reported distributing 3.3 million cocoa seedlings and 740,000 trees for agroforestry and income diversification.[33] These interventions combined farm rehabilitation, yield-oriented support, forest-risk education and diversification options.

In its 2024 Cocoa & Forests Initiative reporting, ofi reported distributing 815,812 multi-purpose trees in Côte d’Ivoire and 469,481 in Ghana through direct investment since 2018.[52]

Olam’s direct relationships with selected farmer groups were commercially deep. Its 2022 annual report stated that it purchased more than 75% of the total production of each of ten selected Farmer Groups.[47] Six groups were in Côte d’Ivoire; the remaining four were in Ghana, Ecuador, Papua New Guinea and Indonesia.[47] The report did not identify all groups as cooperatives, so they should be described as Farmer Groups as Olam did. The arrangement shows that Olam could have a dominant purchasing relationship with specific supplier groups without owning their farms.

The company reported environmental performance measures for those ten selected Farmer Groups. In 2021, greenhouse-gas natural-capital costs from crop-residue management fell 9%, while procured cocoa-bean volumes rose 7%.[47] Five groups reduced fertiliser-related greenhouse-gas costs per tonne and eight reduced land-use-change-related costs per tonne.[47] Olam attributed the overall lower land-use-change costs per tonne to lower deforestation and higher yield in 2021.[47] These are selected-supplier sustainability indicators, not company-wide farmer-income results.

Olam also used village savings and loans associations. Its cocoa teams in Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Nigeria supported approximately 740 active VSLAs with more than 44,500 members in 2022, around 75% of whom were women.[47] VSLAs provide community-managed savings and credit; they can support farm investment, alternative businesses and household resilience. The figures represent association membership, not necessarily the number of cocoa farmers receiving a specified cash benefit.

Olam’s cocoa programmes included child-labour monitoring and remediation. In 2019, Olam reported systems covering 95,000 cocoa farmers in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana.[45] A later account reported that CLMRS covered 183,000 households, about 18% of the more than one million households Olam worked with, and identified approximately 12,000 child-labour cases in 2019/20, with roughly 8,000 in remediation.[33] In its 2023 annual report, Olam said CLMRS covered all nine direct cocoa-sourcing countries and, across the systems, more than 260,000 farmer households.[46]

Olam’s deforestation and traceability programme made its direct-chain model more operational. In 2019, Cocoa Compass set 2020 targets of 100% deforestation monitoring and 100% traceability in its direct cocoa supply chain.[48] Olam stated that it achieved 100% traceability in its sustainable supply chain in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana under its 2020 traceability commitment.[45] It also stated that it had achieved 100% traceability for directly sourced cocoa across its global supply chain in 2020.[49]

Those claims refer to specified supply-chain scopes—sustainable supply in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, and global direct sourcing—not all Olam cocoa purchased through all routes. The distinction is illustrated by 2020/21 figures cited in a market study: Olam reported 380,000 metric tonnes of traceable cocoa, representing 38% of all beans sourced and 58% of 650,000 metric tonnes directly sourced through in-country operations.[33] Traceable volume and direct-sourced volume were therefore distinct categories in Olam’s own reported activity.

Olam reported that 100% of AtSource cocoa included deforestation monitoring.[33] These metrics show broad direct-chain forest-risk coverage. They do not turn the company’s total procurement into a fully direct or fully mapped supply chain, because direct sourcing is a defined subset of the broader business.

Olam increasingly used farm polygons rather than approximate purchasing-location methods. In 2023, it said its AtSource Digital Footprint Calculator increasingly used actual farm polygons and GPS points in OFIS to calculate land-use-change emissions for farmer groups, replacing a prior approach based on a buying-station point and radius.[48] It reported updated farm polygons for more than 960,000 individual farms.[48] The farm count was group-wide rather than cocoa-specific, but the methodological shift is important: polygons are better suited than buying-station radii to farm-level forest-risk assessment and differentiated supplier management.

Olam also used satellite information at scale. Satelligence reported that Olam’s sustainability team sought satellite-based environmental-risk information for more than 600,000 farms across all cocoa-origin countries.[50] Satellite monitoring can detect tree-cover loss and identify areas requiring investigation, but it depends on reliable farm boundaries and transaction-level links between the mapped farm and the beans delivered. OFIS, bag coding and first-mile transaction records are therefore complementary to satellite data.

Olam’s Digital Direct application addressed another aspect of sourcing integration. The company stated that Digital Direct gave farmers a more direct route to market, bypassed third parties and improved traceability.[47] It was used by 22 business profit centres in 2022, up from 16 in 2021.[47] The measure is group-wide and not cocoa-specific, but it shows Olam’s broader direction of using digital tools to reduce intermediary dependence.

Olam’s living-income strategy was more explicitly assessment-led than its reported outcome figures. Cocoa Compass set a target of enabling 60,000 farmers to achieve living income in 2024 and 150,000 in 2030.[33] Olam’s Living Income Calculator, developed in 2021, had been calibrated to assess living-income gaps across 17 supply chains by 2023.[46] In 2023, ofi and Olam Agri worked with Sustainable Food Lab and the Living Income Community of Practice toward making the tool publicly available and encouraging sector collaboration.[46]

Living income means the net annual income required for a household to afford a decent standard of living in a particular place, including food, housing, education, health care and other essentials. It differs from an international poverty line, which is a lower threshold designed for cross-country poverty measurement. Olam’s 2019 Cocoa Compass announcement illustrated this difference using Côte d’Ivoire benchmarks: it cited USD 2,300 per year as an extreme-poverty line for an average cocoa-growing family and USD 7,300 as a living-income line.[45]

Olam’s evidence therefore shows a strategy centred on identifying income gaps, tailoring farm-development plans, paying premiums, supporting diversified livelihoods and setting household-level living-income targets. It does not provide a reported average before-and-after income change for Cocoa Compass farmers in the supplied disclosures. The company’s stated 2030 target of 150,000 farmers reaching a defined living income remained a future commitment at the end of 2024.[45]

Olam’s West African field support was broader than cocoa-specific programming. In 2023, its field teams reported providing training, agricultural inputs, credit and infrastructure to more than 940,000 farmers in its global sourcing network.[46] In 2024, Olam reported directly supporting 531,737 farmers with livelihood support, including 122,365 women, and providing GAP training to 464,265 farmers.[44] These are group-wide figures; they establish the scale of Olam’s farmer-support machinery but should not be converted into cocoa-cooperative participation numbers.

Olam’s model also connected sustainability to customers. AtSource was designed as a customer proposition with different service levels, including footprint calculations, traceability, certification readiness and community-created programmes.[33] Its managed programmes involved customer investment in sustainability requirements.[33] This arrangement resembles Barry Callebaut’s Cocoa Horizons in one respect: both convert customer demand for sustainable ingredients into funding and interventions in origin countries.

The organisational form differs, because Olam’s offer used the AtSource platform alongside Cocoa Compass and OFIS, whereas Barry Callebaut used Cocoa Horizons as its preferred delivery vehicle.

Olam’s reported customer collaborations included an April 2021 partnership with Mondelēz International to develop a sustainable commercial cocoa farm in Indonesia.[51] The project lay outside West Africa, but it demonstrates the customer-facing reach of the Cocoa Compass model.

In Ghana, ofi’s 2024 Cocoa & Forests Initiative report stated that it worked with Lindt & Sprüngli, Ferrero and the Ghanaian Nature Conservation Research Center in the Kakam landscape on community-led conservation.[52] It trained more than 10,000 farmers and community members there on sustainable land-use practices.[52] The Kakam collaboration establishes ofi relationships with Lindt & Sprüngli and Ferrero, not a Lindt cocoa-supply or finished-chocolate manufacturing award.[52]

The Ghana collaboration shows that ofi had sustainability relationships with two premium chocolate brands in a West African cocoa landscape. It was a conservation and farmer-training collaboration, not a disclosed manufacturing contract. The same distinction applies to the Mondelēz collaboration: customer sustainability partnerships can strengthen commercial relationships and programme credibility, but they do not by themselves identify a processor as the manufacturer or sole cocoa supplier for the customer’s finished chocolate.

**Olam’s distinctive vertical-integration strategy was data-enabled direct sourcing: Cocoa Compass defined farmer and forest outcomes, AtSource translated them into customer offers, and OFIS, farm polygons and transaction systems supplied the origin-level information needed to manage those programmes.** The model gave Olam a strong direct-chain traceability position, while its reported third-party certification proposition remained one component—principally Rainforest Alliance—within a larger proprietary system.[45][48][49][33]

The strategy makes Olam well positioned to provide customers with traceability, footprint and programme-design services tied to farm-level data. It also means comparisons with Barry Callebaut and Cargill must separate a direct-supply-chain achievement from a total-procurement achievement, because all three companies used direct and indirect sourcing pathways.[33][47][28]

Olam launched AtSource as a customer-service product for sustainable sourcing in 2018.[53]

Barry Callebaut combined customer-linked cooperative contracts, Cocoa Horizons funding, farm-service delivery and chocolate-manufacturing integration. Its Côte d’Ivoire three-year cooperative contracts and Labour Groups programme established continuing operational service relationships beyond purchasing and certification claims.[2] Its product measures also remained distinct from sourcing measures: 49.4% of cocoa and chocolate products sold contained 100% sustainable cocoa or chocolate in 2021/22, rising to 55.5% of products containing 100% verified or certified cocoa or chocolate in 2023/24.[5][2]

Cargill’s model was strongest in the integration of cooperative administration and data infrastructure. CMS combined inventory, payments, loans, costs and farm-level bean tracking; CocoaWise aggregated that information into customer-facing reporting.[30][3] Cargill’s 128 Côte d’Ivoire cooperative offices and seven Ghana buying stations gave this digital system a direct operational network through which it could collect data and deliver Cocoa Promise activities.[30] Its commercial differentiator was the ability to present a farm-to-customer information chain alongside industrial ingredient capacity.

Olam’s model had the clearest stated definition of direct sourcing and the strongest reported direct-chain traceability claim. It defined direct supply as buying from farms, Farmer Groups or cooperatives and stated that it achieved 100% direct-chain traceability in 2020.[47][49] Cocoa Compass, AtSource and OFIS formed a more explicitly platform-based architecture than the Barry Callebaut or Cargill models. The commercial value was the ability to design customer sustainability tiers and footprint propositions around farm-level data and direct-supply-chain monitoring.[45][48][33]

The three programmes also differed in their treatment of certification. Barry Callebaut positioned Cocoa Horizons as a preferred impact and customer-delivery vehicle while combining it with external standards.[13][5] Cargill combined independent Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade routes with Promise Verified and customer programmes.[28] Olam positioned Rainforest Alliance as the principal third-party certification in managed programmes while providing AtSource as a proprietary service framework.[33]

**The shared pattern was not farm ownership but managed access to smallholder supply: each processor invested in systems that made farmer, cooperative or first-mile relationships more visible, serviceable and commercially usable.** The differences lay in emphasis: Barry Callebaut used customer-funded cooperative programmes and farm services; Cargill used cooperative administration and customer data tools; Olam used a direct-supply platform combining data, sustainability tiers and farm-development planning.[18][30][45][47][2][3]

The companies’ direct sourcing disclosures cannot be converted into one comparable direct-versus-broker table. Barry Callebaut disclosed direct cooperatives and a Côte d’Ivoire indirect-sourcing estimate from Trase, while Cargill disclosed direct and indirect origin counts and Olam disclosed direct-supply volumes alongside traceable volumes.[24][28][33] These metrics answer different questions: a country-specific indirect share, a count of sourcing origins, and a direct-sourcing volume. The sourcing, processing expenditures and farmer-income comparison is addressed in the next section using those distinct bases rather than manufactured percentage equivalence.

Customer demand provided the commercial logic for these investments. Barry Callebaut reported that customer demand for sustainable and traceable cocoa had grown in recent years.[5] Cargill built CocoaWise as a customer portal and Promise Cocoa as a directly sourced sustainability offer.[34][43] Olam designed AtSource expressly as a client proposition with progressively deeper assurance, footprint, traceability and community-programme features.[33] Each company was therefore competing not only on cocoa ingredients and processing capacity, but on the ability to turn upstream practices into customer-ready evidence and differentiated sourcing propositions.

Premium chocolate manufacturers also had their own requirements. Lindt & Sprüngli identified five responsible-cocoa sourcing principles: long-term supplier partnerships; its own sustainability programme; traceable and transparent supply chains; continuous improvement through monitoring and external verification; and partnerships and collective action.[56] Lindt reported that 100% of its cocoa beans were sourced through its Farming Program or other responsible sourcing programmes by the end of 2024, and that it aimed to reach 100% of all cocoa products—including butter, powder and chocolate mass—by the end of 2025.[57] Processors able to provide reliable identity preservation, segregation, farm mapping and customer-specific programme support were therefore better aligned with this procurement direction.

Lindt’s requirements also show why cocoa butter and powder matter. In 2023, Lindt reported that 72.3% of total sourced volumes had first-mile traceability and that 99.7% of farms participating in its Farming Program were mapped.[56] It described identity preservation for beans from farm to factory door, segregation for responsibly sourced butter, and Rainforest Alliance mass balance as the minimum target for cocoa powder where segregation is more difficult.[56] A processor’s value to a premium manufacturer consequently depends on traceability and chain-of-custody performance across beans, butter, powder and chocolate mass—not merely on farm-level cocoa-bean claims.

Barry Callebaut’s relationships with customers included programmes with Mondelēz International. Cargill’s relevant competitive strengths were its processing and ingredient range. The 2024 cocoa-price crisis increased the value of resilient, documented origin relationships. Barry Callebaut reported average cocoa-bean prices 131% higher in 2023/24 than the prior-year period, in a supply-constrained market.[58] Olam reported that cocoa prices more than doubled in 2024 on West African supply concerns.[4] Lindt described record-high cocoa prices as its principal raw-material challenge in 2024 and cited continuing crop failures from disease.[57] Under such conditions, long-term cooperative engagement, farm services and local processing can support supply continuity, but they cannot remove exposure to the regional crop deficit.

Barry Callebaut’s cooperative contracts, Cocoa Horizons funding and farm services provide a documented route to tailored premium-customer sourcing solutions.[2] Cargill’s CMS, CocoaWise and Promise Cocoa provide a documented route to direct-network transparency and customer-specific data.[30][34][43] Olam’s Cocoa Compass, AtSource and OFIS provide a documented route to direct-chain monitoring, footprint services and managed programmes.[45][48] These are capability advantages in competing for sustainability-sensitive ingredient and outsourcing business, not evidence that a named brand awarded a contract because of them.

A broker purchase is a purchase through a broker or other intermediary. It is narrower than indirect sourcing, which can include purchases through exporters or suppliers, and distinct from traceable cocoa, which identifies cocoa that can be followed through specified supply-chain stages. Olam defines direct cocoa supply as purchases from a farm, Farmer Group or cooperative; its 2020/21 disclosure separately reported direct-sourced and traceable volumes, while Trase estimated indirect sourcing in Côte d’Ivoire for Barry Callebaut and Olam.[47][33][24] Cargill likewise distinguishes direct-supply-chain traceability to the first point of purchase from its wider indirect-origin footprint.[28]

In Côte d’Ivoire, indirect purchasing can involve *pisteurs* and *traitants*, intermediaries who collect and aggregate cocoa from villages.[24]

## Sourcing, processing expenditures, and farmer income

Direct sourcing, origin-country grinding and farmer-income outcomes are related but different measures of vertical integration. Direct sourcing means buying from farmers, farmer groups or cooperatives rather than through intermediaries; origin processing means turning beans into cocoa liquor, butter, powder or other ingredients near the farm; and a living income means the net annual household income needed for a decent standard of living. **These three processors built upstream capability, but only Cargill disclosed comparable facility investments in both Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana. The Royal Tropical Institute reported a 38% increase in net income from cocoa and non-cocoa sources for all Income Accelerator Program households assessed in 2023, including approximately 2,000 households in cooperatives engaged through Cargill.**[60][35][28]

## EUDR compliance responses

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) turned cocoa traceability from a voluntary sustainability feature into a market-access requirement for covered EU trade. For processors, the practical test is not whether a programme can name a cooperative or certify a farmer: it is whether every cocoa-bearing product can be linked to every producing plot, supported by legality and deforestation evidence, risk assessed, and accompanied by a due-diligence statement.

**Claim.** By the end of 2024, Barry Callebaut, Cargill and Olam/ofi had disclosed materially different traceability systems and coverage measures. Those measures demonstrate substantial preparation, especially in direct or programme-linked supply chains, but do not establish equivalent, whole-supply-chain EUDR readiness or quantified exclusions of non-compliant cocoa.

### EUDR requirements and the position at 31 December 2024

Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 covers cocoa beans and cocoa-derived products under CN codes 1801–1806, including cocoa paste, butter, powder and chocolate. A covered cocoa product may be placed or made available on the EU market, or exported from it, only if it is deforestation-free, produced in accordance with the relevant law of the producing country, and covered by a due-diligence statement. These are cumulative conditions: satisfying one does not cure failure on another.[74]

For cocoa, deforestation-free means that the beans were not produced on land subject to deforestation after 31 December 2020. The legality limb is broader than forestry law. It includes applicable rules on land-use rights, environmental protection, third-party rights, labour rights, internationally protected human rights, free, prior and informed consent, and tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs requirements.[74]

The EUDR due-diligence system has three stages. First, the operator collects, organises and retains product, supplier, recipient, producing-country, production-date and plot-geolocation information, with supporting evidence, for five years. Second, it assesses non-compliance risk, including supply-chain complexity, the reliability of evidence, the risk of mixing with unknown-origin cocoa and deforestation prevalence. Third, where risk is more than negligible, it must mitigate it through measures such as further information, surveys, audits, or supplier capacity building and investment.[74]

Plot-level geolocation is therefore the critical EUDR data point. The traceability requirements apply to each batch of relevant commodity, and operators and non-SME traders must trace every relevant commodity to its producing plot before making a product available, placing it on the market or exporting it. Where cocoa in a product came from more than one plot, the operator must provide the geolocation of every contributing plot. For cocoa plots larger than four hectares, geolocation must be a polygon of latitude-and-longitude points to six decimal places that describes the plot perimeter; for plots under four hectares, it may be a polygon or a single coordinate point to six decimal places.[74][75] Deforestation or forest degradation on a plot disqualifies cocoa from that plot from EU placement or export.

Mass-balance chains of custody that mix deforestation-free cocoa with unknown-origin or non-deforestation-free cocoa are not permitted: compliant cocoa must be segregated from those goods at every supply-chain step. If non-compliant cocoa cannot be identified and separated, the whole relevant product is ineligible for EU placement or export.[76][75]

A due-diligence statement (DDS) is the operator’s formal, digital confirmation that diligence was performed and that no more than negligible non-compliance risk was found. The Commission had to establish the DDS information system by 30 December 2024; Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/3084 enabled operators and traders to register and submit statements before the EUDR application date.[74][77]

Certification is useful evidence, but it is not a legal substitute for diligence. The Regulation permits certification and other third-party schemes as complementary risk-assessment inputs only where their information meets EUDR information requirements; it leaves responsibility with the operator.[74][76]

The dates require careful separation. Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 was published in the Official Journal on 9 June 2023 and entered into force on 29 June 2023. Its original general application date for the principal obligations was 30 December 2024, with qualifying micro- and small-undertaking operators generally scheduled for 30 June 2025.[74]

That original timetable was overtaken in December 2024. Regulation (EU) 2024/3234 was published on 23 December 2024 and amended the application dates: the principal obligations were to apply from 30 December 2025 generally, and from 30 June 2026 for qualifying micro- and small-undertaking operators, subject to the specified timber-product exception. Thus, by the 31 December 2024 cutoff, the relevant compliance timetable had been deferred by one year; entry into force in June 2023 did not itself mean that cocoa due-diligence obligations had begun applying.[77]

The country-benchmarking timetable also moved. All countries had been assigned standard risk on 29 June 2023, and the December 2024 amendment required the Commission to publish low- and high-risk country or sub-country classifications by 30 June 2025. Until then, sourcing could not rely on a low-risk designation to remove the need for full risk assessment and mitigation.[77][78]

**The regulatory consequence is clear: certification, farm mapping and a traceability portal each help, but EU eligibility depends on an operator’s product-specific, plot-level evidence and no-more-than-negligible-risk determination.**[74]

Geolocation means latitude and longitude coordinates for the producing plot. For plots larger than four hectares, the EUDR requires a polygon describing the plot perimeter.

There is no quantity or value threshold below which an Annex I cocoa product is outside the Regulation. Operators may collect geolocation and legality information directly or through cooperatives, certification bodies, national traceability systems or other intermediaries, but remain legally responsible for its correctness and for verifying illegality risk.[75]

The due-diligence statement information system is the repository for submitted statements, not a farm-mapping tool; operators must obtain plot geolocation outside that system. Non-SME operators must also publicly report annually on their due-diligence system.[75]

### Barry Callebaut

Barry Callebaut’s response combined farm mapping, geospatial risk assessment, satellite monitoring, first-mile procurement controls and Ghanaian bag traceability. In fiscal year 2023/24, it mapped 669,174 cocoa plots, 22.2% more than in the preceding year, and stated that cocoa sourced from those farms had farm-level traceability.[2]

Its method identifies supplying farmers, collects farm-size and polygon data, and overlays those polygons with land-use, land-cover and tree-cover-loss information. Barry Callebaut then classifies sourcing areas as high, medium or low risk and tailors sourcing and mitigation responses accordingly. The company says this method applies to both direct and indirect supply chains, although its quantified coverage is principally for direct sourcing.[2][1]

The company disclosed two different direct-chain coverage measures for 2023/24. It reported that 83.0% of plots in its direct cocoa supply chain were mapped, compared with 78.9% in 2022/23. Separately, it said its cocoa farmer database covered 80% of the direct cocoa supply chain and delivered farm-level traceability. The first is a share of plots; the second is database coverage of the direct chain. They are not interchangeable mapping-completion percentages.[2]

Barry Callebaut expanded its deforestation methodology after 2022/23 beyond Cocoa Horizons volumes to all other directly sourced cocoa in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana, and broadened its forest scope in anticipation of EUDR. For non-direct volumes, it stated that suppliers are expected to follow the same protocol; that is a supplier requirement rather than a reported indirect-chain completion rate.[2][1]

The company’s due-diligence toolbox includes high-resolution satellite imagery to identify forest disturbance in and around mapped plots. It also uses regionalised, evidence-based yield controls: estimates based on location, weather, soil and tree age cap the cocoa volume that can plausibly come from a plot, helping detect infiltration of untraceable or deforestation-related cocoa. Barry Callebaut embedded yield-control thresholds in its first-mile system for automatic procurement-stage risk mitigation.[2][79]

In Ghana, Barry Callebaut launched digital bag traceability in fiscal year 2023/24. A barcode attached to each bag records movements, giving the company farmer-to-factory tracking and digitising each step in the bag’s journey.[2]

The company reported that 46.5% of sourced raw-material volume was demonstrated not to contribute to deforestation in 2023/24, compared with 34.1% in 2022/23. Within that result, 78.0% of directly sourced cocoa was demonstrated not to contribute to deforestation. Barry Callebaut stated that the remainder did not necessarily imply deforestation; for part of it, traceability and/or deforestation monitoring had not yet been implemented across all sources. These are deforestation-monitoring measures, not EUDR-compliance rates or all-cocoa mapping rates.[2]

Barry Callebaut had also used exclusion before EUDR. In March 2019, it said mapping had led it to exclude purchases from cocoa farms wholly or partly within protected-area boundaries. It did not disclose a tonnage or percentage excluded. Likewise, its 2023/24 reporting describes controls designed to prevent non-traceable or deforestation-related cocoa from entering supply, but does not quantify actual EUDR-related volume exclusions.[80][2]

In 2023/24, Barry Callebaut consulted global NGOs on EUDR compliance and grievance handling, and stated that it would cover technical-compliance costs for farmers and farmer groups. It also said it would be EUDR compliant by the application date; that was a stated readiness commitment rather than a disclosed completed compliance result.[1][2]

In the first half of FY2023/24, Barry Callebaut said it had formed a cross-functional traceability-and-segregation team and expected to meet EUDR requirements by the end of calendar 2024.[109]

**Barry Callebaut’s position is strongest in detailed direct-chain mapping and procurement controls, but its disclosed 83.0% and 80% measures do not establish completed mapping or EUDR qualification across indirect and total cocoa purchases.**[2][1]

Barry Callebaut had begun extending traceability controls to indirect supply before the EUDR was adopted. In fiscal year 2020/21, it finalised a procedure for selected indirect suppliers to undertake farmer mapping and census activities and piloted it with selected indirect suppliers in Côte d’Ivoire. In 2019/20, it also publicly disclosed, and regularly updated, its direct-cocoa-supplier map for Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Cameroon.[81]

Cocoa Horizons is a sustainability programme; participation in it does not itself establish an EUDR-compliance determination.

Barry Callebaut reported that 24.5% of raw-material volume was proven free from deforestation in 2021/22, rising to 34.1% in 2022/23 and 46.5% in 2023/24.[1]

As of December 2020, Barry Callebaut reported that all cocoa from its direct supply chain in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire was traceable to the farm, while it was working with selected third-party suppliers to improve indirect-supply-chain traceability.[62] In 2021/22, Barry Callebaut mapped 399,413 farms, representing 79.7% of farms in its direct cocoa supply chain, and reported farm-level traceability for cocoa volumes from those mapped farms.[5]

Barry Callebaut reported that its FY2021/22 farm-mapping KPI included more than 12,000 farmers in the indirect supply chain; their inclusion did not make the associated cocoa directly sourced.[5]

As of its July 2024 update, Barry Callebaut’s map of direct suppliers covered 100% of its direct supply chain across six cocoa origins; the map identified warehouses, cooperatives and districts rather than every producing plot.[2][1][110]

In fiscal year 2023/24, Barry Callebaut reported traceability to farm level for 83% of cocoa volumes in its direct supply chain.[16] A separately reported 2024 figure was 91% of cocoa farmers’ plots mapped.[64]

Barry Callebaut engaged Cognizant to develop a solution to trace cocoa beans to the individual-field level for both direct and indirect suppliers.

### Cargill

Cargill’s EUDR preparation rests on an integrated system of farm polygon mapping, barcode-based cooperative traceability, GIS deforestation screening and customer-facing data tools. It said it had implemented an EUDR due-diligence management system, supported by data and technology platforms, to ensure future compliance.[82]

CocoaWise is Cargill’s customer-facing portal, while CocoaWise 360 centralises sustainability and first-mile traceability data gathered through its digital tools. The portal can show purchasers their sustainable deliveries, cocoa origins, supply chains, programme investments and reported impacts. These capabilities provide a practical route for customers to obtain traceability evidence, although they do not by themselves prove a DDS conclusion for a given EU-bound parcel.[34][43]

At origin, CocoaWise BeanTracker uses bag barcodes to associate cooperative cocoa with individual farms and help prevent beans from deforested areas entering the supply chain. The Cooperative Management System (CMS), established in Côte d’Ivoire from 2018 and powered by Farmforce, connects cooperative-held beans to farms while also supporting inventory, payments and cooperative financial management.[34][30]

Cargill described its Cooperative Management System as introduced in Côte d’Ivoire in 2017; it used barcode scans to trace cocoa bags from farms to Cargill warehouses.[6]

Cargill also uses GIS software to overlay mapped farm polygons with Global Forest Watch data whenever a farm is mapped or remapped, assessing significant forest loss. This is a deforestation-risk-screening tool, not merely a record of farm identity.[40]

Cargill’s 2024 disclosures distinguish direct-chain traceability from Promise-programme mapping. Its crop-year 2023/24 dashboard reported that 100% of cocoa in its direct supply chain was traceable to the first point of purchase. Within the global Cargill Cocoa Promise supply chain, 117,898 tonnes—76.6% of sustainable volume—were managed through the digital First Mile Traceability system; the dashboard reported 100% for Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana and 42% for Cameroon.[28]

The same report stated that 257,856 cocoa plots, or 92.2% of plots in the global Promise supply chain, were polygon mapped. It also reported that 183,027 farmers, or 91.9%, had every cocoa plot polygon mapped. In West Africa, Cargill reported that 92% of Promise-supply-chain farmers were polygon mapped and monitored for deforestation risk, and that 99% of mapped plots showed no primary-forest loss since 2014.[28]

For crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported polygon mapping of 99.9% of Cocoa Promise supply-chain plots in Côte d’Ivoire and 96.0% of Cocoa Promise supply-chain plots in Ghana.[28]

In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported that digital First Mile Traceability covered 78% of Cocoa Promise farmers in Côte d’Ivoire and 61% of Cocoa Promise farmers in Ghana.[28]

Cargill’s crop-year 2023/24 Cocoa Promise KPI table reported 143,554 polygon-mapped plots in Côte d’Ivoire, or 99.9% of plots in that programme scope, and 81,406 in Ghana, or 96.0%. It reported that all cocoa plots were polygon-mapped for 130,983 Côte d’Ivoire farmers (99.9%) and 30,302 Ghanaian farmers (93.1%) in the respective programme populations.[28]

In crop year 2023/24, Cargill reported that 101,735 Cocoa Promise farmers in Côte d’Ivoire (78%) and 19,817 in Ghana (61%) delivered cocoa through First Mile Traceability; its global programme total was 133,352 farmers (68%). These delivery-participation rates measure something different from the programme’s polygon-mapping rates.[28]

Those results indicate a major expansion over earlier disclosed programme metrics. Cargill reported in 2019–20 that 64% of Cocoa Promise farmers were mapped, using GPS polygons as its reporting standard. The later 92.2% plot and 91.9% fully mapped-farmer rates cannot be treated as a mechanically comparable trend, because the earlier measure was farmers participating in Cocoa Promise and the 2023/24 metrics used different plot and farmer denominators.[6][28]

Cargill disclosed a specific exclusion-oriented control in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire. In addition to Rainforest Alliance or Promise Verified certification, it activated measures to exclude, as far as possible, farmers whose cocoa plots were not all polygon mapped or showed signs of deforestation since December 2014. It did not disclose tonnes or a percentage of cocoa excluded under that rule.[28]

Cargill also stated a 2030 goal of full farmer-to-plant first-mile traceability. That target confirms that its direct-chain first-purchase traceability should not be read as completed end-to-end traceability for all cocoa or as a completed EUDR result.[43][6]

**Cargill disclosed the most specific 2024 farmer-screening exclusion policy in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire and high mapping within Cocoa Promise, but neither measure quantifies the excluded cocoa volume nor establishes EUDR completion for total Cargill cocoa.**[28][43]

Cargill reported 117,898 tonnes, or 76.6%, of Cocoa Promise sustainable volume managed through digital First Mile Traceability in crop year 2023/24; subtracting that reported volume from the implied total leaves 36,016 tonnes not managed through that system. This is the remaining volume within that specific Cocoa Promise sustainable-volume measure, rather than a measure of Cargill’s total cocoa procurement.[28]

Cargill reported that more than 60% of its cocoa beans were sourced directly from farms or farmer groups; it sourced indirect volumes through intermediaries because local regulatory environments did not permit 100% direct sourcing everywhere.[67] From 2022 to June 2023, 72% of farmers in its direct supply chain had been fully GPS-polygon-mapped.

The corresponding reported farmer-mapping rates were 96% in Côte d’Ivoire, covering 134,791 farms and 550,429 hectares, and 94% in Ghana, covering 73,844 farms and 193,141 hectares.[67]

Cargill’s Promise Cocoa Verified scheme requires all cocoa plots to be polygon mapped, to show no signs of deforestation and not to be located in protected areas; third-party audits externally verify compliance with those Cocoa Promise requirements.[83] Cargill also reported that 6%–22% of its mapped farmers, depending on country, had duplicate polygons or polygons overlapping another polygon by at least 20%, and made resolution of those geometry issues a priority.[67]

## Competitive positioning in premium chocolate contracts

Premium-chocolate manufacturing contracts depend on more than bean availability. Buyers need assured quality, tailored ingredients, continuity of supply, and increasingly traceable cocoa that supports their own sustainability and regulatory claims.Judgment — Vertical integration matters competitively when a processor can connect origin-level farmer services and data to segregated ingredients, product development and long-term customer delivery; it does not itself prove a contract award.

### Lindt

Lindt & Sprüngli’s stated supplier model favours long-term strategic suppliers that implement its Farming Program, meet commercial and quality terms, and provide traceable, transparent supply chains with monitoring and external verification. By end-2024, Lindt said 100% of its cocoa beans came through its Farming Program or other responsible sourcing programs; it targeted 100% first-mile traceability—tracking from farm to first buyer—for all cocoa products by end-2025.[56][57]

Barry Callebaut offers disclosed customer-manufacturing capacity and an origin-linked sustainability proposition. It reported three-year contracts with 90% of the Côte d’Ivoire cooperatives from which it directly sourced in 2023/24, and 55.5% of cocoa and chocolate products sold contained 100% sustainable cocoa or chocolate. Its November 2024 presentation identified traceability and segregation—keeping compliant material physically separate—as EUDR work, while its partnership agreements can run for three to ten years and include annual purchase commitments. No specific Barry Callebaut–Lindt relationship in the 2018–2024 record is established.[2][88][10]

Cargill’s fit rests on CocoaWise, launched in 2020, which provides customers origin and sustainability information for beans from Cargill’s direct supply chain used in their products, plus reporting on deliveries, supply chains, finance and program impacts. Cargill also presents Promise Cocoa as its premium sustainability offering. No specific Cargill–Lindt relationship in the 2018–2024 record is established.[34][6][43][29]

ofi offers an integrated model spanning origin and destination manufacturing, sourcing, value-added processing, innovation and customer solutions; it says this helps customers address provenance and regulatory-reporting needs.[4] Olam stated that ofi’s value chain includes its own farming operations, farm-gate origination and manufacturing facilities, and that AtSource enables customers to track a product’s social and environmental footprint from the farmer group to the factory gate.[4][89] Olam listed Lindt & Sprüngli among the major chocolate-confectionery businesses it supplies; this is a disclosed supply relationship, not a finished-chocolate manufacturing award.[90] Olam also reported a 2023 Ecuador pilot with Lindt involving approximately 90 smallholder farmers: 45% applied dynamic agroforestry and the remainder standard agroforestry.[55] **The competitive implication is that all three can address Lindt’s stated supplier criteria; ofi has a disclosed Lindt supply relationship and agroforestry collaboration, while the record here identifies no Lindt relationship for Barry Callebaut or Cargill.**[91][56][2][6][89][55]

The premium-chocolate market is distinct from bulk cocoa: specialty cocoa serves the high-range segment and fine-flavour cocoa the highest-quality products. CBI says Swiss premium dark-chocolate buyers want beans that are clean, consistent and traceable and require proof of cocoa origin and flavour; it says origin and terroir stories are becoming more important.[61] Olam cited a forecast that premium chocolate would grow 9% between 2019 and 2024 to US$33.15 billion, driven by consumer interest in origins, purity, taste, flavour and ethical credentials.[87][92] Multinational traders and processors are the principal channel through which large brands buy cocoa, and Barry Callebaut, Cargill and Olam together accounted for more than half of global cocoa sales in 2021.[61][87]

Barry Callebaut stated in its FY2023/24 results call that it was well ahead in EUDR preparation, that its technology could handle the regulation’s complexity, and that customers turned to it for assistance. It also described its decades-long physical presence in cocoa-origin countries as an asset alongside global-scale benefits. These statements add a customer-service and origin-access dimension to Barry Callebaut’s traceability-and-segregation proposition.[93]

Cargill’s customer proposition also includes its House of Chocolate, opened on 31 May 2022 for customer inspiration, co-creation and innovation. Cargill describes its cocoa-and-chocolate offer as combining security, sustainability and sensory expertise in a bean-to-bar supply chain.[94]

In results reported on 28 February 2019, Olam said its first AtSource trial supplied about 17 large global customers. Olam said AtSource, AtSource Plus and AtSource Infinity differentiated its offering, produced a larger share of customers’ spending and greater customer stickiness; it said AtSource Plus enabled a pricing premium.[95]

### Ferrero

Ferrero’s tier-1 cocoa-supplier lists for September 2022–August 2023 and September 2023–August 2024 name both Barry Callebaut and Cargill; the September 2023–August 2024 list does not name Olam/ofi. These listings establish commercial cocoa-supply relationships for Barry Callebaut and Cargill in both periods, not a premium chocolate-manufacturing award, supplier share or contract value.[31][64]

Mondelēz and other customers supported its Labor Groups program, which expanded to 43,631 hectares across all West African cocoa-producing regions in 2023/24; the program provides farmers with labour support and creates paid work for local workers.[2] The company also says its strategic customer agreements address quality, quantity, pricing, service levels, innovation and ethics, and it added around 35,000 tonnes of annualized volume through new outsourcing contracts and partnerships in FY2023/24.[1][15]

Olam listed Ferrero among the major chocolate-confectionery businesses it supplies; this is a disclosed cocoa supply relationship, not a finished-chocolate manufacturing award. Olam’s customer-facing proposition combines Cocoa Compass with AtSource, a three-tier offering of sustainability data, metrics, insights and impact; ofi states that traceable, sustainable, high-quality ingredients underpin customer loyalty.[55][87][96]

On 21 April 2021, the companies announced an Indonesian collaboration to create what they described as the world’s largest sustainable commercial cocoa farm; this was a sustainability partnership, not a disclosed finished-chocolate manufacturing award.[51] Olam Cocoa also partnered with Mondelēz International and other organisations in Pará, Brazil, to develop cocoa-based agroforestry supply chains, targeting 1,250 hectares by 2022; participating farmers could receive a cocoa price premium for zero-deforestation and restoration commitments.[48][90] In Ghana, ofi has been a key partner since 2020 in the Landscapes for Cocoa Livelihoods pilot: six cocoa communities are incentivised to protect more than 1,000 hectares in the Tano Ofin forest reserve, with Mondelēz payments for meeting conservation targets directed to shared community infrastructure.[91] Olam also listed Mondelēz International among the major chocolate-confectionery businesses it supplies.[55]

No specific Cargill–Mondelēz relationship in the 2018–2024 record is established. CocoaWise can give customers product-delivery, origin, supply-chain, investment and impact information, but the disclosed portal is a direct-supply-chain transparency tool rather than a reported EUDR mapping or exclusion system.[43] **Thus, Mondelēz has documented sustainability partnerships with Barry Callebaut and ofi, but neither relationship establishes that either processor won a premium manufacturing award.**[43][2][51]

Judgment — On disclosed 2018–2024 evidence, the competitive-positioning ranking for premium manufacturing work is: first Barry Callebaut, second Cargill, third ofi. The deciding criterion is disclosed capacity to manufacture chocolate and other customer-facing products, and demonstrated conversion of that capacity into outsourcing business; origin integration, sustainability and traceability are supporting criteria. The ranking does not treat tier-1 cocoa supply, sustainability partnerships or ofi’s named-customer supply statement as finished-chocolate manufacturing awards.[88][15][66][46][31][55][28][21][51][91]

Barry Callebaut ranks first because it combines cooperative purchasing arrangements, customer-funded West African services, traceability-and-segregation work, and measurable outsourcing traction of about 35,000 tonnes annualized volume in FY2023/24. Its FY2023/24 Food Manufacturing and Gourmet businesses recorded 397 kMT of volume and an end-to-end portfolio covering coatings, decorations, fillings and inclusions. In November 2023, Barry Callebaut identified deeper outsourcing partnerships as a strategic growth priority and said it aimed to win two thirds of outsourcing-partnership volumes expected to come to market in the coming years.[88]  Cargill ranks second because its US$100 million Yopougon expansion and US$13 million Tema expansion, completed in 2021, paired origin grinding with CocoaWise reporting and the clearest named Ferrero-linked programs.[12] In Ghana in 2019, ten local processors handled 259,416 metric tons of cocoa beans; Cargill held about 26% of domestic cocoa processing and, together with Niche Cocoa Industries Limited’s approximately 17% share, accounted for 43%.[3][66][97][2][88][43][28]

ofi ranks third only on this criterion: it has strong end-to-end capabilities and a Mondelēz partnership, but less disclosed evidence tying EUDR-oriented implementation to premium-manufacturing conversion. Its 2022–end-2024 Côte d’Ivoire project for an unnamed large chocolate brand nevertheless planted more than 825,000 shade and fruit trees, reported 80% survival, and estimated sequestration above 32,000 tonnes of carbon emissions.[73][51][46][90][91][55]

1. Treat Barry Callebaut as the strongest current outsourcing-partnership candidate, subject to customer-specific due diligence.
2. Treat Cargill as particularly credible where Ferrero-style origin services, processing capacity and customer reporting are decisive.
3. Re-rank the firms if named finished-chocolate manufacturing awards, delivered costs, verified farmer outcomes, or comparable EUDR-ready volumes show another processor materially ahead; comparable disclosure of mapped supply, exclusions, contract volumes or renewals would also change the ordering. [15] [88] [43] [4] [73]

ofi ranks third only on this criterion. It disclosed the capacity to provide customers a complete package from single ingredients to value-added finished products, using manufacturing in origin and destination markets, but the record provides less evidence of conversion into disclosed outsourcing business. Its Mondelēz activities were sustainability partnerships, while its statement that it supplies Lindt, Ferrero and Mondelēz establishes supply rather than finished-chocolate manufacturing awards.

Barry Callebaut’s Cocoa Horizons programme and Forever Chocolate strategy give customers access to programme participation, traceability information and, where a specification calls for it, certified cocoa. Cargill’s vertically integrated offer spans cocoa liquor, butter, powder and industrial chocolate within its wider food-ingredients business. ofi’s OFIS links farmer, farm and delivery information in the first mile, complementing the AtSource customer-data offer.

Olam said in its 2020 Annual Report that “Training and support for farmers secures volumes and quality, increases loyalty versus competitors.” In a September 2021 investor presentation, ofi described a strategic partnership with an unnamed leading global food company involving multiple value-added solutions and projects under discussion across nuts, cocoa and dairy, with cocoa-bean supply to that customer increasing sevenfold in the past five years.[87] The same presentation reported 9.7% revenue CAGR growth with ofi’s top global chocolate company, its number-one customer, from 2017A to 2020A.[98]

Company-wide direct-sourced versus broker-purchased cocoa percentages remain open where processors report traceability, programme participation or supplier-group coverage instead of purchase-channel volumes. Comparable capital expenditure by company and by processing facility in Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana remains open. Comparable before-and-after farmer household-income changes attributable to each programme remain open. A definitive EUDR readiness ranking would require common 2024 measures for farm or plot mapping, compliant-volume screening and volumes excluded from EU-bound supply.
