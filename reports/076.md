Rank first a cooperative-controlled Farmforce implementation, subject to a paid country-and-language proof of delivery, with Fairtrace and Rainforest Alliance’s own systems retained as certification transaction layers. Farmforce is the strongest documented option for offline, enumerator-led Android mapping and quality control in Ethiopia’s difficult coffee terrain, but it is not a feature-phone self-service platform and its prices, Colombian and Indonesian delivery capacity, and direct certification-portal integrations must be contracted.

Koltiva is the strongest alternative where its confirmed operations in Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia and farmer-facing app ecosystem outweigh the absence of documented offline workflow, mountain-accuracy results and public pricing.

AtSource with OFIS is viable only where an ofi/Olam buying relationship makes it available, while Fairtrace is essential for Fairtrade transaction assurance but cannot operate farm records, plots or offline first-mile traceability.

For a low-budget baseline, ODK Cloud Standard’s published subscription is US$0.4776 per farmer per year at 5,000 farmers, excluding devices, implementation, mapping, staffing, training and support, but it requires the cooperative to build and run certification workflows itself.[1][2][3][4][5]

## Compare AtSource, FairTrace, and other solutions

**Selection principle.** This comparison assesses seven procurement tests: usability for farmers with basic phones; accurate GPS plot mapping in mountainous terrain; Rainforest Alliance integration; Fairtrade integration; multi-language support; offline functionality; and cost per farmer. Evidence is current to 20 November 2026. A basic phone is used here for calls and SMS rather than an Android application; an enumerator Android device is a smartphone or tablet used by trained field staff. Offline capture means recording data without a connection and synchronising it after reconnection. A farm-location point identifies one location, whereas a plot-boundary polygon is a closed series of points defining the plot area.[6][5][7][8] The cooperative should buy a system for the work farmers and staff actually need to do: collect and correct farm records offline; map and quality-check plots; connect farm, batch, payment and certification records; and export the data required by buyers and certification bodies. A buyer-facing sustainability dashboard, a certification transaction portal and a field-data application are complementary layers, not interchangeable products.

Judgment — For 5,000 farmers across three countries, the practical architecture is normally: one cooperative-controlled first-mile farm and traceability database; trained enumerators with Android devices for mapping and inspections; a simple farmer communications channel that reaches feature phones; and separate official certification portals for Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade transactions and reporting. The decisive procurement tests are country support, field workflow, export formats, data rights, implementation cost and recurring support—not a vendor’s general sustainability claim.

### Olam’s AtSource

AtSource is an ofi/Olam sustainable-sourcing and buyer-information system. It provides product-origin traceability and sustainability data for customers, and ofi describes it as a sustainability management system for its supply chains. AtSource was created in 2018 and is governed by ofi on behalf of Olam Group companies.[9][10][11][12]

AtSource covers farms, farmer groups, intermediaries, facilities and processing sites, using farm-level data to provide ofi customers with social and environmental footprint information.[12][18]

AtSource is not the same thing as OFIS, the Olam Farmer Information System. OFIS is the first-mile field-data system: ofi field teams use it to collect farm and community-location data, manage training, and track financing, input distribution and purchases; ofi then feeds OFIS data into AtSource for customer supply-chain visibility.[13][6]

This distinction decides usability. OFIS is designed around field teams and an Android application, whereas AtSource is presented as a system for buyers and other ofi supply-chain users. OFIS was developed to address farmers’ limited internet access, but the described users entering data are field teams rather than individual farmers.[13][6][14]

OFIS has a documented farmer-contact feature that can reach basic phones: its SMS function allows messages on pricing, weather and agricultural advice to be sent directly to farmers. That is useful for outbound notifications on prices, weather and agricultural advice, but it is not a basic-phone workflow for farm registration, GPS mapping, internal inspections or traceability data entry.[6]

OFIS’s described first-mile functions also include digital payments; its online tools support analysis and mapping.[6][48]

OFIS uses a multi-language Android OS application, and its 2017 brochure describes the application as designed for remote areas. The brochure does not identify the languages, so the cooperative cannot treat Spanish, Amharic, Oromo, Tigrinya or Bahasa Indonesia as contractually available until ofi confirms them for each country.[6]

OFIS can record GPS points on the ground for farms and infrastructure, create farm GPS maps, and calculate distances to points of interest. It can also support end-to-end traceability through bag coding and provide an Internal Management System capability for farmer groups and cooperatives.[6]

AtSource’s own methodology includes GPS-mapping coverage as an optional farmer-group metric for high-risk groups. It also requires a compulsory Landscape Deforestation Risk Index based on deforestation in the surrounding landscape during the prior five years; a result above 4% is classified as high risk.[12]

The available AtSource material identifies GPS coordinates and landscape-risk monitoring, but it does not provide a mountain-coffee plot-boundary accuracy result. This matters because a point identifying a farm and a polygon defining a farm-unit boundary are different data products: certification and EUDR workflows often require the second.[6][12][15]

AtSourceV is traceable to the farmer-group level or ofi estates. AtSource+ adds supply-chain-specific primary data, granular metrics and annual third-party verification, but the documented traceability endpoint remains farmer group or estate rather than every individual coffee plot.[10][11][12]

AtSource’s sustainability workflow is substantial within an ofi/Olam supply chain. It uses the Sustainability Assessment Checklist for annual self-assessments and third-party verification; outcomes inform action plans. Its requirements include member and farm lists, documented procedures, training, production records, grievance channels, labour-risk mechanisms, pesticide records, climate-risk assessment and sustainability metrics.[16][12]

AtSource’s land-use requirements call for boundaries around valuable natural areas, protection against encroachment on critical ecosystems, and no conversion of natural forest since January 2014.[16]

AtSource can support buyer sustainability reporting and due diligence. It supplies country-risk screening, product-footprint information and sustainability performance assessments; AtSource+ uses primary data from across the supply chain, including more than 100 agricultural, processing and distribution data points for its footprint calculator.[10][11][17][12]

AtSource has a documented connection with Rainforest Alliance only in a limited sense: one optional living-income metric uses a tool developed by Agri-Logic following an initial Rainforest Alliance assignment and made available to other interested parties through TRACT. That provenance does not make AtSource a Rainforest Alliance Certification Platform integration.[12]

AtSource also appears alongside Rainforest Alliance in buyer sourcing. A coffee-sector report states that Nescafé worked at scale with both Olam AtSource Verified and Rainforest Alliance, and that 75% of Nescafé coffee was sourced through 10 sustainability standards, primarily 4C, and traceable to farmer-group level. This describes a sourcing relationship, not a data exchange or certification-system integration.[14]

AtSource Entry Verified and AtSource Plus were accepted as second-party Baseline Coffee Code Equivalent schemes for Global Coffee Platform reporting in February 2021. That recognition applies to GCP sustainable-coffee-purchase reporting; it is not Rainforest Alliance or Fairtrade certification.[18]

AtSource’s access model is a central procurement constraint. AtSource is open to ofi and Olam Group product supply chains, and Tier 1 and Tier 2 access are allocated to organisations purchasing AtSourceV or AtSource+ products from ofi.[17][12]

AtSource therefore fits a cooperative selling through an ofi/Olam relationship. It is not presented as a stand-alone farm-management product that an independent cooperative can simply license for all members.[10][12]

The AtSource terms provide for accounts and subscription access by agreement, but ofi retains discretion over account acceptance and can change or restrict access. The terms also say that ofi retains ownership of Individual Data shared with users, while each party acts as an independent Data Controller for personal data it processes.[17]

Judgment — A cooperative should treat data ownership, rights to export member and plot data, post-contract access, correction rights, country hosting, and buyer visibility as contractual requirements. These terms matter especially where the cooperative supplies data that buyers will use for commercial claims and certification evidence.

No published AtSource subscription price, implementation fee or per-farmer price is provided in the cited materials. A cost per farmer for 5,000 members therefore cannot be calculated from a published price.[10][17]

Country coverage is also not interchangeable. OFIS had more than 550,000 registered farmers in over 30 countries as of 2021, and the FAO profile lists Indonesia among its adoption countries; the cited OFIS material does not name Colombia or Ethiopia. Its brochure refers broadly to coffee smallholders in Asia, Africa and South America.[19][6]

An earlier OFIS brochure reported more than 100,000 farmers in 21 countries and stated a target of 500,000 farmers by 2020.[6][48]

**What AtSource decides.** AtSource is a viable buyer-linked sustainability-assurance arrangement if the cooperative has or seeks an ofi/Olam commercial relationship. It should not be selected as the cooperative’s independent, all-country farmer-facing system until ofi contractually commits to country deployment, language coverage, offline workflow, plot-polygon quality controls, farmer-data rights, implementation scope and price.

ofi’s cooperative model includes OFIS data capture by cooperative managers, warehouse transaction capture in the Digital Supplier Engagement (DSE) app, and a unique Lot ID for every dispatch. ofi states that OFIS and DSE information provides full traceability for the first mile of products it procures from cooperatives.[20]

OFIS’s documented mobile-app and portal functions are farmer registration, identification, farm mapping, activity planning, field activities, farmer surveys and training. Field teams collect farm GPS locations and yields. These functions strengthen OFIS’s role as the operational first-mile system beneath AtSource.[21]

ofi also distinguishes two other components from AtSource and OFIS: ofi Direct, a farmer mobile app for direct transactions, advice, financing, supplies and crop traceability; and ofi Track and Trace, which integrates digital-app and ERP information to trace products from farm plots to customers. ofi identifies its enhanced Track and Trace system as the backbone of meeting EUDR compliance.[21]

In Indonesia, the LASCARCOCO partnership uses Olam’s Farmer Information System application for registered farmers to create transparent and traceable coffee and cocoa supply chains. It plans training for 6,500 coffee and cocoa farmers in North Sumatra, South Sulawesi and East Nusa Tenggara and conservation of 14,000 hectares of watershed and buffer vegetation by 2025. The US$8.2 million joint investment is made by USAID, ofi, Rikolto, Hershey’s and the Indonesian government.[22]

AtSourceV and AtSource+ received SAI Platform Farm Sustainability Assessment (FSA) 3.0 equivalence in November 2024: AtSourceV at Silver and AtSource+ at Gold, for coffee as well as spices, cocoa and nuts. FSA 3.0 is a framework to assess, improve and validate on-farm sustainability in agricultural supply chains.[23]

AtSourceV provides traceability to farmer-group level or ofi estates, risk and performance assessment against reference sustainability requirements, targeted action plans, country-level risk profiles, generic carbon and water footprints, deforestation due diligence for EUDR compliance, and third-party verification every three years.[11] AtSource+ includes all AtSourceV attributes and adds assessment against sustainability requirements beyond reference practices, supply-chain-specific granular metrics, advanced environmental footprints based on primary data, annual third-party verification, deforestation due diligence for EUDR compliance, and impact through targeted programmes and field stories.[11][21]

ofi states that independent third-party auditors verify AtSource data under the AtSource Verification Protocol. AtSource+ is used to track ofi coffee-goal progress and social and environmental impact, and to calculate overall GHG emissions for specific coffee blends across agriculture, processing and transport.[24][21]

Judgment — AtSource is strongest where a cooperative sells through ofi and needs a buyer-facing first-mile traceability, sustainability-assessment and reporting arrangement: cooperative managers and field teams capture source data, DSE assigns dispatch Lot IDs, Track and Trace connects plot-to-customer records, and customers access verified traceability through AtSource.[20][21]

OFIS can collect and synchronize data in remote rural areas with limited internet connectivity. This supports an enumerator-led low-connectivity workflow, not farmer-operated use on basic phones.[19][13][6]

ofi separately describes its unified Track and Trace system as integrating information from on-the-ground digital applications and ERP systems, enabling traceability from farm plots to customers and supporting EUDR compliance. This is an ofi traceability system distinct from the buyer-facing AtSource sustainability-management system.[25][26]

AtSource sustainability requirements state that farmer groups in high-risk areas develop climate-change mitigation plans after assessing climate risk, and work towards identifying the main greenhouse-gas emission sources in crop production and processing by 2024 and documenting them in subsequent years.[16]

ofi presents AtSource as a three-tier solution, with each tier providing increasingly enhanced data, metrics and insights for customers’ sustainability priorities; the tiers are not described as separate farmer applications.[21]

AtSource enrolment has minimum safeguards classified as unacceptable practices: worst forms of child labour; forced and trafficked labour; physical and/or sexual harassment or violence; forced evictions or displacement of local communities, or no proof of Free, Prior & Informed Consent; illegal or banned pesticides and fertilisers; and deforestation or destruction of important natural habitats, including legally protected areas and areas of high conservation value.[12]

AtSourceV follows a three-year verification cycle, with a third-party verification audit at least once every three years and internal monitoring in the other years. AtSource+ has annual third-party verification audits within its three-year verification cycle: a full-scope verification audit during the first enrolment year, followed by surveillance audits in the next two years for continuing compliance with sustainability requirements, data integrity and action-plan implementation.[12]

For farmer groups and estates, AtSource makes Land Use Change, Carbon Sequestration - Trees, Carbon Sequestration - Soil, and Seeds Seedlings Tree Planting and Clearing compulsory climate metrics. Land Use Change reports carbon emissions from deforestation over 20 years; the tree and soil metrics report carbon sequestered in biomass and soil; and the planting-and-clearing metric reports emissions from growing seeds and seedlings and from uprooting and planting trees, in tonnes CO2-e.[12]

AtSource also makes the following farm-operation emissions metrics compulsory for farmer groups and estates: Fertiliser Production, Fertiliser Use, Pesticide Production, Pesticide Use, Irrigation, Farm Machinery, Electricity, Post Harvest Activities and Crop Residues Management. These report emissions in tonnes CO2-e from fertiliser and pesticide production and application, irrigation infrastructure and water use, farm machinery, grid electricity, post-harvest activities such as washing or residue removal, and harvested or unharvested crop-residue management.[12]

AtSourceV covers requirements in 24 key sustainability areas. It collects and reports five basic Portfolio metrics annually to give a general overview of farmer groups in the AtSourceV supply chain. AtSource+ adds further and more advanced requirements in each principle and makes wider-ranging economic, environmental and social metrics available for more granular supply-chain information.[12]

AtSource records compulsory processing-emissions metrics, in tonnes CO2-e, for grid electricity, fossil fuels, biomass, renewables, incineration and other processing activities such as composting. Its compulsory transport metrics, also in tonnes CO2-e, cover inland origination, inland processing, sea freight to processing, sea freight delivery and inland delivery.[12]

AtSource terms require prior written approval for certain public AtSource Claims. ofi does not allow on-pack AtSource Claims on AtSource Products or Finished Goods concerning the AtSource system or products; off-pack claims may be made under the Claims Use and Communications Guidelines. ofi may verify AtSourceV and AtSource+ supply chains, and a claimant using a claim continuously must report annually on its use and seek annual renewal of prior approval.[17]

The FAO STI Portal profile cited for OFIS country adoption identifies its record as 《AI-assisted extraction of information》 and gives its status as 《Newly extracted from original record - Not checked, not enriched》.[19]

### FairTrace

Fairtrace is FLOCERT’s online collaboration and assurance platform for Fairtrade-certified supply-chain actors. It records Fairtrade transactions and uses the principle of mutual assurance: one supply-chain partner reports a transaction and the other verifies it through a virtual handshake.[27][28][29][30]

Fairtrace is primarily a Fairtrade trade-reporting system. Its transaction records cover traded volumes, Fairtrade Price and Premium payments, buyer and seller identifiers, product codes, invoice references and transaction dates.[27][28][31]

The system’s reporting workflow is specific. Traders report Fairtrade sales and direct purchases from producers quarterly, then verify transactions reported or corrected by business partners within 30 days. Reporting deadlines are 30 April for January–March transactions, 30 July for April–June, 30 October for July–September and 30 January for October–December.[32]

Only completed Fairtrade transactions should be reported or verified: the goods must have been delivered and the Fairtrade Price and relevant Premium paid. A trader responsible for paying or conveying price or premium must include the relevant payment information.[32]

Producers generally do not report Fairtrace transactions. The stated exception is a producer-trader that directly buys from other certified producers.[27]

Fairtrace supports individual online reporting and bulk Excel upload. Its workflow requires FLOCERT credentials and a business-partner relationship created through invitations and acceptance.[27][32]

Fairtrace’s operating model is therefore back-office, account-based and supply-chain-partner-based. FLOCERT says it assists users through technical support, password resets, platform management support and Fairtrace onboarding.[30]

Fairtrace uses data-entry validations and automated email reminders for missing reports. FLOCERT also analyses data with internal tools to identify potential errors that cannot be fully checked automatically and, in most cases, contacts the reporting organisation to correct a mistake.[28]

Fairtrace’s strongest value is Fairtrade transaction assurance. It helps FLOCERT monitor Fairtrade compliance and flag deviations before audit, including trade with non-certified companies and prices or premiums that are too low.[28]

Fairtrace also provides a confidential feedback channel within reported transactions for concerns about business-partner non-compliance, including price, premium and traceability concerns. The feedback is not visible to the business partner.[27]

Fairtrace is not a documented farm-management system. Its published functions concern transactions, counterparties, volumes, prices, premiums and verification, rather than member-registration surveys, agronomy, farm inspections, GPS plot boundaries or field mapping.[27][28][29]

Fairtrace is also not a Rainforest Alliance traceability portal. Rainforest Alliance coffee traceability runs through MyRA, RACP and MultiTrace, while Fairtrace is FLOCERT’s Fairtrade assurance platform.[28][30][33][34][35]

For Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia alike, Fairtrace is an account-based Fairtrade trade system: users need FLOCERT credentials and linked business-partner invitations before reporting. The cooperative must obtain Fairtrace interface language, local support language, account-access terms and any access charge for each country; FLOCERT’s English, Spanish, French, German and Portuguese website selector is not an application-language commitment.[27][30][31]

No Fairtrace farmer-facing basic-phone channel or offline collection workflow is described. The documented reporting routes are online form completion, Excel upload, email reminders and web login.[27][28]

No Fairtrace GPS-mapping, polygon-quality-control or mountain-terrain performance capability is described. The cooperative should not assign mapping responsibilities to Fairtrace; it should receive already reconciled transaction, price and premium data from the farm-management and batch-traceability system.[29][30]

No Fairtrace price is published in the cited materials. Fairtrade-certified businesses are stated to receive access to Fairtrace and Intact Platform, but the materials do not state whether Fairtrace access carries a separate fee, is bundled with certification, or depends on certification type and country.[30][31]

**What Fairtrace decides.** Fairtrace is FLOCERT’s account-based Fairtrade trade-assurance layer for certified supply-chain actors in Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia, not a field system to deploy to farmers. Ordinary producers generally do not report Fairtrace transactions; the stated exception is a producer-trader directly buying from other certified producers.[27] Price and/or Premium Conveyors/Payers report direct purchases and sales, verify supplier-reported purchases and ensure transactions are correct.[27][36]

FLOCERT’s trade-certification criteria make Fairtrace responsibilities explicit for coffee. Price and/or Premium Conveyors/Payers must report direct Fairtrade purchases or declare nothing to report, verify supplier-reported purchases, report Fairtrade sales or declare nothing to report, and ensure reported or verified transactions are correct; coffee has been within this requirement since Q1 2018. Reporting is due within 30 calendar days after each quarter ends, and purchase verification within 30 calendar days after reporting.[36]

Fairtrace therefore complements, rather than replaces, the cooperative’s internal transaction system. The internal system must retain the contract, delivery, payment and trace-back evidence; Fairtrace records and verifies the Fairtrade transaction information between certified supply-chain partners.[37][36]

FLOCERT provides a calculator for personalised estimates of initial Fairtrade certification costs and annual renewal fees. Its certification fee is described as all-in, with no additional charge for audits, travel or customer attendance. These are certification-cost estimates, not a published Fairtrace platform or per-farmer price.[38]

Fairtrade certification audit costs vary with a cooperative’s size, structure and commodities; that variation does not establish a Fairtrace platform price per farmer.[31][119]

In September 2026, Fairtrade defined an Operational Traceability Anchor (OTA) as an existing reference associated with a product, commercial transaction or key tracking event that helps preserve producer-originated sustainability data throughout the supply chain. An OTA does not contain all sustainability information or automatically make it trustworthy or tamper-proof; trust comes from governance and assurance over access, purpose, commercial relationship, quality and provenance.[39]

Fairtrade states that a potential OTA should reuse existing business-process data rather than create new reporting requirements; establish a reliable, trusted relationship with producer data providers; connect information without exposing producer identities, sourcing relationships or other commercially sensitive information unless authorised; connect identifiers even if they change through aggregation or transformation; and provide value that justifies the effort to capture and maintain the reference.[39]

FLOCERT was established in 2003 to independently certify Fairtrade.[31]

FLOCERT provides customer service and documentation in at least English, Spanish, French, Portuguese and German, and says it will try to provide service in a customer’s regional language where possible.[40]

Fairtrace has a language setting that allows users to change the platform language to their preference, but the published material does not name the languages available in the platform.[33]

Farmforce has the strongest documented commercial-platform evidence for an enumerator-led, offline field model. Its mobile application is explicitly usable offline in rural areas with patchy or absent connectivity, and the system syncs after reconnection.[1][45]

Farmforce’s Ethiopia evidence is especially relevant. In Bale and Illubabor, the ECOM coffee pilot onboarded 4,543 producers between August 2025 and March 2026, added 4,096 farms to the system, mapped 3,619 farms with GPS polygons and managed 4,548 hectares.[2]

That Ethiopian operating model required staff capacity. More than 80 mobile data collectors conducted producer registration, Android GPS boundary mapping, EUDR-specific surveys and consent capture; four Zone Administrators supervised quality control, while 20 Orbit Web users monitored collection and data quality.[2]

The pilot’s mapping controls matter in mountainous and forest-coffee areas. Farmforce reports that dense canopy and indistinct adjoining boundaries can reduce GPS accuracy; supervisors reviewed polygons within 72 hours, and farmers were revisited when polygons failed basic checks.[2]

In Bale and Illubabor, patchy 2G and 3G coverage allowed Farmforce’s offline-first field collection to continue, but synchronisation delays meant supervisors often worked with field data one or two days behind.[2]

Farmforce Origin is the offline mobile app that PFMC administrators use to register every coffee bag at aggregation, providing bag-level traceability from the forest cooperative onward. Farmforce’s offline workflow is therefore practical for staff-held Android devices.[2] It is not evidence that farmers with basic phones can independently map farms, complete surveys or manage certifications.[1][2]

Farmforce can store data relevant to certification. Its farmer database gives each farmer a unique traceability ID and each field a unique Field ID; it supports GPS polygons, certification IDs, tags such as Rainforest Alliance enrolment, customer-defined attributes, field activities, training and payment records.[47]

Farmforce presents its Certification Registry as a way to manage farmers proposed for certification, audit stages and field mapping status required for Rainforest Alliance. It also says that compliance surveys can be adapted to major certification bodies and specific standards.[49]

Farmforce’s Fairtrade evidence requires careful reading. In cocoa, Fairtrade’s standard required certified cooperatives to implement Farmforce Origin IMS from 2019, and Farmforce and Fairtrade developed a Côte d’Ivoire cocoa project. This supports Farmforce’s Fairtrade-linked implementation experience, but it is not a coffee requirement for Colombia, Ethiopia or Indonesia.[50]

Farmforce has a coffee-specific Ethiopian deployment and states that its Origin, Orbit and Insights suite has built-in workflows for EUDR, Child Labor Monitoring and Remediation Systems (CLMRS), organic, fair-trade and other compliance schemes.[2] It does not have a documented direct interface with Fairtrace, FairInsight, MultiTrace, MyRA or RACP in the supplied materials.[2]

Koltiva’s KoltiTrace is a more extensive first-mile data and traceability ecosystem than a simple map application. It combines geolocation, supply-chain mapping and compliance reporting; its components include farmer profiles, supplier registration, surveys, training, price notifications, farm-boundary polygons, farmer applications and transaction tracking.[42][55][43]

KoltiTrace’s configurable certification workflow includes standard-specific stages, approvals, imports, terminology, inspections, audits, corrective actions and issuance.[134]

Koltiva separates roles between its tools. FarmXtension is for agronomists and field teams to register farmers, collect sustainability data and map boundaries; FarmCloud is the farmer-facing application; FarmGate tracks transactions and supply-chain interactions; KoltiSkills combines field support, polygon mapping and agronomic training.[43]

Koltiva has evidence across the cooperative’s three countries at company-presence level. It says it operates in Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia, and it identifies Colombia and Indonesia among its country list.[4][3]

An ODK Collect and RapidPro combination requires deliberate integration; using both products does not automatically connect farmer messages to certified plot and purchase records.

Farmerline’s voice or SMS approach is a basic-phone communication candidate, but availability of its full farm-management and traceability workflow in Colombia, Ethiopia, and Indonesia requires confirmation.

Farmerline’s basic-phone channel does not replace agent-operated GPS mapping. Its Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade compatibility, offline agent workflow, required-language support, and 5,000-farmer price require separate confirmation.

The Gayo Highlands example is relevant because it is a highland coffee origin, but it is not a published mountain GPS-accuracy test. The candidate also identifies elevation controls among Koltiva’s mapping capabilities, without specifying a measured mountain-boundary accuracy result.[135][55][134][136][3][12][16][31]

FarmXtension is designed for offline field collection with pre-downloaded maps and satellite signals and synchronizes on reconnection. Its free App Store listing is not a free cooperative implementation: field services, platform access, mapping, integration, devices and support may be separately charged.[53][4][57]

ODK is different from Farmforce and KoltiTrace. It is open-source data-collection software that can be self-hosted and self-supported for free, or purchased as ODK Cloud, which is hosted, managed and supported by ODK’s creators.[5]

ODK is best understood as a configurable field-data component. It supports forms with GPS locations, multiple languages, media, skip logic, calculations and external datasets, but it does not deliver a ready-made coffee Internal Management System, bag traceability design, certification workflow or buyer integration without configuration.[5][7][63]

INATrace, Open Foris Ground and KoboToolbox can serve as components of a low-cash-cost digital internal control system for farmer registries, polygons, internal inspections, training, grievances and corrective actions.[130] Ground is designed for non-technical smallholders, cooperatives, exporters, aggregators and producer organisations.[130]

For that digital internal control system, purchase ledgers, bag-level chain of custody, Fairtrace reporting, Rainforest Alliance templates, dashboards, buyer APIs and long-term implementation support require custom development or separate systems.[130]

ODK Collect can capture points, lines and closed geoshape polygons. Each point includes latitude, longitude, altitude and an accuracy radius in metres; it can calculate a geoshape’s land area in square metres.[7]

ODK exposes useful data-quality controls. The standard GPS capture target is 5 metres, and the documentation advises against setting a target below 3 metres without an external GPS device. ODK’s warning-accuracy setting defaults to 100 metres. At or worse than the configured value, the accuracy dialog turns red and states that accuracy is unacceptable, but the threshold does not prevent capture.[7]

ODK’s displayed accuracy radius is an Android estimate with 68% confidence, based on available GPS, network and other sensor inputs. Dense forest canopy can make strict thresholds impractical, and mock-location or manually placed points can produce a zero accuracy value.[7]

ODK can therefore preserve the quality information needed to manage mapping risk, but it does not automatically make a smartphone measurement accurate. In difficult coffee terrain, the cooperative needs a field protocol, external GNSS equipment where necessary, supervisory review and revisits for failed polygons.[7]

ODK’s offline capability is strong. ODK Collect stores enumerator forms offline and syncs them after reconnection; an SMS or voice service is a separate component for basic-phone interactions.[5][59] ODK Collect can also use device-stored MBTiles layers for offline maps in geopoint, geotrace and geoshape questions.[5][59]

Offline map deployment has an operational cost. A project manager prepares MBTiles layers and distributes them by device transfer, cloud sharing, links or manual loading; wider zoom ranges produce larger files.[59]

ODK’s language position is unusually transparent. ODK Collect’s localisation page lists 64 languages; Spanish is 98% complete and Indonesian 97%, while Amharic is 26% and Tigrinya 2%. Form labels can also be translated independently through XLSForm.[7][63]

ODK Cloud Standard costs US$199 per month for one active project, unlimited users, unlimited forms and 10,000 monthly submissions. ODK Cloud Professional costs US$499 per month for one active project, unlimited users, unlimited forms and 25,000 monthly submissions, plus API access and Entities for data flows.[5]

Judgment — The Standard plan’s 10,000 monthly submissions may be sufficient only if the cooperative limits routine activity to a small number of forms per farmer. A design with monthly household surveys, repeated inspection forms, transaction forms, photos and multiple programme forms can exceed it quickly; the cooperative should model submissions before selecting a tier.

ODK’s low licence cost does not mean zero operating cost. ODK Central is licensed under Apache 2.0, and a Central installation also includes third-party software under their respective licences. Self-hosting shifts work to the cooperative, which must provide technical administration, network configuration, backups, power-failure arrangements and user support.[60][67][5]

Farmer-Switch and Crop2Cash show that USSD can serve farmers who have feature phones, but neither is a verified candidate for the three-country coffee cooperative. Farmer-Switch allows basic-phone users to check prices, list produce, apply for loans and enrol in insurance through USSD; Crop2Cash’s Nigerian USSD wallet supports payments, records and input services without smartphones or mobile data.[68][69]

Farmer-Switch presents GPS farm boundaries, certification records, cooperative functions and farm-to-warehouse traceability. It also identifies itself as an operating system for African agriculture and gives Zambian farmer examples, so the stated deployment context is not Colombia, Ethiopia or Indonesia.[68]

ODK is the most price-transparent low-software-cost field-data platform. None is verified as a full feature-phone farm-management solution across Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia.

Restoration Scope Field supports an offline field-team workflow that keeps plot boundaries, producers and repeat field visits together: staff can walk or tap a boundary offline, receive a warning when GPS accuracy is weak, export boundaries as GeoJSON or KML, reopen a plot and record a repeat visit offline.[86]

For quotation-based systems, compare annualised total cost per farmer over a fixed contract term: divide the annual licence, hosting, support, integration and transaction charges plus the agreed annual share of one-time implementation, baseline mapping, devices and training by 5,000. Require vendors to separate these inputs so that a donor- or buyer-funded launch is not mistaken for the cooperative’s renewal cost.

ECTMS registration has six access categories: Exporter, Warehouse, Supplier, Union, Cooperatives and Primary Industry; individual farmers are not an access category.[88] The published registration guide says individual producers are registered by approved organisations rather than using the registration page themselves.[88]

FarmForce launched in Nairobi, Kenya, in 2012.[46]

Koltiva was established in Indonesia in 2013 and incorporated in Switzerland in 2017; it says its solutions are used by clients and suppliers in 28 countries.[56]

ActivityInfo’s interface-language list includes Spanish, Amharic, Indonesian, Oromo and Tigrinya, and each user can select an interface language independently.[89]

ActivityInfo forms and database structures can be designed and used in any language because the system stores text as Unicode, although translations that lack automatic machine translation must be added manually.[89]

Farmforce also states that it has customers in 30 countries, manages more than 700,000 farmers across 45 crop value chains, and offers the platform in 15 languages.[87]

Its basic-phone channel does not replace agent-operated GPS mapping, and its Rainforest Alliance integration, Fairtrade integration, offline agent workflow, required-language support and 5,000-farmer price require confirmation.

Configurable ODK fields do not themselves provide ready-made Rainforest Alliance or Fairtrade certification integration.[5][60]

### Rainforest Alliance integration requirements

Rainforest Alliance integration is not a vendor badge. It is an operating workflow involving the cooperative’s member registry, farm-unit records, geolocation files, certification roles, internal inspection evidence, RACP submissions and MultiTrace transaction reporting.[8][34][35]

For farm groups, the Group Member Registry, or GMR, is the binding member-and-farm data structure used to add group members and farms for compliance and audit preparation. All member-farm information must be correct and kept current.[90][35]

Each farm unit needs a unique farm-unit ID. The Group Member Registry point record requires a unique internal farm ID, farm-unit ID, area in hectares, latitude and longitude in decimal degrees.[8]

Rainforest Alliance requires 100% geolocation data for farm units with certified crop. The required submission package includes a location-point file using the Group Member Registry template and polygon files in KML or GeoJSON; farms must also have a polygon file that includes all farm units.[8]

Coordinates must use WGS 84 (EPSG:4326), decimal degrees, numerical values only and at least four decimal places.[8] The system requires correct coordinate signs and avoidance of transposed latitude and longitude.[8]

Farm-unit polygons must have valid geometry and a unique farm-unit ID. They cannot overlap another polygon by more than 20% of area, although adjacent polygons may share a boundary.[8][90]

Rainforest Alliance requires field collection for polygon reference points. A polygon derived only from a paper or digital map is not accepted, and field-collected boundaries must be reviewed in GIS software such as QGIS, Google My Maps or Google Earth Pro.[8]

The standard directly acknowledges difficult terrain. Location points should be as close as possible to the centre of the farm unit, taking account of cliffs, rivers, irregular terrain and danger; the mapper must mark the collection location so an inspector or auditor can verify it later.[8]

RACP validates farm-unit coordinates and polygons. Its GIS Risk Assessment checks whether units are on land, in the country of certification scope and free of polygon overlaps; it then assigns deforestation and protected-area encroachment risk levels.[90]

When validation fails, RACP provides an Excel validation table. The certificate holder must correct the data, re-upload polygons and restart the risk assessment.[90]

RACP also requires indicator data for audit preparation. Its questionnaire covers farm information, shade trees and natural vegetation, fertilizer use, pest management, irrigation, processing, energy, child labour, forced labour, gender discrimination, other discrimination, violence and harassment.[90]

Farm groups must operate an internal inspection system. Before first certification audit, all actors in scope must be inspected against applicable requirements; subsequent inspections are risk-based, while all new members must be fully inspected before the external audit.[8]

For small farms, at least 35% must be inspected annually, so that every farm is inspected after three consecutive years. Rainforest Alliance may require a higher sample in a specified crop, country or region.[8]

Certification work remains within Rainforest Alliance’s own systems. MyRA is the entry portal; RACP supports application, audit preparation and compliance; at least one organisational user must hold the Certification role.[35]

The CFI role enters farm data, polygons and required certification/EUDR indicators. The Traceability role records certified-product transactions and reconciles volumes.[35]

MultiTrace is mandatory under the Rainforest Alliance standard and is accessed through the Rainforest Alliance certification environment. Its activities include trade reporting, confirmations, conversions, mixing, redeeming, removal and downgrading of certified product.[34]

Coffee traceability activities in MultiTrace have current quarterly deadlines: 15 April, 15 July, 15 October and 15 January. These replace the expired Traceability Annex v1.3 schedule, which required reporting within two weeks after quarter end and ceased to apply on 1 March 2026.[91][34]

The cooperative must also retain transaction discipline. Transaction volume and quality must match physical-trade documents, transaction references are mandatory for sales, and transactions must be confirmed for traceability.[33]

For coffee sold under different Rainforest Alliance standards, MultiTrace keeps Sustainable Agriculture Standard, Regenerative Agriculture Standard and dual SAS+RAS volumes separate. Volumes certified under different standards cannot be mixed.[34]

Rainforest Alliance integration has an EUDR dimension. Its EUDR-aligned coffee and cocoa process requires geodata for all plots, polygons above 4 hectares and six decimal digits, whereas the current Rainforest Alliance certification geodata requirement cited above uses at least four decimal places.[61][90]

Rainforest Alliance certification does not automatically make coffee EUDR-compliant. Companies placing coffee on the EU market or exporting it from the EU remain legally responsible for EUDR compliance by 30 December 2026.[61]

Rainforest Alliance does not prescribe a geodata-collection tool. It provides guidance and forms, including KoBoCollect guidance, but the cooperative can choose another system if it produces the required identifiers, data fields, file formats and correction workflow.[61]

Spanish, Amharic and Bahasa Indonesia versions of Rainforest Alliance Farm Requirements and the Management Annex are available. This supports local interpretation of requirements in Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia, but it does not establish candidate-platform interfaces in those languages.[92][8]

The Rainforest Alliance EUDR page refers to five EUDR-aligned requirements in one passage and four criteria in another. The differing count is not explained on that page, so the cooperative should use the current detailed standard and buyer instructions when configuring its compliance forms.[61]

**What Rainforest Alliance decides.** Require vendors to demonstrate a complete export-and-correction workflow: unique internal and farm-unit IDs; GMR-ready records; WGS84 location points; valid KML/GeoJSON polygons; overlap checks; GIS review; six-decimal EUDR configuration where relevant; and a repeatable process for RACP and MultiTrace users. A platform that only displays farms on a map does not meet this operating requirement.

Farmforce’s published first-mile scope adds bag-level traceability, two-way SMS communication, certification management, and production, social, economic and environmental indicator collection. Its stated primary users are extension agents, farmers and traders.[46]

For an Indonesia deployment, Koltiva’s FarmXtension Cocoa Google Play disclosure states that the application collects location, personal information and two other data types, encrypts data in transit, and does not permit data deletion. Its FarmGate Cocoa listing instead states that no data are collected or shared with third parties, data are not encrypted, and users can request deletion.[93][94]

For an EU due-diligence workflow alongside Rainforest Alliance certification, the cooperative must retain plot-level geolocation for every plot and polygons for plots above four hectares, connect export lots to those plots, and maintain risk, legality and shipment evidence for DDS filing. Certifications support risk mitigation but do not replace these records.[77]

For a 5,000-farmer group, Rainforest Alliance recommends importing farm data through the Group Member Registry template rather than entering farms individually. The registration record also requires crop area in hectares, estimated harvest in kilograms, previous harvest in kilograms and volume delivered in kilograms for each applicable crop.[95]

Rainforest Alliance specifies the interchange format as well as the data fields. Polygon uploads accepted by the platform are .json, .geojson, .kml and .kmz; GeoJSON uses WGS 84 (EPSG:4326), longitude-latitude coordinate order, and identical first and last coordinates to close the polygon. GMR farm-unit coordinates use WGS84/EPSG:4326 and contain four to eight decimal places.[95] The risk assessment checks overlap, whether the farm unit is on land and in the certification country, deforestation risk, protected-area risk and invalid geometry including self-intersections and short segments.

Its stated overlap categories are Low at 0%, Medium up to 1.2%, and High above 1.2%.[95] EUDR validation checks coordinate decimals and requires polygon data for farms of at least 4 hectares.[95]

Rainforest Alliance provides FieldEntry for farm groups without visibility of all farm data. FieldEntry combines KOBO and the FieldEntry web App to collect member-level data, aggregate it and report indicator data in RACP. Indicator Data is structured information on alignment with the Sustainable Agriculture Standard and is crucial to the Certification Body’s audit.[95] The certificate holder completes Indicator Data directly in RACP, reviews pre-filled data from registration and scope confirmation, and submits it; a submitted-data PDF can be downloaded for offline reference.[95]

Rainforest Alliance certification data requires continuing maintenance. On renewal, the certificate holder updates or uploads farm details, polygons and other geographic information, runs a new risk assessment, and submits updated indicator data.[95] A scope extension is required when certified volume, area or group membership increases, a crop, site or processing activity is added, or the traceability level changes, including from Mass Balance to Identity Preserved.

The certificate holder updates relevant member, farm, crop, activity, polygon or traceability information in MyRA; where both an updated GMR and polygons are required, it uploads both before running the risk assessment.[95]

MyRA is the single access point for Rainforest Alliance certification, traceability and claims systems. MyRA navigation and instructions support multiple languages, but organization names, crop details and documentation are entered in English.[96] At least one organizational user needs the Certification role to begin and manage certification in RACP; the CFI role enters farm composition, structure, polygons and indicators on farming practices, labour and inputs, while the Traceability role accesses MultiTrace to record purchases and sales, generate applicable transaction certificates and reconcile volumes.[96] Bahasa Indonesia and Vietnamese MyRA user guides are available, and Rainforest Alliance provides Amharic versions of key Knowledge Hub documents including the Certification Standard, Annexes and Policies.[96]

Demonstrable Rainforest Alliance flow. The cooperative maintains member and farm-unit IDs and Group Member Registry data, then captures field-collected geodata, checks it in GIS, uploads and corrects it through RACP, submits indicators through the CFI role, and separately reconciles certified-product transactions in MultiTrace through the Traceability role.
The polygon acceptance test and GIS risk categories are distinct. Polygon files require unique pre-registered farm-unit IDs and accepted formats; field polygons require GIS review. RACP risk assessment then tests overlap, on-land location, country location and invalid geometry, including self-intersections and short segments; it rates deforestation and protected-area risk as High, Medium or Low. Its cited overlap risk categories are Low at 0%, Medium up to 1.2%, and High above 1.2%; these are risk categories, not positional-accuracy measures.[8][90][95]

Rainforest Alliance gives two EUDR polygon-size formulations: its EUDR-support page specifies polygons for plots above 4 hectares, while its platform validation description requires polygon data for farms greater than or equal to 4 hectares. Both require six decimal digits for the EUDR workflow, compared with four currently required for Rainforest Alliance certification.[61][95]

Rainforest Alliance defines a Group Member as the person who actually operates the farm, such as a sharecropper; the group member does not need to be the landowner. The cooperative’s member-and-farm data model must therefore separately retain farm operator and landowner where they are different.[97]

Rainforest Alliance defines geographical scope as the area covered by separate farm units within one country under a common management body. Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia must therefore be treated as their respective country scopes rather than as one combined geographical scope.[97]

In MyRA, the Visiting Country field is mandatory because the Central Farm Information system uses it to determine geolocation details required for risk-assessment calculations.[96]

The certification agreement between the Certificate Holder and the selected Certification Body is signed outside RACP. After both parties sign, it is confirmed in RACP through a handshake, which gives the Certification Body access to the Certificate Holder’s full profile for audit preparation and progress monitoring.[97]

For EUDR alignment in RACP, all relevant non-conformities must be closed, geodata must be valid in EUDR formats within the Certificate Holder’s scope, and risk-assessment results within that scope must not be blank.[97]

A Certificate Holder with open non-conformities against EUDR-aligned requirements cannot make EUDR claims and is not included in the EUDR-aligned report. Compliance with EUDR-aligned requirements does not affect the Certificate Holder’s ability to remain Rainforest Alliance certified.[97]

The Rainforest Alliance audit checklist can be uploaded in any Rainforest Alliance language, with a maximum of 2,000 characters per cell. The accepted values for conformity, non-conformity and closure status are language-specific.[97]

Rainforest Alliance defines a Farm Unit as a continuous piece of land with defined boundaries that is part of a farm. A farm unit can have point data, comprising latitude and longitude, or polygon data.[95]

For certification, each farm must submit geospatial data as a polygon. The polygon must show each farm or farm-unit boundary, correspond to the real farm-unit area, and match the area and location details already entered in the platform.[95]

Rainforest Alliance uses satellite mapping and field-based third-party audits to assess deforestation and encroachment risk. Satellite-based risk assessments indicate risk rather than definitive evidence of deforestation or non-deforestation; ground truthing through internal inspections and third-party auditing validates the results.[61]

Rainforest Alliance farm Certificate Holders must comply with applicable laws and collective bargaining agreements within the standard’s scope. Where applicable law or a collective bargaining agreement is stricter than the standard, it prevails unless obsolete; where it is less strict, the standard prevails unless it explicitly allows that law or agreement to apply. The legal and legitimate right to use the land is a Sustainable Agriculture Standard requirement.[61]

Rainforest Alliance royalties are calculated for each crop from traceability activities and are charged once in the supply chain.[34]

For Farm Group Certificate Holders, Rainforest Alliance states that indicator data should be reported in aggregate using tools such as FieldEntry or FIA. FieldEntry is not needed where the group already uses FIA; both tools are optional and not mandatory parts of the reporting process.[35]

MultiTrace assigns a royalty rate to each certification standard. When a buyer pays royalties, the cooperative must ensure that the buyer receives the correct certified volume because mislabelling the standard can result in incorrect royalty charges.[34]

MultiTrace’s Finance tab displays royalty per commodity and an overview of invoices; the invoice overview does not include payment history.[34]

Rainforest Alliance states that its systems and tools can provide information supporting EUDR due diligence and compliance efforts for eligible Identity Preserved and Mixed Identity Preserved supply chains. Companies seeking certified-product supply-chain geolocation data and risk assessments must purchase coffee or cocoa using the Identity Preserved or Mixed Identity Preserved traceability levels.[61]

Rainforest Alliance defines a Farm Unit as 《a piece of continuous land with defined boundaries that is part of a farm》. A farm unit can hold point data or polygon data, but certification submission requires a polygon showing the real boundary of each farm or farm unit and matching the area and location details already entered in the platform.[95]

For EUDR alignment in RACP, the Certificate Holder must close all non-conformities against the relevant requirements, hold geodata valid for EUDR formats within its scope, and have non-blank risk-assessment results within that scope. A Certificate Holder with open relevant non-conformities cannot make EUDR claims and is not included in the report; EUDR-aligned compliance does not affect its ability to be Rainforest Alliance certified.[97]

Satellite risk assessments are not definitive proof of deforestation or non-deforestation, and internal inspections and third-party audits provide the required ground truthing. Farm Certificate Holders must also comply with applicable laws and collective bargaining agreements within the standard’s scope; the stricter applicable law, collective bargaining agreement or standard requirement prevails under the stated rules, and the Sustainable Agriculture Standard includes the legal and legitimate right to use land.[61]

MultiTrace financial controls also require correct certification-standard and certified-volume records. The MultiTrace Finance tab shows royalty by commodity and an invoice overview, but that overview does not include payment history.[34]

For EUDR geolocation, one Rainforest Alliance formulation permits a point or polygon for plots of 4 hectares or less and requires a polygon for plots larger than 4 hectares; it also requires GeoJSON in WGS84 with longitude first and at least six decimal places.[86] RACP’s EUDR Data status is Valid when the Certificate Holder has correctly added polygons and six decimals, and Invalid when polygons and/or six decimals were not added correctly.[97]

### Fairtrade integration requirements

Fairtrade compatibility also has distinct layers: producer-organisation member and farm controls; coffee-plot geolocation; annual FairInsight reporting; Fairtrade trade reporting in Fairtrace; buyer, price and premium records; and audit evidence.[62][31]

For the Fairtrade Coffee Standard published on 12 February 2024 and generally applicable from 1 January 2025, requirements marked NEW 2025, NEW 2026 and NEW 2027 take effect on their specified dates. The cooperative should build its data model against the applicable date of each requirement rather than treat the whole standard as effective at one time.[62]

The major future mapping requirement is marked NEW 2027. A producer organisation must have geolocation data for 100% of members cultivating coffee, in GPS points or polygons; plots over four hectares require polygons at minimum, while smaller plots require GPS points. The organisation must prioritise additional polygon mapping using deforestation-risk criteria and use the information in deforestation-prevention planning.[62]

In September 2025, Fairtrade International’s Standards Committee postponed the deadline for certified coffee cooperatives to complete geolocation data collection for their full farmer membership. The underlying direction remains full-member coverage; the cooperative should obtain the current deadline from Fairtrade before finalising implementation milestones.[98]

Fairtrade producer organisations report to Fairtrade International annually through FairInsight. The cited Coffee Standard specifically includes available geolocation data for member coffee plots and deforestation-prevention and mitigation support received in the past year, including estimated monetary value.[62]

Fairtrace is separate from FairInsight. Fairtrace records Fairtrade supply-chain transaction data, including volumes, Minimum Price and Premium payments, while FairInsight is the stated annual producer-organisation reporting channel.[30][62]

Fairtrade verification extends beyond system data. Compliance is checked through regular audits by independent certification bodies and can include physical inspections of fields, plants and offices; document and finance reviews; and confidential staff interviews. FLOCERT is Fairtrade’s main independent certification provider and is ISO 17065-accredited.[99]

Fairtrade producer organisations receive an initial on-site audit before selling certified products. According to Fairtrade’s certification description, audit effort reflects membership, organisational complexity and products; its three-year cycle normally includes up to two confirmation audits before renewal in year three.[119]

Judgment — The cooperative should configure its central data model so that a member, farm, plot, harvest estimate, delivery, lot, buyer invoice, price, premium and payment can be linked. That allows staff to answer a Fairtrade audit question from source evidence rather than reconstruct records manually across spreadsheets.

Fairtrade trade records need counterparties and payment data. Fairtrace asks for seller and buyer FLO IDs, invoice number and date, product code, volumes, price and Fairtrade Premium information.[27]

Fairtrade reporting is a completed-trade control, not merely a sales forecast. Records should show that goods were delivered and that Price and Premium were paid before reporting or verification.[32]

The Fairtrade Standard also distinguishes producer organisations from producer-traders. A producer is the first actor growing, harvesting or extracting the raw material; a producer-trader has a trader certificate permitting trade in products supplied by other certified producers.[27]

**What Fairtrade decides.** A farm-management platform should feed the cooperative’s internal member, plot, crop, delivery and payment records; Fairtrace should remain the Fairtrade transaction-assurance layer; FairInsight should remain the annual Fairtrade reporting route. Do not ask one of these systems to do the other two systems’ jobs.

Fairtrade transaction integration must preserve the documentary chain. Fairtrade products must be identified as Fairtrade in invoices, delivery notes, purchase orders and other relevant trade documentation. Records must identify the traders’ names and FLO-IDs, transaction dates, product quantities and physical form and, for producer-organisation, conveyor and payer transactions, Fairtrade Price, Fairtrade Premium and pre-financing details. Records of entries, processing and sales must allow a certification body to trace any Fairtrade output back to Fairtrade inputs.[36]

For Fairtrade coffee purchase contracts, the platform should retain agreed volumes, quality specifications, price, price and Premium adjustment calculations, the Premium separately from price, the responsible Price and Premium payer, traceable payment terms, exchange-rate date where relevant, pre-finance terms and amount, and quality-problem procedures. Coffee contracts must separately state the prevailing quality and/or country differential, the organic differential where applicable, and the Fairtrade Premium.[36]

The transaction record must support coffee payment controls. The producer or conveyor receives at least the relevant market price or Fairtrade Minimum Price, whichever is higher; quality discounts cannot reduce the Fairtrade Minimum Price; and new Minimum Prices apply to contracts signed after the validity date in the Fairtrade pricing database. A coffee payer must pay price, Premium and applicable organic or price differentials no later than 15 calendar days after receiving ownership-transfer documents.[36]

Where a conveyor conveys the Fairtrade Premium, it pays the producer or Fairtrade Premium Committee without discounts. For coffee, the conveyor pays the applicable differential and/or Premium within 15 calendar days after receiving payer payment, or under a written agreement no later than 30 calendar days after quarter-end. For each purchase contract, the producer receives a report of exact sold volumes, any price differential, Premium due and buyer FLO-ID quarterly unless another timeline is agreed.[36]

Coffee price-to-be-fixed contracts require seller’s-call price fixation. Stop-loss orders are prohibited except during harvest and in consultation with the producer to manage risk for both sides; if price is fixed before harvest, buyer and seller must jointly agree a risk-management strategy in writing. Outright-priced contracts require the specified conditions and written evidence, and prices cannot be fixed for longer than one crop period.[36]

Judgment — A Fairtrade-compatible cooperative platform must link contract, lot, invoice, FLO-ID, physical form, price components, Premium, pre-finance, ownership-transfer date, payer-payment receipt date and onward-payment date. This is the record set that allows Fairtrace reporting, producer reports and audit trace-back to be reconciled without reconstructing the transaction from separate files.[37][36]

For Fairtrade coffee trade controls, the cooperative’s system should retain purchase contracts that separately record the product price, Fairtrade Premium and, where applicable, organic differential. The Fairtrade Premium must be paid in addition to the product price and not incorporated into the agreed differential.[36]

The cooperative should link each Fairtrade contract to any pre-finance record. For applicable coffee contracts signed after 1 September 2015, the first buyer must offer or facilitate pre-finance unless there is proven high risk, the producer verifiably declines it, or it is legally prohibited in the country of operation; the buyer must not pressure the producer to decline. Where pre-finance is provided directly, the written agreement covers amount, duration, payment terms, interest charges, other charges, and consequences of quality problems or non-delivery; the stated general minimum is 60% of contract value. For coffee, pre-finance must be made effectively available as soon as possible after contract signing and no later than eight weeks before shipment.[36]

Where a first buyer facilitates pre-finance through a third-party lender, the platform should preserve the buyer’s effective-facilitation evidence: acting as a reference, confirming that the Fairtrade contract is valid and can be collateral, and agreeing with the producer whether contract payment goes to the producer or lender.[36]

For coffee, the system should retain a sourcing plan covering at least 12 months and record its renewal at least three months before the preceding plan expires. It should also reconcile green-bean purchases and sales against the Fairtrade minimum price, Fairtrade Premium and applicable organic differential.[36]

Fairtrade producer audits can cross-check contracts, invoices, minutes and membership lists; inspect production sites, processing installations, storage areas, farms and fields; observe working conditions, health and safety and production practices; interview workers and members; check product traceability; and check whether Premium money was used as agreed. Trader audits check Fairtrade transactions against documents, contracts and invoices, inspect premises, check timely payment of the correct Fairtrade Price and Premium, and check continuing product traceability.[100]

FLOCERT audits producers on a three-year initial certification cycle and traders on a five-year cycle, with periodic and potentially unannounced audits; certification follows resolution of non-conformities. The platform must therefore preserve retrievable evidence throughout the relevant cycle, including for announced and unannounced audits at the cooperative’s and subcontractors’ premises.[100][40]

The Fairtrade International and Fair Trade USA systems must be configured separately. Fair Trade USA sets its own coffee standards and pricing, while coffee traded under Fairtrade International terms can enter Fair Trade Certified supply chains through Fair Trade USA’s Recognition Program. A producer organisation with a valid FLOCERT certificate must also be registered in that Recognition Program to participate, subject to its requirements.[101]

For Fair Trade USA coffee contracts, the cooperative’s record must retain the applicable Fair Trade USA pricing terms and both buyer and seller Fair Trade USA IDs. The Fair Trade USA coffee schedule effective for contracts signed on or after 1 February 2027 specifies, in USD per lb, minimum prices of $2.00 for washed Arabica, $1.95 for natural Arabica, $1.35 for washed Robusta and $1.30 for natural Robusta, with a $0.20/lb Fair Trade Premium and a $0.30/lb Organic Differential.[101]

Fair Trade USA’s commodity schedule also identifies product, species or variety, characteristic, processing, form, organic status, quality, country or region, Incoterm, unit measurement, units, effective date, end date, currency, minimum price, premium rate, premium percentage and period. These are the financial and product dimensions that the cooperative should maintain for Fair Trade USA transaction reconciliation.[102]

For Fairtrade International coffee, the cooperative must maintain harvest-level sourcing plans renewed at least three months before expiry, buyer contracts with the prevailing differential, organic differential and Fairtrade Premium separately stated, and records showing that the Premium is paid in addition to the product price rather than incorporated in the differential.[103]

The cooperative must record the General Assembly’s decisions on use of the Fairtrade Premium and the use of those funds. At least 5 cents of the Fairtrade Premium must be invested in improving Fairtrade-coffee productivity and/or quality.[103]

The Fairtrade Trader Standard applies to traders that buy and sell Fairtrade products or handle the Fairtrade Price and Premium, and covers product composition, traceability and use of the FAIRTRADE Mark. It applies to all Fairtrade Traders from 1 July 2026, subject to the transition periods in the standard, and works in combination with product-specific standards.[104]

Operators taking ownership of Fairtrade-certified products or handling the Fairtrade Price and Premium are audited and certified. Fairtrade transaction terms and conditions must be detailed in contracts signed by producers and buyers; producers receive at least the Fairtrade Minimum Price or the higher market price, and receive the Fairtrade Premium in addition to the price.[103][104]

The Fairtrade International Trader Standard is available in English, Spanish, French and Portuguese. Spanish-language materials support Colombian staff, while the English version of the Coffee Standard is the basis for certification decisions where it conflicts with a translation.[104][103]

For Fairtrade coffee in Ethiopia, the cooperative must preserve producer and lot identity through the selected export route. Historical analysis identifies traceability as a prerequisite for coffee certification and describes Fairtrade among common certifications, while also showing that ECX route design, rather than a farm platform alone, can sever origin identity.[105]

Fairtrade’s September 2026 trusted-traceability model adds a direct plot-to-transaction workflow. Plot Insights is where Fairtrade coffee and cocoa producer organisations manage member plot geolocation data and receive deforestation-risk analysis; Fairtrace tracks Fairtrade sales and transactions; GeoLink securely sends data between Plot Insights and Fairtrace.[39] An exporter creates the Fairtrace contract between the producer organisation, exporter and importer; the cooperative accesses it in Plot Insights, attaches plots and associated geolocation data, and GeoLink sends that information to Fairtrace for the trade partners.[39]

For coffee, Fairtrade made ICO Mark reporting mandatory in Fairtrace in 2025 wherever the mark is available.The cooperative should therefore retain its own member, plot and crop-year identifiers and link them to lots, contracts and ICO Marks.[39]

Choose the buyer’s Fair Trade scheme first. For Fairtrade International/FLOCERT coffee, the cooperative’s system must link member and plot records, deliveries and lots, contracts, Fairtrade Price, Premium, pre-finance and payments. Annual producer-organisation outputs go to FairInsight; Fairtrade transactions go to Fairtrace; for coffee and cocoa plot-to-transaction exchange, Plot Insights holds member-plot geolocation and GeoLink sends attached plot data to Fairtrace.[62][36][39]

For Fair Trade USA, keep a separate workflow using Fair Trade USA buyer and seller IDs and the applicable Fair Trade USA pricing terms. The listed minimum-price schedule taking effect for contracts signed on or after 1 February 2027 is a future-effective schedule, not a current transaction price.[101]

For Fairtrade International, record the General Assembly’s Premium-use decisions and the resulting expenditure; at least 5 cents of the Fairtrade Premium is invested in productivity and/or quality improvement.[103]

Under the Fairtrade Coffee Standard requirement marked NEW 2026, payers and conveyors support the producer organisations from which they source with deforestation- and forest-degradation-risk assessment and mitigation planning to conserve forest and vegetation. Support may be direct or through a partnership, in the form of funding, data sharing, training, partnership facilitation and/or advocacy.[62]

Fairtrade’s environmental-risk guidance encourages producer organisations to map member-farm locations and cluster farms by common territorial characteristics. It identifies high-altitude farms as potentially facing environmental risks different from lower-altitude farms, and states that geo-mapping and polygon maps, including farm boundaries, can map risk areas more accurately.[62]

Where a payer or conveyor can identify the specific plots supplying the coffee it purchases, it may use those plot geolocations. Where that sourcing traceability is unavailable, it may declare the geolocation data for all registered farms associated with the Fairtrade producer organisations from which it sources; at minimum, the data should be collected upon arrival at the destination port.[62]

For the FSI coffee and banana model, Fairtrade requires physical segregation and does not allow mass balance.[106]

Fairtrade’s September 2026 trusted-traceability model creates a governed connection between producer information, commercial relationships and operational traceability without requiring participants to use the same digital system. It gives producer organisations greater visibility over what information is shared and in which commercial context, while downstream supply-chain actors can access the information they need.[39]

Fairtrade’s producer-centred information-governance principle is that producers should be the first to access and benefit from their information and retain visibility over what is shared, with whom, for what purpose and in which version. Producers should know what data is collected, why it is collected and who can access it; retain control over its use, sharing and storage, including the ability to withdraw consent where appropriate; and provide informed consent before collection or sharing, understanding potential benefits and risks.[39]

Fairtrade describes its trusted-traceability proposal as an experimental approach and a set of emerging principles tested through its own systems and commodity research, not a blueprint for all supply chains or commodities. Its research did not identify a single technical solution applicable across all commodities and supply chains.[39]

Fairtrade’s standards-work programme lists a suspension project concerning requirement 3.1.9, Trader reporting.[107] It also lists 2026 work on updates to the Trader Standard, Interpretation Notes and Contract Production standard, including a Revised Trader Standard - 2026.[107]

Fairtrade states that the extended timeline maintains the goal of cooperatives owning their members’ farm-geolocation data.[98]

Fairtrade’s three regional producer networks provide coffee and cocoa cooperatives with training, advice and guidance. Fairtrade states that 18 data analysts and GIS experts have conducted training and advise cooperatives on collecting high-quality geolocation data for coffee and cocoa plots.[98]

Fair Trade USA lists its Trade Standard as version 2.1, published on 8 February 2021, with a major revision scheduled.[108] Fair Trade USA states that it reviews its standards regularly and releases major revisions to each standard at five-year intervals, as required by ISEAL.[108]

Fair Trade USA’s Partner Portal Help Center lists English, Spanish, French, Bahasa Indonesia and Portuguese language options.[109] Fair Trade USA lists email support in English at partnersuccess@fairtradeusa.org; email support in Spanish, French, Bahasa and Portuguese at psoperations@fairtradeusa.org; and a WhatsApp channel for Spanish speakers.[109]

In September 2026, Fairtrade described the Plot Insights–Fairtrace connection as a governed link between producer information, commercial relationships and operational traceability without requiring participants to use the same digital system.Fairtrade describes this trusted-traceability model as an experimental approach and emerging principles tested through its own systems and commodity research, not a blueprint for all supply chains or commodities; its research found no single technical solution applicable across all commodities and supply chains.[39]

The cooperative should also retain an auditable record of Fairtrade Premium allocation, expenditure and intended beneficiaries, linked to the relevant governance decision. This prevents Premium reporting from being separated from the member benefit it was approved to deliver.

The Fairtrade Plot Insights–GeoLink–Fairtrace workflow changes the Fairtrace decision: use Plot Insights and GeoLink, where available, to connect member plot geolocation data to Fairtrace contracts and transactions, while retaining the cooperative-controlled first-mile system as the operational record.[39] The deciding criterion is governed exchange of plot and commercial data, including producer visibility over what is shared and in which commercial context.[39]

### Basic phones

No listed platform is verified as a complete basic-phone farm-management and traceability solution across Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia. OFIS provides SMS messages; Farmer-Switch and Crop2Cash demonstrate USSD models elsewhere; Farmforce Connect is an app for basic smartphones; ODK Collect is Android-based; and Fairtrace is an online portal.[6][1][5][68][69][27]

Judgment — For this cooperative, basic-phone inclusion should mean that no farmer loses membership, price information, payment confirmation, training reminders, grievance access or ability to correct records because they lack a smartphone. It should not mean attempting to force GPS polygon mapping onto USSD, which is unsuitable for drawing boundaries, displaying satellite imagery and collecting evidence.

The best evidenced operating model is therefore staff-led field capture plus feature-phone communications. Farmforce’s Ethiopia coffee model used 80-plus mobile collectors and four Zone Administrators; OFIS describes field teams collecting farm data while SMS reaches farmers.[13][6][2]

Mountain coffee mapping needs a quality-control process rather than a promise of perfect GPS. KahawaTrace’s mapping guidance, attributing the ranges to the European Forest Institute, states that smartphones typically achieve 3–5 metres in good conditions and can degrade to 10–15 metres under dense canopy or steep terrain; dedicated handheld GPS is stated at 1–5 metres. These are guidance ranges, not measured accuracy of a shortlisted device in the cooperative’s terrain.[110]

The same guidance recommends waiting for a stable reading, averaging points, mapping in canopy gaps, holding the device in the open and storing the accuracy figure. For boundaries, it recommends walking the edge with the farmer, adding corners at intervals and sharp turns, then comparing the area and shape against farmer declarations and satellite imagery.[110]

Rainforest Alliance requires field-collected polygon reference points and GIS review. Farmforce’s Ethiopian pilot used review within 72 hours and farmer revisits for failed basic checks, which provides a workable model for Colombia and Indonesia as well.[8][2]

Judgment — The cooperative should procure rugged Android devices for enumerators, external GNSS receivers for problem plots, offline base maps for each field area, and a supervisor review queue. Farmers should be present during boundary walks because local knowledge is essential for disputed or indistinct boundaries.

**Cost and country decision**

ODK Cloud provides the clearest published software-price benchmark. Standard is US$199/month, or US$2,388/year; divided by 5,000 members, this isDerived — **US$0.4776 per farmer/year**. It includes one active project, unlimited users and forms, and 10,000 monthly submissions. Professional is US$499/month, or US$5,988/year; divided by 5,000 members, this isDerived — **US$1.1976 per farmer/year**. It includes one active project, unlimited users and forms, 25,000 monthly submissions, API access and Entities. ODK Cloud is hosted, managed and supported by ODK’s creators.[5]

Total-cost formula: annual platform subscription or self-hosting cost + setup and configuration + devices and external GNSS receivers + field visits + mapping correction and revisits + connectivity and messages + translation + training + continuing technical and field support.[5][59][2]

The Standard plan includes 10,000 submissions per month. For 5,000 members, that equals two submissions per farmer per month if all submissions were evenly allocated, before staff inspections, mapping corrections, delivery records, payments, photos and repeated training forms.Derived — 10,000 monthly submissions ÷ 5,000 farmers = two potential submissions per farmer per month; the source plan limit is.[5]

ODK self-hosting can avoid a software subscription, but not implementation cost. The cooperative would still bear devices, mobile data or synchronisation points, staff, training, form design, map preparation, technical support, hosting or local servers, backups and data governance.[5][67]

Farmforce and Koltiva do not publish a comparable subscription price. Farmforce offers a demo route; Koltiva’s app listing is free but gives no cooperative platform, services or implementation price.[52][53]

AtSource and Fairtrace also lack public per-farmer pricing. AtSource access is tied to an ofi agreement and purchase relationship; Fairtrace access is linked to Fairtrade-certified supply-chain actors.[17][12][30][31]

Rainforest Alliance’s EUDR offering has no additional charge for supply-chain partners who choose access through certification, but geodata collection and on-site verification can create extra cost. Rainforest Alliance explicitly encourages buyers to help fund this collection because EUDR responsibility lies with companies placing goods on the EU market or exporting from the EU.[61]

Judgment — The cooperative should separate software cost from total cost of ownership. For 5,000 farmers, the dominant costs are often first-time farm enumeration, polygon remapping, devices, field staff, training, data cleaning, supervision and certification preparation—not the monthly cloud licence.

Country-specific fit remains uneven:

- **Colombia:** Koltiva reports coffee validation that includes Colombia and deployment of KoltiTrace to support coffee cooperatives there. A separate 2023 project in Cauca mapped 1,321 coffee plots belonging to 1,196 farmers with a mobile application, captured farm outlines and agronomic data offline, and later managed the data through an online platform. [43][80][111]

A basic-phone messaging product such as Farmerline is not a replacement for plot mapping and certification-record systems unless its three-country deployment and integrations are demonstrated.

- **Ethiopia:** Farmforce has the clearest specific coffee implementation. From August 2025 to March 2026, its ECOM pilot mapped 3,619 GPS-polygon farms out of 4,096 entered farms; 477 farms were left without a completed polygon in the cited account. The pilot used ECTA-compliant export files while direct MIMS API integration was deferred. [2][88]

- **Indonesia:** Koltiva has the clearest supplied coffee deployment evidence, in the Gayo Highlands with more than 1,900 smallholders across 30 villages. Its stated Indonesian presence includes one legal entity and one customer-success office. Rainforest Alliance’s Indonesia coffee assessment identifies soil-fertility and agrochemical-use problems, undifferentiated harvest maturity and weak incentives for harvest and post-harvest investment, coffee-linked deforestation risk and encroachment-control constraints, and women’s more difficult access to training, subsidies and cooperatives. The platform configuration should record input and soil-management practices, harvest and post-harvest practices, deforestation-risk information, and women’s participation and land-registration information. [73][4][112]

Ethiopia also has an interoperability issue that affects procurement. Ethiopian coffee records may sit in national, union, EU-CAfE and private-provider systems; registration, completed geolocation and national-system integration are distinct steps.[88]

Judgment — In Ethiopia, require a contractually specified export mapping to ECTA/ECTMS fields and a reconciliation procedure for duplicate or corrected farmer and plot records. In Colombia and Indonesia, require equivalent buyer- and authority-specific export specifications before field collection begins.

Operating responsibilities. The cooperative’s data steward must control member IDs, correction histories and exports. Field supervisors must resolve failed synchronisations, review polygons and order revisits for failed polygons. Certification staff must submit farm, polygon and indicator data in RACP, reconcile Rainforest Alliance product volumes in MultiTrace, and report and verify completed Fairtrade transactions in Fairtrace. Vendor proposals must state which of these duties the vendor will perform, the service level for each duty, and the cooperative staff accountable for the remainder.[2][90][35][32]

ODK can be operated without continuous internet through manual USB retrieval with ODK Briefcase or a locally hosted ODK Central server over local Wi-Fi; these workflows remain Android-device field-collection arrangements rather than basic-phone farm-management channels.[113][72]

Ethiopian implementation must accommodate low upstream adoption of digital traceability, unreliable farmer internet access, minimal record keeping and financial constraints to invest in traceability technology. These conditions reinforce the staff-led collection and telephone-communication model rather than farmer self-entry on a smartphone application.[65][114][2]

ECTMS and TraceX are mobile-app and mobile-first Ethiopia workflows respectively, not basic-phone workflows. Their operational role is therefore trained-staff geolocation and traceability capture, not direct feature-phone plot mapping.[65][66]

Country data and trading constraints. In Colombia, SICA holds georeferenced records on around 1.8 million coffee lots and nearly 500,000 growing families; FNC field teams collect written grower consent for registered exporters to use farm coordinates. SICA geolocation alone does not supply a DDS: the workflow also needs risk assessment, legality evidence and proof that an exported lot came from those plots. A Colombia platform should therefore demonstrate consent-led SICA import, plot-to-lot reconciliation and evidence management.[77]

In Ethiopia, ECTA announced on 27 March 2026 the technical handover of the Ethiopian Coffee Traceability and Management System (ECTMS), described as the first national digital coffee traceability platform, developed with GIZ. ECTMS integrates geolocation data, supply-chain tracking, deforestation-risk tools and a mobile application for field farm-data collection. It is an Ethiopia-only national layer, not a substitute for the cooperative’s common three-country field platform.[115]

Ethiopian procurement must separately test the route from farm records to export lots. The reported ECX practice mixes lots from thousands of farms before exporter purchase, making plot-to-shipment linkage nearly impossible; ECTA said it would require regional authorities to centralize geolocation data in ECTMS, while reform to handle segregated traceable lots had not been announced. Historical ECX analysis likewise describes coffee purchased through ECX as non-traceable by design, whereas producer-direct export coffee was traceable because buyers could contact producers.[115][105]

The Ethiopian evidence makes bag- and lot-identity preservation a procurement requirement, not merely a mapping feature.

A cooperative-owned-device model can include members without smartphones: a trained field collector visits the plot with a cooperative-owned smartphone or Garmin device while the member confirms boundaries. The Kenyan guide estimates KES 12,000–25,000 per cooperative smartphone.[116] KoboCollect or ODK can collect records offline and synchronise when the collector returns to a Wi-Fi or mobile-data area.[116]

For remote highlands, the Kenyan coffee-cooperative guide lists Garmin GPSMAP 64s/66s at KES 35,000–55,000 per unit, with offline track-log polygon collection and GPX/KML export; its table states ±3 m accuracy, while its prose states 3–5 metre accuracy.[116]

An Indonesian coffee-export case used KoboCollect/ODK offline to capture farmer identity, village, consent, GPS point, farm size, main species, intercrops and first planting year. Its chain-of-custody records linked Farmer ID, date, weight and moisture on purchase receipts to a lot code printed on bags and recorded in drying, hulling and grading logs.[117]

Field-mapping acceptance test. In Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia, each candidate must map representative steep and canopy-covered plots using the proposed phone or external receiver and offline base maps. The cooperative should compare repeated collections against independently surveyed reference boundaries or positions; record boundary completeness and a positional-error measure in metres; and require field-collected reference points, GIS review, supervisor correction and revisits. Farmforce’s Ethiopian review within 72 hours and revisit process is a quality control, not a measured mountain-boundary accuracy result. ODK’s Android accuracy radius is a 68%-confidence estimate and its 5-metre capture setting is a target, not a measured mountain-boundary accuracy result.[8][2][110][7]

The operating model remains trained staff with Android or dedicated GNSS devices for registration and boundary walks, combined with separately operated SMS or telephone communications for feature-phone holders.[13][6][1]

For members without smartphones, the communications design should include payment or delivery confirmations by SMS, voice, USSD, printed receipt or cooperative meeting, according to local access. These channels complement, rather than replace, staff-held devices for plot mapping and inspection.

For a quote-based platform, comparable annual cost per farmer equals the annual licence, hosting, support, integration and transaction charges plus the annualised share of implementation, baseline mapping, devices and training, divided by 5,000 farmers.

### Ranked procurement verdict

1. **Select an ODK-based, staff-led pilot as the price-transparent budget benchmark, with farmer phone communications and certification exports implemented as separate layers.** ODK Cloud Standard is US$0.4776 per farmer/year at a 5,000-member denominator, while its offline Android collection, polygon capture and configurable exports support a controlled three-country test. Fairtrace and Rainforest Alliance portals remain complementary required certification layers; AtSource remains conditional on an ofi commercial relationship. [6][27][5][17][32]

2. **Solicit competing all-in three-country offers from Farmforce and KoltiTrace before a production award.** Neither managed product has yet proved the complete basic-phone, mountain-mapping and certification workflow across Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia. A managed product moves ahead of ODK only by demonstrating contracted country support; Spanish, Amharic and Bahasa Indonesia workflows; staff-led offline capture and synchronisation; the mapping acceptance test; Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade export flows; and lower total cost under the stated formula. Farmforce’s Ethiopia completion counts were 3,619 farms mapped with GPS polygons out of 4,096 farms entered,Derived — **88.4%**, during August 2025–March 2026; this is a completion rate, not a positional-accuracy result. [1][5][2][3][88]

2. **Use ODK as the budget benchmark and possible low-cost fallback.** Its published Standard subscription is US$0.0398 per farmer per month at 5,000 farmers, assuming one active project serves all three countries, with offline forms, GPS and polygon capture, offline maps and transparent language status. ODK Cloud is hosted, managed and supported by ODK’s creators; the cooperative must still fund devices, field operations, configuration, mapping and training. [5][7][63] Farmforce moves to first place if its all-in proposal meets the cooperative’s budget and its pilot demonstrates Spanish, Amharic and Bahasa Indonesia workflows, country deployment and required exports. KoltiTrace moves to first place if its pilot demonstrates those workflows and a lower all-in cost. ODK moves to first place if its configured pilot delivers the required certification, batch and buyer data flows at a lower all-in cost than either managed product. [5][2][3]

3. **Use Fairtrace where Fairtrade-certified trading requires it, but do not procure it as the farm platform.** It is fit for price, premium, volume and partner verification; it is not the mapping, member-management or field-inspection system. [27][28][29][32]

4. **Use Rainforest Alliance MyRA/RACP/MultiTrace as mandatory certification layers, not as a substitute for first-mile field management.** The cooperative’s selected farm platform must export and reconcile the data required by these official systems. [34][35]

5. **Use AtSource only if an ofi/Olam buyer relationship makes it commercially valuable.** Its documented strengths are buyer-facing sustainability assurance, farmer-group traceability and ofi field-data integration; its published access model and unspecified price make it unsuitable as the cooperative’s stand-alone selection basis. [13][17][12]

6. **In Ethiopia, demonstrate interoperability with ECTMS and compare TraceX before contracting a private platform.** ECTMS supports mobile-app farm geolocation, goods-flow documentation, deforestation-risk analysis and farm-to-export-gate traceability; TraceX describes mobile-first farmer registration, GPS mapping and lot identifiers through the export warehouse. The deciding test is a successful export, correction and reconciliation of cooperative records with Ethiopia’s required coffee-traceability workflow. [66][65]

6. **Treat national and origin-specific data systems as integrations, not replacements for the selected first-mile platform.** In Colombia, test consent-based SICA coordinate import and plot-to-shipment evidence; in Ethiopia, test ECTMS/ECTA export, geolocation centralization and preservation of farm-to-lot identity through the trading route; in Indonesia, require a demonstrated implementation and field-support plan. [77][115][75]

**Single conditional recommendation.** Select an ODK Collect-based, enumerator-led pilot with a separately integrated SMS or voice channel as the limited-budget three-country baseline, because ODK has published hosting prices and offline field forms while a basic-phone channel can be added separately.[5][59] This recommendation holds only if the cooperative funds integration, agent mapping, language support and continuing maintenance.[5][67] Farmforce or KoltiTrace should replace it only if a binding 5,000-farmer proposal proves offline field collection, required languages, validated mountainous-plot polygons, Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade exports, basic-phone participation and lower all-in operating burden across Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia.[118][57]

**The evidence supports a layered rather than single-platform decision.** AtSource is an ofi/Olam buyer-sustainability arrangement; Fairtrace is a Fairtrade transaction-assurance portal; Rainforest Alliance requires its own RACP/MultiTrace workflow. Farmforce, KoltiTrace and ODK are the candidates that can supply the cooperative’s missing first-mile data, mapping and operational layer.[13][28][34][2][3][5]

Farmforce has the clearest evidence for offline enumerator-led GPS polygon work in Ethiopian coffee, including canopy-related quality controls; KoltiTrace has the clearest stated footprint across all three countries and coffee deployments in Colombia and Indonesia; ODK offers the only transparent subscription price. None is verified as a full basic-phone farm-management platform across all three origins.[2][3][5][6][1]

In Farmforce’s Ethiopia ECOM pilot, completed GPS polygons covered 88.4% of farms entered in the system (3,619 of 4,096) during August 2025–March 2026; measured against producers onboarded, the completed-polygon coverage was 79.7%. This quantifies the field-completion gap that a cooperative must budget to revisit and resolve.[2][88]

- **Indonesia — additional AtSource/OFIS evidence:** The LASCARCOCO partnership states that registered farmers use Olam’s Farmer Information System application for transparent and traceable coffee and cocoa supply chains in North Sumatra, South Sulawesi and East Nusa Tenggara. [22]

TraceX adds an Ethiopia-focused vendor candidate with stated mobile-first onboarding, GPS mapping, lot-level farm-to-export traceability and EUDR Due Diligence Statement generation, but no published cooperative price. ECTMS adds a separately developed Ethiopian public-sector system, tested in Sidama, for mobile-app geolocation, goods-flow documentation and deforestation-risk analysis through the export gate.[65][66]

For Fair Trade USA transactions, the cooperative needs separate pricing-term and buyer/seller-ID records; for Fairtrade International transactions, it needs harvest sourcing plans, contracts that separate differentials from Premium, and auditable Premium-allocation records. Fairtrace remains the Fairtrade International transaction-assurance layer rather than a substitute for these cooperative records or the distinct Fair Trade USA requirements.[101][103][30]

Koltiva’s stated one-time registration and tailoring fee, together with training priced according to requirements, must be included in its implementation-cost comparison.[75]

Mergin Maps starts at €15.8 per contributor per month billed annually, rather than per farmer, and is free for nonprofits with income below US$30,000. QField is free and open-source; both are QGIS-based mapping options.[86] KoboToolbox is free for nonprofits and paid for companies, with polygon questions and built-in GeoJSON export.[86]

Fairtrade’s September 2026 Operational Traceability Anchor model provides a way to retain producer-originated sustainability data across product, transaction and tracking-event references. The cooperative should use existing operational references, protect authorised commercial information, retain links when identifiers change through aggregation or transformation, and ensure that the value of traceability data justifies the effort required to maintain the anchor.[39]

In Colombia, the GRAS project customised its database system and mobile mapping application to meet defined requirements, including processing data to assess smallholder farm areas for deforestation risk and adapting both tools to the latest EU regulations.[111]

In Farmforce’s Bale and Illubabor pilot, the 4,548 hectares under management equated to **1.26 hectares per GPS-polygon-mapped farm** (4,548 hectares divided by 3,619 mapped farms). This is a useful fieldwork-density benchmark for smallholder coffee mapping in the Ethiopian pilot area.[2]

Indonesia planning benchmark — Koltiva’s Gayo Highlands deployment averaged **63.3 smallholders per village** (1,900 smallholders across 30 villages). This gives a practical starting point for estimating village-level training, collector routing and synchronisation support in a highland Indonesian coffee rollout.[3]

Access and control. AtSource is ofi’s sustainable-sourcing system for customers and its product supply chains, rather than an independently licensed cooperative farm-management product.[48][17] Fairtrace is FLOCERT’s online collaboration and assurance platform for Fairtrade-certified supply-chain actors.[29][27] Rainforest Alliance certification is operated through MyRA, RACP and MultiTrace, while the cooperative needs a separately controlled first-mile system for member, plot, delivery and lot records.[90][34][35]

The open procurement points are each vendor’s all-in 5,000-farmer price and contract term; confirmed deployment and support in Colombia, Ethiopia and Indonesia; Spanish, Amharic, Oromo, Tigrinya and Bahasa Indonesia interface and support coverage; feature-phone workflows beyond SMS; offline capture, sync and dispute-resolution performance; plot-polygon accuracy and quality-control acceptance in mountainous coffee areas; direct Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrace data exchange; and cooperative ownership, export, correction and post-contract access rights for farmer, plot and transaction data. These should be settled through country-specific demonstrations, a representative field pilot and binding contract schedules before award.
