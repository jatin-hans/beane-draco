### Do I need to go to the ER now?

**Yes. Go to the ER now. Do not wait for a clinic callback or try to manage this at home first.**

Crohn’s disease can cause a stricture, meaning a narrowed part of the bowel. A stricture can make it hard for food and liquid to pass through, and if it becomes narrow enough it can cause a bowel obstruction.[11][6][12][13]

Your last scope found narrowing that was being watched. That makes today’s severe cramping and vomiting more concerning than ordinary flare symptoms because severe cramping and vomiting are recognized symptoms of a stricture or possible intestinal obstruction.[6][12][14][15][16]

An intestinal obstruction means that food or liquid cannot move normally through part of the intestine. It may be partial, meaning some material still passes, or complete, meaning nothing can pass through.[17][15][8]

A partial obstruction can cause cramping, pain that comes and goes, nausea, and sometimes vomiting. A complete obstruction can cause sudden severe pain and vomiting, often with inability to pass gas or stool.[18][19][20]

You do not need to prove that you have stopped passing gas or stool before going. Obstruction symptoms vary depending on the location and cause of the blockage, and a partial obstruction can still allow some stool to pass.[21][8]

Crohn’s-related narrowing can result from active inflammation, scar tissue, or both. Inflammation can temporarily swell an already narrowed bowel segment, while repeated inflammation and healing can leave permanent scar tissue that narrows the bowel over time.[22][20]

That distinction matters because an inflammatory narrowing and a scar-related narrowing may need different treatment. The ER and your gastroenterology team need to determine the cause rather than assuming this is a routine flare.[22][23][20]

Severe cramping with vomiting is specifically a reason to seek immediate care when obstruction is possible. Cleveland Clinic advises immediate care for severe cramping with vomiting, and Mayo Clinic advises immediate medical care for severe abdominal pain or other obstruction symptoms.[17][1]

Healthdirect advises going straight to a hospital emergency department if you have symptoms of a bowel obstruction. Its guidance specifically says that if you think you may have a bowel obstruction, do not eat or drink and go immediately to the nearest hospital.[8]

A change from your own baseline, especially new or different abdominal pain, is a reason to contact your Crohn’s team; here, the severity and vomiting push the decision beyond a routine outpatient call.[12][24][23]

Crohn’s flares themselves can cause pain and dehydration. Severe flares can cause intense pain, fever, and dehydration, so a flare remains possible; the same symptoms can overlap with obstruction and other complications.[14][13][23]

Judgment — Your history of recognizing flares is valuable, but it should not overrule a new danger pattern. In someone with known narrowing, being doubled over with cramps and vomiting everything since yesterday morning is enough to treat obstruction as a concern until an emergency clinician evaluates it.

An obstruction is not diagnosed by symptoms alone. Clinicians assess your history and abdomen and may use blood tests, an abdominal X-ray, a barium contrast study, CT, MRI, contrast fluoroscopy, or other imaging to look for an obstruction and its complications.[21][16][7]

Emergency clinicians can also assess whether the narrowing is contributing to the problem now. Imaging can show the severity of a stricture, and CT can identify complications such as obstruction, perforation, abscess, or bleeding.[6][25][7]

It is important not to assume that severe pain only means inflammation. Ongoing abdominal pain in inflammatory bowel disease can signal a flare, an intestinal blockage, or a severe infection, and pain that does not improve or becomes worse should not be ignored.[26]

Some people with Crohn’s have severe pain during flares, but severe pain can also accompany an acute complication. Acute Crohn’s complications need assessment because the treatment depends on the person’s clinical condition and the severity and location of their underlying disease.[25][14]

Untreated obstruction can damage the bowel. Gas and fluid can build up before a blockage, distending the bowel and raising pressure inside it; this can reduce its blood supply and lead to bowel tissue death, perforation, infection, sepsis, or death.[27][19][8][7]

These complications are possible outcomes, not a prediction that they are already happening to you. Prompt care can often treat an obstruction successfully and can prevent progression to the complications that make emergency surgery necessary.[17][28][7]

A complete obstruction is a medical emergency and often requires surgery. A more complicated obstruction, such as one involving reduced blood flow or a tear in the intestine, may need surgery right away.[15][16]

Most acute Crohn’s complications are managed without surgery. That is another reason to go now: emergency assessment does not mean you will need an operation, but it determines whether you need fluids, bowel rest, medicines, a procedure, monitoring, or surgery.[25][20]

A bowel obstruction can sometimes be partial and may sometimes clear without surgery. Even then, it needs diagnosis because a partial blockage can be more serious than it first appears.[21][29][7]

Medical guidance that a partial blockage may settle refers to care after clinical assessment and stabilization. It is not advice to stay home with severe pain, repeated vomiting, and a known narrowing.[21][7][19]

Hospital treatment for obstruction generally includes monitoring, intravenous fluids, and treatment based on the cause. Some people also need a tube through the nose into the stomach to remove trapped air and fluid and relieve pressure and swelling.[19][8][7]

What this decides: go to the ER now. Your symptoms raise concern for a bowel obstruction related to the known narrowing, although only an examination and testing can determine whether that is the cause.[1][7]

### Can I ride this out at home with clear liquids?

**No. Do not try to ride this out at home with clear liquids.** Clear liquids and small sips are for mild dehydration or vomiting when fluids can be retained. They are not a safe home plan when you are vomiting every drink and possible obstruction guidance says not to eat or drink before emergency assessment.[30][31][2][10][8] Home care can be reconsidered only after clinicians assess the possible obstruction and you can retain fluids—not after a trial of drinks before leaving.[10][8][7]

Clear liquids and small sips can help with mild nausea, vomiting, or mild dehydration when a person can keep fluid down. MedlinePlus advises small amounts of clear liquids often for ordinary difficulty keeping liquids down, and other guidance recommends small frequent sips for mild dehydration.[30][31][9][2]

That is not your situation. You have vomited everything you have tried to eat or drink since yesterday morning, so oral fluids are not replacing what you are losing.[32][33][31]

Dehydration occurs when the body loses more fluid than it takes in. Vomiting causes fluid loss, and being unable to drink successfully prevents you from replacing that loss.[32][33][34]

Dry mouth is a recognized symptom of dehydration. Dizziness or lightheadedness is also a recognized symptom, and dizziness on standing can occur when dehydration lowers blood volume and blood pressure.[32][35][2]

Your dry mouth and lightheadedness therefore add urgency to the vomiting. They do not tell anyone exactly how dehydrated you are, but they are warning signs that home drinking is not working adequately.[9][2]

Being unable to consume fluids without vomiting is listed as a symptom of severe dehydration for which medical attention should be sought. UnityPoint Health specifically lists inability to keep fluids down and significant dizziness or lightheadedness as reasons to go to the emergency room right away.[2][3]

Moderate dehydration may require intravenous, or IV, fluids in urgent care, an emergency department, or hospital. Severe dehydration needs medical treatment, and IV fluids may be needed when drinking cannot restore fluid and electrolyte balance.[32][33][10]

Electrolytes are minerals that help regulate body water, nerve and muscle function, heart rhythm, and blood pressure. Severe vomiting can disturb this balance, which is one reason emergency clinicians may check blood electrolytes and kidney function.[34][32]

The ER can check vital signs, assess dehydration, test blood and urine when needed, give IV fluids, and correct electrolyte problems. These are treatments and assessments that clear liquids at home cannot provide.[36][32][1]

Mild dehydration is different. Drinking water or an oral rehydration solution may be enough when the person can retain fluids and does not have major warning symptoms.[32][37][10][3]

Your inability to retain fluid is the deciding difference. You cannot safely treat dehydration by mouth if each attempt to drink leads to vomiting.[38][2][39][3]

A low-fiber or low-residue diet can sometimes reduce symptoms in a person with a known stricture by reducing undigested material passing through the narrowed bowel. That is a planned dietary approach directed by an IBD clinician or dietitian; it is not a safe treatment for this acute episode.[6][8]

Clear-liquid bowel rest is also sometimes used in obstruction care. But this is usually part of a clinician-directed plan after assessment, often alongside IV hydration and sometimes nasogastric decompression, not a stand-alone way to manage suspected obstruction at home.[25][18][14]

Some people with severe Crohn’s symptoms are prescribed a period of bowel rest, which may involve certain liquids or nothing by mouth. A doctor decides whether this is appropriate and whether it can occur at home or requires hospital care.[40][1]

For suspected bowel obstruction, Healthdirect gives the opposite instruction from general home-vomiting advice: do not eat or drink before you are examined in the emergency department.[30][31][8]

The reason is practical as well as medical. If obstruction is possible, your care team may need to decide whether you should have bowel rest, IV fluids, imaging, medicines, stomach decompression, or a procedure.[25][14][19][7]

Judgment — Do not use clear liquids as a test to see whether this passes. Repeated vomiting after attempts to drink is already the result of that test: oral hydration is failing, and the possible obstruction needs assessment.

Vomiting for longer than 24 hours is itself a reason to contact a healthcare provider immediately. In your case, the duration combines with severe cramping, known narrowing, and dehydration symptoms, which makes the ER the appropriate destination rather than routine urgent care or home observation.[30][18][1][3]

It is not the best choice here because you also need assessment for a possible bowel obstruction, including imaging and access to hospital-based treatment if it is found.[38][21][19][8]

An emergency department can evaluate the dehydration and possible obstruction at the same time. It can check your abdomen, vital signs, urine output, electrolytes, kidney function, and imaging as needed, then involve your gastroenterology or surgical team if necessary.[32][21][8][7]

If your abdomen becomes swollen, rigid, or very tender; you have severe, constant pain; you cannot pass gas or stool; or you faint, tell emergency services or the ER immediately. Severe, constant pain may mean that a blockage is affecting the bowel’s blood supply.[21][8][7]

Call 999 or go to A&E if dehydration occurs with blue, grey, pale, or blotchy skin, lips, or tongue; cold skin; difficulty breathing or lots of quick breaths; confusion; or unusual sleepiness or difficulty waking. These can be signs of shock.[35]

Get medical help right away if dehydration symptoms include confusion, fainting, lack of urination, rapid heartbeat, rapid breathing, or shock.[32]

You do not need to wait for any of those additional symptoms before leaving. Persistent vomiting and inability to keep fluids down are reasons to call 911 or go to the ER; your severe cramping and standing lightheadedness add to the need for emergency care now.[41][1][2][3]

What this decides: clear liquids are not a viable home plan for you now. They are used for mild illness when fluids stay down or as part of clinician-directed bowel rest, not when every drink is vomited and obstruction is a concern.[30][10][8]

Mayo Clinic recommends drinking plenty of fluids to help prevent or relieve some types of dizziness, but you cannot keep fluids down, so that general measure cannot replace the emergency assessment already needed.[42][2][3]

For general dehydration self-care, the Red Cross says, “If your urine is dark, yellowish brown, and smells strong, you should drink water immediately,” and that pale, “orderless” urine means someone is drinking enough. It also advises avoiding sugary, caffeinated, or alcoholic drinks.[43]

Brief observation is for a mild change: a day of looser stools after a restaurant meal, with no blood or fever, normal appetite, and the ability to drink and keep fluids down.[23]

Other dehydration clues are dark yellow, strong-smelling urine and urinating less often than usual—less than 3 or 4 times a day.[31]

It cannot guarantee that the narrowing has remained unchanged over the following six months.[20] Current symptoms and current examination matter because inflammation can increase swelling and make an already narrow section tighter during active disease.[20]

Clinicians may need to assess the severity and location of a stricture with imaging. Ultrasound, CT, or MR enterography can show the severity of narrowing, and endoscopy may also be used to confirm a narrowing. For suspected acute obstruction, clinicians may use an abdominal X-ray, CT scan, MRI, or other contrast studies depending on the situation.[6][16][7] The purpose is not merely to label the problem; it is to determine whether bowel contents are passing and whether there are signs of complications.

CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast is rated “Usually Appropriate” for suspected acute small-bowel obstruction.[57][56]

The 2026 IBD Standards say that, if imaging is indicated after admission, MRI or ultrasound should be available within 24 hours; X-ray or CT may be needed when they provide information those tests cannot.[58]

A narrowing can be inflammatory, fibrotic, or mixed. Inflammatory narrowing may improve when inflammation is treated. Fibrotic narrowing is scar tissue and does not respond to anti-inflammatory treatment. Doctors can treat an existing fibrotic stricture with endoscopic balloon dilation, but approximately 75% of people need the procedure again or surgery in the future. This distinction is one reason not to self-treat presumed obstruction as an ordinary flare with diet changes or flare medication alone.[22]

Some strictures are managed medically, some with endoscopic dilation, and some with surgery. Surgery may be needed for severe strictures that do not respond to medical treatment, repeated blockages with perforation risk, or complicated obstruction. That does not mean you will need surgery; it means a possible obstruction has to be assessed before anyone can choose safe treatment.[6][25][16]

A complete obstruction is an emergency because it can prevent the movement of food and stool entirely. CDC advises immediate medical attention if someone thinks they are experiencing a life-threatening Crohn’s complication such as complete obstruction. UCSF states that a complete intestinal obstruction is life-threatening and requires medical attention right away.[11][40]

**The scope finding does not prove that the narrowing is blocked now, but it means today’s severe cramping and vomiting cannot safely be treated as a familiar flare without urgently considering a stricture-related obstruction.**[6][12][23]

A stricture is a narrowing of the lumen—the hollow inner space—of the digestive tract, and Crohn’s strictures can occur in the small intestine or colon.[20]

The Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation and CDC describe intestinal obstruction as the most common complication of Crohn’s disease.[12][11]

A diagnosed stricture or narrowing should be monitored regularly by your IBD team to reduce complication risk and manage it.[6]

### Why is being unable to keep down anything I've tried

Being unable to keep down any drinks since yesterday morning is urgent because vomiting loses fluid and prevents you from replacing it.[33][32] Clear liquids are not a workable home plan when they do not stay down: inability to keep fluids down is a reason to go to the ER right away.[3] Dehydration occurs when you lose more fluid than you take in, and vomiting is a recognized cause.[32][33] In your case, there are two fluid problems at once: fluid is leaving through vomiting, and the usual way to replace fluid—drinking—is not working.

NHS guidance similarly suggests small sips when someone is sick.[30][35] That general advice does not fit as a plan to stay home when every attempt at food or drink has been vomited and you have severe abdominal pain, a known narrowing, dry mouth, and standing lightheadedness.

Emergency guidance treats inability to keep fluids down as a reason to seek emergency care. Mayo Clinic advises contacting a health care professional if you cannot keep fluids down, and MedlinePlus advises immediate contact for severe abdominal pain or dehydration signs such as dry mouth, infrequent urination, or dark urine.[3][37][30]

A Crohn’s-specific source also identifies this threshold. It advises going to hospital if you cannot tolerate liquids and are at risk of dehydration, or if you have pain that cannot be managed at home.[18] Another source advises emergency-department care for severe or constant abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, signs of dehydration, or a swollen or rigid abdomen.[23]

Home clear liquids are therefore not viable here—not because clear liquids are inherently harmful, but because you have already shown that you cannot retain them. Continuing to try to hydrate by mouth may delay treatment while dehydration worsens, and it does not address a possible blockage.[33][3][16]

Dehydration is more than thirst. Losing fluid reduces the amount of circulating blood volume and can disturb electrolyte balance. Electrolytes help regulate body water, support muscle and nerve function, maintain heart rate and rhythm, and help keep blood pressure stable.[2][34] With ongoing vomiting, clinicians may need to check sodium, potassium, kidney function, and urine concentration, as well as your vital signs.[32][34]

Mild dehydration can often be treated with oral fluids. Severe dehydration needs medical treatment right away.[10][37] If an obstruction is suspected, standard initial management includes bowel rest and IV hydration; when vomiting is present, a tube through the nose into the stomach may also be used to remove accumulated air and fluid.[25][7]

The difference between an oral-fluid problem and an IV-fluid problem is practical: if fluids stay down, the gut can replace some losses; if fluids do not stay down, oral replacement is unreliable. Hospital care can provide fluid and electrolytes through a vein while clinicians assess the reason for the vomiting and monitor urine output.[1][8]

**Because you cannot keep down fluids, clear liquids at home cannot correct the dehydration risk; this needs in-person assessment and may require IV fluids.**[30][10][3]

The Red Cross defines dehydration as too little fluid in the body because the body uses or loses more fluid than it takes in, and identifies vomiting or diarrhea from a virus as its most common cause.[43]

Minimal urine, confusion, or a racing heart are additional dehydration warning signs that can escalate quickly in people with colitis.[41]

### Does my very dry mouth add to the concern about

Yes. Very dry mouth is a dehydration symptom, and it adds to the concern because you are also vomiting and cannot keep fluids down.[32][33] MedlinePlus lists dry mouth as an adult dehydration symptom, and Healthdirect includes a dry mouth, lips, and tongue among signs of mild to moderate dehydration.[32][9]

Dry mouth matters more in your situation because it is occurring alongside persistent vomiting and inability to drink successfully. Dehydration means fluid loss exceeds fluid intake. You have described both sides of that imbalance: ongoing losses through vomiting and inadequate replacement because liquids are not staying down.[32][33][37]

Dry mouth alone is not a measure of how dehydrated you are. It can have other causes, including medications or conditions that reduce saliva production.[50][51] But it should not be dismissed here because it fits the broader dehydration pattern.

A clinician assesses dehydration severity through examination, vital signs, symptom history, and sometimes tests. Blood tests can check electrolytes and kidney function; urine tests can help assess dehydration and its cause.[32] Those assessments matter because dehydration can worsen from mild symptoms to low blood pressure, electrolyte imbalance, kidney problems, shock, seizures, or worse if severe and untreated.[36][37]

Crohn’s & Colitis UK describes mild Crohn’s as including being able to eat normally and having no signs of dehydration. You cannot keep food or drinks down and have dehydration symptoms, so this does not fit that mild-pattern description.[13]

The urgent issue is not whether dry mouth alone proves dehydration. It is that dry mouth strengthens concern when it appears with the much more important facts that fluid is being lost, fluids cannot be retained, and you feel lightheaded on standing.[30][9][3]

**Your dry mouth is a meaningful dehydration warning sign in this combination of symptoms, even though it cannot by itself measure dehydration severity.**[32][35]

### Does feeling lightheaded when I stand add to the concern

Yes. Lightheadedness on standing can occur when dehydration reduces blood volume and lowers blood pressure.[2] Together with dry mouth, persistent vomiting, and inability to retain fluids, it makes dehydration an urgent concern that cannot be safely managed with clear liquids at home.[32][33][3][2] UT MD Anderson explains that dehydration can reduce blood volume, causing low blood pressure and dizziness on standing.[35][2]

Standing lightheadedness may reflect a blood-pressure drop after changing position, called orthostatic hypotension. MedlinePlus defines this as blood pressure dropping when shifting from lying down to standing. A clinician can check for this by measuring whether blood pressure drops after you stand.[5][33] Feeling lightheaded does not prove that this is the cause, but dehydration and low blood pressure are plausible concerns when you have been unable to keep fluids down.

Healthdirect specifically lists being dizzy or lightheaded, particularly when standing, among possible signs of mild to moderate dehydration. NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 help if dizziness when standing does not go away, because this can be a sign of serious dehydration needing urgent treatment.[9][35] In the setting of persistent vomiting, the concern rises because the potential fluid deficit is ongoing rather than already corrected.

Cleveland Clinic advises immediate medical attention for suspected severe dehydration and lists dizziness among symptoms for which someone should go to an emergency room right away. UnityPoint Health lists significant dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting as emergency-room warning signs and explains that reduced blood volume during dehydration can make a person feel lightheaded on standing.[10][3]

Lightheadedness also creates an immediate safety issue. Dizziness can worsen when you stand or walk and can make you lose your balance.[45] The practical next step is covered in **Getting emergency care safely**: do not drive yourself while lightheaded.

The symptom is especially important because it combines with dry mouth and vomiting. Individually, dry mouth or dizziness may occur for many reasons. Together with inability to retain fluids, they indicate that dehydration may be clinically important. Electrolyte levels can show whether dehydration is severe; if severe dehydration is found, fluids are needed immediately.[9][32][1]

**Standing lightheadedness adds urgency because it may reflect reduced blood volume or low blood pressure from dehydration, and you cannot reliably correct that loss by drinking at home.**[2][5][3]

Mayo Clinic describes feeling lightheaded as though you might pass out as a type of dizziness. It says dizziness can happen when the brain does not receive enough blood, including with not being well hydrated or with orthostatic hypotension (postural hypotension).[42] MedlinePlus says that dizziness, lightheadedness, or nausea can occur when someone is about to faint; it lists heat or dehydration, emotional distress, standing up too quickly, certain medicines, a drop in blood sugar, and heart problems as causes of fainting.[46]

NHS lists dizziness, sweating, feeling warm or hot, feeling sick, and changes to vision among fainting symptoms. NHS also says standing up too quickly can signal low blood pressure and lists not eating or drinking enough and severe pain as possible causes of fainting.[47] Mayo Clinic advises emergency medical care for new, severe dizziness or vertigo with ongoing vomiting.[42]

If you feel as though you may faint while waiting for emergency transport, NHS advises lying down with your legs raised. If you cannot lie down, sit with your head lowered between your knees; NHS also advises drinking water.[47]

Dizziness or lightheadedness is also a listed sign of severe dehydration.[33]

### Synthesis

Go to the ER now, rather than waiting at home with clear liquids. Severe cramping and repeated vomiting with the narrowing seen six months ago raise concern for a possible bowel obstruction, and vomiting everything you drink with dry mouth and standing lightheadedness raises urgent concern for dehydration.[6][1][3][2] The ER can assess whether the narrowing is involved and treat dehydration, including with IV fluids if needed.[7][10] Do not drive yourself while lightheaded; have someone take you or call emergency services if you may faint or cannot get there safely.[12][14][8][2][10][32][33][9]

Clear-liquid advice applies when fluids can be retained. Emergency guidance takes precedence here because you cannot retain fluids and have severe pain with a known narrowing. The cause remains unsettled until clinicians examine you and, if needed, use imaging and laboratory tests—but the need for emergency assessment now does not depend on proving the diagnosis at home.[30][3][7][32]

## Getting emergency care safely

**Go to the emergency department now. Do not try to ride this out at home with clear liquids.** Your severe cramping, repeated vomiting, Crohn’s disease, and the known bowel narrowing need urgent in-person assessment. You do not need to prove that this is a blockage before going; the purpose of the emergency visit is to find out whether there is an obstruction and to treat dehydration or other complications safely.

### What should I do right now

Severe belly pain and vomiting are reasons to seek immediate care in Crohn’s disease, particularly when vomiting could be a sign of a blockage. A bowel narrowing (stricture) can cause severe cramping and vomiting, but those symptoms do not by themselves diagnose an obstruction.[24][6]

**This is an ER situation, not a wait-for-the-clinic situation.** Mayo Clinic advises immediate medical care for severe abdominal pain or other symptoms of intestinal obstruction, and UPMC advises going to the emergency department if you think you may have obstruction symptoms. A suspected obstruction can become dangerous if untreated, while prompt medical care can often treat it successfully.[17][21]

The immediate concern is a possible bowel obstruction: a narrowing can become tight enough that food and fluid cannot pass through normally. Only an examination and, if needed, imaging such as an X-ray or CT scan can assess that. Emergency clinicians may also check for abdominal swelling or tenderness, assess dehydration, and use blood or urine tests; severe vomiting may require fluids through an IV rather than by mouth.[21][30]

Do **not** treat this as proof that you have an obstruction. It is a serious possibility that cannot safely be confirmed or ruled out at home. Even a partial blockage that might clear still needs diagnosis because it may be more serious.[21][1]

Clear liquids are not a viable home plan in your situation. General vomiting advice does suggest taking small, frequent amounts of clear liquid when a person is having trouble keeping fluids down. But that is general self-care advice, not advice for someone with severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, and possible obstruction. For suspected bowel obstruction, Healthdirect says not to eat or drink and to go immediately to the nearest hospital; it also says not to have anything by mouth before a doctor examines you in the emergency department.[30][8]

Your dry mouth and lightheadedness on standing add urgency because you have been unable to keep fluids down. Being unable to consume fluids without vomiting is a severe-dehydration warning sign and can contribute to dehydration.[2] Dehydration reduces blood volume, which can lower blood pressure and cause dizziness or lightheadedness when standing.[2] Severe dehydration may need IV fluids and monitoring.[9][2]

Some guidance directs people with moderate symptoms to a same-day urgent-care or infusion setting. That does not fit the combination here: severe pain, repeated vomiting, inability to retain fluids, and a known narrowing. Emergency guidance identifies severe abdominal pain, vomiting, dizziness, and suspected obstruction as reasons for emergency assessment.[41][23][10]

Do not drive yourself to the emergency department: lightheadedness when you stand can make you faint or lose control of the vehicle.[47][46] NHS guidance specifically says, 《Do not drive yourself to A&E.》[47][52][35][4]

While you wait for transport, if you feel about to faint, lie down with your legs raised; if you cannot lie down, sit with your head lowered between your knees. When you need to move, stand up slowly, and sit or lie down again until the lightheaded feeling passes.[47][42] This can reduce the chance of fainting while you are waiting.

Do not eat or drink on the way, including clear liquids, because the emergency team may need to assess you for obstruction.[53][5][8]

If you become confused, faint, pass little or no urine, have a rapid heartbeat, are hard to wake, have trouble breathing or rapid breathing, develop cold or pale/grey/blotchy skin, or your condition worsens, call emergency services rather than waiting for a ride. These can be signs of severe dehydration or shock.[32][35][33]

Bring or have someone bring your medication list, your written Crohn’s flare plan if you have one, and any available report or screenshot from the scope that documented the narrowing. Write down your symptoms and medicines, what brought you to the ER, and any similar past episodes and what helped.[54] Tell the emergency team that you have Crohn’s, that your last scope showed a narrowing, when the vomiting began, that you have not been able to keep down food or drink since yesterday morning, and which gastroenterologist or IBD team follows you. This information helps the ER team assess the problem and coordinate follow-up.[54][23]

If you have an IBD-team after-hours instruction that tells you how to reach them, you can have the person travelling with you call them or notify them after you are on the way. Do not wait for a callback before leaving for emergency care: guidance for Crohn’s emergency red flags says not to wait until morning for clinic contact.[41]

Emergency care can provide things that clear liquids at home cannot: an abdominal assessment, testing for dehydration and other causes of vomiting, imaging if clinicians suspect blockage, IV fluids, and hospital monitoring if needed. Suspected bowel obstruction is usually treated and monitored in hospital, where urine output can be monitored and fluids can be given by IV.[21][9][8][7]

The decision is not between clear liquids and the ER. The relevant choice is emergency assessment now versus risking progression of a possible blockage and dehydration at home. Severe pain and vomiting with known narrowing outweigh the general advice for small sips used in uncomplicated vomiting.[30][8][24]

1. **Go to the emergency department now.**
2. **Do not eat or drink, including clear liquids, until you have been examined.**
3. **Do not drive while lightheaded; have someone take you or call your local emergency number for an ambulance if no safe ride is available.**
4. **Lie down and get up slowly while waiting, and call emergency services immediately if you faint, become confused, have breathing trouble, or rapidly worsen.**

Tell the emergency team about any medication allergies.
