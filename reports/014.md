Prefer EFSL for a retiree requiring the stronger documented issue rating, but only within a diversified allocation and with a consolidated-leverage flag at 3.43x debt to equity as at 30 September 2025.[1] KLM Axiva offers the higher common-assumption post-tax annual cash yield of 7.05%–7.57%, versus EFSL’s 6.09%–6.95%, but its reported Acuité BBB (Stable) issue rating is materially weaker than EFSL’s CRISIL A+/Stable and the spread is compensation for greater credit risk.[2][3] Senior-citizen status does not itself improve NCD interest returns, so both investor cases earn the same after-tax yield at the assumed 31.20% marginal rate.[4][5] Muthoot Mercantile was open for subscription on 8 December 2025 but should not be purchased or ranked until its series terms, instrument rating and dated capitalization metrics are specified.[6] The three-offer public-market universe was small and should not be treated as evidence of broad refinancing capacity, particularly for financial issuers exposed to liquidity shocks, collateral stress and thin secondary-market trading.[6][7][8]

Prachay Capital’s offer document was released on 8 December 2025, three days before its scheduled subscription opening.[13]

A public prospectus-based offer must also remain separate from a private placement. Motilal Oswal Financial Services approved ₹300 crore of NCDs on a private-placement basis on 2 December 2025; this was not a public subscription offer.[14] Hero FinCorp’s 1 December 2025 transaction was likewise expressly for private circulation and private placement, notwithstanding that it contemplated rated, listed, secured NCDs of up to ₹400 crore.[15] Under the NCS framework, public issues use a prospectus, shelf prospectus or tranche prospectus; private placements use a placement memorandum, and the listed-private-placement framework uses a General Information Document and Key Information Document.[16][17]

The resulting rule is operationally straightforward: first, confirm public-offer status; second, confirm that the opening date had passed and the closing date had not passed; third, confirm no effective early-close notice displaced the published window. KLM’s prospectus expressly permitted early closure or extension within the applicable regulatory limits, so its published 1–12 December window identifies scheduled availability rather than an irrevocable subscription period.[9]

This census permits a valid contemporaneous primary-market screen and rules out ranking announced, closed and privately placed instruments as though they were available on 8 December.

### ratios

Judgment — For this review, debt-to-equity means total borrowing or total debt divided by total equity, using the issuer’s stated reporting perimeter. A ratio above 2.0x is flagged as high for screening purposes. This is an analytical threshold, not a regulatory capital limit, a CRA classification or a default trigger; it identifies issuers where creditor claims materially exceed equity loss-absorption capacity.

EFSL provides the only directly comparable debt-to-equity data among the three open issuers. Its 30 September 2025 capitalization statements show a sharp difference between standalone and consolidated leverage.

EFSL’s consolidated leverage merits the flag: the reported 3.43x ratio exceeded the 2.0x analytical screen and rose from 2.87x on the consolidated basis at 31 December 2024.[1][18] The issuer’s 0.61x standalone ratio is not inconsistent with the 3.43x consolidated ratio; each uses a different economic perimeter.

EFSL’s FY2025 consolidated results, for the year ended 31 March 2025, reported profit before tax of ₹802 crore, net debt of ₹11,170 crore, corporate net debt of ₹6,325 crore and net worth of ₹5,918 crore; capital adequacy at its credit entities exceeded 32%.[110]

The consolidated measure incorporates group debt and equity, which is the more relevant measure where subsidiaries originate assets, incur obligations or require upstream support.[1]

Using EFSL’s management-reported FY2025 consolidated net debt of ₹11,170 crore and net worth of ₹5,918 crore gives net-debt-to-net-worth of approximately 1.89x as at 31 March 2025; this is not the gross-debt-to-equity measure used for the leverage screen.[110]

The indicative post-issue ratio of 3.47x is not an observed leverage outcome. EFSL stated that it assumed full ₹2,500 million issue proceeds and that actual leverage would depend on debt and equity on the deemed allotment date.[1] The debt-funded nature of the business therefore remains central to issuer analysis even though the NCD itself is secured.

KLM Axiva’s FY2025 reported assets were ₹1,933.41 crore, revenue was ₹340.66 crore and profit after tax was ₹20.19 crore. In FY2024, assets were ₹1,938.25 crore, revenue ₹315.92 crore and PAT ₹23.03 crore.[2] Revenue thus increased while PAT declined and assets edged lower. The reported results support profitability monitoring, but do not produce a total-debt-to-total-equity ratio on the defined basis.[2]

Muthoot Mercantile’s identified public-issue entry supplies a ₹75 crore base size and a 4–17 December subscription window.[6] Its debt-to-equity classification remains unsettled until issuer financial statements or the final prospectus provide both dated total-debt and total-equity inputs on a stated basis.

This ratio analysis decides the leverage flag: EFSL is flagged at the consolidated level; KLM and Muthoot Mercantile cannot be assigned a comparable debt-to-equity result from the disclosed figures above.

For NBFC and HFC issuers, debt is a core funding input, so a gross debt-to-equity ratio above 2.0x should not alone be treated as evidence of weak credit. The decision-useful comparison is against relevant NBFC/HFC peers and regulated capital, asset quality, liquidity, asset-liability gaps and funding-maturity dependence.

### Tax-adjusted investor cases

Judgment — NCD coupon interest is ordinary income. For a reproducible illustrative comparison, assume an Indian resident investor in FY2025–26, taxable under a regime producing a 30% marginal tax rate plus 4% health-and-education cess, or a 31.20% effective marginal tax rate; assume purchase at par, no brokerage or transaction charges, no capital gain or loss, full and timely payment, and tax paid when each coupon is received. Senior-citizen status alone does not alter the contractual coupon or the tax rate on NCD interest; only the investor’s applicable taxable-income position and chosen tax regime can do so.

Interest income from NCDs is added to annual income and taxed at the investor’s slab rate. This applies to payout and cumulative alternatives.[4][5] The reported coupon and stated effective-yield terms also apply across holder categories rather than creating a separate contractual senior-citizen yield.[19][20][13]

For transparent like-for-like calculation, the table uses annual-payout series only. Derived post-tax yield is the annual coupon multiplied by 68.80%, because the investor receives the same ₹1,000 principal at maturity and the assumptions exclude price effects.

| Issuer and annual-payout series | Gross coupon p.a. | Generic investor post-tax yield | Senior citizen post-tax yield under same 31.20% marginal rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| EFSL, 24 months | 8.85% | Derived — **6.09%** from 8.85% × 68.80%. [3] | Derived — **6.09%** under the same tax assumptions. [3] |

The generic-investor and senior-citizen columns are equal only because the stated illustration deliberately holds the marginal rate constant. A senior citizen with lower taxable income, a different regime or deductible income could have a different tax-adjusted return; age itself is not the driver.

The Muthoot Mercantile monthly-series post-tax figures are annual cash-income rates, not internal rates of return, and assume that monthly coupons are not reinvested.[107]

Muthoot Mercantile’s 0.50% senior-citizen coupon enhancement applies only to initial allottees in Categories II and III who are senior citizens on the deemed allotment date and continue to hold the NCDs on each record date. It does not transfer to a secondary-market purchaser and is lost on sale, gift or transfer, except for the stated death-related transfer exception.[107]

Muthoot Mercantile’s contractual 0.50% annual incentive for qualifying initial senior-citizen allottees is distinct from an age-based concession in income-tax rates; it means their specified series need not have the same after-tax cash flows as a generic allottee, even at the same marginal tax rate.[107]

Monthly and cumulative series require a cash-flow-specific after-tax internal-rate-of-return calculation. Monthly coupons produce earlier taxable receipts and reinvestment assumptions; cumulative structures require an explicit tax-recognition convention. The reported gross effective yields, including EFSL’s 10.10% monthly 120-month option and KLM’s 11.30% five-year monthly option, must not be relabelled as after-tax returns.[3][2]

The tax comparison therefore permits annual-payout ranking under common assumptions, while preventing a misleading claim that a retiree automatically receives a tax advantage on NCD interest.

For FY2025–26, the new-regime basic exemption threshold is ₹4 lakh and does not vary by age. Under the old regime, the basic exemption threshold is ₹3 lakh for a resident senior citizen aged 60 years or more and ₹5 lakh for a resident super-senior citizen; any resulting yield difference reflects taxable-income and regime choice rather than a concession attached to NCD interest.

The 8 December 2025 public-NCD choice set comprised EFSL, KLM Axiva and Muthoot Mercantile. EFSL combined CRISIL A+/Stable with lower gross yields and a material 3.43x consolidated debt-to-equity ratio; KLM offered higher stated yields, including 11.30% gross on its five-year monthly series, but carried a reported Acuité BBB (Stable) rating.[1][2] Muthoot Mercantile was open but cannot be series-ranked until its final terms, rating and capitalization figures are specified.[6] On a common 31.20% marginal-tax assumption, KLM’s annual-payout options produced 7.05%–7.57% post-tax yields, above EFSL’s 6.09%–6.95%; the yield premium is compensation for different credit and tenor exposures, not a free return advantage.[3][2]

1. Treat EFSL as the stronger documented rating choice, subject to a consolidated-leverage flag.
2. Treat KLM as the higher-income, lower-rated choice requiring a higher credit-risk tolerance.
3. Do not rank Muthoot Mercantile until its final series terms, instrument rating and dated debt-to-equity inputs are available.

## Historical precedents and pattern recognition

Public NCD history is useful for identifying how an issuer funds itself and how finance-company debt behaves when liquidity contracts. It is not a substitute for the issue-level terms, ratings, leverage and tax-adjusted returns assessed elsewhere in this report. The relevant historical question is whether prior market access would remain available when borrower cash flows, collateral values or market risk appetite weaken.

The evidence supports two distinct precedents for the December 2025 public-issue cohort: first, repeated access to retail corporate-debt funding by Edelweiss Financial Services Limited (EFSL), KLM Axiva Finvest Limited and Muthoot Mercantile Limited; second, the severe and asymmetric repricing of NBFC debt in the 2018–20 stress cycle. The first establishes funding-channel use; the second establishes the tail-risk mechanism.

### Earlier issue outcomes

SEBI’s public corporate-debt record identifies three issuers with subscription windows spanning 8 December 2025: EFSL’s ₹125 crore base issue, open 5–11 December 2025; KLM Axiva Finvest’s ₹50 crore base issue, open 1–12 December 2025; and Muthoot Mercantile’s ₹75 crore base issue, open 4–17 December 2025. Each issuer had also used the public corporate-debt market in July 2025: EFSL, 8–18 July for ₹150 crore; KLM Axiva, 9–22 July for ₹75 crore; and Muthoot Mercantile, 16–29 July for ₹75 crore. Muthoot Mercantile had additionally issued ₹50 crore in May 2024 and ₹75 crore in August–September 2024.[6]

**This repeated issuance is evidence of recurring access to public retail debt, not evidence that any issuer can refinance in all market conditions.** A public tranche that reaches the market records a completed funding opportunity. It does not show the issuer’s all-in funding cost, the degree of investor concentration, the maturity profile of its wider liabilities, the availability of bank lines, or the capacity to refinance during a market closure. That distinction is central for financial-company NCDs, whose liability structures commonly depend on continuing access to banks, debentures, commercial paper and securitisation rather than stable operating cash flow alone. The wider NBFC sector mobilises resources largely through debentures and bank borrowings; RBI reported that, at end-March 2020, debentures represented ₹9,40,499 crore, or 41.2% of NBFC borrowings, while bank borrowings represented ₹7,08,035 crore, or 31.0%.[8]

The direct issuer-specific evidence nevertheless matters. EFSL’s repeat issuance is especially clear: the company’s public-debt history includes three ₹100 crore base issues opening in April, July and October 2024, a ₹150 crore issue in July 2025, and the ₹125 crore issue opening 5 December 2025.[6] This pattern establishes continuing use of public NCDs as a financing channel over successive years.[6] It does not identify whether proceeds replaced maturing debt, funded operations or supported expansion; that use-of-proceeds analysis belongs in **Specific financial metrics** and **Comprehensive risk assessment**.

KLM Axiva’s July and December 2025 public issues similarly demonstrate repeat market access within one half-year: ₹75 crore in July followed by ₹50 crore in December.[6] The smaller December base amount should not be read as either deteriorating demand or lower financing need.

Base size is set by the issuer, and public NCD offers may carry oversubscription-retention options that make stated base size an incomplete measure of the issuer’s target funding.

Muthoot Mercantile shows the longest repeat-public-issue sequence among the three: May 2024, August 2024, July 2025 and December 2025.[6] For an institution lending against gold and other collateral, that sequence is consistent with public debt serving as a recurring component of funding rather than an isolated capital-markets transaction. It does not show that the 2025 liabilities were self-liquidating against matched assets or that the December offer had low refinancing risk.

The historical public-issue market is structurally small beside private placements. SEBI’s 2013–14 annual report recorded 1,924 listed private-placement issues raising ₹2,76,054 crore, compared with 35 public debt issues raising ₹42,383 crore.[21] A later compilation reported that in FY2021–22, private placements totalled ₹5,88,037 crore while 28 public offerings totalled ₹11,589 crore.[22] Public NCD issuance therefore gives a meaningful view of retail-market access, but not a full view of each issuer’s debt-funding capacity or the institutional market’s willingness to provide liquidity.

EFSL’s demand record illustrates the distinction between subscription liquidity and credit performance. The company reported early closure and oversubscription for a ₹200 crore issue in January 2021, and reported 1.18-times subscription, ₹4,700.08 million of subscriptions and a one-week-early close in January 2023.[23] These outcomes support the narrower conclusion that retail demand can accelerate an offer’s close during favourable issuance windows.[23] They do not establish that a high coupon was adequate compensation for risk, that secondary-market liquidity was available after listing, or that later debt-service capacity improved.

Historical offers also show that “secured” and “redeemable” describe legal form, not realised recovery. Muthoot Finance’s 2014 public issue combined secured NCDs with unsecured subordinated NCDs, was rated [ICRA]AA-/Stable for up to ₹2,000 million, and offered different tenors, coupons and cumulative-redemption structures.[24] Its terms demonstrate that the investor’s risk can differ materially across security ranking, coupon timing and redemption mechanics even within a single shelf programme.[24] The December 2025 NCD comparison must therefore use each open issue’s own contractual security and series terms rather than treating an issuer’s prior public NCD label as a substitute.

The historical record also cautions against assuming exchange listing creates dependable liquidity. A Muthoot Finance prospectus disclosed that one listed debenture had only 16 units traded on a single NSE date in 2013 and no reported NSE or BSE trades in the six calendar months before the 2014 prospectus.[24] Other series showed prices based on only 20, 25, 32 or 35 debentures on the cited dates.[24] **For retirees, the relevant liquidity assumption is therefore hold-to-maturity unless a demonstrable, current secondary-market bid exists.** This is a market-structure conclusion from the historic evidence, not a claim that the December 2025 issues cannot trade.[24]

Muthoot Mercantile’s historical debt terms provide a further issuer-specific financing comparison. Historical secured NCD examples allotted in 2020 carried coupons of 13.33% or 14.29% per annum and contractual maturities in 2027–28.[27] Examples allotted in 2020–21 carried coupons of 12.50%, 13.33% or 13.79% and contractual maturities in 2028–29.[27] The 2022 examples listed coupons of 8.50%, 9.50% and 11.00%, each on five-year terms maturing in 2027.[27]

Derived — the disclosed coupon ranges were 13.33%–14.29% for the cited 2020 series, 12.50%–13.79% for the cited 2020–21 series, and 8.50%–11.00% for the cited 2022 series, using the underlying historical instruments in, and.[27] The direction indicates substantial variation in the issuer’s contractual cost of debt over time.

Differences in maturity, investor type, security structure, collateral and prevailing market conditions mean these coupons are not a clean spread series and should not be extrapolated into the December 2025 required return.

The first conclusion from earlier outcomes is therefore practical: past issuance establishes that the three current issuers have accessed the retail public-debt market repeatedly, while past issue demand establishes only that retail funding can be available quickly in favourable windows. It rules out neither a later refinancing constraint nor a valuation loss on sale before maturity.

Franklin Templeton India’s winding up of six debt schemes in April 2020 is a further liquidity precedent: secondary-market liquidity can disappear before contractual credit loss is established. For a retiree needing an exit before redemption, exchange listing does not ensure an executable bid near par.

SEBI authorised BSE and NSE to establish and maintain corporate-bond reporting platforms through circulars dated 12 December 2006 and 1 March 2007, and subsequently permitted FIMMDA to operate a reporting platform; transactions were required to be reported within 30 minutes.[28]

Corporate-bond trading, clearing and settlement infrastructure was in place, but secondary-market activity remained subdued for a variety of reasons.[28]

### Credit-cycle comparison

India’s 2018–20 NBFC stress period is the most relevant cycle precedent for finance-company NCDs because it combined a default shock, risk aversion, uneven access to refinancing and severe credit-spread widening. Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services Ltd. (IL&FS), a core investment company, defaulted on debt-payment obligations in September 2018.[8] The NIPFP analysis identifies that event and the COVID-19 pandemic beginning in India in March 2020 as the two major recent bond-market shocks, while also noting subsequent problems at DHFL, Indiabulls Housing Finance and Yes Bank.[29]

The key result was not simply higher benchmark rates. NIPFP’s Bloomberg-sourced analysis found that five-year AAA NBFC credit spreads nearly doubled from September to November 2018 and reached almost 160 basis points by February 2019.[29] Before the IL&FS shock, the corresponding five-year AAA spreads had been about 40–60 basis points, while three-year AAA spreads were 50–100 basis points.[29] The market repriced even top-rated NBFC paper because the event altered perceived sector liquidity and credit risk.

The repricing persisted. Five-year AAA NBFC spreads returned to around 140–150 basis points by July 2019 and remained around that level, with fluctuations, through November 2019. NIPFP linked the persistence to further concerns involving DHFL, Indiabulls Housing Finance and Yes Bank.[29] Private-corporate and PSU spreads also widened after IL&FS, but less than NBFC spreads; NIPFP characterised the immediate liquidity crunch as primarily affecting NBFCs.[29]

The divergence between issuer categories also matters for relative value. Before the IL&FS event, NBFC, private-corporate and PSU spreads were closely bunched, despite all being AAA rated. From October 2018, the gap between private/NBFC paper and PSU paper widened, particularly in the second half of 2019.[29] During March and April 2020, average PSU spreads were only 30–35 basis points, while the gap between NBFC/private-corporate spreads and PSU spreads widened to around 100 basis points.

The source attributed the lower PSU spreads to investors’ perception of implicit government support.[29] This is evidence that a credit rating did not fully determine traded credit risk or liquidity premium.

CRISIL’s FY2015–FY2025 ratings-transition study reported average three-year cumulative default rates of 0.29% for AA, 0.66% for A and 2.21% for BBB ratings.[113]

**The historical comparator supports credit selection beyond rating labels: equal ratings did not prevent sector- and ownership-linked spread divergence.** A pensioner or institutional portfolio should therefore compare an open NCD not only with same-letter-rated securities, but also with securities of similar maturity, issuer type, security ranking, payment structure, liquidity and government-linkage characteristics. The specific relative-value calculation belongs in **Competitive positioning and relative value analysis**.

The COVID-19 episode intensified the same mechanism. NIPFP reported that NBFC and corporate spreads rose by roughly 30–40 basis points from February to April 2020, then peaked in the first half of May at close to 180 basis points for NBFCs and 170 basis points for corporates.[29] This occurred even as government yields fell sharply: the five-year risk-free rate declined from about 8.4% in September 2018 to about 5.5% in June 2020, a 300-basis-point reduction, and the three-year rate fell nearly 350 basis points to about 4.5%.[29] NIPFP concluded that risk-free rates were declining while credit spreads remained elevated, limiting the transmission of policy easing into lower corporate funding costs.[29]

That relationship is the **credit-spread override**: falling government yields can coexist with rising or persistent corporate credit spreads. For a holder of a fixed-rate NCD, the consequence is twofold. First, a lower policy-rate environment does not assure refinancing at a lower coupon for the issuer. Second, a security’s secondary-market price can fall if its credit spread widens by more than the government yield falls. This is a mark-to-market and liquidity risk, even if contractual coupons remain current.

RBI evidence reinforces the funding-channel transmission. In 2019–20, banks reduced subscriptions to NBFC debentures and commercial paper because of risk aversion; in the first half of 2020–21, bank exposure revived through direct lending and debenture investments, supported by the Partial Credit Guarantee Scheme, targeted long-term repo operations and the Special Liquidity Scheme.[8] Mutual-fund confidence in NBFC paper remained weak through 2019–20 and June 2020, with investments concentrated in a few large, well-rated NBFCs; mutual funds exited NCDs and commercial paper of small NBFCs during March and June 2020 amid heightened risk aversion.[8]

The cycle also showed that system liquidity does not ensure issuer liquidity. RBI reported that system liquidity was comfortable from the second half of FY2018–19, but vulnerable NBFCs and HFCs still faced increased funding costs and partial loss of market access.[30] In June 2019, RBI described stronger companies as continuing to raise funds while firms with asset-liability mismatch or asset-quality concerns faced higher borrowing costs.[31]Judgment — This is the appropriate historical lens for the December 2025 issues: assess each issuer’s rollover capacity, asset-liability matching, collateral realisation, funding diversification and covenant headroom rather than assuming that sector liquidity reaches every borrower.

CRISIL’s methodology assesses financial-sector liquidity through cumulative asset-liability mismatches by maturity bucket and liquid assets relative to maturing liabilities. It also notes that banks and primary dealers can access the RBI liquidity adjustment facility and call-money markets, whereas NBFCs and HFCs cannot.[114]

The 2019 DHFL debt-payment crisis is a separate Indian housing-finance NCD precedent within the post-IL&FS stress sequence. NIPFP records that the crisis at Dewan Housing and Finance Limited (DHFL), alongside problems at IndiaBulls Housing Finance and Yes Bank, added to bond-market risk perception while five-year AAA NBFC spreads remained around 140–150 basis points from July through November 2019.[29]

CRISIL defines an annual default rate as defaults during a period divided by firms rated at its beginning, and recognises default at the first missed rupee of principal or interest regardless of the amount or length of delay.[113][114]

India Ratings defines a transition rate as migration between beginning- and ending-period rating categories; it treats the first failure to make a full interest or principal payment on the pre-agreed date as default.[115]

Policy intervention softened but did not erase the stress. By the third week of June 2020, NIPFP found NBFC and corporate spreads had declined about 50 basis points from their mid-May peaks after RBI and government measures, but private-sector and NBFC spreads remained substantially high and similar to post-IL&FS December 2018 levels. PSU spreads, in contrast, had returned around pre-IL&FS levels.[29] RBI also reported that private-placement NCD mobilisation declined in Q4:2019–20 amid low investor confidence, then rebounded in Q1:2020–21 when surplus liquidity returned; it described this funding channel as largely occupied by highly rated, well-functioning NBFCs.[8]

The historical interventions were broad and conditional rather than NCD-holder guarantees. TLTRO funding was directed toward investment-grade corporate bonds, commercial paper and NCDs, with up to 50% of incremental eligible holdings acquired in the primary market.[32] TLTRO 2.0 offered up to ₹0.5 trillion to support entities with liquidity constraints or restricted market access, including smaller NBFCs and MFIs, and required at least half of bank investments to reach small and mid-sized NBFCs and MFIs.[32] The Special Liquidity Scheme later disbursed ₹7,125.51 crore through 28 instruments to 23 NBFCs/HFCs and reported repayment of ₹7,249.60 crore in principal and interest without default by 31 December 2020; the scheme itself had expired on 30 September 2020.[25] These programmes demonstrate that policy can stabilise funding markets during systemic stress. They do not convert an ordinary public NCD into government-guaranteed debt.

The RBI tightened the policy repo rate by 250 basis points, from 4.00% in May 2022 to 6.50% in February 2023. This illustrates that fixed-rate NCD issuers can face a higher marginal borrowing cost and refinancing burden even where the existing instrument’s coupon is fixed.

The relevant tail-risk mechanism is now more explicitly described in the IMF’s 2025 India Financial Sector Assessment Program. In its adverse NBFC liquidity scenario, rising non-performing loans reduce cash inflows; borrowers may then fail to repay maturing commercial paper, bank debt and other borrowings; mutual-fund redemptions can induce bond sales or non-rollover of commercial paper; and the resulting market dislocation further tightens funding.[33] The IMF also identifies covenant-triggered acceleration as a risk: lenders can front-load repayments after defaults, worsening a funding squeeze.[33] This mechanism directly connects asset-quality stress with refinancing risk and then with bond-market liquidity.

RBI’s September 2025 stress testing confirms that liquidity mismatch and concentration remain live sector-level risks. In a one-year stress test of 174 NBFCs, the baseline projected GNPA rising from 2.3% in September 2025 to 2.9% in September 2026 and aggregate CRAR falling from 22.8% to 21.7%; eight NBFCs could breach the 15% minimum CRAR in the baseline, and 11 under medium and severe scenarios.[34] A separate liquidity test found that three NBFCs could have a negative cumulative liquidity mismatch of more than 20% under baseline assumptions, four under a 5% inflow decline and 5% outflow increase, and seven under a 10% inflow decline and 10% outflow increase.[34] These are sector stress results, not forecasts of outcomes for EFSL, KLM Axiva or Muthoot Mercantile.

The period before December 2025 was nevertheless stronger than the earlier stress cycle in aggregate. The IMF reported that NBFC gross NPAs fell from 6.8% in FY2019–20 to 4.0% in FY2023–24, net NPAs fell from 3.4% to 1.1%, risk-weighted capital adequacy increased from 23.7% to 26.6%, and return on assets increased from 1.3% to 3.3%.[33] RBI’s June 2025 Financial Stability Report characterised the sector as resilient but identified household-balance-sheet stress, asset quality and funding costs—especially for lower-rated companies—as vulnerabilities; retail-loan GNPA was 3.1% for NBFCs against 1.2% for banks in March 2025.[7]

India Ratings’ adjusted ten-year transition matrix found that, after one year, 88.3% of IND BBB ratings remained IND BBB, 4.1% migrated to IND BB and 1.5% migrated to IND D.[115]

CRISIL reported an overall FY2025 annual default rate of 0.7%, 371 defaults and a 17-year low across approximately 7,150 rated companies, excluding INC issuers.[113]

In FY2025, India Ratings reported stable outlooks for 84% of ratings, negative directional indicators for 6% and positive directional indicators for 10%.[115]

CRISIL cautioned that the growing share of BBB-or-higher ratings partly reflected shrinkage of its lower-rated portfolio following higher bank thresholds for externally rated exposures and increased non-cooperation, rather than a comparable improvement in banks’ underlying loan books.[113]

Among firms that ultimately defaulted over the 37 years through FY2025, CRISIL reported an average time to default of 62 months from AA, 50 months from A and 39 months from BBB. Textiles, construction and engineering, food products, real-estate development, distributors, and metals and mining accounted for almost half of defaults.[113]

This improves the baseline relative to 2018–20 but does not overturn the historical pattern. RBI reported that NBFC reliance on bank funding had decreased as higher risk weights on bank lending to NBFCs took effect.[7] As of March 2025, non-HFC NBFCs had gross payables of ₹21.15 lakh crore against gross receivables of ₹2.26 lakh crore; more than half of their funding came from scheduled commercial banks, while more than 70% came through long-term loans and long-term debt, although the share of short-term loans and commercial paper increased during the second half of 2024–25.[7] For the common set of upper-layer NBFCs, borrowing growth continued to exceed credit growth through September 2025, while GNPA and provision coverage remained stable against March 2025.[34]

The second conclusion is therefore conditional but firm: the 2018–20 experience supports a refinancing/liquidity-tail-risk interpretation for finance-company NCDs. A renewed asset-quality shock or confidence event can reprice even highly rated private finance-company debt more severely than government-linked paper, impede rollover and weaken secondary-market exit liquidity. It does not support a prediction that any December 2025 issue will default.

The 2013 taper-tantrum episode is a broader fixed-income precedent for long-tenor retail NCDs: an abrupt global-rate and capital-flow repricing can raise domestic yields, pressure the rupee and reduce demand for lower-liquidity credit. The resulting mark-to-market loss is greater for longer-duration fixed-rate series and can coincide with a wider liquidity premium.

The global credit-market crisis that began in mid-2007 destabilised bank and financial-institution lending, reduced liquidity and made loan renewals more difficult; Muthoot Finance warned that a similar event could raise borrowing costs and impair access to debt.[24]

The IMF identifies a 2016 banking-sector distress episode triggered by defaults of large power and infrastructure companies that were unrelated to macrofinancial conditions.[33] This episode shows that concentrated corporate-sector defaults can generate financial-sector stress independently of a broad macroeconomic shock.[33]

The historical record establishes recurrent public-debt access for EFSL, KLM Axiva and Muthoot Mercantile, with Muthoot Mercantile showing the longest sequence of public issues across 2024–25. It also establishes that prior access and even oversubscribed retail offerings do not eliminate refinancing risk.[6][23]

The stronger precedent is the 2018–20 credit cycle: AAA NBFC spreads widened sharply after IL&FS, stayed elevated despite falling risk-free rates, and remained wider than PSU spreads after policy support.[29] The direction of the evidence is that recurring issuance is a funding-access indicator, whereas resilience depends on issuer-specific liquidity, asset quality, leverage, security enforceability and maturity matching.[29] Aggregate NBFC capital and asset quality improved by 2023–24, but RBI and IMF stress tests still identify liquidity-mismatch, concentration and funding-feedback risks.[33][34]

## Multiple authoritative sources

Indian primary disclosures—not terminal screens, market commentary or overseas filings—are the authoritative record for whether a public NCD issue was open, its contractual cash flows, security, instrument rating and issuer financial disclosures. Commercial data platforms are useful for secondary-market valuation only when the committee retains a dated extract showing the exact security, pricing convention and benchmark curve used.

### Bloomberg

Bloomberg describes a fixed-income platform with security-level data, Bond Search, price/yield/spread analytics, comparisons against benchmark bonds and curves, sovereign-market monitoring, swap-curve analysis, corporate-bond and CDS spread monitoring, and curve graphing that can include new-issue points. These functions are suitable in principle for matched-tenor Indian government, swap and credit-relative-value analysis. Bloomberg also describes its platform as providing fixed-income coverage from idea generation through execution.[35]

**Bloomberg was potentially suitable for calculating a matched Indian NCD spread on 8 December 2025, but no dated Bloomberg extract in the record identifies an open public NCD series, its price or yield, its rating, its subscription status, or a matched Indian sovereign, swap or credit benchmark on that date.** The platform descriptions establish functionality rather than a market observation, and index coverage of more than 35 million instruments likewise does not identify coverage of a particular Indian public issue.[35][36]

The accessible Bloomberg Markets page displayed India’s 10-year government-bond yield as 7.27%, with changes labelled +2 for one day, –9 for one month and +79 for one year, at 6:56 AM. That display cannot serve as an 8 December 2025 valuation input because the India row carries no calendar date and the same page included a New Zealand observation dated 17 November 2026.[37]

Bloomberg-attributed sources remain useful for historical context. NIPFP’s analysis of the IL&FS-to-pandemic period used Bloomberg data to examine three- and five-year AAA spreads for NBFCs/HFCs, private corporates and PSUs from April 2018 through June 2020. RBI’s June 2025 Financial Stability Report also used Bloomberg for international inflation, policy rates, global asset prices, financial-condition measures and analyst yield forecasts. These sources demonstrate Bloomberg’s role in historical spread and macro analysis, not the primary-market clearing spread or curve level for the December 2025 cohort.[29][7]

Judgment — For a reproducible relative-value calculation, the committee should preserve the Bloomberg security identifier or ISIN, clean price, settlement date, yield convention, benchmark curve identifier, benchmark tenor and timestamp. A displayed coupon is a contractual cash-flow term; it is not a traded spread, and cannot substitute for a contemporaneous secondary-market valuation.

This source distinction permits Bloomberg to support valuation only where the underlying dated terminal output is retained; it rules out treating general Bloomberg capability or an undated web display as a cutoff-date benchmark.

The retained Bloomberg record should also identify the pricing source and whether the observation is an executed trade, evaluated price, indicative yield or reference-curve point. Small retail-NCD series may have sparse directly comparable secondary-market observations, so observed liquidity and quote quality are separate inputs to any spread conclusion.

### FactSet

FactSet’s fixed-income capability was substantial before the cutoff. Its 2017 arrangement with IHS Markit gave FactSet clients access to pricing data on more than two million fixed-income securities, including corporate, sovereign and agency bonds, CDS, European ABS and specified US securitised products. FactSet later described global coverage of more than 10 million unique fixed-income instruments and derivatives.[38][39]

FactSet’s Fixed Income Calculation API can derive analytics ranging from yields and spreads to option-adjusted sensitivities using supplied prices and security inputs. Its documented output fields include price, accrued interest, full price, yield to worst, actual spread to worst call, effective duration, OAS and spread duration. The API accepts bond identifiers including ISIN, CUSIP and SEDOL; it requires a security symbol, face value, calculation method and value, and a discount curve.[40]

The API permits a yield-curve date, defined as the as-of date for the curve environment and associated market data. This makes it technically capable of supporting a controlled historical valuation, provided the user supplies the correct Indian issue identifier, contractual cash flows, settlement convention, observed price and Indian discount curve. Its published example, however, used a fictional corporate-bond price set from European exchanges dated 1 December 2020.[40]

**FactSet was a potentially useful analytics engine for the December 2025 public NCDs, but its documented coverage and calculation functions do not establish issue-level offer terms, subscription status, observed prices, Indian credit spreads or Indian rate-curve levels for 8 December 2025.** The earlier distribution through FactSet of IHS Markit pricing and iBoxx datasets shows broad fixed-income distribution, but does not identify an Indian public-NCD series or a dated Indian benchmark.[38][39][40]

Judgment — FactSet should be used here as a calculation and control environment, not as a substitute for the prospectus. The prospectus supplies coupon, maturity, call/put and redemption cash flows; FactSet can then calculate yield, duration and spread from a documented market price and curve. A yield generated from incomplete or mismatched security inputs is precise-looking but not comparable.

This distinguishes analytical capacity from verified market data and prevents a platform’s global coverage claim from becoming evidence of December 2025 Indian-NCD pricing.

For every FactSet-derived financial or market figure, the committee record should retain the field definition, standalone or consolidated basis, fiscal period, currency and units, as-of date, and source provenance. This is particularly necessary where issuer financials, corporate actions or market prices are aggregated across sources.

### SEC filings

US SEC filings are relevant only where an issuer or relevant guarantor has a US registration, listing or reporting obligation. A statement that securities are not registered under the US Securities Act is instead an offering-distribution restriction: it limits how the securities may be offered or sold in the United States or to US persons. It does not determine whether the issuer files periodic reports with the SEC.

ICL Fincorp’s separate 2026 NCD prospectus states that those NCDs had not been and would not be registered under the US Securities Act and could not be offered or sold in the United States except through an exemption or a transaction outside the registration requirement. That language concerns that later NCD offering; it does not establish ICL Fincorp’s SEC reporting obligations.[41]

Tata Capital’s investor page contains similar US Securities Act language for an equity-share IPO, including exempt offers to qualified institutional buyers and offshore Regulation S transactions. That disclosure concerns Tata Capital equity shares, not an NCD issue, and does not establish the US registration or SEC reporting status of any public NCD issuer.[42]

An SEC-hosted exhibit exists for historical Sterlite Industries (India) Limited, whose disclosure stated that Sterlite was listed on BSE, NSE and NYSE. This demonstrates that an Indian issuer with a NYSE listing could have SEC-hosted disclosure; it does not extend SEC applicability to Edelweiss Financial Services Limited, Adani Enterprises Limited, or another Indian issuer merely because it issues NCDs in India.[43]

**US Securities Act non-registration language should therefore not be treated as evidence of an SEC periodic-reporting obligation, and the historical Sterlite exhibit should not be used as a proxy filing for the December 2025 Indian public-NCD issuers.** Indian offer documents, stock-exchange filings, rating rationales and issuer financial statements remain the applicable issue-level records.[41][42][43]

The cited Muthoot Mercantile draft prospectus records a board approval dated 13 August 2026 and a rating letter dated 21 September 2026; neither establishes the terms or rating of its December 2025 offer.[27] A separate CRISIL rationale for Muthoot Money is dated 18 March 2026 and does not establish Muthoot Mercantile’s December 2025 instrument rating.[112]

This decides the filing hierarchy: SEC material is issuer-specific and conditional; it cannot displace the Indian primary documents governing a domestic public NCD offer.

### industry reports

SEBI’s public corporate-debt issue table identifies three public issues with subscription windows spanning 8 December 2025: Edelweiss Financial Services Limited, open 5–11 December with a ₹125.00 crore base issue; KLM Axiva Finvest Limited, open 1–12 December with a ₹50.00 crore base issue; and Muthoot Mercantile Limited, open 4–17 December with a ₹75.00 crore base issue. The same table distinguishes a closed ICL Fincorp issue, which ran 17–28 November, from Prachay Capital, scheduled to open only on 11 December.[6]

This public-issue census must not be mixed with private placements. BSE itself distinguishes debt announcements, lists of debt public issues and lists of privately placed debt instruments. Hero FinCorp’s 1 December 2025 Key Information Document, for example, expressly states that its NCDs were offered on a private-placement basis and that the document was neither a prospectus nor a statement in lieu of a prospectus.[44][15]

Edelweiss’s 21 November 2025 draft prospectus identifies an instrument rating—not merely an issuer rating—of CRISIL A+/Stable for ₹12,000 million under CRISIL’s 11 November 2025 rating letter, read with its 9 September 2025 rationale. At 30 September 2025, Edelweiss reported standalone total debt of ₹35,023.21 million, total equity of ₹57,041.94 million and debt-to-equity of 0.61x; it separately reported consolidated total debt of ₹193,277.69 million, total equity of ₹56,364.33 million and debt-to-equity of 3.43x. These are different reporting perimeters and must not be merged.[1]

Edelweiss stated that, net of issue expenses, proceeds would repay or prepay existing interest and principal and fund general corporate purposes. It also stated that deployment relied on internal estimates, had not been appraised by a bank or financial institution, management retained significant discretion over deployment priority, and no monitoring agency was appointed. The issue therefore combines a CRISIL-rated refinancing instrument with proceeds-allocation and refinancing risk that requires monitoring after allotment.[1]

KLM’s BSE-hosted prospectus confirms a public issue of ₹1,000 face-value NCDs open 1–12 December 2025. The NCDs were direct, secured obligations ranking pari passu among themselves, with a first charge on identified movable assets; claims were stated to be superior to unsecured creditors, subject to law. The prospectus says the issue was not underwritten and that the company would maintain security cover of 100.00% or more of outstanding principal and interest before listing.[9]

KLM’s security package is conditional on creation and registration of the charge or independent verification by the debenture trustee; proceeds remain in the public-issue account until execution of the Debenture Trust cum Hypothecation Deed. The issuer also disclosed an RBI ₹10 lakh penalty remitted on 11 April 2025 and supervisory observations relating to loan sanctioning and disbursement, NPA provisioning, accounting, governance, related-party monitoring, microfinance, KYC and IT controls. Secured status is consequently relevant to recovery ranking but does not eliminate operational, regulatory or asset-quality risk.[9]

SEBI’s Master Circular requires public-issue offer documents to disclose fictitious-application provisions, underwriter-resource declarations where applicable, reservations, prior three-year utilisation of issuer and group-company issue proceeds, promoter/director interests in the issue objects, and material contracts. For NBFCs and HFCs, disclosures also cover lending from debt-security proceeds over the previous three years, lending policy, loan classifications, top-20-borrower exposure and overdue or non-performing loans.[45][46]

The framework also requires Actual/Actual cash-flow illustrations, specifies coupon and maturity payment-date conventions, and requires an abridged prospectus in the prescribed format for public issues opening from 1 October 2023. Abridged-prospectus disclosures must be adequate, accurate and not misleading, while qualitative claims must have quantitative support.[46]

Industry reports add sector context but cannot replace audited issuer figures or contractual terms. ICRA’s April 2022 report 《Retail-NBFC Credit Trends》 revised the retail-NBFC outlook to Stable from Negative and expected FY2023 recovery to strengthen; its scope covered funding, liquidity, capital, asset quality, earnings and retail asset classes. RBI’s June 2025 Financial Stability Report found that Indian financial conditions tightened in January–March 2025 as money-market and corporate-bond spreads widened, then eased from April after liquidity measures and policy-rate cuts.[47][7]

In its 13 October 2025 rationale, CRISIL placed Sammaan Capital’s long-term debt instruments and bank facilities at `Crisil AA/Watch Developing` following a proposed controlling-stake acquisition by International Holding Company through Avenir Investment RSC Ltd., subject to regulatory and other approvals. CRISIL reaffirmed ₹1,000 crore of short-term NCDs and ₹25,000 crore of commercial paper at `Crisil A1+`; the developing watch was not a stable outlook.[116]

CRISIL reported Sammaan Capital liquidity of approximately ₹9,193 crore at 31 August 2025 against approximately ₹3,313 crore of debt due through 28 February 2026. Its 13 October 2025 rationale also reported that liquidity covered 90–100% of debt repayments over the following 12 months and that its cumulative asset-liability maturity gap was positive across all buckets at 30 June 2025, excluding pending loan commitments and derivative exposures.[116]

CRISIL reported that Sammaan Capital’s consolidated gross non-performing asset ratio rose from 1.32% at 31 March 2025 to 1.45% at 30 June 2025; at the latter date, mortgage GNPA was 1.06% and commercial-credit GNPA was 5.81%.[116]

The IMF’s February 2025 Financial Sector Assessment Program found that the corporate-bond market remained less liquid than developed market segments and that financial institutions were major corporate-bond issuers. It also recorded three major mutual-fund redemption episodes since 2018 triggered by corporate-bond-market distress: during the 2018–19 episode, mutual funds reduced NBFC commercial-paper exposure by more than 60%; in March 2020, they cut one-quarter of NBFC CP exposure as refinancing conditions deteriorated.[33]

**The sector evidence supports a funding-sensitive credit discipline: public NCD selection should focus on contractual maturity, issuer liquidity, refinancing needs, security perfection and borrower-asset quality rather than coupon alone.** The June 2025 easing in financial conditions is favourable context, but the 2018–20 episodes show how quickly NBFC funding access can reverse in a stress cycle.[7][33]

The conflicting Edelweiss close dates require careful treatment: SEBI’s public-issue table records 5–11 December 2025, while IPO Watch reported 5–18 December 2025. For the 8 December census both schedules include the issue; for subscription-period reporting, SEBI’s statutory public-issue record should govern unless an issuer or exchange notice documents an amended close date.[6][3]

This primary-source hierarchy establishes the open-issue universe and the fields that can support committee review, while industry analysis supplies only the funding-cycle and market-liquidity context.

Edelweiss’s December 2025 issue was secured and redeemable, had a face value of ₹1,000 per NCD, a base issue of up to ₹1,250 million, an oversubscription retention option of up to ₹1,250 million and a total issue limit of ₹2,500 million.[48]

### Matched-credit relative value

Judgment — A valid “matched spread” compares each series’ residual maturity, seniority/security, rating and cash-flow structure with same-segment traded or matrix yields, not its coupon. FIMMDA’s methodology likewise groups bonds by sector and rating, applies spreads to the Government-security base curve using residual maturity, and uses qualifying trades, polls and interpolation.[56]

The BSE report illustrates the range of nominal financial-sector yields: Poonawalla Fincorp due March 2028 showed 8.20%; Bajaj Finance due June 2028, 8.43%; HDB Financial Services due June 2028, 8.58%; Muthoot Finance due May 2028, 8.99%; and Muthoot MCred due October 2029, 10.19%.[57] The displayed rows have no observation date and differ in issuer and maturity, so they are context rather than an 8 December 2025 benchmark. **No generic-investor ranking among Edelweiss, KLM Axiva and Muthoot Mercantile follows from coupon alone.**

Sakthi Finance’s 2025 prospectus listed seven ₹1,000 secured NCD options spanning 24 to 85 months. Its 24- and 36-month monthly options carried 9.00% coupons and its 60-month monthly option 10.25%; stated cumulative effective yields were 9.31% for 24 and 36 months, 10.65% for 60 months and 10.38% for 85 months.[111]

The duration benchmark should first be the contemporaneous Indian sovereign or state-development-loan curve, followed by similarly rated, similarly senior secured or unsecured corporate or NBFC market yields. An apparent spread must compensate for expected credit loss, liquidity, subordination or security differences, refinancing risk, reinvestment or call risk, and tax drag.

A credit rating is not a recommendation to buy, hold or sell, and it is not intended to measure liquidity risk, pre-payment risk, interest-rate risk, secondary-market loss risk or exchange-loss risk.[58]

FIMMDA publishes its corporate-bond spread matrix twice a month, with CRISIL as calculating agent.[56] For a quoted bond with a transaction in the 15 days before the valuation date, the adopted value should not exceed the recorded exchange transaction rate.[56] For an issuer-callable bond, the methodology uses the lower of the values to final maturity and to each call date.[56]

### Investor alternatives

SBI paid senior citizens 6.95% for two-to-less-than-three-year deposits and 7.05% for five-to-ten-year deposits under rates effective 15 July 2025.[55] SCSS offered 8.2% annually for Q1–Q3 FY2025-26, paid quarterly, with a ₹30 lakh maximum investment and taxable interest.[59]

Judgment — Senior-citizen status does not itself alter an NCD interest tax rate; it alters returns only where a product grants an age-linked rate premium or where the investor’s taxable-income profile produces a different marginal rate. SBI’s 50-basis-point We-care premium applies to specified five-to-ten-year senior deposits, not NCDs.[55] NCD yield calculators also expressly exclude taxes.[12] **Therefore, neither stated NCD effective yield nor senior-deposit premium is a senior citizen’s post-tax NCD yield.**

For a plain annual-coupon NCD bought and redeemed at par, the one-year after-tax coupon-yield approximation is coupon × (1 − marginal tax rate); it is not an after-tax yield-to-maturity. Discounted, premium-redemption, cumulative, non-annual-payment, callable, amortising or secondary-market series require payment-date after-tax cash flows and an internal-rate-of-return calculation using the stated tax regime, marginal rate, surcharge, cess, purchase price, holding period, coupon-interest treatment and capital-gains treatment. A tax-deducted-at-source threshold affects cash-flow timing and possible refund administration, not the investor’s final tax liability.

The reviewed NCDs may offer a gross yield premium over bank deposits, but that premium purchases unsecured-or-credit-sensitive issuer exposure and uncertain secondary-market liquidity; listed NCDs can trade below issue price and may lack an active market.[12][60] Poonawalla’s growth profile and Manappuram’s gold-loan scale illustrate why issuer comparison cannot be reduced to coupon, while KLM’s regional retail franchise and Edelweiss’s diversified group create different risk transmission channels.[50][51][2][3]

1. Prefer only a series whose issue rating, seniority and cash flows justify a positive matched spread against same-rating, same-tenor alternatives.
2. For retiree mandates, compare the after-tax cash-flow schedule with SBI deposits and SCSS before accepting NCD liquidity and credit risk.
3. Do not rank the open issues on the presently stated gross coupons or effective yields alone.

## Comprehensive risk assessment

### tail risks

Judgment — For a retiree or a mandate requiring predictable cash flows, the relevant risk is not merely a missed coupon. It is the sequence in which weaker collections raise funding costs, refinancing becomes unavailable, collateral loses realisable value, and a listed security cannot be sold at a usable price. The issue terms and issuer disclosures discussed in Specific financial metrics should therefore be read as contractual protections, not substitutes for credit underwriting.

India’s 2018–20 non-bank cycle supplies the closest domestic precedent. System liquidity was comfortable from the second half of FY2018-19, yet vulnerable NBFCs and HFCs faced higher funding costs and partial loss of market access. In March 2020, mutual funds cut one-quarter of their NBFC commercial-paper exposure; the funding squeeze intensified when funds ceased refinancing NBFC borrowings, although banks and RBI-supported facilities partly filled the gap.[30][33] The IMF records that all three major mutual-fund redemption episodes since 2018 were triggered by corporate-bond-market distress, and that mutual funds reduced exposure to NBFC commercial paper by more than 60% in the 2018–19 episode.[33]

The transmission mechanism is material for non-deposit-taking NBFC issuers: weaker borrower collections reduce cash inflows; lenders can enforce covenants or curtail facilities; maturing commercial paper, NCDs and bank debt must then be refinanced into a risk-averse market; and debt-fund redemptions can create asset sales and lower rollover rates. The IMF’s adverse scenario expressly combines these elements, including covenant-triggered acceleration, corporate-bond and CP-market dislocation, mutual-fund redemptions, fire sales and reduced refinancing.[33] This is a plausible tail scenario, not a forecast of default for a named issuer.

Indel Money has the clearest disclosed near-term refinancing exposure. As of 30 June 2025, ₹21,280.15 lakh—₹212.8015 crore—of its outstanding NCDs, excluding interest but including matured and unredeemed NCDs, were due within the following 12 months. Indel stated that repayment would require refinancing, equity capital or sufficient internally generated cash, and that refinancing may become difficult in volatile credit markets.[49] Its liabilities include fixed-rate NCDs, while other liabilities are linked to bank benchmark prime lending rates or base rates; it states that it does not hedge interest-rate exposure. A mismatch between asset yields and funding costs can therefore reduce profitability precisely when refinancing needs rise.[49]

The macro tail risk is an upward shift in sovereign and credit curves, accompanied by market illiquidity. The IMF assessed monetary-policy miscalibration as a medium-likelihood, medium-high-impact risk over one to three years; its downside channel includes a sharp rise in the domestic sovereign yield curve, corporate-bond dislocation, mutual-fund redemptions and second-round asset liquidation.[33] The same IMF risk matrix assessed deepening geoeconomic fragmentation or intensification of regional conflict as high likelihood, while RBI survey respondents in May 2025 identified geopolitical conflict, capital outflows, trade disruption and higher US Treasury yields as relevant risks to Indian financial stability.[33][7]

A listed NCD does not solve this exit risk. India’s corporate-bond market is less liquid than developed-market corporate-bond segments, and the IMF cautions that infrequent corporate-bond trading and stale prices limit the reliability of liquidity haircuts; in a complete market freeze, even funds with securities may struggle to meet redemptions.[33] Fixed-rate NCD prices move inversely to prevailing interest rates, so a holder selling before maturity may realise a loss even if the issuer continues to service the debt.[60]

The system-wide picture is more resilient than the stressed tail. The IMF reported aggregate NBFC gross NPAs declining from 6.8% in FY2019/20 to 4.0% in FY2023/24, net NPAs declining from 3.4% to 1.1%, capital adequacy rising from 23.7% to 26.6%, and return on assets rising from 1.3% to 3.3%.[33] It nevertheless identified a weak tail of a few nonsystemic NBFCs and urban cooperative banks with below-minimum or negative capital even under baseline conditions.[33] **The committee implication is that sector resilience lowers the probability of system-wide distress but does not reduce issuer-specific refinancing, governance, collection or collateral risk to an acceptable proxy for sovereign risk.**

For investment-committee reporting, payment/default risk, recovery risk after default and the risk of loss on a pre-maturity sale should be assessed separately. A high stated yield compensates for some combination of credit, liquidity, duration and optionality risk; it is not evidence that those risks are acceptable.

RBI’s one-year stress test for 158 NBFCs projected the aggregate GNPA ratio rising from 2.9% in March 2025 to 3.3% in March 2026 under the baseline, while aggregate CRAR fell from 23.4% to 21.4%.[61] RBI’s medium- and severe-risk scenarios reduce NBFC CRAR by an additional 80 basis points and 100 basis points, respectively, relative to baseline.[61] A severe deterioration in collections therefore raises impairment charges, reduces earnings and capital, and can impair the recoverable value and cash-generation capacity of receivables pledged to NCD holders.[61]

At September 2025, borrowings represented 68.7% of funding for the combined NBFC Upper and Middle Layer group, compared with 67.7% in September 2024.[34] Secured funding represented 61.4% of Upper Layer sources of funds and 30.8% of Middle Layer sources, while unsecured funding represented 8.9% and 37.1%, respectively.[34] A funding-market closure can therefore prevent a debt-funded NBFC from replacing maturing NCDs or bank facilities even before its loan book deteriorates, forcing more expensive borrowing, asset sales or contraction of lending and weakening debt-service capacity.[34]

## Regulatory landscape and policy implications

Public NCD regulation determines the disclosure, listing, trustee and payment architecture available to investors. It does not convert an issuer’s repayment obligation into a government, SEBI or RBI guarantee. For the NCD IPO assessment, the operative distinction is between a publicly accessible offer made through a prospectus and a select-group private placement under Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013.[16][66]

### Issue and investor rules

SEBI’s Issue and Listing of Non-Convertible Securities Regulations, 2021, last amended on 28 October 2025, governed public issues and listing of fixed-maturity debt securities, including debentures. A debt security is a fixed-maturity non-convertible instrument that creates or acknowledges indebtedness; a public issue uses a prospectus, shelf prospectus or tranche prospectus. In contrast, a private placement is an offer to a select group under Section 42 rather than a public offer, and a draft public-issue prospectus or shelf prospectus is filed with SEBI and stock exchanges.[67][16]

The legal distinction has practical consequences. A Section 42 private placement cannot be advertised or distributed to the public, is capped at 200 offerees in a financial year for each security type, excluding QIBs and eligible employee offers, and requires private-placement process controls such as designated offerees, separate application-money accounts and return-of-allotment filings. A publicly marketed retail NCD subscription offer is therefore not a private placement merely because a distributor calls it a “bond issue” or uses a private-placement label.[66][68]

Public issues were required to remain open for at least two and no more than ten working days. A revision to the price band or yield required at least one additional working day, within the ten-working-day cap. From 1 November 2025, public debt issues were required to list within three working days after closure, replacing the previous six-working-day timetable; this reduced settlement and blocked-fund duration but did not reduce credit risk.[18][69]

The public offer document carried material due-diligence inputs. It had to disclose provisions against fictitious applications, any reservation, the board’s declaration that underwriters had sufficient resources, utilisation of the issuer’s and group companies’ prior issues over the preceding three years, promoter or director benefit or interest in the issue objects, and specified material contracts. Abridged-prospectus disclosures had to be accurate and quantitatively support qualitative statements; copies and QR-code links to the prospectus were required on issuer, merchant banker and registrar channels.[45][46]

For NBFC, HFC and public financial institution issuers, the disclosure burden is more credit-specific because issue proceeds can finance lending books. Where a public issue’s stated object includes lending to a group company, the issuer must disclose the borrower, rupee exposure and exposure as a percentage of AUM. NBFC/HFC offer documents also require three years of lending-from-debt-proceeds information, lending policy, classification by relationship, type, LTV, sector, ticket size, geography and maturity, top-20 borrower concentration, overdue and non-performing loans; portfolio sectoral distribution, gross and net NPAs, provisions and secured-versus-unsecured borrowings must also be disclosed.[45][46]

**The committee should treat these documents as the starting evidence for credit work, not as a substitute for it.** The disclosures permit analysis of deployment, related-party exposure, borrower concentration, asset quality and funding structure; they do not ensure that reported security cover or future cash generation will be sufficient for interest and principal.[45][9]

Listed-NBFC public NCDs had a redemption-protection structure different from the historical Debenture Redemption Reserve model. KLM Axiva Finvest and Indel Money each stated in their 2025 prospectuses that, as listed NBFCs making public debenture issues, they were not required to create a DRR. Both stated, however, that they must deposit or invest by 30 April each year at least 15% of debentures maturing by the following 31 March, in permitted bank deposits or unencumbered government, trust-eligible or notified-company securities; the amount is restricted to repayment of maturing debentures.[9][49]

MCA’s DPT-3 framework excludes listed unsecured NCDs issued in accordance with SEBI regulations and debentures secured by a first or pari-passu first charge over qualifying tangible assets from the definition of deposits. Its 35%-of-net-worth maximum-deposit calculation applies to deposits, not automatically to compliant listed NCD borrowings.[118]

This 15% investment or deposit requirement is a liquidity buffer for the next year’s maturities, not full prefunding of all debt obligations. Sammaan Capital’s February 2025 tranche prospectus made the constraint explicit: it stated that the company could be unable to make the prescribed investment or deposit if it did not generate adequate profits.[60]

Debenture trustees provide a second protection layer. For unsecured debt securities, the issuer must submit a trustee due-diligence certificate when filing the draft offer document and again at the listing-application stage. Trustees can inspect issuer books, records and charged assets, while issuers report payment defaults, security-cover certificates, pledged-security values and debt-service reserve-account or other-security values. A Recovery Expense Fund can finance legal enforcement after default, but it funds enforcement costs rather than payment of coupon or principal.[70][9][71]

SEBI’s October 2025 amendments also increased the importance of reading the final trust deed. Regulation 18 was amended to standardise the trust-deed framework while permitting deviations where a summary and rationale are disclosed in the relevant General Information Document, Key Information Document or shelf prospectus and accepted by the trustee. The former two-part structure separating standard and issue-specific information was removed.[72][73][71]

Listing affects the mechanics of realised cash flows. Coupon payments falling on a Sunday or holiday move to the next working day while subsequent payment dates remain unchanged; maturity redemption falling on a Sunday or holiday is paid on the preceding working day. When a leap year occurs during the tenor, interest uses an Actual/Actual convention with 366 days for the full year. These conventions should be reflected in the cash-flow schedule used in the tax-adjusted-yield analysis.[45]

SEBI regulates CRAs through registration, general obligations, inspections, investigations and conduct rules. Its CRA framework covers rating scales, rating process, monitoring and review, rating press releases, withdrawals, provisional ratings and information sharing with debenture trustees. SEBI does not make the credit assessment itself; India’s rating model is issuer-paid.[74][58]

Accordingly, a CRISIL or ICRA rating is a regulated analytical input, not a SEBI endorsement or repayment assurance. The appropriate committee test is the issue-level rating, its date, instrument seniority, security and ongoing surveillance, alongside the issuer’s own financial profile and covenants.[58][74]

The public-issue framework thus makes the prospectus, trustee documents, listing disclosures and post-listing payment information available for review; it does not eliminate default, security-realisation or refinancing risk.

For a domestic Indian public NCD, the applicable primary transaction record is the Indian offer document; a US SEC filing is ordinarily not applicable merely because an Indian issuer offers NCDs in India. Public NCDs are issued in dematerialised form and listed on a recognised stock exchange, but listing does not assure secondary-market liquidity or payment.

Listed-debt issuers are subject to periodic financial-results disclosure and event-based disclosure of material information, including payment default or delay and rating actions. Investors should monitor exchange announcements and rating surveillance through maturity, rather than relying only on the offer-stage prospectus.

NCD coupon interest is taxable as Income from Other Sources at the resident investor’s applicable marginal income-tax rate, plus applicable surcharge and health-and-education cess; senior-citizen status alone does not create a separate concessional NCD-interest rate. Tax deducted at source is a credit against the final tax liability rather than a cap on it, so the after-tax yield analysis must specify tax regime, marginal rate, cash-flow timing and whether coupon is assumed to be taxed annually.

For FY 2025–26, interest on an NCD is taxable to an individual as Income from Other Sources at the investor’s applicable slab rate, rather than at a preferential rate merely because it is NCD interest.[75][12]

From 1 April 2023, tax is deducted at source at 10% on interest payable on a company-issued security other than a Central or State Government security; this withholding is distinct from, and creditable against, the investor’s final marginal-rate liability.[12][76]

A sale of an NCD to a third party before maturity is a transfer that can give rise to capital gains.[77]

For listed NCDs, gains on sale within 12 months are taxable at the investor’s slab rate, while gains on sale after 12 months are long-term capital gains taxable at 12.5% without indexation.[75][78]

For unlisted bonds or debentures transferred, redeemed or maturing on or after 23 July 2024, section 50AA treats the gain as arising from a short-term capital asset.[79]

In Khushaal C. Thackersey, the Mumbai ITAT held that redemption of conventional NCDs did not give rise to capital gains and that the redemption premium was interest taxable under Income from Other Sources.[77]

SEBI’s Integrated Filing framework applied to the specified governance and financial periodic filings of listed entities from the quarter ended 31 December 2024 onward; the Governance filing was due within 30 days after quarter-end, while the Financial filing was due within 45 days after each of the first three quarter-ends and within 60 days after the fourth quarter and financial year.[80]

The Integrated Filing (Financial) included quarterly disclosure of outstanding default on loans or debt securities under LODR Regulation 30 and section V-B of the LODR Master Circular.[80]

SEBI’s October 2025 amendment to LODR Regulation 56(1) required a listed entity to forward specified information to its debenture trustee within 24 hours of the event or receipt of information, including default status, payment confirmations and asset-security information.[73]

Debenture trustees must assess issuer creditworthiness before accepting an issue, verify that asset security is real and properly registered, and check covenant compliance.[81]

A resident individual aged 60 years or more but under 80 years during the previous year is a senior citizen, and a resident individual aged 80 years or more is a super senior citizen.[82]

Under the old regime, a resident senior citizen aged 60 to under 80 has nil tax up to ₹3,00,000, 5% on ₹3,00,001–₹5,00,000, 20% on ₹5,00,001–₹10,00,000 and 30% above ₹10,00,000; a resident individual aged 80 or more has nil tax up to ₹5,00,000, 20% on ₹5,00,001–₹10,00,000 and 30% above ₹10,00,000.[83]

Section 115BAC is the default regime for an individual, while eligible non-business taxpayers may choose the old regime each year in the income-tax return by the specified filing deadline.[82]

For FY 2025–26, section 193 applied a 10% TDS rate to interest on securities, including debentures, paid to a resident with a valid PAN when annual interest exceeded ₹10,000; the rate was 20% where PAN was absent or invalid.[84][85]

Section 193 withholding occurs when interest is credited or paid, whichever is earlier, and the recipient claims the TDS credit in the income-tax return through Form 26AS; an excess deduction may be refunded through that return.[84][86]

For transfers on or after 23 July 2024, gains on unlisted debentures or unlisted bonds, including on redemption or maturity, are deemed short-term capital gains.[87]

For listed bonds and debentures held for more than 12 months, the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 treatment applicable at the cutoff taxed gains at 12.5% without indexation.[88]

The alternate personal tax regime was the default FY 2025–26 regime unless an individual elected otherwise; a taxpayer without business income could choose it for each tax year.[89]

Under the old regime, the basic exemption limit was ₹3 lakh for a resident individual aged 60 to under 80 and ₹5 lakh for one aged 80 or more, whereas the new-regime basic exemption limit was ₹4 lakh without an age distinction.[90]

A resident individual aged 60 or older could submit Form 15H for nil TDS only if all eligibility conditions were met; Form 15H changes withholding, not the final taxability of NCD interest.[91]

Section 80TTB allowed a resident senior citizen a deduction of qualifying deposit interest up to ₹50,000 from deposits with specified banks, co-operative banks or a Post Office; corporate NCDs are not among those qualifying deposits.[92]

Section 80TTA concerns savings-account interest for eligible non-senior individuals and Hindu undivided families, not corporate NCD interest; resident senior citizens are excluded from that deduction.[119]

A listed-debt issuer’s security-cover certificate is prepared under Regulation 54 of the SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015.[93]

SEBI’s Master Circular for Credit Rating Agencies is addressed to registered credit rating agencies, registered debenture trustees, issuers that have listed or propose to list non-convertible securities, recognised stock exchanges and SEBI-registered depositories.[74]

### Policy and supervisory implications

RBI supervises NBFCs and other financial intermediaries, while SEBI regulates capital-market issuers, intermediaries and investors. For an NBFC issuer, SEBI governs the public-debt offer and disclosure route; RBI prudential regulation governs capital, liquidity, asset quality, concentration, conduct and funding conditions. The two systems are complementary rather than interchangeable.[33]

RBI’s November 2025 NBFC Directions define “public funds” to include debentures, commercial paper, bank finance and inter-corporate deposits.[120]

RBI’s scale-based regime divides NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper and Top Layers according to systemic importance, with more stringent requirements and supervisory intensity at higher layers. The regulatory layer and functional classification matter: an NBFC-HFC, microfinance institution, infrastructure finance company or gold-loan lender may face additional rules beyond its scale-based layer.[33][94]

Under RBI’s scale-based framework, non-deposit-taking NBFCs with assets below ₹1,000 crore generally fall in the Base Layer; all deposit-taking NBFCs, non-deposit-taking NBFCs with assets of ₹1,000 crore or more, HFCs and infrastructure-finance NBFCs fall in the Middle Layer. The Top Layer is intended to remain empty unless RBI judges an Upper Layer entity to pose substantially increased systemic risk.[121]

RBI requires group-level asset consolidation for scale-based classification: where NBFCs under common promoters have combined assets of at least ₹1,000 crore, the relevant group entities become Middle Layer entities, with annual statutory-auditor certification of group asset size.[120]

The Reserve Bank issued the 2025 NBFC Prudential Norms on Capital Adequacy Directions on 28 November 2025, effective immediately. For NBFC-Middle Layer and above, the Directions set a minimum Tier 1 capital ratio of 10% of aggregate risk-weighted assets and a minimum CRAR of 15%; NBFC-Upper Layer also requires CET1 capital of at least 9%. A gold-jewellery NBFC—defined as one with gold-jewellery loans of at least 50% of financial assets—must maintain Tier 1 capital of 12% and CRAR of 15%.[95]

Under RBI’s asset-classification glide path, the Base Layer threshold for recognising a non-performing asset moved from more than 150 days overdue by March 2024 to more than 120 days by March 2025 and more than 90 days by March 2026. The minimum Net Owned Fund for specified NBFC-ICC, NBFC-MFI and NBFC-Factor entities is scheduled to rise to ₹10 crore by 31 March 2027.[121]

The regulatory leverage ratio is not the debt-to-equity ratio used in this report’s issuer comparison. RBI defines NBFC leverage as total outside liabilities divided by “owned fund,” a prudential capital measure including paid-up equity, qualifying free reserves and share premium but deducting accumulated losses, intangibles and deferred revenue expenditure. The Base Layer ceiling is seven times owned fund. Debt-to-equity instead compares the analyst-defined debt numerator with accounting equity; the two ratios should not be substituted for each other.[95]

NBFC capital and liquidity regulation moderates but does not remove funding risk. RBI’s June 2025 Financial Stability Report found that bank funding reliance had declined over the preceding year as higher risk weights on bank lending to NBFCs took effect, and that NBFC-Upper Layer relied more on bank borrowings and public deposits than NBFC-Middle Layer. In RBI’s baseline credit-risk stress test, aggregate CRAR for the 158 Upper- and Middle-Layer NBFC sample was projected to decline from 23.4% in March 2025 to 21.4% in March 2026; ten Middle-Layer NBFCs, representing 2.1% of sample advances, could breach the 15% minimum.[7]

The severe scenario was less benign: RBI projected that 15 Middle-Layer NBFCs representing 3.7% of Upper- and Middle-Layer advances could fail to meet the 15% CRAR floor, with incremental capital erosion of 100 basis points relative to baseline. Its liquidity test of 244 Upper- and Middle-Layer NBFCs, representing ₹36.01 lakh crore of assets, projected one institution with a negative cumulative liquidity mismatch exceeding 20% in baseline, two in medium stress and three in high stress over the next year.[7]

These are sector stress tests, not forecasts for a named NCD issuer. They nevertheless support a direct investment implication: NCD underwriting must test issuer-specific capital headroom, maturity ladder, liquidity mismatches, asset-quality sensitivity, secured funding dependence and refinancing capacity rather than treating a regulatory minimum as an investable target.[7]

Gold-loan NBFCs face a particularly relevant funding-channel constraint. RBI’s 1 April 2025 bank-finance circular, as described by Muthoot Fincorp, caps a bank’s total exposure to a single NBFC predominantly engaged in gold-jewellery lending at 7.5% of the bank’s capital funds and requires internal aggregate sub-limits for such NBFCs. The issuer stated that the restriction could constrain bank borrowing and raise funding cost.[63]

For the December 2025 roster, issuer-specific prudential attribution must remain exact. The supplied record does not establish that Adani Enterprises Limited’s Tranche III Issue was issued by an NBFC, HFC or public financial institution. The conditional SEBI disclosure rule for group-company lending by an NBFC/HFC/PFI therefore cannot be used to attribute NBFC capital, leverage, ALM or lending rules to Adani Enterprises.[45]

RBI rules permit an NBFC to issue debentures only for deployment on its own balance sheet, not to fund a parent, associate or group entity; an NBFC also may not lend against the security of its own publicly or privately placed debentures.[122]

By contrast, issuer disclosures can reveal concrete regulatory risk. KLM Axiva disclosed a ₹10 lakh RBI penalty remitted on 11 April 2025 for non-compliance with dividend-declaration provisions and an ongoing compliance review following an October 2023 show-cause notice alleging inaccurate reporting of Net Owned Funds, insufficient Tier 1 capital, incorrect debt and NPA reporting, and related-entity non-disclosure. Kosamattam disclosed a ₹13.38 lakh RBI penalty imposed in October 2023 and paid in November 2023, while stating that RBI closed all inspection-report paragraphs on 6 November 2025.[9][62]

The policy implication is asymmetric: closure of historical observations reduces a named compliance overhang, while an open supervisory review, weak control environment or funding constraint can affect growth, profitability, capital adequacy and refinancing even where the NCD remains rated investment grade.[9][62][63]

For an NBFC with gold-jewellery loans of at least 50% of financial assets, RBI’s bank-finance rule capped a bank’s single-NBFC exposure at 7.5% of the bank’s capital funds and required banks to set an internal aggregate sub-limit for such exposures.[49][41]

The Indian framework as at 8 December 2025 provided meaningful process protections: public prospectuses, detailed NBFC/HFC loan-book disclosures, accelerated T+3 listing, trustee due diligence, security-cover monitoring, payment reporting and Recovery Expense Fund support for enforcement. It also imposed prudential capital and liquidity disciplines on covered NBFCs, with scale-based escalation and new 2025 capital-adequacy Directions.[45][69][70][9][95]

**The combined result is disclosure- and monitoring-based investor protection, not repayment protection.** A listed public NCD can have a trustee, security cover, a 15% maturity-year investment requirement and an investment-grade rating, yet still depend on issuer liquidity, asset performance, refinancing access and recoverable collateral. Regulatory compliance therefore supports eligibility and monitoring; the investment decision remains issue-specific credit underwriting.[9][49][7]

1. Verify that each candidate is a public issue open for subscription, not a Section 42 placement or an announced/closed offer.
2. Review the final prospectus, trust deed, trustee certificate, security cover and disclosed deviations before relying on “secured” status.
3. Apply RBI capital, liquidity and leverage rules only after confirming the issuer’s actual regulated entity type and NBFC layer.
4. Treat ratings and regulatory compliance as inputs to, not substitutes for, issuer credit and refinancing analysis.

## Forward curve and leading indicators

### As-of-date rates and curve

The valuation reference for a fixed-rate NCD is the rupee spot curve at the security’s residual maturity, plus compensation for credit and liquidity risk. A forward curve instead represents rates implied for future periods by derivative or bond-market prices; it is not interchangeable with a forecast of RBI policy or with an issuer’s NCD coupon.

The closest dated sovereign reference available before 8 December was the October 2025 FBIL monthly traded-rate series. Three-, six- and 12-month Treasury-bill yields were 5.44%, 5.54% and 5.54%, respectively; five-, 10- and 15-year average G-Sec YTMs were 6.22%, 6.46% and 6.83%. FBIL selected the nearest ISIN by residual maturity for the G-Sec averages. Treasury-bill YTMs are primary-market cut-offs, rather than secondary-market prices.[96][97]

FBIL’s November 2025 average G-Sec yields to maturity were 6.22% at five years, 6.48% at 10 years, 6.89% at 15 years, 7.34% at 40 years and 7.38% at 50 years. These nearest-residual-maturity security yields differ from the November par yields used to construct spread curves.[123][101]

In November 2025, traded OIS rates averaged 5.43%–5.46% across six-month to one-year tenors. November modified MIFOR was 6.0855% at one month, 6.4349% at six months and 6.7864% at 12 months.[123]

The October spot curve was upward sloping from 5.44% at the three-month bill to 6.83% at the 15-year G-Sec. Accordingly, a five- or ten-year NCD cannot be assessed against a short Treasury bill: its stated yield should first be decomposed into the matched-maturity sovereign benchmark and then the issuer’s credit and liquidity spread. FIMMDA’s rated-bond methodology follows this principle by adding a credit spread to the base yield at residual, rather than original, maturity.[96][56]

RBI cut the policy repo rate by 50 basis points to 5.50% on 6 June 2025 and retained a neutral stance on 1 October, with the standing deposit facility at 5.25% and the MSF and Bank Rate at 5.75%. MUFG reported on 5 December that the RBI had then reduced repo by a further 25 basis points to 5.25%, retained neutrality, and announced approximately ₹1.4 trillion of liquidity operations: ₹1 trillion of G-Sec purchases and a three-year USD5 billion FX buy/sell swap.[98][96][99]

The rate-derivatives market provided directional information during July–September 2025. The one-month MIBOR-OIS rate was around 5.32% in July following the June cut. The two-year/one-year OIS spread moved from approximately negative 5.8 basis points at the start of the quarter to near zero at quarter-end, while the five-year/one-year spread widened as longer rates edged higher. Government yields rose relative to sticky OIS rates in August under long-end supply pressure and then moderated in September as global rates eased.[100]

**The rate evidence indicates limited scope for further near-term easing by late September and greater exposure to long-end term-premium repricing in long-dated fixed-rate NCDs.** A shorter NCD reduces mark-to-market sensitivity but forces earlier reinvestment; a longer NCD locks contractual cash flows but has greater price sensitivity when sovereign yields or credit spreads rise.[100][33]

Judgment — INR OIS, FRAs and government-security forward contracts are the appropriate tools for extracting market-implied rate paths when live quotes are available. RBI permits forward contracts in G-Secs alongside swaps, options, futures, swaptions and FRAs, while FIMMDA uses OIS quotes to convert MIBOR-linked bonds to notional fixed coupons.[7][56]

Curve observations used for valuation should retain the observation date and time, quoted tenor, rate convention, underlying instrument and source field, so that a government-bond yield, an OIS fixed rate and a bootstrapped forward rate are not conflated. The NCD comparison should use weighted-average life or duration, coupon and payment frequency as well as final tenor, because an amortising or callable series does not have the same interest-rate exposure as a bullet bond with the same legal maturity.

### Leading credit and liquidity indicators

The Indian credit-curve reference was FIMMDA’s daily yield and spread matrix. It segments issuers into PSU/FI/banks, NBFCs and corporates; covers AAA to BBB- ratings and 0.5- to 15-year tenors; and calculates each daily spread by deducting the corresponding FBIL G-Sec par yield from its daily yield matrix. It is a spot valuation framework, not a prediction of future credit spreads.[101]

FIMMDA gives priority to qualifying trades, then uses fortnightly submitter polls, market-yield adjustments and interpolation. For credits below AA-, it determines spreads from traded levels in the preceding three months and fixes them for three months. This means a matched comparison must use issuer sector, the relevant instrument rating and residual maturity; lower-rated matrix points will adjust less quickly than actively traded AAA or AA paper.[101][56]

SEBI requires credit-rating agencies to publish standardised probability-of-default benchmarks and weighted-average one-, two- and three-year cumulative default rates for long-run and recent rating cohorts.[124]

For public NCD relative value, the applicable calculation is issue yield less the FBIL G-Sec par yield at the same residual tenor, compared with the corresponding FIMMDA sector-and-rating spread. FIMMDA also explicitly incorporates illiquidity adjustments for 15-year NBFC and corporate curves: 25 basis points for AAA, 30 for AA+, 35 for AA and 40 for AA-.[101][56]

The 2025 funding signals were mixed. RBI reported that financial conditions tightened in January–March as money-market and corporate-bond spreads widened, but eased from April after rate cuts and liquidity measures. The RBI had injected around ₹9.5 lakh crore of durable liquidity since January 2025, and a staggered 100-basis-point CRR reduction was expected to release a further ₹2.5 lakh crore from September through December. CD–OIS spreads and NBFC commercial-paper/matched-T-bill spreads narrowed as system liquidity moved into surplus.[7][102]

In November 2025, RBI’s 21 November seven-day variable-rate repo accepted ₹16,363 crore against ₹50,000 crore notified at 5.51%, while its 14 November three-day reverse repo accepted ₹57,380 crore against ₹1,00,000 crore notified at 5.48%.[123][125]

As of 28 November 2025, scheduled commercial-bank deposits were ₹242.60 lakh crore, non-food credit was ₹194.48 lakh crore and the credit-deposit ratio was 80.49%; M3 growth was 6.80% and the free-fund ratio was 98.15%.[126]

Corporate-bond spreads did not move uniformly with short-term funding indicators. RBI reported that median spreads across rating categories rose by 20–30 basis points between October 2024 and March 2025 despite softer yields, citing tight liquidity, trade uncertainty and weaker growth prospects. This means lower benchmark yields can coexist with wider NCD risk premia.[7][103]

NBFC refinancing remains a central leading indicator because non-HFC NBFCs had gross payables of ₹21.15 lakh crore at March 2025, against gross receivables of ₹2.26 lakh crore. More than 70% of their funds came through long-term loans and long-term debt, although short-term loans and CP increased in the second half of FY2024-25; for the common upper-layer NBFC set, borrowing growth continued to outpace credit growth through September 2025.[7][34]

The relevant issuer dashboard therefore combines funding spreads, bank-line availability, covenant headroom, collections, asset quality, profitability and maturity mismatches. The IMF cautioned that NBFCs may assume inflows from committed credit lines that become unreliable in distress; Prachay Capital disclosed that a significant part of its NCD, inter-corporate-deposit and promoter-related liabilities is fixed-rate, bank borrowing is floating against repo/MCLR/EBLR, and it does not hedge interest-rate exposure.[33][13]

CRISIL reported that Sammaan Capital had a positive cumulative asset-liability gap in every bucket at 30 June 2025, excluding pending loan commitments and derivative exposures. It reported approximately ₹9,193 crore of liquidity at 31 August 2025 against approximately ₹3,313 crore of debt maturing through 28 February 2026, or 2.8 times stated liquidity-to-near-term-debt coverage.[116]

Sammaan Capital reported a consolidated RBI-defined liquidity coverage ratio of 297% at 30 September 2025, against a 100% requirement. Its assets and liabilities were ₹16,650 crore and ₹3,392 crore within six months, ₹21,209 crore and ₹7,234 crore within one year, and ₹38,133 crore and ₹35,239 crore within three years, respectively.[117]

Historical stress demonstrates why both spread and liquidity monitoring matter. During the March–April 2020 market freeze, corporate yields rose sharply across AAA and AA maturities before RBI rate cuts, TLTROs and NBFC liquidity support compressed yields. From 26 March 2020 to 7 June 2021, three-year AA and BBB-minus spreads narrowed by 274 and 194 basis points, respectively.[32]

**The practical conclusion is that a quoted NCD spread is compensation for default, liquidity, uncertainty and market-risk premia, not a standalone estimate of default probability.** The NIBM credit-spread study defines the corporate spread as the corporate-minus-same-maturity-government yield and explains that it also contains liquidity, uncertainty, tax and regulatory components.[104]

For NBFCs, the issuer dashboard should separately track Stage 3 or gross and net NPA ratios, early-bucket delinquencies, collection efficiency, restructured assets, write-offs, provision coverage and collateral or vintage performance. Deterioration in early delinquencies or collections can precede a rise in reported NPAs; profitability and capital review should also test net interest margin or yield on assets, credit costs, return on assets, return on equity, tangible net worth, regulatory capital and dividend retention.

Refinancing review should quantify the next 12–24 months’ maturity ladder, commercial-paper and other short-term-debt dependence, secured versus unsecured funding, undrawn bank facilities, lender concentration and asset-liability mismatches. For non-financial issuers, the corresponding leading indicators are operating cash flow, EBITDA-to-interest, net debt-to-EBITDA, working-capital days, order-book and customer concentration, capex commitments, and commodity and foreign-exchange exposure.

Secondary-market yields, bid-ask spreads and trading volumes, subscription quality, rating outlooks and actions, auditor qualifications, exchange disclosures, promoter-share pledges and peer stress are external warning indicators. A high debt-to-equity ratio is not by itself evidence of credit deterioration for a regulated lender; it must be assessed with asset quality, capital, liquidity and profitability.

RBI’s Financial System Stress Indicator includes the three-year AAA corporate-bond/G-Sec yield spread, the three-year BBB-minus-AAA yield differential, and the difference between three-year BBB yields and their maximum.[7]

RBI’s Financial System Stress Indicator also includes the three-month non-NBFC CP/T-Bill spread, realised volatility of the three-month CP rate, and the three-month NBFC CP/T-Bill spread.[7]

RBI’s government-debt-market measures include realised volatility of the 10-year G-Sec yield, the 10-year G-Sec/three-month T-Bill spread, and the increase in the 10-year yield from its two-year rolling minimum.[7]

RBI’s NBFC stability indicator comprises soundness, asset quality, profitability, liquidity and efficiency, measured through capital adequacy, net and gross NPAs, returns, margins, short-term liabilities, long-term assets and cost-to-income.[7]

The available curve points to a moderately upward-sloping October sovereign term structure, a short end anchored near the policy corridor, and a long end vulnerable to supply, inflation and term-premium repricing. The OIS curve had largely removed expectations of additional near-term easing by September, while the December repo cut and liquidity measures improved the near-term funding backdrop.[96][100][99]

For NCD selection, the decisive comparison is therefore the offered yield versus the matched residual-tenor G-Sec yield and matched FIMMDA sector/rating spread, not the coupon versus a savings rate or short bill. Short-term funding conditions improved, but corporate spreads had widened earlier despite lower sovereign yields and NBFC borrowing remained structurally dependent on external funding. The committee should prefer issue series whose incremental spread compensates for duration, liquidity and issuer-specific refinancing risk; it should not treat expected policy easing as protection against long-end or credit-spread losses.[101][7][102]

## Clear investment thesis

For a conservative retiree, the investment decision is not a search for the highest advertised coupon. It is a choice between reproducible, after-tax cash flows and the risk that the issuer cannot pay them when due.Judgment — the appropriate suitability distinction is risk tolerance and tax position, not age alone: senior-citizen status does not itself change the tax rate applicable to NCD interest.

### supporting evidence

KLM Axiva Finvest’s December 2025 public NCD assessment reported a maximum coupon of 11.00% and a BBB (Stable) rating. It described the NCDs as secured by company assets equivalent to the outstanding NCD amount, but also identified declining FY25 PAT, NBFC sensitivity to interest-rate movements and economic slowdowns, and credit risks in microfinance and MSME lending. The assessment characterised BBB as moderate safety rather than risk-free.[2]

The security package is a contractual recovery mitigant, not a substitute for repayment capacity. KLM’s asset backing must therefore be weighed against its profitability trend and exposure to borrower-default risk in economically sensitive lending segments. **This evidence supports avoidance by conservative fixed-income investors despite the stated 11.00% maximum coupon.**[2]

Sector conditions reinforce that distinction. The IMF’s February 2025 India Financial Sector Assessment Program observed that NBFCs using wholesale financing and market financing had increased the financial system’s diversity and interconnectedness. That structure can transmit funding or asset-quality stress rapidly through an NBFC’s refinancing channels.[33]

Fusion Finance illustrates the severity that microfinance stress can reach, although it is not used here as evidence that Fusion had an NCD IPO open on 8 December 2025. ICRA reported a FY2025 net loss of Rs. 1,225 crore and an H1 FY2026 loss of Rs. 114 crore; gross stage 3 assets had improved to 4.6% at 30 September 2025 from 7.9% at 31 March 2025 and a 12.9% peak at 31 December 2024, substantially aided by write-offs of Rs. 732 crore in H1 FY2026 and Rs. 1,333 crore in FY2025.[105] This demonstrates why a high coupon and an investment-grade rating label cannot be treated as complete downside protection in microfinance-linked credit.[105]

### Decision criterion and ranking

Judgment — the deciding criterion is risk-adjusted, tax-aware expected return: comparable post-tax yield must compensate for the incremental probability of impairment, refinancing stress, illiquidity and mark-to-market loss relative to safer alternatives of matched maturity. Coupon alone fails this test because it omits payment frequency, redemption schedule, tax treatment, reinvestment assumptions and credit-loss severity.

KLM Axiva ranks as **avoid for a conservative retiree**. The available suitability assessment limits it to moderate-to-high-risk investors seeking higher interest rates and advises conservative investors to avoid it. Its stated 11.00% maximum coupon does not outweigh the combined evidence of BBB (Stable) moderate safety, declining FY25 profitability and exposure to microfinance/MSME defaults, rate volatility and economic slowdown.[2]

No preferred-series ranking between generic and senior-citizen investors follows from the stated coupon alone.Judgment — a valid post-tax comparison requires each selected series’ coupon or yield, payment dates, redemption amount, purchase price, applicable marginal tax rate and tax regime; the same marginal rate produces the same interest-tax treatment regardless of investor age. Senior-citizen eligibility may affect other tax provisions, but it cannot be presumed to create an NCD-interest concession.

Ratings remain relevant but bounded inputs. CRISIL assigned Embassy Office Parks REIT’s proposed Rs. 1,400 crore NCDs a CRISIL AAA/Stable rating on 7 May 2025, describing AAA as its highest degree of safety and lowest credit risk; that letter does not establish that the proposed issue was accepting subscriptions on 8 December 2025.[106] Similarly, ICRA’s November 2025 Fusion report records rating deterioration from [ICRA]A+ (Stable) in January 2024 to [ICRA]A- (Negative) in February 2025, before an [ICRA]A- (Stable) outcome in November 2025.[105] Ratings are credit opinions, not buy, sell or hold recommendations.[105]

ICRA classifies non-convertible debentures as Simple on its instrument-complexity scale, but says that this concerns the ease of estimating associated returns rather than the risk of timely payment, which is indicated by the credit rating.[105]

A credit-rating FAQ states that ratings are not intended to measure liquidity risk, pre-payment risk, interest-rate risk, secondary-market loss risk or exchange-loss risk.[58]

### Conditions that change the verdict

The KLM avoidance decision would change only if the incremental after-tax spread over matched higher-quality alternatives rose sufficiently to compensate for its identified credit risks, or if issuer results showed sustained profitability and asset-quality improvement. A verified improvement in the instrument rating, stronger capital and liquidity metrics, or materially more protective issue-specific covenants could also change the risk-adjusted assessment.

For microfinance-oriented NBFCs, the leading credit triggers are collections, asset quality, capital, gearing and lender access. Fusion’s September 2025 current-bucket collections reached 98.5%, while its managed gearing fell to 3.1 times from 4.4 times at March 2025 and CRAR rose to 31.3% from 22.4%; however, Rs. 2,077 crore of borrowings remained subject to covenant breaches and repayable on demand.[105] ICRA identified managed gearing above 6 times, stretched liquidity, or sustained asset-quality deterioration and profitability pressure as rating-negative conditions.[105]

A rating downgrade indicates a higher risk of instrument default than previously predicted, and ratings may change when new information changes repayment expectations.[58]

ICRA states that its ratings are not recommendations to buy, sell or hold rated debt instruments and that surveillance can lead to a rating revision.[24]

Issue-level terms—not generic issuer descriptions—determine an investable NCD yield. The available KLM evidence provides the maximum stated coupon, rating characterisation, security description and suitability view, but not the series-by-series cash-flow inputs needed to calculate and rank generic-investor and senior-citizen post-tax yields.Judgment — no post-tax yield ranking should be approved until the relevant series’ payment and redemption terms and investor marginal-rate assumptions are fixed.

The evidence points in one direction for a conservative retiree: avoid KLM Axiva’s December 2025 NCD notwithstanding its 11.00% stated maximum coupon. Asset security offers a recovery mitigant, but the relevant assessment pairs it with BBB (Stable) moderate safety, declining FY25 PAT and material microfinance/MSME, interest-rate and slowdown risks.[2] NBFC interconnectedness and the Fusion stress case show why refinancing, collections and covenant compliance matter alongside a rating or coupon.[2][33][105]

1. **Avoid KLM Axiva for conservative fixed-income allocations.**
2. **Do not rank generic and senior-citizen post-tax returns without series cash flows and stated tax assumptions.**
3. **Reconsider only after verified improvements in credit, liquidity, leverage and matched post-tax spread.**

Muthoot Mercantile’s issue-level coupon, tenor, payment and redemption terms, instrument rating, and dated total-debt and total-equity figures remain open. EFSL’s conflicting published closing-date references require confirmation of the effective final subscription timetable. Comparable post-tax IRRs for monthly and cumulative series remain open until tax-recognition and reinvestment assumptions are specified.
