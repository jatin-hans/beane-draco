# Rubber Leaf’s Reported and Relocation-Adjusted Q1 Gross Margins

## Executive summary

Rubber Leaf’s core profitability weakened during the shift to an all-indirect sales mix, rather than the Q1 2024 margin pressure being explained solely by relocation costs. Reported gross margin fell from 24.9% in Q1 2023 to 1.2% in Q1 2024, a 23.7-percentage-point decline.

Removing the $190,703 factory-relocation loss included in Q1 2024 cost of sales raises the Q1 2024 margin to 7.7%, or 6.5 percentage points above the reported margin.

That adjustment still leaves margin about 17.3 percentage points below Q1 2023, consistent with Rubber Leaf’s statement that the loss of higher-margin direct-supply revenue depressed gross profitability.[1]

## Supply-model transition

### How did Rubber Leaf shift from direct to indirect supply

Judgment — Rubber Leaf moved from a mixed revenue base in Q1 2023 to an entirely indirect-supply revenue base in Q1 2024: direct-supply revenue fell from $897,432 to nil, while indirect-supply revenue increased from $1,448,109 to $2,954,607.[1] Rubber Leaf stated that the direct supply model had a higher gross profit margin and that lower direct-model sales were the main reason for the overall Q1 margin decline. This identifies the loss of direct sales as a continuing sales-mix pressure distinct from the factory-relocation loss recorded in cost of sales.[1]

Rubber Leaf managed and reviewed direct supply and indirect supply as two operating and reporting segments, and managed all long-term assets under the direct supply model. Indirect supply therefore existed before Q1 2024 rather than being introduced in that quarter.[2] The company’s direct supply model, Model A, placed Rubber Leaf Sealing Products (Zhejiang) Co., Ltd.[1][3] (RLSP), its operating subsidiary, in the role of a first-tier supplier.

Rubber Leaf described RLSP as its wholly owned Chinese subsidiary and stated that it had directly controlled RLSP since May 27, 2021.[14]

A first-tier supplier sells directly to an original equipment manufacturer (OEM), the vehicle maker. After passing an OEM’s on-site inspection, RLSP could be listed as a supplier, sign purchase or supply agreements directly with the OEM, procure raw materials, manufacture finished goods and deliver them to the OEM’s warehouse. The OEM paid RLSP directly after the goods passed inspection and control transferred. eGT was the company’s stated example of such a direct OEM customer.[4][1]

Rubber Leaf later identified Volkswagen among the OEMs served by its direct-supply model.[6][3][24][5]

The direct model therefore combined customer access, procurement, manufacturing and delivery within Rubber Leaf’s operating subsidiary. It also required RLSP to retain the supplier qualification needed to serve an OEM. Rubber Leaf described customized automotive rubber and plastic sealing strips as a business in which direct sales were the predominant approach because products had to be tailored to particular vehicle models. The company directly engaged with OEMs or their first-tier suppliers to obtain qualifications, define product specifications and models, negotiate prices and finalize orders.[5][6]

RLSP supplied synthetic rubber, rubber compound, automotive window seals, and other rubber and plastic automotive sealing products into OEM-related production chains in China.[14]

The indirect supply model, Model B, changed that commercial route. RLSP received purchase orders from Shanghai Xinsen Import & Export Co., Ltd. and Xinsen Sealing Products (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd., collectively described as the Xinsen Group. The company identified Xinsen Group as a related-party, certified second-tier supplier and production facilitator. A second-tier supplier supplies a first-tier supplier, which in turn supplies an OEM. In this arrangement, automakers issued consolidated orders for vehicle components to their first-tier suppliers, and the first-tier suppliers subcontracted the production of rubber and plastic seals to second-tier suppliers.[4][6]

The Xinsen Group thus inserted an intermediary between Rubber Leaf and the OEM supply chain. It coordinated orders to first-tier suppliers rather than manufacturing products itself. Under Model B, RLSP received purchase orders from related parties Shanghai Xinsen and Xinsen Sealing Products (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou Xinsen), collectively the Xinsen Group; RLSP procured rubber materials from its vendors and could outsource production to third-party manufacturers for either work-in-process (WIP) products or finished goods, based on management’s operational circumstances. WIP means partly completed goods that still require further processing before they can be sold as finished products.[4]

Rubber Leaf described two distinct processing paths within Model B:

- **Outsourced WIP production:** RLSP purchased raw materials and subcontracted the production of WIP to third-party manufacturers. Once the WIP reached RLSP’s warehouse, RLSP performed specified manual work, including welding and construction, then performed a rigorous quality inspection before completion. [4] [4]

- **Outsourced finished-product production:** RLSP purchased raw materials and subcontracted third-party manufacturers to produce finished products. RLSP monitored the production process and focused on final quality control. [4] [4]

The indirect model did not therefore make Rubber Leaf a pure distributor. Under one arrangement it retained manual finishing and final inspection; under the other it retained monitoring and quality-control responsibilities while external manufacturers completed the physical production. Finished products could be delivered from RLSP’s locations or directly from the third-party manufacturer to warehouses of first-tier suppliers designated by Shanghai Xinsen and Shanghai Huaxin; quality inspections were performed by inspectors appointed by the end customers, and the performance obligation was fulfilled upon delivery and successful quality inspection.[7][4]

The candidate-cited account describes Xinsen personnel as inspecting indirect-model goods delivered to warehouses of Xinsen’s upstream first-tier customers before revenue was recognized upon upstream acceptance.[25][3][24][5]

Revenue recognition also followed this indirect delivery and acceptance chain. For Model B, invoices were provided after quality and quantity confirmation and acceptance into Xinsen Group customers’ warehouses. Rubber Leaf stated that the invoice date marked the transfer of ownership and control from the company to Xinsen Group and indirectly to the upstream first-tier supplier, and that it recognized revenue when the Xinsen customer accepted delivery.[7][1]

The direct and indirect channels were not merely descriptions of customer types. Rubber Leaf identified them as operating and reporting segments based on sales channels for the three months ended March 31, 2024 and March 31, 2023. This means management formally reviewed the business through those two channels when allocating resources and assessing performance.[1]

The quarter-to-quarter change was substantial: direct-supply revenue declined from $897,432 in Q1 2023 to nil in Q1 2024, while indirect-supply revenue increased from $1,448,109 to $2,954,607. As of March 31, 2024, production had not resumed at the relocated factory because QS16949 recertification and subsequent direct-client on-site approval were required; Rubber Leaf stated that this reduced direct-model sales.[1]

Shanghai Xinsen accounted for 78% of Rubber Leaf’s revenue for the nine months ended September 30, 2023.[24]

Rubber Leaf’s shift was therefore observable in reported revenue, not only in descriptive business language. Q1 2023 retained a mixed model with a direct OEM component; Q1 2024 consisted entirely of indirect-channel sales. That change had two effects at once: it increased sales through Xinsen Group and removed the direct-supply contribution from the quarterly revenue base.[1]

The company characterized the direct channel as the higher-gross-margin model. In its Q1 2024 management discussion, Rubber Leaf stated that the decline in overall gross profit margin compared with Q1 2023 was mainly attributable to less revenue from the direct supply model, which carried a higher gross profit margin.[1]

This statement identifies sales mix as an ongoing profitability variable. A one-time cost, such as a relocation expense, affects one period’s cost of sales. A sales-mix change affects the underlying average profitability of the revenue base because the company replaces sales from a higher-margin channel with sales from another channel. Rubber Leaf’s own Q1 explanation places the loss of direct sales in that second category.[1]

The indirect model also involved related-party distributors. Rubber Leaf stated that it authorized Shanghai Xinsen and Hangzhou Xinsen as distributors “in order to stabilize customer relationships and maintain long-term orders.” In a related description, Rubber Leaf said it authorized Shanghai Xinsen to distribute products to Shanghai Hongyang Sealing Co., Ltd. and Wuhu Huichi Auto Parts Co., Ltd., both of which it identified as unrelated certified first-tier suppliers to auto manufacturers.[4]

The distributor arrangement had a stated cost. Before September 30, 2022, Xinsen Group charged a sales commission of 1% of total sales before value-added tax (VAT); following renegotiation, the rate became 0.25% effective October 1, 2022.[11] The company recorded that commission in selling expense, not cost of sales.[4][1]

That classification is important to the gross-margin analysis. Selling expenses sit below gross profit in the income statement. The reduction in Xinsen’s commission may have supported operating profit after gross profit, but it does not itself explain a movement in reported gross margin.[4][1]

The ownership context also changed before the periods compared. Effective October 1, 2022, Rubber Leaf’s CEO, President and Chairperson, Xingxiu Hua, reduced her ownership in Shanghai Xinsen from 90% to 15%. Because Shanghai Xinsen held 70% of Hangzhou Xinsen, her indirect ownership in Hangzhou Xinsen declined from 63% to 10.5%.[7][1]

The reduced ownership did not eliminate the related-party nature of the channel. Shanghai Xinsen remained identified as a related party and, during Q1 2024, represented all reported revenue. The company’s later annual disclosures also reported that Shanghai Xinsen accounted for approximately 100% of 2024 revenue, compared with 86% in 2023.[1][4]

Rubber Leaf’s customer concentration adds commercial risk to the strategic transition. A direct model concentrated exposure in a major OEM such as eGT, while the Q1 2024 indirect model concentrated reported revenue in Shanghai Xinsen. The route changed, but concentration did not disappear; it shifted from a direct OEM relationship toward a related-party intermediary.[4][1]

The company had disclosed earlier that it was significantly dependent on eGT for a substantial portion of direct-supply revenue. Rubber Leaf supplied eGT as a first-tier supplier with automotive rubber and plastic sealing strips, and said its financial performance partly depended on eGT’s operational stability and procurement policies.[12][4]

eGT’s operating disruption later added pressure to the direct channel. In June 2023, eGT temporarily suspended factory production. Rubber Leaf stated that the suspension directly and adversely affected direct-model order volume and revenue, contributed to lower sales and an operating loss, and that eGT resumed production in late October 2023.[6][5]

The direct-to-indirect shift should therefore not be described as a simple elective channel conversion. The Q1 2024 mix reflected increased indirect demand, but it also reflected the removal of direct output after the factory move and a direct-customer environment that had become more fragile during 2023.[1][6]

**The immediate Q1 change was a complete revenue-mix shift toward indirect supply, from 61.74% of sales in Q1 2023 to 100.00% in Q1 2024, while the company identified the displaced direct channel as higher margin.** The next subsection establishes how the factory relocation interrupted direct production and why that interruption must be separated from the recurring economics of the indirect model.[1]

Within Model B, Rubber Leaf stated that it could either purchase raw materials and subcontract manufacturing or procure products directly in the market, depending on the requirements of the order.[9]

Rubber Leaf identified the commission fee from indirect-supply-model sales as its main selling cost. Because the commission is recorded in selling expense, it affects operating profitability rather than reported gross margin.[11]

The shift toward indirect supply had already occurred on an annual basis before the Q1 2024 all-indirect revenue mix. For the year ended December 31, 2023, Rubber Leaf reported $8,709,595 of indirect-supply revenue and $1,280,555 of direct-supply revenue from total revenue of $9,990,150, compared with $5,388,728 of indirect-supply revenue and $5,259,447 of direct-supply revenue from total revenue of $10,648,175 in 2022.Derived — indirect supply represented 87.2% of 2023 total revenue and 50.6% of 2022 total revenue.[3]

After the Q1 2023–Q1 2024 comparison period, Rubber Leaf established wholly owned Hong Kong subsidiary Rubber Leaf Limited (RLHK) on September 22, 2025 and substantially transferred to it the principal orders, customer contracts and supply arrangements formerly associated with RLSP. Rubber Leaf stated that all of its business had previously been conducted through RLSP until RLSP was disposed of on November 20, 2025.[13]

Rubber Leaf’s later RLHK indirect-supply process placed procurement and finished-product manufacturing with third-party vendors after RLHK received customer orders, while RLHK monitored key manufacturing stages with particular emphasis on final quality control. Finished products were delivered either from company facilities or directly from third-party manufacturers to first-tier-supplier warehouses designated by Shanghai Xinsen and Shanghai Huaxin; end-customer-appointed inspectors performed quality inspections, and RLHK’s performance obligation was fulfilled upon delivery and successful inspection.[13]

Scope and measure — This section compares the three months ended March 31, 2023 and March 31, 2024 using Rubber Leaf’s Q1 2024 Form 10-Q, which presents both quarters in unaudited consolidated financial statements stated in U.S. dollars and prepared under U.S. generally accepted accounting principles (U.S. GAAP). Gross margin means gross profit divided by revenue: **(revenue − total cost of sales) ÷ revenue**.[8]

Under both Model A and Model B, Rubber Leaf stated that RLSP was primarily responsible for providing the goods, bore inventory risk before control transferred and during the post-transfer warranty period, set prices at its discretion, and settled all payments. The indirect model therefore changed the route to market and permitted outsourced manufacturing, but did not transfer these core commercial responsibilities from RLSP.[14]

Rubber Leaf stated that it authorized Shanghai Xinsen and Shanghai Huaxin, both related parties with certified first-tier supplier qualifications, as distributors to Shanghai Hongyang and Wuhu Huichi, unrelated certified first-tier suppliers to automobile manufacturers. The stated purpose was to access automobile manufacturers’ supply chains and maintain stable long-term orders.[12][7]

Rubber Leaf disclosed that RLSP’s indirect sales through Shanghai Xinsen to two certified first-tier suppliers were $8,593,998 in 2023 and $6,914,900 in 2024.[5] Rubber Leaf expected future sales to Shanghai Xinsen to remain unaffected by the ownership and management changes because RLSP had an established sales system with Shanghai Xinsen; it also stated that Shanghai Hongyang and Wuhu Huichi had used RLSP products consistently for many years.[5]

Rubber Leaf disclosed that, since July 2021, it had borne extra outsourcing-manufacturing costs averaging around 9% after changing its business model.[14]

Rubber Leaf stated that its subsidiary purchased approximately 70%, and potentially 90%, of raw materials from Yongliansen, in which Chief Executive Officer Xingxiu Hua held 30% of the outstanding equity, and described its strategy as significantly reliant on Yongliansen for supply needs.[5]

Shanghai Xinsen accounted for 100% of Q1 2024 revenue, and Rubber Leaf stated that loss of this distributor could materially adversely affect results of operations unless and until it replaced the customer.[10][3]

Rubber Leaf’s 2023 raw-material sourcing was highly concentrated: Shanghai Haozong, a related party of one director, supplied 95% of total raw-material purchases. Rubber Leaf stated that an increase in purchase costs from this major vendor could materially adversely affect results of operations unless it replaced the vendor.[3][14]

### What in Rubber Leaf’s direct-to-indirect supply shift and factory relocation

Rubber Leaf’s factory relocation was the operational event that linked the channel mix change to the Q1 2024 cost base. RLSP initiated relocation to a newly constructed facility in early August 2023 and obtained the property title certificate in December 2023. The facility became operational in January 2024.[1][4]

The new-factory project had a longer history. RLSP signed a construction engineering contract with Ningbo Rongsen on August 5, 2022 to build a factory and new production line with stated annual capacity of up to four million sets of automotive seals. The disclosed project budget was approximately $4,931,105, or RMB35 million. The project was completed on October 25, 2023.[4][6]

Rubber Leaf also described a projected capacity framework for the facility: 15 TPV production lines and 10 EPDM production lines, sufficient for the requirements of 3 million vehicles.[6] TPV is thermoplastic vulcanizate, and EPDM is ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber; both are materials used in automotive sealing products.[6]

The Q1 2024 Form 10-Q states that building completion did not restore direct production immediately.[1] As of March 31, 2024, production had not resumed at the new factory because the automotive manufacturing industry required QS16949 quality certification for production.

The relocation required recertification, which Rubber Leaf said typically took two to three months. Following certification, direct-supply clients had to conduct on-site evaluations before formal production could begin.[1]

Rubber Leaf explicitly connected this approval sequence to the Q1 sales mix. The company said that recertification and direct-client on-site evaluation resulted in decreased direct-supply sales in the three months ended March 31, 2024 compared with the same quarter in the prior year. It anticipated in that Q1 filing that RLSP would resume production in June 2024.[1]

The subsequent annual reporting shows that the recovery of direct supply was slower than that expectation. Rubber Leaf stated that direct-supply production had been suspended since July 2023 and had not resumed at the new factory as of December 31, 2024. Although it received a production license certificate from the government on June 6, 2024, it still expected an eGT inspection to require about two to three months, and it reported that no direct-supply orders resumed during 2024.[6]

This sequence matters because the direct-sales shortfall was not caused solely by an accounting charge. The factory move interrupted direct production, imposed recertification requirements and required direct-customer approval; the higher-margin direct channel was absent from Q1 2024 revenue.[1] These operational requirements are distinct from the separately reported relocation-loss line in cost of sales.[1]

Rubber Leaf reported a $190,703 factory-relocation loss in Q1 2024 cost of sales and no comparable Q1 2023 charge.[1] The Q1 2024 Form 10-Q directly reports the $190,703 factory-relocation loss for the three months ended March 31, 2024.[8] Rubber Leaf included the relocation loss in cost of sales and reported no comparable relocation-loss amount in Q1 2023; it linked the cost to its production suspension since July 2023.[15][1]

That $190,703 charge belongs to the gross-margin calculation because Rubber Leaf reported it within cost of sales. It is therefore appropriate for the later Relocation-adjusted Q1 2024 margin section to remove that charge from Q1 2024 cost of sales while holding revenue unchanged. The relevant adjustment is the quarter-specific $190,703 amount, not the full-year relocation figure.[1]

Full-year disclosures confirm that relocation remained a burden beyond Q1. Rubber Leaf reported a loss on factory relocation of $284,987 for the year ended December 31, 2023 and $359,015 for the year ended December 31, 2024. It stated that production was suspended in July 2023 and resumed operations in July 2024.[4][6]

The annual 2024 figure cannot replace the Q1 adjustment because it covers the full year, whereas the requested adjusted margin concerns only the three months ended March 31, 2024. The Q1 filing supplies the period-matched relocation loss of $190,703. The annual figure nevertheless shows that the Q1 charge was a substantial portion of the 2024 relocation burden: approximately 53.12% of the $359,015 full-year amount.[1][4]

Derived — $190,703 divided by $359,015 equals 53.12%. This calculation compares Rubber Leaf’s Q1 2024 relocation loss with its full-year 2024 relocation loss; it does not imply that the remainder was recognized evenly through the year.

The March 2024 construction-cost dispute is a distinct commitment, not part of the $190,703 Q1 cost-of-sales adjustment.[1] RLSP’s August 2022 construction contract had an agreed cost of $4,931,105.[1] An initial appraisal valued the project at $6,519,991, and a January 7, 2024 settlement agreement set the final settlement price at $7,171,990. A later evaluation put the project cost at $5,221,922, which Rubber Leaf said differed by 26.32% from the settlement-agreement amount.[1]

On March 1, 2024, RLSP filed a complaint challenging the new factory’s construction costs, and the court notified RLSP on March 5, 2024 that the contract-dispute case had been filed.[1] Concurrently, Zhejiang Fengrong Construction Co., Ltd., also known as Ningbo Rongsen, filed a complaint seeking full construction costs, overdue penalty, attorney fees and other costs totalling $7,163,361, or RMB50,844,103.89. The company stated in its Q1 filing that it could not predict the outcome of the legal proceeding at that time.[1][13]

The litigation involved large claimed or assessed project values, but the Q1 gross-margin adjustment uses the reported $190,703 relocation loss rather than the disputed construction project value. The project cost was recorded in plant and equipment, while the relocation loss was reported as a cost-of-sales line item in the quarterly statement.[1]

The plant-and-equipment accounting provides further context. After the project was completed on October 25, 2023 and local-government fire-protection acceptance was received, Rubber Leaf transferred $5,221,922 (RMB37,064,159) into plant and equipment, net, labelled Factory and Building, on December 25, 2023.[1]

RLSP and Ningbo Rongsen entered into the Construction Engineering Contract on August 5, 2022 at an agreed project cost of $4,931,105 (RMB35 million); the project was completed on October 25, 2023.[3] A December 30, 2024 Ningbo Intermediate People’s Court judgment set the final contract price at $6,764,506 (RMB49,378,191.44), and the $1,686,946 difference from the previously assessed $5,221,922 (RMB37,064,159) was transferred to plant and equipment.[6] The Ningbo Intermediate People’s Court also ordered RLSP to pay $6,956,830.46 (RMB49,378,191.44), late-payment interest at 1% per month from March 1, 2024 through full payment, and $4,226 (RMB30,000) in other expenses; RLSP decided to seek a retrial.[6]

The different U.S.-dollar translations do not alter the Q1 gross-margin adjustment. They describe a later construction dispute and asset accounting, while the quarter-specific income-statement charge relevant to gross margin is the reported $190,703 loss on factory relocation.[1][6]

The move also created capacity-utilisation issues. Rubber Leaf defined loss on idle capacity as costs arising when production slowdowns underutilize manufacturing facilities and equipment, including utilities, maintenance and salaries and benefits of indirect manufacturing personnel.[4]

For the year ended December 31, 2024, the company reported $365,310 of idle-capacity loss. It stated that, after RLSP resumed operations in July 2024 and initiated an OEM supply model, production capacity was not fully utilised.[4][5]

Idle-capacity loss is economically distinct from factory relocation loss. Relocation loss reflects the costs associated with moving and restarting operations. Idle-capacity loss reflects the cost of maintaining manufacturing infrastructure that is not producing at a normal level. Both can depress gross margin, but only the specific Q1 relocation-loss line is the adjustment requested in the following margin analysis.[1][4][5]

The cost-of-sales definition confirms why broad operational changes may affect gross margin. Rubber Leaf stated that cost of revenues comprises raw materials consumed, manufacturing costs, third-party logistics and distribution costs including packaging, freight, transportation, shipping and handling, and inventory adjustments for defective products and inventory counts. The annual report also included depreciation of manufacturing equipment in that definition.[1][6]

The direct-to-indirect transition could therefore affect gross margin through several recurring mechanisms: third-party manufacturing prices, raw-material procurement, logistics and distribution costs, manufacturing overhead, inventory adjustments and the utilisation of retained facilities. This is different from the one-time relocation-loss line, which is separately identified.[4][1]

Rubber Leaf’s sourcing arrangements underscore the related-party and outsourced nature of the indirect channel. The company said it purchased raw materials from Yongliansen Import and Export Trading Company and Shanghai Haozong Rubber & Plastic Technology Co., Ltd., and purchased equipment and rubber products under the indirect supply model from Shanghai Huaxin Economic and Trade Co., Ltd., with the stated aim of reducing purchase costs and enhancing purchasing power.[6]

The company’s annual reporting identified Shanghai Haozong as a major related-party supplier. Rubber Leaf reported that Shanghai Haozong represented 95% of total raw-material purchases in 2023 and 100% in 2024. A Rubber Leaf director, Jun Tong, held a 30% ownership interest in Shanghai Haozong.[7][4]

The reporting also contains a current-sourcing narrative that refers to Yongliansen as the main raw-material supplier. Rubber Leaf stated that its operating subsidiary mainly purchased raw materials from Yongliansen to reduce purchase costs and enhance purchasing power, while separately reporting that Shanghai Haozong accounted for 100% and 95% of total purchases for the years ended December 31, 2024 and 2023, respectively.[4]

The annual purchase table gives the period-specific amounts behind the 2024 sourcing disclosure. Rubber Leaf reported nil raw-material purchases from Yongliansen in 2024, compared with $438,230 in 2023. It reported purchases of rubber products from Shanghai Haozong of $6,913,738 in 2024 and $8,552,684 in 2023.[4]

The difference between the annual purchase table and the current-sourcing narrative is one of timing and stated scope. The annual figures describe purchases for completed 2023 and 2024 financial years. The Yongliansen narrative describes the sourcing position at the time of a later disclosure. They should not be combined into a single Q1 2024 supplier-cost conclusion.[4]

Related-party concentration also extended beyond material purchases. The auditor identified the evaluation of related-party identification and transactions as a critical audit matter because management disclosed that the chief executive officer had or had had controlling ownership or significant influence over certain main vendors and one of the major customers, and because certain directors overlapped among the companies. The company had related-party transactions including purchases of raw materials, finished goods, equipment and machinery, sales contracts and financing agreements.[4]

This context does not determine gross margin by itself. It does, however, show that the channel shift was not simply a move from internal production to unrelated outside counterparties. Q1 2024 revenue depended entirely on the Xinsen intermediary, while major sourcing relationships also involved related parties.[1][7][4]

Rubber Leaf’s stated rationale for the indirect distributor structure was customer continuity rather than an asserted gross-margin improvement. It also stated that first-tier supplier certifications from major automobile manufacturers involved extensive qualification processes and stringent requirements that could be difficult and time-consuming to obtain.[4][12]

That rationale helps explain why the indirect model could preserve access to first-tier supply chains during a period when the company’s direct facility was awaiting recertification. A qualified second-tier intermediary could coordinate orders while RLSP’s own direct-model production remained inactive. The company’s reported Q1 revenue pattern is consistent with this function: indirect revenue increased while direct revenue fell to zero.[4][1]

The company also disclosed payment-term pressure in the indirect channel. Since the end of 2021, Shanghai Xinsen had received some customer payments in bank notes with maturities of three to six months. Because RLSP did not accept bank notes, it agreed to extend Xinsen’s payment terms to four months from two months.[1]

Longer customer payment terms affect working capital and cash collection rather than gross margin directly. They nevertheless matter to the strategic durability of a margin recovery because a profitable sale is less valuable if the associated receivable remains outstanding for longer or becomes harder to collect.[1]

Rubber Leaf’s later annual results reinforce that management viewed the loss of direct sales as more important to overall margin than the intermediary commission. For the full year 2024, the company reported no direct-supply revenue, $6,914,900 of indirect-supply revenue and $14,806 of OEM-supply revenue, compared with $1,280,555 of direct-supply revenue and $8,709,595 of indirect-supply revenue in 2023.[6]

For the years ended December 31, 2023 and 2024, Rubber Leaf reported direct-supply gross margins of 23% and -%, respectively; indirect-supply gross margins of (4)% and (1)%; and total gross margins of (4)% and (11)%.[6]

Those full-year figures belong to a different period from the requested Q1 comparison, but they support the company’s stated distinction between a higher-margin direct channel and a low or negative-margin indirect channel. They also show that the company-wide margin can deteriorate when the higher-margin direct component disappears even if the indirect-channel margin becomes less negative.[6]

Rubber Leaf explicitly attributed the full-year overall margin decline mainly to generating less sales from the direct supply model, which it described as higher margin, after suspending direct production in July 2023. It separately said that its OEM supply model had not yet scaled because of limited customer orders, producing a low margin.[6]

The OEM model should not be conflated with Q1 2024 indirect supply. Rubber Leaf stated that it established the OEM supply model with Shanghai Xinsen starting in October 2024. Because that arrangement began in October 2024, it did not contribute to Q1 2024 revenue or margin. The adjustment substantially changes the interpretation of the quarter. On a reported basis, a negative 6.7% gross margin indicates that cost of sales exceeded revenue by $196,625. On an adjusted basis, the deficit falls to $5,922. Thus, approximately 97% of the reported gross loss—$190,703 out of $196,625—is accounted for by the identified relocation charge, although the gross-margin comparison is more appropriately expressed as the 6.46-percentage-point improvement shown above.[8]

Judgment — The ratio of the relocation charge to reported gross loss should not be read as proof that 97% of every underlying operating problem was relocation-related. It shows only that the disclosed charge accounts for that proportion of the quarter’s arithmetic gross-loss dollars. The remaining $5,922 adjusted loss may reflect normal sourcing, manufacturing, logistics, inventory, pricing, customer mix, or other cost-of-sales factors; this calculation does not allocate those remaining costs among causes.

Rubber Leaf’s disclosure links the relocation charge to RLSP’s production suspension beginning in July 2023.[1] The Q1 adjustment therefore identifies a transition-related expense associated with the company’s factory circumstances, rather than treating the full reported gross loss as evidence of recurring gross-cost performance. At the same time, the adjusted negative margin shows that the quarter was not quite gross-profitable even after the disclosed one-time-type cost was removed.

**The reported Q1 2024 margin was materially depressed by the separately identified Q1 2024 relocation charge, but the adjusted margin remained slightly negative; the disclosed charge explains most of the reported gross-margin deficit, while underlying gross profitability was not yet positive.** The broader comparison with Q1 2023 and the direct-to-indirect supply-model shift is addressed in the surrounding sections’ reported-margin and underlying-profitability analysis.[1][8]

Derived — On a gross-profit-dollar bridge, the $190,703 relocation loss represents 72.31% of Rubber Leaf’s $263,746 year-over-year deterioration from Q1 2023 gross profit of $67,121 to Q1 2024 gross loss of $196,625; $73,043, or 27.69%, remained after excluding that charge. This quantifies that the identified relocation expense was the larger arithmetic driver of the Q1 deterioration.[1]

Compared with Q1 2023’s positive 2.86% reported gross margin, the negative 0.20% relocation-adjusted Q1 2024 margin was 3.06 percentage points lower.[8] The adjustment therefore removes most of the Q1 2024 reported-margin decline, but underlying Q1 2024 gross margin remained below the Q1 2023 level.[8]

With direct-supply production suspended from July 2023, adjusted cost of sales increased 29.94% from Q1 2023 to Q1 2024, faster than revenue growth of 25.97%, a 3.97-percentage-point gap; this is consistent with cost pressure persisting beyond the relocation charge.[4][1]

Rubber Leaf recorded a separately identified **$190,703 factory-relocation loss** in Q1 2024 cost of sales; Rubber Leaf attributed that Q1 charge to RLSP’s production suspension that began in July 2023.[1] Removing that amount from reported cost of sales reduces the Q1 2024 cost base from $3,151,232 to $2,960,529 and changes gross loss from $196,625 to $5,922.[1]Derived — Gross margin correspondingly improves from approximately negative 6.7% to negative 0.2%, a 6.5-percentage-point improvement.

## Underlying profitability

### How does the adjusted Q1 2024 gross margin compare with

Judgment — Gross margin is gross profit divided by revenue: the share of sales remaining after cost of sales. The relevant underlying-profitability test therefore holds Q1 2024 revenue unchanged and removes only the identified factory-relocation loss from cost of sales. This distinguishes a discrete transition charge from the recurring economics of selling through the indirect supply model.

Rubber Leaf’s March 2024 Form 10-Q reports Q1 2024 revenue of $2,954,607, total cost of sales of $3,151,232, gross loss of $196,625, and a $190,703 loss on factory relocation. For Q1 2023, it reports revenue of $2,345,541, cost of sales of $2,278,420 and gross profit of $67,121.[1] The reported comparison is addressed in Reported gross margins and year-over-year change; the table below isolates what remains after the relocation charge is excluded.

The $190,703 relocation loss equals 72.31% of the $263,746 year-over-year gross-profit deterioration, leaving $73,043 of deterioration after its removal.[1][10] Removing the relocation-cost line recovers 6.45 percentage points, or 67.82% of the 9.52-percentage-point reported year-over-year gross-margin deterioration, calculated from unrounded rates; adjusted Q1 2024 gross margin of (0.20)% nevertheless remains 3.06 percentage points below Q1 2023’s 2.86% margin. Thus, the identified relocation charge explains most, but not all, of the reported decline.[1]

The relocation was a real operational interruption, rather than merely an accounting reclassification. RLSP suspended production in July 2023, began relocating to its new facility in early August 2023, obtained the property title certificate in December 2023, and had not resumed production by March 31, 2024 because QS16949 recertification and subsequent customer approval were required. Rubber Leaf expected production to resume in June 2024.[1] This establishes the $190,703 charge as a factory-relocation cost.

After its removal, the residual $5,922 gross loss is consolidated margin pressure associated with the changed sales mix and other remaining cost-of-sales items.[1][8]

Those displayed percentages do not reconcile with the reported revenue and gross-profit/(loss) amounts; this analysis therefore uses the dollar-statement calculation of gross profit divided by revenue shown in the table above.[8]

As of December 31, 2023, Rubber Leaf reported an accumulated deficit of $3,217,901 and negative working capital of $11,300,145. Management said it had not completed its efforts to establish a “stabilized source of gross profit sufficient to cover operating costs over a reasonable period of time” and anticipated needing additional investment capital in the near future to fund operating expenses and construction of a new production line.[3]

### Do the margin results indicate fundamental challenges in the new

Rubber Leaf’s Q1 revenue mix changed sharply toward indirect supply. Direct-supply revenue fell from $897,432 in Q1 2023 to zero in Q1 2024, while indirect-supply revenue rose from $1,448,109 to $2,954,607. Management said that direct supply had the higher gross profit margin and identified lower direct-supply sales as the main reason for the overall margin decline.[1] Rubber Leaf separately attributed the 26% increase in total quarterly revenue, from $2,345,541 to $2,954,607, primarily to rising demand from the indirect supply model.[1] Thus, the pivot supported sales growth but replaced a positive-margin direct-sales component with substantially more indirect revenue during a quarter that still produced a small adjusted gross loss.

Q1 2024 indirect-supply revenue of $2,954,607 exceeded Rubber Leaf’s entire Q1 2023 revenue of $2,345,541 while direct production was unavailable.[10]

The residual 3.06-percentage-point deficit is consolidated margin pressure associated with an adverse **sales-mix effect**—the effect on margin when the composition of revenue changes—and other remaining cost-of-sales items after the relocation cost is stripped out. Cost of revenues includes raw materials consumed, manufacturing costs, third-party logistics and distribution costs, including packaging, freight, transportation, shipping and handling, and inventory adjustments for defective products and inventory counts.[4] Indirect demand supported higher Q1 2024 revenue, while the higher-margin direct channel disappeared and consolidated gross margin remained lower after the charge was removed.

Management attributed the overall margin decline mainly to lower sales from direct supply, rather than identifying the indirect model’s standalone economics as the cause.[1]

Customer concentration adds to the importance of that distinction. In Q1 2024, Customer B generated all $2,954,607 of revenue, versus $1,448,109, or 62% of revenue, in Q1 2023; Customer A contributed the remaining $897,432, or 38%, in the earlier quarter. Customer B was Shanghai Xinsen, the related-party distributor.[1] The new indirect-led revenue base was therefore both larger and fully concentrated in one customer in Q1 2024, making consolidated margin highly sensitive to that relationship’s pricing, purchasing and logistics terms.

Later full-year figures point in two directions and should not be substituted for the Q1 calculation. The company reported that indirect-supply margin improved from negative 4% in 2023 to negative 1% in 2024, while total gross margin deteriorated from negative 4% to negative 11%; direct supply, which had a 23% margin in 2023, had no 2024 revenue, and OEM supply had a negative 54% margin in 2024.[5] This later pattern is consistent with indirect supply becoming less loss-making while the disappearance of higher-margin direct sales, low-margin OEM supply, and other transition costs weakened the company-wide result.

For the six months ended June 30, 2024, Rubber Leaf reported zero direct-supply revenue, $4,912,365 of indirect-supply revenue and a 0% indirect-supply gross margin. Its total gross margin for that six-month period was negative 7%, including relocation and other cost-of-sales items.[15]

Following RLSP’s disposition, RLHK sourced approximately 70% of its raw materials from Yongliansen and approximately 30% from Shanghai Haozong, and those proportions could change. In a separate risk discussion, Rubber Leaf said it was substantially reliant on Yongliansen and currently made 100% of raw-material purchases from Yongliansen; an increase in Yongliansen’s purchase cost could materially adversely affect results unless the vendor could be replaced.[7]

The supply models have different operating structures. Under direct supply, RLSP independently procures raw materials, manufactures final products and directly delivers finished goods to auto-OEM warehouses. Under indirect supply, RLSP procures rubber materials and outsources production to third-party manufacturers for work-in-process products or finished products; depending on order requirements, it may instead procure products directly in the market.[4][9]

Xinsen Group’s indirect-model sales commission was reduced from 1% of total sales before VAT through September 30, 2022 to 0.25% effective October 1, 2022. Rubber Leaf records that commission in selling expense, rather than in cost of sales, so it is not removed in the gross-margin relocation adjustment.[4]

The $2,954,607 of Q1 2024 indirect-supply revenue was sold through Xinsen Group to two certified first-tier suppliers of auto manufacturers. Rubber Leaf manages direct supply and indirect supply as two operating and reporting segments based on sales channels.[11]

For the year ended December 31, 2023, Rubber Leaf reported a 23% direct-supply gross-profit margin, a negative 4% indirect-supply gross-profit margin, and a negative 4% total gross margin. Management said the annual overall-margin decline was mainly attributable to lower sales from the higher-margin direct model after eGT, RLSP’s major direct-supply customer, temporarily suspended factory production in June 2023.[3]

Derived — Excluding the relocation loss, each additional $1.00 of Q1 2024 revenue versus Q1 2023 was accompanied by $1.1199 of additional cost of sales: the $682,109 increase in relocation-adjusted cost of sales divided by the $609,066 increase in revenue. This incremental comparison quantifies why the adjusted Q1 2024 gross result remained marginally negative despite revenue growth.[8][1]

Selling prices, product mix, material costs, labour, factory utilisation, freight and other cost-of-sales items can also affect gross margin; post-relocation quarters are needed to assess whether the residual pressure persists.

### Synthesis

**The evidence indicates that temporary relocation expense explains most, but not all, of Rubber Leaf’s Q1 2024 gross-margin deterioration.** Excluding the $190,703 relocation loss improves the margin by 6.45 percentage points, from negative 6.65% to negative 0.20%, but still leaves it 3.06 percentage points below Q1 2023’s positive 2.86%.[1] The remaining shortfall coincided with the disappearance of higher-margin direct-supply revenue and the expansion of indirect supply, although indirect demand increased revenue and later annual indirect-supply margin was less negative.[1][5]

1. **Consolidated gross-margin pressure remained in Q1 2024:** gross margin remained slightly negative after the relocation adjustment, while management linked the reported decline chiefly to the loss of higher-margin direct-supply sales. [1]
2. **Do not attribute the entire decline to indirect supply:** the identified relocation loss accounts for the larger portion of the reported quarterly margin fall.
3. **Monitor post-relocation direct/indirect mix and indirect gross margin:** these measures determine whether the residual deficit was transitional or becomes a persistent feature of the new operating model.

### Open questions

The available quarter-level disclosures do not separately quantify gross margin for the direct and indirect supply segments. Segment-level cost of sales, third-party manufacturing costs, and pricing by channel would establish the precise recurring margin economics of the indirect model.
