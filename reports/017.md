# Designer and Contemporary Source for Nature’s Castles

## Executive summary

Nature’s Castles: The Treehouse Reimagined opened at Longwood Gardens on April 25, 2008, as a three-treehouse installation.[1] Lookout Loft was designed by Forever Young Treehouses of Burlington, Vermont, while Peter Nelson of Seattle’s TreeHouse Workshop designed Canopy Cathedral and The Birdhouse.[2] Eileen Smith Dallabrida’s 11 August 2008 Delaware Today article, 《There’s Nothing Lovely as a Tree》, is a contemporaneous account of both the site-led conception and the construction: it describes designers reading the landscape and responding to particular trees, and reports the long-pipe, precast-concrete pin foundations used to avoid root damage and frost heave.[3] Brian Bingaman’s 24 April 2008 《The sky’s the limit at treehouse exhibit》 independently confirms that all three used five-foot steel-pipe pin foundations rather than being bolted to trees or set on conventional concrete foundations.[4]

## The installation and its creator

This account uses evidence published on or before 31 December 2008. Longwood Gardens opened Nature’s Castles: The Treehouse Reimagined on April 25, 2008, as part of its Arbor Day Weekend activities, with three structures—Lookout Loft, Canopy Cathedral, and The Birdhouse. Longwood’s announcement said that the display would continue through Labor Day and invited guests to explore “three large-scale treehouses designed by the country’s best treehouse designers.” Longwood credited Forever Young Treehouses of Burlington, Vermont, with Lookout Loft and Peter Nelson of Seattle’s TreeHouse Workshop, Inc., with Canopy Cathedral and The Birdhouse.[1][2] Tree protection was the project’s governing construction constraint: Longwood said preserving the trees’ health and the surrounding landscape was paramount, so the houses did not rely on trees for structural support.[2] Longwood opened the installation on April 25, 2008, in connection with its Arbor Day Weekend programme.[1]

### What was Longwood Gardens’ 2008 Nature’s Castles

Nature’s Castles: The Treehouse Reimagined was a seasonal, site-specific installation of three large-scale treehouses distributed through Longwood Gardens: Lookout Loft, The Birdhouse, and Canopy Cathedral. Longwood invited visitors to explore the structures in picturesque locations around the Gardens, rather than treating them as a single building or a conventional indoor exhibition.[2][5][6] The *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette* describes The Birdhouse as one of three “custom-designed treehouses” at Longwood Gardens; it says that Forever Young Treehouses, a Vermont nonprofit that builds only universally accessible treehouses, designed Lookout Loft, and that Peter Nelson of Seattle’s TreeHouse Workshop made the other two. Another account reports that visitors could climb into the structures and view the Gardens from above.[7][8]

The installation opened as part of Arbor Day Weekend. Longwood’s April 25, 2008 press release initially said that it would continue through Labor Day, while the Gardens’ August calendar listing and contemporary newspaper accounts advertised a daily run from Friday, April 25 through Sunday, November 23, 2008, at Longwood Gardens in Kennett Square, Pennsylvania.[2][5][3] Thus the project was presented as a 2008 seasonal attraction, even though the structures themselves were built as permanent works and their longer-term retention was initially under consideration.[2][5][3][9][4]

The *Delco Times* described the exhibition as opening “today” on 24 April 2008, whereas Longwood announced a 25 April 2008 opening.[2][8]

Longwood described the display as three “large-scale treehouses designed by the country’s best treehouse designers.” Their individual names and locations made the exhibition a sequence of destinations: Lookout Loft stood in Forest Walk; Canopy Cathedral occupied the Italian Water Garden area; and The Birdhouse stood near the historic Peirce-du Pont House and Peirce’s Woods.[1] Forest Walk was a shaded woodland of tulip-trees and other decididuous hardwoods, while Canopy Cathedral was near the Large Lake and Italian Water Garden.[10][11]

The exhibition brief joined horticultural interpretation to play and architectural spectacle. Longwood director Paul Redman said the installation was designed to celebrate trees and engage guests with their beauty and importance; he also stressed the structures’ size, artistry, and intricate detail as a way to reconnect children with nature and evoke childhood memories for adults.[12] Arbor Day programming reinforced that interpretation: visitors could meet the designers, tour historic Peirce’s Park, learn about arborists, and watch Longwood arborists demonstrate tree climbing and rigging.[8]

The project also drew meaning from Longwood’s institutional history. Pierre S. du Pont had acquired the Gardens’ original 202 acres to save an historic arboretum threatened by a lumber mill, and many of those trees remained at Longwood in Peirce’s Park.[2][8] In this context, treehouses were not simply picturesque visitor amenities: they made the Garden’s preservation mission physically legible by bringing visitors into the woodland canopy without assigning structural loads to the trees themselves.[2][8]

Pierre du Pont purchased the land in 1906 to protect its great trees.[15][7][21]

**Nature’s Castles was therefore a three-part garden installation: its exhibition medium was not only the treehouse structures, but also the experience of moving through, looking from, and learning about Longwood’s living arboretum.**[2][8][7]

A 2008 account characterized the treehouses as “Two years in the making,” offering picturesque views around the Gardens in connection with Arbor Day.[4] The installation also drew visitors to groves of tulip poplars in seldom-visited parts of Longwood Gardens’ 1,050-acre former du Pont estate.[13]

On its advertised daily schedule of 25 April through 23 November 2008, the installation had an inclusive operating period of 213 calendar days, underscoring that it was a full-season garden installation rather than an Arbor Day-only event.[5]

### What about Longwood Gardens’ 2008 Nature’s Castles

The Garden’s brief made tree protection a design constraint, not a decorative theme. Longwood stated that preserving the health of trees and the surrounding landscape was “of paramount importance,” and therefore the houses did not rely on trees for structural support.[2] This distinguishes the project from the familiar type of treehouse bolted to, suspended from, or otherwise structurally borne by a trunk or branch.

The construction solution was a **pin foundation**, a system of long steel pipes driven into the ground to support a building independently of nearby trees. Contemporary accounts say the pipes anchored each treehouse while leaving roots and limbs unscathed; another description specifies that long pipes passed through precast concrete units at ground level, reducing frost heave as well as root damage.[7][3] One report describes Lookout Loft’s pin foundation as concrete footings with extended steel rods placed between the roots.[15] Brian Bingaman’s *The Reporter* account says that all three treehouses at Longwood Gardens used five-foot steel pipes in a “pin foundation,” rather than being bolted to trees or given a poured concrete foundation.[4]

The designers nevertheless made trees active components of the spatial experience. All three treehouses had trees growing through their decks, and Lookout Loft incorporated two giant tulip trees spiralling through its roof.[7] Its floor and roof openings gave individual trees clearance to grow through the structure, while The Birdhouse was built over a large rotted trunk retained for visitors to inspect.[15][16] This is an architectural strategy of accommodation: the building is shaped around the tree’s continued presence and growth rather than removing or subordinating it.

Material selection reinforced the preservation rationale. At a press preview, Longwood horticulture head Sharon Loving said that recycled or reclaimed lumber had been used and that construction disturbed no root systems.[8] Canopy Cathedral also used Douglas-fir flooring remilled from timbers in the former Toronto toothpaste factory.[7][3] Redman stated that each treehouse contained some recycled, reclaimed, salvaged, or sustainably harvested wood.[4]

The three structures translated this common environmental constraint into markedly different architectural experiences:

- **Lookout Loft:** In Forest Walk, Forever Young Treehouses of Burlington, Vermont, designed a 720-square-foot rustic Adirondack-style structure with two viewing platforms—one roofed and one covered by a trellis—connected by a walkway. [1] Its ornate handrail was modeled on the Gothic Folly in Longwood’s recently opened Indoor Children’s Garden, an element inspired by the Longwood conservatory. [12] A separate contemporary account places the 720-square-foot structure behind the Peirce House on Forest Walk and reports that 13,000 pounds of salvaged steel supported its two joined platforms. [15] The Reporter describes it instead as a 900-square-foot, two-in-one Adirondack-style treehouse in Forest Walk looking onto Longwood’s 35-acre Meadow. [4] Its large ramp provided universal access; a 2008 account describes 75 feet of ramps leading to three viewing platforms. [17][1][7] Lookout Loft’s universal-access ramp fits Forever Young Treehouses’ stated goal of making treehouses accessible to people who cannot climb many steps. [12][9] The trellis was intended to become shaded by the forest foliage in summer, making the seasonal canopy part of the structure’s overhead enclosure. [4]

- **Canopy Cathedral:** Situated by the Italian Water Garden and centred on towering tulip poplars, this was an ornate two-storey house designed by Peter Nelson of Seattle’s TreeHouse Workshop, Inc., and inspired by a Norwegian Stave Church. [1] Longwood described an intricate glass façade, hand-carved fantastical creatures, and a deck overlooking the garden. [1][13] The Post-Gazette adds diamond-shaped façade windows, a soaring cathedral ceiling, twin stairs, and the 118-square-foot balcony. [7] The designer began by scouting Longwood during a February 2007 snowstorm and selected a site near the Italian Water Garden, showing that siting preceded the elaboration of the building’s church-derived form. [9]

- **The Birdhouse:** Near the Peirce-du Pont House, Peter Nelson’s 255-square-foot cedar Birdhouse was designed to provide elevated views of Peirce’s Woods; its sliding barn-door handle was fashioned from a tree limb. [2][1][13] A contemporary account describes built-in benches under its windows, a Western red cedar sliding barn door, and an overall resemblance to a giant bluebird box. [7] Its site made a visitor’s ascent an encounter with woodland as well as an elevated outlook. [7]

The project directed visitors into more rustic parts of Longwood that they might otherwise bypass. Longwood communications manager Patricia Evans described this as a “different kind of wow from nature” from the designed gardens, and the Post-Gazette reported that the Birdhouse and Lookout Loft occupied areas visitors did not often enter.[9][7] The structures therefore used architectural circulation—ramps, stairs, decks, walkways, and platforms—to change how visitors encountered the Garden’s woodland landscape.

Accounts differ over the amount of steel used to support Lookout Loft: the Delco Times reported more than 1,300 pounds, while the Daily Local reported 13,000 pounds of salvaged steel.[8][15] They agree, however, on the decisive architectural principle: independent support systems enabled the exhibition to preserve trees while making trunks, canopy, foliage, and woodland views integral to the visitor experience.[2][8][7][3]

### Synthesis

Nature’s Castles reimagined the treehouse as an independently founded garden building threaded around living trees, not as a small structure carried by a tree. Its three designs had separate authorship and distinct formal languages—an accessible Adirondack-style woodland loft, a stave-church-inspired garden structure, and an elevated birdhouse—but all served the purpose that Longwood Gardens Director Paul Redman said was to “celebrate trees and engage our guests in an exciting and unique way about the beauty and importance of trees,” while directing visitors into the arboretum.[2][8][1] The common mechanism was the combination of pin foundations, root-conscious siting, openings around trunks, and reclaimed or sustainably sourced material.[8][7][3][4] The contemporaneous figures for individual floor areas and Lookout Loft’s steel weight conflict, but they do not alter that shared design and construction logic.[2][8][15][7][3]

### What does that account say about the treehouses’ design concept?

The exhibition’s concept joined three distinct architectural characters under a common aim: to create elevated experiences of Longwood’s trees without treating the trees as building supports. Longwood director Paul Redman described Nature’s Castles as designed to celebrate trees and engage visitors with their beauty and importance. The design therefore made the existing landscape part of each visitor route, view, and architectural reference rather than merely the backdrop to a conventional tree platform.[12][7]

The individual structures gave that shared idea different forms:

- **Lookout Loft:** Forever Young Treehouses of Burlington, Vermont—co-founded by Bill Allen and Phil Trabulsy—designed this rustic Adirondack-style structure as an accessible sequence of viewing platforms linked by a walkway; one platform was roofed and another used a trellis. Its handrail was inspired by the Gothic Folly in Longwood’s recently opened Indoor Children’s Garden, and a large ramp made the structure accessible to all. [8] [8] [8]

- **Canopy Cathedral:** Peter Nelson of Seattle’s TreeHouse Workshop, Inc. designed this ornate, two-storey, 400-square-foot interpretation of a Norwegian stave church—a traditional Norwegian timber church—at the Italian Water Garden. It had an intricate glass façade, hand-carved fantastical creatures, and a large deck overlooking the garden; Dallabrida described it as centered on towering tulip poplars. [12] [13]

- **The Birdhouse:** Peter Nelson also designed the Birdhouse, which stood near the historic Peirce-du Pont House. The Delco Times described it as the tallest of the three, rising almost 20 feet, with 320 square feet of area; its bird’s-eye viewpoint over Peirce’s Woods was central to its identity. The Daily Local described salvaged dairy-barn wood, cedar construction, and slab-wood benches, while also reporting that the structure was built over a retained rotted trunk so visitors could look into its habitat. [8] [8] [15] [15]

Together, these three forms—Adirondack lookout, stave-church-inspired cathedral, and bird’s-eye perch—used access, outlook, and architectural reference to make particular parts of Longwood’s landscape central to the visitor experience.[8] Lookout Loft’s ramps organized movement through the Forest Walk; Canopy Cathedral’s deck framed the Italian Water Garden; and the Birdhouse used elevation to turn Peirce’s Woods into a view.[8][15] In exhibition-design terms, these are interpretive routes: architectural circulation that teaches visitors to notice a landscape through controlled changes of proximity, height, shelter, and outlook.

**The design concept was thus three different architectural narratives—Adirondack lookout, stave-church-like cathedral, and bird’s-eye perch—made coherent by a conservation-led relationship to the trees.**[8][15][9]

### What does that account say about the treehouses’ construction process?

The key construction technique was the **pin foundation**, a ground-based foundation system in which inclined steel pipes are driven through a precast concrete head or unit and carry a post and steel framing above. Dallabrida described each house as supported by long pipes driven through precast concrete units at ground level; the reported purpose was to prevent frost heave—ground movement caused by freezing—and avoid damage to tree roots.[3]

A July 2008 Star Stuff Life account gives the most complete published description of the load path, meaning the route by which a building’s weight reaches the ground. It states that all three treehouses were supported by a Pin Foundation, also called a Diamond Pier. Each pin foundation had four five-foot pipe pins driven at roughly 45 degrees through a precast head; a support post was bolted to the head, and steel beams supported the building above. The Pin Foundations made it unnecessary to bolt the houses to trees or excavate and pour a concrete foundation.[16]

The *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette* published its construction account, “At Longwood Gardens, fantastic treehouses grow,” on 5 July 2008.[7]

This system allowed the three structures to be placed among trees rather than on them. Lancaster Online reported that all three were permanent structures nestled around groups of trees and held up by pin foundations, rather than sitting on tree trunks; the stated result was that the trees would not be harmed. Dallabrida likewise explained that the houses were anchored in technology rather than trunks or branches.[9][3]

Longwood staff and TreeHouse Workshop participated in construction using methods intended to preserve the trees.[23][1]

The treehouses nevertheless made trunks physically present in their architecture. All three deliberately had trees growing through their decks; Lookout Loft also had an opening in its roof above a viewing platform for a tree to continue upward. This is a designed clearance condition, not structural dependence: openings were left around growing trees while the loads went to the ground through the pin foundations.[3][9]

Lookout Loft illustrates how structural, access, and landscape decisions worked together. Its more than 75 feet of ramps led to three platforms up to 14 feet above the ground, while the ramps used Vermont hop-hornbeam and Brazilian ipe and were shaped like tree limbs with wrought iron and metal. The structure’s lead builder, Erych Stauffer of Forever Young Treehouses, explained that the apparently open trellis roof would gain overhead cover when the forest foliage was fullest.[9][4]

Accessibility was therefore designed into the building rather than added as an afterthought. Longwood’s 25 April 2008 release said Forever Young Treehouses was campaigning to create an ADA-accessible treehouse in every state by the end of 2008 and identified Lookout Loft’s large ramp as providing universal access. Forever Young’s stated purpose was to create entirely accessible treehouses so that people unable to climb many steps could also enjoy them, and the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette characterized Lookout Loft as ADA-friendly, with an ipe hardwood walkway and two large tulip trees spiraling through its roof.[17][9][7]

Reused and salvaged wood was also part of construction: the Birdhouse used lumber from an old Washington dairy farm, floorboards from a dismantled Toronto toothpaste factory, rafters from a dismantled Vancouver warehouse, and Douglas-fir window timber from trees felled in landslides or storms; Canopy Cathedral’s Douglas-fir columns, beams, and rafters were milled from logs recovered from a 2002 Oregon wildfire.[8] Forever Young described walking forests near Burlington, Vermont, to find the right curve or twist and amending designs to fit unexpected finds; it used Vermont-local materials where possible, while the treehouses used recovered, reclaimed, or repurposed wood to reduce the need to cut living trees.[8][7][16] The material approach therefore combined searching forests for suitable wood forms and adapting designs to found pieces with reuse and local sourcing intended to reduce the need to cut living trees.[16]

Canopy Cathedral’s construction used cedar shakes and cedar planking alongside its reclaimed Douglas-fir timbers.[15][21][9]

The Birdhouse used western red cedar from logs sourced in southeast Alaska.[7][21][8]

Bingaman reported that every treehouse contained some recycled, reclaimed, salvaged, or sustainably harvested wood. Dallabrida specified that Canopy Cathedral’s floors were Douglas fir salvaged from an old toothpaste factory in Toronto; Bingaman noted that apparently random holes in reused beams were evidence of their earlier life in that dismantled factory. Other reported sources included a Washington dairy barn, Oregon wildfire logs, Vermont cedar, ironwood and eastern hemlock, and sustainably harvested Brazilian hardwood.[13][4]

Dallabrida reported a four-month, $1 million build, and the Post-Gazette independently repeated the same duration and cost while describing the materials as largely salvaged. Canopy Cathedral itself incorporated 37 steel columns, 34 steel beams, and eight steel braces, demonstrating that the apparently informal treehouse image rested on conventional engineered framing as well as timber craft.[3][7][9]

**The construction process reconciled an elevated treehouse experience with landscape protection: permanent, steel-framed buildings stood on pin foundations, left clearances for living trees, and used reused or responsibly sourced materials where possible.**[7][9][3][4]

For Canopy Cathedral, the published specification totals 79 named steel members—37 columns, 34 beams, and eight braces—making the extent of engineered framing behind its timber-treehouse appearance concrete.[9]

At the reported four-month, $1 million project scale, the implied average expenditure was $250,000 per construction month for all three treehouses.[7]

Using the Star Stuff Life description of four pipe pins per pin foundation and its count of 34 foundations for Lookout Loft, that structure used 136 pipe pins.[16]

Evans stated the construction priority directly: “It was important to us that no trees were harmed,” because “the focus of treehouses is the trees.”[13]

The accounts distinguish a project described as two years in the making from a reported four-month construction period.[4][3]

Opening-day reporting records that finishing work remained at both structures: Canopy Cathedral lacked its creatures and railings, while the Birdhouse still awaited a large piece of viewing glass.[15]

Lookout Loft’s built sequence began at a small uncovered platform with a tree growing through a floor opening, continued to a larger platform under an open white-cedar trellis canopy, and ended at a third, largest platform under a curved wooden-shingle roof. Each tree passing through a floor had a corresponding roof opening.[16]

For Lookout Loft, the reported 34 pin foundations, each using four five-foot pipe pins, amount to 136 pins and 680 linear feet of pipe-pin material.[16]

Scheel & Sharp of Norristown made Canopy Cathedral’s large prow window.[4]

A copper cable suspended from the tallest trees around the Birdhouse served as a lightning rod.[4]

### Synthesis

Nature’s Castles used site-specific architectural exhibition design: the designers began by reading the site and its trees, then created differentiated visitor experiences around those settings.[3] First, designers read particular groves, trees, views, and access conditions; this produced three differentiated experiences: the accessible Adirondack Lookout Loft, the Norwegian-stave-church-inspired Canopy Cathedral, and the elevated Birdhouse lookout.[8] Second, construction separated the buildings’ structural loads from the trees through pin foundations, while decks and roofs were cut around trunks.

Third, reclaimed, salvaged, local, and sustainably harvested materials gave environmental responsibility a visible material form as well as a stated rationale.[3][8][9][4]

The measurements reported for individual structures differ among contemporary accounts, but their agreement on the governing strategy is strong: site-specific interpretation and non-invasive ground support defined the project.[12][7][9][3]
