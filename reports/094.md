# Vicarious Liability for a Platform Courier’s Pedestrian Injury

## Executive summary

On the stated facts, neither side should ordinarily obtain summary judgment on respondeat superior because a reasonable factfinder could find either employee-like platform control and work-connected driving or independent-contractor autonomy.

The company’s mandated routes, acceptance-rate warnings, automatic suspension rule, branded gear, lack of pricing discretion, and quota pressure support employee status and a finding that rushing to complete an overdue surge-period delivery was within the scope of work and partly served the company.

The courier’s self-selected schedule, personal vehicle, insurance obligation and lapse, contractual independent-contractor label, and occasional direct hiring of his son support the contrary classification, but none is dispositive. The collision and surgically treated fractured ankle establish an accident and injury, not by themselves the courier’s negligence or causation, which remain necessary predicates to derivative liability.

The result may change under the governing jurisdiction’s employee-classification and scope rules, and it also depends on which party moved and whether that party can eliminate genuine disputes of material fact.

## Contested question and governing law

Respondeat superior is the common-law doctrine that can make a business answer for a worker’s tort even when the business did not itself drive negligently or directly injure the claimant. In ordinary terms, the pedestrian seeks to shift liability for the courier’s alleged driving tort to the delivery company because the courier was performing the company’s delivery work. That question is contested because the courier had features of both an independent businessperson and a controlled worker.

No governing jurisdiction is specified. That omission matters: there is “not a national standard for respondeat superior”; states create their own standards, and most jurisdictions use either a benefits test or a characteristics test. The analysis below therefore states the prevailing Restatement-style common-law framework and identifies jurisdiction-specific variations that could affect the ultimate rule.[1] The Second and Third Restatements of Agency are principal sources of agency law, influential and persuasive to many courts but not binding.[2]

### What respondeat superior framework applies to determine whether the company

**Claim.** Under the ordinary common-law framework, the pedestrian must establish four distinct inquiries: (1) an underlying tort by the courier, ordinarily negligence; (2) employee status under the applicable right-to-control test; (3) that the tort occurred within the scope of employment; and (4) business benefit as evidence that the courier acted at least partly to serve the company. Business benefit is ordinarily relevant to the employer-serving-purpose component of scope and to the policy rationale for enterprise liability, not a universally separate element.[3][4][5][6][7]

Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(1) states the core rule directly: “An employer is subject to vicarious liability for a tort committed by its employee acting within the scope of employment.” Respondeat superior is therefore *derivative liability*: the company’s liability derives from the courier’s established tort.[3] It does not require proof that the company separately breached a duty, but it does require proof that the courier did.[4][5]

The sequential structure is important. The pedestrian cannot establish company liability by proving only that a collision occurred, that an ankle fractured, or even that the company’s app exerted economic pressure. The pedestrian must prove the courier’s underlying negligence claim, then the employee relationship, and then work-related scope. Colorado’s pattern instruction makes the structure explicit: recovery against an alleged employer requires proof of the negligence claim against the alleged employee, employee status at the relevant time, and action within the scope of employment.[5]

#### The relationship requirement: employee versus independent contractor

The relationship inquiry asks whether the courier was an *employee*—historically called a servant—for vicarious-liability purposes. Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(3) (2006) defines an employee as an agent whose principal controls or has the right to control the manner and means of work; it also provides that gratuitous performance does not relieve a principal of liability. Put plainly, the inquiry is whether the company retained authority over how deliveries were performed, not merely the right to demand completed deliveries.[8][7][2] Washington has not adopted this Third Restatement formulation in a published opinion.[8]

Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220(1) formulates the core distinction in terms of physical conduct. A servant is a person employed to perform services in another’s affairs who is subject to that person’s control, or right of control, “with respect to the physical conduct in the performance of the services.” An independent contractor instead undertakes a result while retaining control over the physical manner of doing the work.[9][2]

Judgment — The *right-to-control inquiry* is the short name for this question. It separates a company’s right to specify a commercial result—deliver the order—from a right to direct the worker’s operative methods—where to drive, what route to take, when to accept jobs, or whether refusal will terminate access to the work.

Colorado’s employer-employee instruction identifies the central inquiry as the right to control performance details; actual exercise of that right is not required. It also directs consideration of who selected the worker, paid compensation, held dismissal power, and retained the right to control.[5] The same instruction defines an independent contractor as a person who uses the contractor’s own methods and is not subject to control other than as to the desired result. Control over both the result and the means and methods is inconsistent with independent-contractor status.[5]

The Second Restatement’s § 220(2) supplies a more detailed, nonexclusive multifactor analysis. It identifies ten matters of fact:

- **Control over details:** The agreement’s extent of control over the work details is relevant. This is the leading consideration because it most directly shows whether the worker surrendered operational direction to the alleged employer. [9]

- **Independent occupation and customary supervision:** The factfinder considers whether the worker is engaged in a distinct business, and whether the kind of work is usually done under direction or without supervision in the locality. Community practice and the skill required can carry substantial weight. [9]

- **Skill, tools, and workplace:** The inquiry considers the skill required and which party supplies the instrumentalities, tools, and place of work. Supplying one’s own tools is evidence against servant status, but only evidentiary; it is not conclusive. [9]

- **Duration and compensation:** The relationship’s duration and whether payment is by time or by the job matter. Short engagements and job-based payment tend toward independent contracting, while longer, regular work and employer-furnished instrumentalities can support employee status. [9]

- **Integration, parties’ understanding, and business status:** The inquiry asks whether the work is part of the alleged employer’s regular business, whether the parties believed they were creating a master-servant relationship, and whether the principal is itself in business. [9]

Washington’s civil pattern instruction, which adopts the Second Restatement approach for distinguishing agents from independent contractors, emphasizes that all control-related evidence must be considered, that factors have different weight, and that not every factor must be present.[8] In the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) context, the U.S. Supreme Court held in 1968 that the common-law agency test governs employee–independent-contractor classification and required assessment of all incidents of the relationship, “with no one factor being decisive.” In later classification decisions, the Court used Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220(2), a non-exhaustive multifactor test, as authoritative guidance.[10]

This holistic inquiry prevents a court from treating a written independent-contractor label as automatically dispositive. California’s labor guidance states that calling someone an independent contractor, requiring an agreement with that label, or paying by Form 1099 rather than W-2 does not itself determine legal status; the governing legal test does.[11] That proposition is particularly apt as a general common-law caution, although California’s statutory classification rules are not automatically the governing tort rule here.[11]

The company’s agreement consequently has legal significance, but limited significance. Its prohibition on treating couriers as employees supports the company’s position on the parties’ intended relationship, which is one § 220 consideration. But the label cannot override retained control over how deliveries are made if the governing jurisdiction applies a control-focused common-law test.[9][11][2]

The agreement required couriers to maintain liability insurance; the courier’s lapse may bear on compliance and available coverage, but it does not determine control or employee status.[9] Requiring couriers to obtain and maintain liability insurance can support an inference that couriers bear independent business risks.

The courier’s policy lapse two weeks before the collision strengthens that factual inference as to the courier’s personal compliance failure. But insurance allocation does not itself answer who controlled the route, delivery process, access to work, or the manner and means of performance. It therefore does not resolve employee status under the right-to-control inquiry.[9][12]

The ability to delegate work is a relevant, nonexclusive status consideration because an agreement barring delegation indicates a master-servant relationship.[9] A worker’s ability to delegate may support contractor status because an agreement that work cannot be delegated is one factor indicating a master-servant relationship.

Here, however, the stated fact is only that the courier occasionally hired and paid his son directly; it does not state that the company authorized, prohibited, knew of, or controlled the delegation. The fact may support an inference of some operational independence, but it cannot alone determine the courier’s status.[9]

The requirement that the courier wear company-branded gear likewise is relevant, but does not independently establish employee status. Branding can identify the courier to customers as part of the platform’s delivery operation and may reinforce an inference that delivery work was integrated into the company’s business. Yet the control inquiry concerns the right to control work details, not merely public association with the company.[9][12]

The Restatement distinguishes genuine control from influence. A principal’s right to control includes initially stating what an agent shall and shall not do and retaining the right to give interim instructions or directions after the relationship begins.[12] In contrast, contractual service standards alone do not necessarily create an interim instruction right, and a right merely to veto a provider’s choices does not itself establish the affirmative-direction authority associated with common-law agency.[12]

Together, the manner-and-means formulation and the rule that the required control must concern the injury-producing activity tie the control inquiry to the courier’s driving, rather than merely to the platform’s control over the delivery transaction.[3][13] Mandated app routes tend to concern delivery methods rather than merely delivery results.[3][13] Automatic suspension after more than two consecutive refusals, coupled with automated warnings that acceptance rates affect account standing, may show more than ordinary quality standards because they condition continued access to the work on responding to company-directed assignments.

Conversely, the courier’s self-selected schedule, personal vehicle, personal insurance obligation, and occasional direct payment of an assistant point toward independent operational responsibility. The legal consequence depends on the degree, character, and enforceability of the company’s retained authority over the work as a whole.[5][2][12][9]

The control required for an employment relationship need not amount to constant supervision. The Second Restatement explains that the necessary control “may be very attenuated,” and recognizes both contractual and de facto close supervision as indicators of master-servant status.[9] Thus, the fact that the company may not have personally monitored every mile of driving does not end the control inquiry; a platform can exercise control through programmed dispatch, routing, acceptance requirements, account sanctions, and instructions embedded in the app.

At the same time, not every platform restriction makes a courier an employee. In *Matter of the Claim of Luis A. Vega*, decided June 21, 2018, the Appellate Division, Third Judicial Department, ruled that Postmates courier Luis Vega was an independent contractor rather than an employee entitled to unemployment benefits.[14] The court held that company-set fees and pay rates, real-time tracking, and customer-complaint handling did not, on that record, amount to substantial evidence of employment.[14] That decision arose in an unemployment-insurance context and does not determine this tort dispute, but it illustrates why flexible scheduling alone can carry genuine weight even in app-mediated delivery work.

A contrasting authority, *O’Connor v. Uber Technologies, Inc.*, held under California law that drivers who performed services for Uber’s benefit were presumptive employees, while treating ultimate classification as a mixed question of law and fact typically for a jury.[15] The point is not that *O’Connor* controls an unidentified forum.

It is that app-based delivery and transportation relationships commonly contain mixed evidence, making classification dependent on the governing law and the actual control mechanism rather than the platform’s chosen contractual description.[15][16][9]

Independent-contractor status ordinarily defeats respondeat superior. The ordinary rule is that a master is liable for a servant’s torts within the scope of employment, while a principal is generally not liable for torts committed by an independent contractor in connection with the contracted work.[7][17][2] But the classification inquiry should not be collapsed into the broader agency inquiry.

An independent contractor can also be an agent, and Colorado materials identify disagreement over whether a principal may be vicariously liable for physical injuries caused by an agent independent contractor.[5]

That disagreement is material only if the courier is ultimately found not to be an employee. The Colorado Judicial Branch reports that divisions of the Colorado Court of Appeals disagree: *Daly v. Aspen Ctr. for Women’s Health, Inc.* held that “ordinarily, a principal is not liable for physical injuries caused by the negligence of a nonservant agent,” while *Cheney v. Hailey* held a principal vicariously liable for injuries caused by an agent’s negligent driving without regard to the agent’s independent-contractor status. The Colorado Supreme Court had not squarely addressed that issue.[5] Other potential liability routes—such as negligent selection, training, retention, supervision, authorized torts, or nondelegable duties—are distinct from ordinary respondeat superior and are not decided by the employee-status question.[7][17][18]

The applicable framework therefore makes employee status a control-centered, fact-sensitive threshold question; the independent-contractor agreement, insurance arrangement, branding, schedule freedom, vehicle ownership, and delegation practice are relevant facts, but none alone resolves that threshold.[8][11][9]

#### The tort requirement: derivative liability is not collision liability

Respondeat superior attaches to a *tort*, not simply to an accident. In a negligence action, the pedestrian must prove the courier’s duty, breach, factual causation, legal causation, and damages under the governing jurisdiction’s negligence law; the company’s liability, if any, is derivative of that tort.[4][5]

The facts state a collision and a fractured ankle requiring surgery. Those facts establish that an accident and physical injury allegedly occurred, and they may be powerful evidence on damages. They do not, without more, establish that the courier drove below the required standard of care, that any such breach caused the ankle fracture, or that no other cause contributed. Colorado’s respondeat-superior instruction requires proof of the underlying negligence claim; it does not treat collision or injury as a substitute for that proof.[5]

This distinction also matters procedurally. The Federal Judicial Center identifies whether a driver used due care and whether that driver proximately caused injuries as ultimate factual questions ordinarily for the jury.[19] The Negligence, Causation, and Proof section addresses the available proof of those underlying elements; this section establishes only that they are prerequisites to derivative company liability.

The pedestrian must prove the courier’s negligence before derivative liability can attach; proof of injury alone does not satisfy that first step.[4][5]

#### The scope-of-employment requirement and the role of employer benefit

The scope-of-employment inquiry asks whether the courier’s conduct at the June 8, 2024 collision was part of, or sufficiently connected to, his delivery work.[6][18][20] It asks whether the employee was doing assigned work, performing conduct subject to the employer’s control, or instead pursuing an independent course not designed to serve the employer.[18][20]

The Second Restatement’s familiar § 228 formulation treats conduct as within scope when it is of the kind the employee was hired to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is actuated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer. In a negligent-driving case, the intentional-force condition is not relevant.[6][21] The Utah Supreme Court’s *Birkner* test requires that the conduct be “of the general kind the employee is employed to perform,” occur “within the hours of the employee’s work and the ordinary spatial boundaries of the employment,” and be motivated “at least in part, by the purpose of serving the employer’s interest.”[22][23]

Colorado uses a related formulation: an employee acts within scope when doing work assigned by the employer, work proper, usual, and necessary to accomplish assigned work, or work customary in the trade or business to accomplish it.[5] The precise language varies among jurisdictions, but each formulation distinguishes work-connected conduct from a substantial personal departure.

Colorado describes conduct outside scope as a substantial departure from the principal’s business to pursue an independent purpose unrelated to the business, even if brief.[5]

Employer benefit matters principally at this stage. A courier who is completing a delivery can be acting, at least in part, to serve the platform’s commercial interests even if the courier also seeks personal earnings, avoids account consequences, or improves individual compensation. As a jurisdictional illustration, the Utah Supreme Court treats conduct as within scope when it is of the general kind assigned, occurs substantially within the employment’s authorized time and space, and is motivated at least partly by serving the employer. It includes methods of accomplishing work objectives even when those methods are improper.[23][22]

The Benefit to the Company section applies that framework to surge pricing, completion of orders, quota pressure, and account-standing warnings. At this stage, the critical legal point is narrower: the company’s benefit is not merely a policy observation. Under the prevailing scope formulations, evidence that the courier acted at least partly to complete company-directed deliveries and preserve the company’s delivery operation can bear directly on the employer-serving-purpose element.[5][6][23][22]

Benefit also explains the underlying policy of respondeat superior. The traditional rationale allocates employee-tort losses to the enterprise that benefits from the work and seeks to profit from it, rather than leaving the loss with an innocent injured person.[7][2] The Second Restatement commentary similarly explains that an enterprise that benefits from work and has the right to control the physical activities that make it profitable should bear physical harm caused by servants’ errors while doing the work that makes the enterprise successful.[7][2]

That rationale does not turn every profitable contract into an employment relationship. The Third Restatement’s agency commentary explains that acting on another’s behalf does not necessarily entail that the other will benefit, and contractual performance can benefit the other party without establishing agency.[12] Benefit therefore cannot replace the control inquiry; it helps explain why a worker who is an employee and is carrying out company-serving work may bring the employer within respondeat superior.

Employer benefit is most important as evidence that delivery conduct served the company’s business and as the policy reason for allocating a covered employee’s delivery risk to the enterprise; it is not a substitute for proving employment, tort, and scope.[5][6][7][12]

#### Training records and the boundaries of this framework

The onboarding checklist references completion of safety protocols, and the underlying training records were lost during a system migration.[24] They do not alter the basic derivative-liability elements. The onboarding checklist supports the limited proposition that the company’s onboarding process referenced completion of safety protocols. The loss of underlying training records means the checklist does not itself prove what protocols the courier actually received, understood, or followed.[18]

The company’s safety protocols may show that it prescribed aspects of delivery performance. But a service contract’s quality standards do not, standing alone, establish an interim right to direct a worker’s conduct; the issue remains whether the company had a right to issue operative instructions about the manner and means of the courier’s work.[12] The lost records therefore neither prove employee status nor prove lack of control. They leave the factfinder to assess the remaining evidence of routing, acceptance sanctions, account discipline, branding, and working autonomy.

The training evidence may support an inference that the company established safety expectations, but it does not conclusively establish either actual training, employee status, underlying negligence, or derivative liability.[12][18]

### What is the summary judgment standard?

Summary judgment is a judgment without trial. It is appropriate only when the record leaves no genuine dispute about facts that could affect the result under governing law and the movant is entitled to win as a legal matter. The procedure tests whether a trial is needed; it does not permit the judge to decide which competing evidence is true.[25][26]

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a) provides that the court “shall grant summary judgment” if the movant shows both that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and that the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.[25] Although the governing procedural rule may differ in state court, this two-part Rule 56 formulation is a useful default because comparable state formulations use the same core concepts.

Utah’s Supreme Court, for example, states the same standard and requires courts to view relevant facts and inferences in favor of the party opposing the motion.[23]

A fact is *material* when it could affect the outcome under the substantive law governing the claim. A dispute is *genuine* when the evidence would permit a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmovant.[26] Thus, whether a dispute blocks summary judgment depends on the jurisdiction’s substantive respondeat-superior law.

If employee status, scope, or courier negligence is an element under the governing law, evidence capable of supporting a reasonable finding on that issue is material.[26]

At summary judgment, the court does not weigh evidence, assess witness credibility, or choose between competing reasonable inferences. The nonmovant’s evidence is believed, and all justifiable inferences are drawn in that party’s favor.[26] The Federal Judicial Center likewise explains that resolution of genuine and material disputes over historical facts or reasonable inferences is a jury function.[19]

The allocation of burdens proceeds in stages:

1. **First, the movant identifies the basis for judgment.** The movant must identify the portions of the record that it contends show no genuine factual dispute. A defendant may discharge that burden by pointing to an absence of evidence on an element the plaintiff must prove. The Federal Rules impose no express or implied requirement that the movant submit affidavits or similar materials negating the opponent’s claim: Rules 56(a) and (b) permit claimants and defendants to move “with or without supporting affidavits.” [27]

2. **Second, the nonmovant responds with specific triable facts.** Once the motion is properly supported, the opposing party cannot rest on pleadings, allegations, or denials. The party must identify record evidence from which a reasonable jury could find in its favor on the disputed, outcome-relevant elements. [26][27]

3. **Third, the court determines whether the dispute requires trial.** A mere scintilla of evidence is insufficient, but the court may not grant judgment where a reasonable jury could find for the nonmovant. [26][28]

Rule 56(c)(1) requires factual assertions to be supported by particular record materials, including depositions, documents, electronically stored information, affidavits or declarations, stipulations, admissions, and interrogatory answers.[25] A party may object that material cited to support or dispute a fact cannot be presented in admissible form.[25] The rule therefore requires an evidentiary showing, but not necessarily evidence already presented in its final trial form.[27]

The movant-specific nature of the standard is central here because the problem does not identify who moved for summary judgment. If the company moves, the court views the record and reasonable inferences for the pedestrian. The same factual record may therefore defeat a motion by either side if it supports competing reasonable inferences on material elements.[26][23]

Employee classification and scope often present fact-intensive issues. The Second Restatement says that the trier of fact ordinarily determines whether enough favorable factors establish a master-servant relationship; only when the inference clearly favors one result may the court decide status as a matter of law.[9] Colorado’s agency materials similarly state that agency generally is a factual question, becoming a legal question only when the evidence is undisputed or permits only one reasonable and logical inference.[5]

Scope receives similar treatment. The Utah Supreme Court holds that scope is ordinarily a question of fact and must go to the jury whenever reasonable minds could differ over whether the employee was wholly or partly performing the employer’s business; a court may decide it as a matter of law only where reasonable minds could not differ.[23] The Federal Judicial Center specifically identifies whether a person was driving in the course of employment as a predominantly factual ultimate question for the jury.[19]

This allocation does not make summary judgment unavailable in respondeat-superior cases. If the undisputed facts establish that the courier was plainly an independent contractor under controlling law, that the alleged conduct was an unmistakable personal frolic, or that the pedestrian lacks proof of an essential negligence element after adequate discovery, the company could obtain judgment. Conversely, if undisputed facts establish employment, scope, and the courier’s tort, the pedestrian could obtain judgment on liability.[27][19]

Where essential evidence is temporarily unavailable, Rule 56(d) provides a procedural safeguard. A nonmovant who shows by affidavit or declaration that specified reasons prevent presentation of facts essential to oppose the motion may seek deferral or denial of the motion, time for affidavits or discovery, or another appropriate order.[25] The missing safety-training records may be relevant to such a request if they are genuinely essential to a disputed claim or defense, but their absence does not permit a court to assume what the records would have shown.[25][18]

**The summary-judgment question is not which side’s account is more persuasive; it is whether, under the governing respondeat-superior law and with all permissible inferences favoring the nonmovant, only one reasonable legal result remains.**[25][26][19]

A party may seek summary judgment on an entire claim or defense or only part of a claim or defense.[25] If summary judgment does not resolve the whole action, the court should identify and order which material facts are not genuinely disputed.[29]

### Synthesis

Under the ordinary framework, respondeat superior requires a proved courier tort, employee status under the governing control test, and conduct within the scope of employment.[3][4][6][7] Employer benefit bears most directly on the scope inquiry and on the policy for enterprise liability; it cannot replace proof of employee status or negligence.

Independent-contractor status ordinarily defeats respondeat superior, but the label in the agreement is a factor rather than a conclusive answer, and some jurisdictions recognize unresolved or distinct agency-based theories for contractor-caused physical injuries.[3][5][6][11][7]

Because no jurisdiction or movant is identified, the applicable rule and the direction of factual inferences cannot be fixed in advance. Under the Rule 56 model, the court must identify the governing substantive test and deny the motion if evidence permits a reasonable factfinder to differ on an outcome-relevant issue such as control, scope, or the courier’s underlying negligence.[25][26][9][23]

Although the Third Restatement completely superseded the out-of-print Second Restatement as an American Law Institute publication, the Washington Supreme Court Committee on Jury Instructions states that Washington courts adopted Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220(2) as the test distinguishing agents from independent contractors, including in *Massey v. Tube Art Display, Inc.*[30][8]

Agency arises when a principal assents that an agent act on the principal’s behalf and subject to the principal’s control, and the agent assents or consents to do so. The required control need not cover every activity, the agent’s use of time, or the agent’s exercise of professional judgment.[12]

A tort employee-status inquiry does not automatically use the economic-realities test developed for wage disputes. Washington authority identifies the right-of-control test for common-law vicarious-liability claims and the economic-realities test for wage disputes; the latter focuses on workers’ wages and dependence rather than primarily on control.[31]

In the Postmates unemployment-insurance proceeding, the Unemployment Appeal Board found an employer-employee relationship between Postmates and Luis Vega after reversing an Administrative Law Judge; an intermediate appellate court reversed the Board; and the New York Court of Appeals ultimately upheld the Board’s employee finding based on substantial evidence of Postmates’s control over Vega and similarly situated couriers.[14][32]

The potential benefit to the platform bears on whether completing the June 8, 2024 delivery furthered its business, but an actual profit is not necessarily a separate universal prerequisite to respondeat superior.[5][6][7][12]

## Employee status under the control test

The classification issue is whether the company was the courier’s employer for **respondeat superior** purposes—that is, whether it must answer for a worker’s tort because the worker was its employee. The central common-law inquiry is the **control test**: whether the company controlled, or retained the right to control, the manner and means of the courier’s work, meaning how he performed deliveries rather than merely the completed result. This question matters because an independent contractor ordinarily retains control of the physical work, while an employee performs work subject to the employer’s actual or retained direction.[3][33][9]

Judgment — No jurisdiction is specified. The analysis therefore uses the Restatement-style common-law control framework as a general model, not as a universal rule. The Second and Third Restatements of Agency are principal sources of agency law and are influential and persuasive to many courts, but are not binding. States differ in both their respondeat-superior rules and their worker-classification tests.[1][2]

### Does the courier’s working arrangement make him an employee under

Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220 provides that a servant—now more commonly called an employee for this purpose—performs services in another’s affairs and, in physical performance of them, is subject to the other’s control or right to control.[4] Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(3)(a) similarly defines an employee as an agent whose principal controls or has the right to control the manner and means of work.[3] The right matters even if it is exercised intermittently rather than by a human supervisor at every moment.[3][4][5][12][9]

The common-law test is multifactorial. The Third Restatement of Torts identifies the agreed extent of the principal’s control over work details, the worker’s distinct occupation or business, customary direction or supervision, required skill, who supplies tools, instrumentalities, and workplace, engagement duration, payment by job or time, whether the work is part of the principal’s regular business, the parties’ belief about creating employment, whether the principal is in business, and the principal’s actual control over work details. No one factor is automatically decisive.[4][8][1][9]

The courier’s ability to set his own schedule favors independent-contractor status. Schedule autonomy resembles the traditional contractor example of a specialist who sets hours and carries out work using personal methods and tools, rather than a clerk whose employer fixes hours and gives ongoing instructions.[4][15][34] The fact that he worked 55 hours each week cuts the other way to a limited extent: regular, sustained work can support an inference of an ongoing relationship, though weekly hours alone do not reveal whether the company required that commitment or whether the courier chose it.[9]

The courier’s personal vehicle also favors contractor status because supplying one’s own tools or instrumentalities is evidence that the worker bears a separate business expense and retains some independence. But it is only evidentiary, not conclusive. The Restatement expressly treats a worker’s own tools as one factor, while recognizing that status depends on the overall relation.[4][9] Reported delivery-driver authorities likewise show that personal-vehicle ownership may coexist with employee status where the engaging company controls operational details.[35][36]

The contractor-status evidence considered in *Lawson* included limited platform control over scheduling, vehicle selection and condition, appearance, training, and whether others accompanied or substituted for the courier.[95]

The company’s branded gear points toward employee status, although with limited force standing alone. Branded gear can make a courier appear to customers as part of a company’s delivery operation, but its classification weight depends on whether the company controlled its use and on the overall working arrangement.[9][37] Cases involving delivery services have treated company control over uniforms, logos, and appearance as relevant to worker classification.[35][38][36] Conversely, branded clothing can also be a limited identification requirement imposed on a contractor, as illustrated by the owner-operator case in which a driver displayed the carrier’s placard but remained an independent contractor on the overall facts.[37]

The strongest control evidence is the app’s mandated routes. A company that directs the route by which an order must be completed goes beyond specifying the result—food delivered to the customer—and directs a material means of physical performance. Common-law control analysis distinguishes a principal who only inspects whether a specified output was achieved from one that directs inputs and work details.[5][7][2] Route control is particularly pertinent in a motor-vehicle delivery case because it bears directly on how the courier travels while carrying out the assigned work.[37]

In *Young v. NEA Delivery*, route and delivery-sequencing requirements, delivery technology, dispatch communications, uniforms, and termination leverage allowed a jury to find Amazon vicariously liable.[96][97]

*Cornejo* denied summary judgment where technological route planning, quotas, and control of driver conduct supplied evidence of platform control.[96][97]

The company’s lack of pricing discretion likewise supports employee status. A courier who cannot set delivery prices does not control a central commercial term of the service. Platform control of prices or pay is a recognized indicator of control in several classification frameworks, although its weight varies by jurisdiction and legal context.[32][39][40] In the platform setting, company control over task allocation and pricing can coexist with nominal schedule flexibility; that combination is consistent with algorithmic management, meaning management through app-based instructions, monitoring, and consequences rather than a conventional supervisor.[40][41]

The acceptance-related suspension rule is more significant than a mere incentive. The company automatically suspended any courier who declined more than two consecutive offers, and it sent automated warnings that acceptance rates affected account standing. A power to deprive a courier of app access for refusal of assigned work is evidence of discipline and of a practical right to direct the conditions under which work can continue. The right to dismiss is a listed employee-status consideration, and technological supervision and discipline are relevant forms of control even when administered through software.[5][39][42]

In *Lawson*, a courier’s formal right to reject work did not defeat an inference of control where Grubhub terminated him for failing to remain available during work blocks he had selected himself.[98]

*Lawson* also described Grubhub’s use of scheduled blocks to monitor courier availability and meet anticipated demand.[98]

The relevant distinction is between an app that leaves the worker genuinely free to accept or reject work without consequence and one that conditions continued access on acceptance behavior. ## Scope of employment

### Did rushing to complete an order

Judgment — Scope of employment asks whether the worker was doing the employer’s business when the tort allegedly occurred, rather than pursuing a substantially personal venture. It matters separately from worker classification: respondeat superior can attach only if the courier is ultimately found to be an employee, but the collision-causing driving must also fall within the work assigned or controlled.

The Restatement formulations point in the same practical direction while stating the test differently.[48] Under Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228, conduct is within scope only if it is the kind of work the servant was employed to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is motivated at least partly by a purpose to serve the master; conduct outside those limits, different in kind, or insufficiently employer-directed falls outside scope.[4] Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(2) states that an employee acts within scope while performing employer-assigned work or a course of conduct subject to employer control, but excludes an independent course of conduct not intended to serve any employer purpose.[3] Colorado’s pattern instruction similarly includes work assigned by the employer and work proper, usual, necessary, or customary to accomplish assigned work.[5]

The June 8, 2024 driving was, on the stated facts, undertaken while the courier was rushing to complete an order and was three deliveries behind quota.[4][5] Delivering orders is the enterprise for which the courier was engaged; driving while attempting to complete the order is therefore conduct of the general kind of delivery work, not conduct different in kind from the assigned task.[4][5] Under the Restatement and Colorado formulations, the fact that the courier allegedly rushed does not transform the delivery into a personal undertaking.[4][5]

The legally important distinction is between the authorized delivery objective and the allegedly unsafe method used to achieve it. Colorado’s instruction states that, for negligence, the question is whether the act furthered the employer’s business, not whether the employer authorized the particular manner of performance.[5] The Utah Supreme Court’s Birkner formulation likewise treats acts closely connected with assigned work and reasonably incidental to it as within scope even when they are improper methods of accomplishing employment objectives.[74][23] A delivery driver speeding to meet a company deadline is an illustrative example of conduct serving an employer-directed purpose, although that example does not determine whether this courier drove negligently.[21]

The quota backlog supports an inference that the courier was acting at least partly to advance the platform’s delivery operation. A purpose to retain account standing, earn more money, or avoid the consequences of delay can coexist with an employer-serving purpose; the scope test requires a purpose to serve the employer at least in part, not an exclusively altruistic motive.[4][23][21] Christensen v.

Swenson treats efforts to save time and return promptly to assigned work as relevant to whether conduct was motivated partly by the employer’s interest, because the employer’s scheduling arrangement put a premium on speed and efficiency.[23] Here, rushing while three deliveries behind quota is comparably connected to completing the platform’s delivery business, rather than to a disclosed personal errand.[3][5][74]

The spatial and temporal elements remain fact-sensitive on the stated facts.[4][5][23] A delivery driver’s ordinary workplace is necessarily mobile, and a collision on a public road during an active delivery is not outside scope merely because it occurred away from company premises.

The Utah Supreme Court rejected a bright-line rule that off-premises conduct necessarily lies outside the ordinary spatial boundaries of employment and required a flexible, fact-sensitive application of the test.[23] That reasoning is especially pertinent where the work itself requires travel on public roads to complete the assigned service.[4][5][23]

The Restatement (Second) § 229 considerations include whether the employer supplied the instrumentality, as well as the time, place, purpose, expectedness, similarity to authorized conduct, and extent of departure from the normal method; use of a personally owned vehicle may weigh against scope on one factor, but it does not negate the delivery purpose or convert ongoing delivery work into a personal frolic.[4][21] Warner Trucking, Inc.

v. Carolina Casualty Insurance Co. is cited for the proposition that a master may be liable for a driver’s actions even when the driver violated a company rule against driving after consuming alcohol; safety-rule violation therefore does not automatically make the conduct an abandonment of assigned work.[74][23]

A “frolic” is a substantial departure from employment for purely personal reasons, while a “detour” is a minor temporary departure that ordinarily does not sever the employment connection.[21] Colorado states the same dividing line: conduct is outside scope when the agent substantially departs from the principal’s business to accomplish an independent and unrelated purpose, even if the departure is brief.[5] No fact presented identifies such a personal objective at the moment of the collision; the operative fact is instead that the courier was rushing to fulfill an order.[3][5]

An example of conduct disconnected from delivery work is an off-duty driver searching for a girlfriend after leaving a bar.[100]

The authorities demonstrate why the issue remains fact-sensitive rather than mechanically resolved. In the earlier Christensen appellate decision, the Utah Court of Appeals held a guard’s trip from an assigned workplace to obtain soup outside the workplace’s spatial boundaries and affirmed summary judgment for the employer.[22] The Utah Supreme Court later held, on the same basic lunch-trip collision, that reasonable minds could differ on all three scope criteria and rejected a categorical off-premises rule.[23] The contrast means that time, location, connection to assigned work, and mixed purpose must be assessed together.

The pedestrian must still prove negligent driving and causation. A collision and a fractured ankle establish that an accident and injury occurred, but they do not themselves establish breach of reasonable care, factual causation, or legal causation; driving in the course of employment, due care in vehicle operation, and proximate cause are predominantly factual issues.[19] This scope analysis therefore does not resolve the negligence and causation questions addressed in **Negligence, causation, and proof**.

**On the stated facts, rushing to complete an order while three deliveries behind quota is closer to an improper method of performing an authorized delivery than to a frolic, so—if the courier is found to be an employee—the company has a weak scope-only basis for judgment as a matter of law.[4][5][74][23]** That conclusion leaves room for a factfinder to assess any evidence of a substantial personal departure, but the described backlog and active delivery strongly support a finding that the courier remained at least partly engaged in the company’s business.[4][5][74][23]

The Restatement of Employment Law distinguishes § 4.02, Employer Direct Liability: Conduct of Employer or Controlling Owner, from § 4.03, Employer Liability for Conduct of Employees or Agents. Section 4.03 covers authorized or ratified conduct of an employee and conduct in the course of employment; the present issue is whether the driving that allegedly injured the pedestrian was in the course of the courier’s delivery work.[46]

The delivery task itself ordinarily required travel from the restaurant to the customer. A courier guide instructs the courier to check the order number before leaving the restaurant and heading to the customer’s location.[75] On the stated facts, the courier was driving to complete an active order, which supports the conclusion that the travel was the general kind of activity the courier was engaged to perform rather than a wholly personal endeavor.[75][48][76]

The courier’s three-delivery backlog is also consistent with the operational structure of on-demand food delivery. The authors of the 2026 on-demand-food-delivery study state that couriers’ tasks are often batched into delivery waves; they classify consecutive orders as part of the same wave when the later order is accepted before the earlier order is delivered.[77] The study finds that delays can carry over to later tasks in a delivery wave and identifies both a prior delay and its duration as significant causal drivers of subsequent delays.[77] It further states that peak demand can increase order volume, intensify couriers’ delivery burden, and lead to delayed deliveries.[77] Those findings reinforce the inference that a courier seeking to catch up on outstanding deliveries remains engaged in the delivery enterprise.[77]

Deadline and incentive pressure can supply an employer-serving reason to hurry without making the unsafe manner of driving authorized. Human Rights Watch reported in 2025 that cash-bonus schemes are designed to induce workers to drive or deliver more often during peak periods or in high-demand areas, and described Uber Surges as earnings multipliers for completed rides in high-demand areas and Quests as bonuses for a specified number of rides within a time period.[43] The same report recounted a driver’s statement that looming Quest deadlines led him to remain on the road longer and accept additional rides.[43] Delivery scholarship likewise identifies a rush to fulfill orders as a safety risk factor and states that gig workers seek to complete as many orders as possible to receive company incentives.[78] The authors of the on-demand-food-delivery study state that couriers face penalties, intense pressure, and incentives for unsafe riding, and that decoupling earnings from tight deadlines decreases incentives for risky behavior such as speeding.[77] In this case, the stipulated surge-pricing period, quota backlog, and account-standing warnings support the conclusion that catching up served the delivery operation as well as the courier’s own earnings and account interests.[43][77][78]

Christensen v. Swenson supplies a closely relevant common-law formulation. The Utah Supreme Court stated that conduct is within scope when it is closely connected with assigned work and reasonably incidental to it, including conduct that is an improper method of carrying out employment objectives.[76] Its first Birkner criterion asks whether the worker was about the employer’s business and assigned duties rather than wholly pursuing a personal endeavor; its second asks whether the conduct occurred substantially within the hours and ordinary spatial boundaries of employment.[76] The court rejected a rule that conduct beyond the employer’s premises necessarily lies outside scope and required flexibility where the work relationship extends beyond a fixed workplace.[76] For a courier whose assigned task is transporting food to customers by road, a collision during an active delivery strongly satisfies those work-connection considerations.[75][76]

Christensen also holds that scope is ordinarily a jury question whenever reasonable minds may differ as to whether the worker was wholly or partly performing the employer’s business; a court may resolve it as a matter of law only when reasonable minds cannot differ.[76] There, the court held summary judgment inappropriate after concluding that reasonable minds could differ on all three scope criteria when a guard collided with a motorcycle while returning from a short off-premises soup trip during a permitted break.[76] The active-delivery facts here provide a substantially more direct connection to assigned work than the break-related trip in Christensen.[75][76]

The pedestrian bears the burden to prove that the courier acted within the scope of employment at the time of the collision; proof that the courier was employed by the company, without connecting the collision-causing conduct to the job, is insufficient.[48]

Justice Howe’s concurrence in Christensen identified a recognized lunch-break exception in which a paid worker, for the employer’s convenience, hurries out for food and hurries back under work pressure; it described the quick trip as an effort to conserve the employer’s time and considered the exception applicable although Christensen was not a workers’ compensation case.[76]

The acceptance warnings and delivery quota may explain the courier’s incentive to hurry, but they do not prove that the company instructed him to drive unsafely.[5]

If the company moves for summary judgment, the evidence that the courier was actively trying to complete an overdue delivery permits a reasonable factfinder to find in-scope conduct, so the company should not obtain judgment on the scope element as a matter of law. If the pedestrian moves for summary judgment, judgment should also be denied because employee status, the degree of company control, and the courier’s underlying negligence and causation remain disputed.

It matters chiefly because an employer-serving purpose helps show that conduct was within the *scope of employment*—that is, sufficiently connected to assigned work to attribute a worker’s tort to the enterprise. A delivery completed during a high-demand period can advance a platform’s commercial operation even where the courier also pursues personal earnings or account preservation.

## Negligence, causation, and proof

### Does the collision and the pedestrian’s injury establish the courier’s

Judgment — No. A vehicle-pedestrian collision and a fractured ankle requiring surgery establish that an accident occurred and that the pedestrian suffered physical harm. They do not themselves establish the courier’s negligence—the breach of a duty to use reasonable care—or the causal link between a proven breach and the injury. That distinction matters because the company’s respondeat superior liability, if any, is derivative: it depends first on the courier’s own tort liability.

A prima facie negligence claim ordinarily requires four elements: duty, breach, causation, and injury. Duty is the obligation to use reasonable care; breach is conduct falling below that standard; causation connects that wrongful conduct to the injury; and injury supplies damages. The pedestrian’s fracture addresses injury, but recovery still requires proof of the other elements.[85][86] Thus, the collision supplies a factual setting for a negligence claim, not a completed showing that the courier drove negligently.

The pedestrian bears the ordinary preponderance burden: the evidence must make it more likely than not that the defendant’s wrongful conduct caused the injuries.[87] In a road-collision case, breach may be shown by evidence such as excessive speed, failure to yield, distraction, unsafe turning, disregard of a traffic-control device, or another departure from reasonable driving care.

**Rushing to complete an overdue delivery warrants examination of the courier’s driving, but, without evidence of the collision mechanics or a specific unreasonable driving act or omission, it neither establishes breach nor makes breach a triable issue.** The fact that an accident occurred while a vehicle was involved does not alone establish that a particular unsafe act caused it; the cited illustration makes that point even where a car was speeding and an accident of the general type that speed rules seek to prevent occurred.[85][19][88]

Factual cause, also called cause in fact, asks a counterfactual question: would the injury have occurred absent the alleged tortious conduct? Under the Restatement formulation, conduct is a factual cause when the harm would not have occurred absent that conduct.[89] Applied here, the relevant inquiry is whether a specific unreasonable manner of driving by the courier made a difference to the collision and the ankle fracture, rather than merely whether the courier’s vehicle contacted the pedestrian.

The collision and surgery are consistent with factual causation if negligent driving is proved, but proof of contact and harm alone does not answer the counterfactual inquiry.[86][88]

Legal or proximate cause is distinct from factual cause. Section 29 of the Third Restatement treats the inquiry as “scope of liability”: liability is limited to harms resulting from the risks that made the actor’s conduct tortious, and the approach includes intervening-cause, superseding-cause, and trivial-contribution issues.[86][90] A fractured ankle resulting from a vehicle striking a pedestrian would ordinarily be the kind of physical harm associated with negligent vehicle operation, assuming the fact-finder finds an unsafe driving act caused the impact.

But the applicable jurisdiction may articulate this element through proximate cause, foreseeability, substantial factor, or a related formulation, and no governing jurisdiction is specified. The legal inquiry remains separate even after factual causation is established.[86][88]

The facts that the courier was rushing to complete an order and was behind delivery quota have evidentiary significance, but not automatic dispositive force. They may support a fact-finder’s inference that time pressure contributed to unsafe driving if coupled with evidence of the courier’s actual driving conduct, such as witness testimony, video, vehicle data, a traffic citation, crash reconstruction, or admissions. A fact-finder may infer causation where proven negligent conduct increased the risk of the very type of accident that occurred; the allegedly negligent actor may respond with evidence that the conduct was not a but-for cause.[88] The pressure evidence may direct attention to whether the courier drove unreasonably, but it is not proof of a particular breach or proof that a negligent driving act caused the impact.[89][19]

A close temporal and spatial connection between the vehicle impact and the fractured ankle may permit a fact-finder to infer from ordinary experience that the impact caused the fracture; the Restatement uses an automobile collision followed by a broken limb as an example in which other causal explanations can readily be ruled out. That injury-causation inference remains distinct from the unproved proposition that negligent driving caused the impact.[91][89][90] Traumatic-injury causation does not require epidemiologic evidence because the mechanisms by which trauma produces injury are reasonably understood.[91] Causation can involve both the actual production of harm and the legal attribution of that harm, and courts treat both as fact-sensitive inquiries.[87][88] Whether a driver exercised due care and whether the driver proximately caused the plaintiff’s injuries are factual issues ordinarily for the jury; causation is normally left to the jury unless reasonable minds cannot differ.[19][91] A court may nonetheless resolve causation on summary judgment where the evidence permits no reasonable finding that the alleged wrongdoing made a difference, as the Oregon Supreme Court did where undisputed testimony showed that a missing warning would not have altered the plaintiffs’ exposure to danger.[19][88]

Respondeat superior does not alter this sequence. It is vicarious liability: an employer may be liable for an employee’s negligence because of their relationship even though the employer did not itself breach a duty or cause the harm. But the plaintiff must prove the employee’s negligence and the relationship-and-scope elements supporting employer liability.[4] Colorado’s model instruction likewise requires proof, by a preponderance, of the negligence claim against the alleged employee, employee status, and action within the scope of employment; it describes employer liability as derivative and requiring employee culpability.[5] The employee-status, scope-of-employment, and company-benefit issues are addressed in the preceding sections.

**Accordingly, the collision and ankle injury establish an accident and damages, while the courier’s breach, factual cause, and legal cause remain necessary predicates to both the courier’s personal liability and any derivative liability of the company.**[85][89][90][4]

The 2014 Kansas motor-vehicle accident-report coding manual separates accident reporting from fault analysis. It defines a pedestrian accident as a collision between a pedestrian and at least one vehicle in transport, requires reporting of pedestrian injury severity, and treats an injury accident as a reportable classification when there is no fatal injury and at least one listed injury-severity code.[56] Its traffic-unit numbering is not concerned with fault; fault is to be indicated after investigation in the Contributing Circumstances and Violations sections.[56] The manual identifies contributing-circumstance information as “the main element relied upon to assess the cause(s) of accidents stated in Kansas Statute 8-1612.” It directs that contributing circumstances reflect probable causes indicated by available verbal or physical evidence rather than speculation, and that reports record pre-crash movements, directions, a scene diagram, officer observations, event descriptions, witness statements, and additional information.[56] Those are forms of evidence from which breach and causation may be proved in this case.

If the evidence proves that the courier violated an applicable safety law—such as a speed limit, traffic signal, or duty to yield—and that violation caused the pedestrian’s injury, the jurisdiction’s negligence-per-se doctrine may treat the violation as presumptive negligence.[92] The present facts identify no such violation.[92]

Surgery-related medical expenses may form part of the damages claimed, while duty, breach, actual causation, and proximate causation remain separate elements of negligence.[92][86]

## Summary judgment determination

### Is the company vicariously liable for the pedestrian’s injuries on

Judgment — Deny the pedestrian’s motion insofar as it seeks judgment conclusively imposing vicarious liability: employee status, breach, and causation remain unresolved on the stated record.[26][19] Deny a company motion resting only on the independent-contractor label or an asserted absence of scope: the courier was completing an assigned delivery on the app-mandated route while behind quota, conduct that is delivery work and that furthered the company’s business even if the hurried driving was improper.[5][23][21] Decide a properly supported company motion identifying an absence of admissible evidence of the courier’s negligent driving or causation under the applicable burden rule; a movant may meet its initial burden by pointing out the absence of evidence supporting an element on which the pedestrian bears the burden.[27] *Respondeat superior* is vicarious liability: it makes an employer answer for an employee’s tort when the employee acted within the scope of employment—meaning while doing job-connected work—not because the employer itself necessarily drove negligently. The contested questions are whether the courier was an employee rather than an independent contractor, and whether the courier negligently caused the pedestrian’s injury.

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56’s familiar formulation, summary judgment is available only if the movant shows both that no genuine dispute exists as to any material fact and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A fact is material when it could affect the outcome under the governing substantive law; a dispute is genuine when a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmovant. The court does not decide whose evidence is true, weigh competing evidence, or make credibility determinations. It credits the nonmovant’s evidence and draws justifiable inferences in that party’s favor.[26][93][94]

The controlling substantive law has not been identified. The Restatement (Third) rule provides that an employer is vicariously liable for a tort committed by its employee acting within the scope of employment. The traditional formulation similarly requires a master-servant relationship and negligent conduct within the scope of that relationship. The pedestrian must therefore prove the courier’s underlying negligence as well as the relationship and scope elements; respondeat superior is *derivative liability*, meaning the company’s liability depends on culpable conduct by the courier.[3][4][5][7]

The employee-status issue alone prevents a categorical ruling for the company on the facts described. As addressed in **Employee Status Under the Control Test**, the relevant common-law question is the degree and character of the platform’s actual or retained control over the manner and means of delivery, not the agreement’s independent-contractor label. The app’s required routes, lack of pricing discretion, company-branded gear, acceptance-rate warnings, and automatic suspension after more than two consecutive declined offers support an inference of operational control. The company’s contrary evidence—self-selected scheduling, the courier’s 55-hour workweek, personal vehicle, contractor agreement, personal-insurance obligation, lapsed policy, and the courier’s direct payment of his son—supports an inference that the courier retained meaningful independence. The contract label, insurance clause, insurance lapse, and use of a helper are factors, not conclusive status determinations.[7][2][21][15]

The insurance lapse bears most directly on contractual compliance and insurance coverage. It may support the company’s position that the courier bore vehicle-related operating risks, but it does not resolve whether the company controlled the manner and means of delivery. Nor does the lapse resolve whether the courier breached a duty of reasonable care in the collision or caused the fractured ankle. The same applies to the courier’s occasional use of his teenage son: direct payment by the courier supports a finding of some business autonomy, while the assignment of deliveries, route control, and account discipline may support the contrary finding that the platform controlled the central delivery operation.[4][5][7][2]

The lost safety-training records and the onboarding checklist also create an evidentiary dispute rather than a dispositive answer. The checklist is evidence from which a factfinder could infer that onboarding included the stated safety protocols. The missing training records permit competing inferences about whether training occurred, what it covered, and how the platform administered its program. If the party opposing summary judgment makes the required showing that essential evidence is unavailable for specified reasons, Rule 56 permits the court to defer or deny the motion, allow discovery, or enter another suitable order.[94]

**Accordingly, the control evidence permits reasonable competing findings on employee status, making status a material fact issue unless the governing jurisdiction adopts a rule that renders one set of undisputed facts legally dispositive.** In *O’Connor v. Uber Technologies, Inc.* (March 11, 2015), a California federal court held that drivers who performed services for Uber’s benefit were “presumptive employees,” while holding that their ultimate classification under California law was a mixed question of law and fact typically for a jury. The court denied Uber’s summary-judgment motion because disputed facts included Uber’s control over the manner and means of drivers’ performance and the Borello test did not yield an unambiguous result on that record. Uber could obtain summary judgment only if all material facts and inferences were undisputed and a reasonable jury could reach only the conclusion that the drivers were independent contractors.[15][45][31]

If a factfinder finds employee status, the evidence described in **Scope of Employment** strongly supports submission of scope to the factfinder. Under the Restatement (Second) approach, conduct falls within scope when it is of the kind the worker was employed to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is actuated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer. The Restatement (Third) formulation asks whether the employee was performing assigned work or conduct subject to the employer’s control.[4][21][20]

Here, the courier was actively rushing to complete an assigned order, was three deliveries behind quota, and faced automated acceptance-rate warnings affecting account standing. Driving to complete food deliveries is the general type of work the courier was engaged to do. A public road is also the ordinary place through which a delivery courier performs that work, unlike a purely personal excursion. The app-mandated route supports a finding that the courier remained within the spatial and operational boundaries of the delivery task.[4][5][23]

The hurried manner of driving does not necessarily remove the conduct from scope. For ambiguous conduct not expressly authorized, Restatement (Second) § 229 factors include whether workers commonly perform the act, its time, place, and purpose, whether the employer had reason to expect it, how far it departed from the usual method of work, and whether the employer supplied the instrumentality causing harm.[21] The Restatement-based rule encompasses conduct closely connected with assigned work and fairly incidental to it, even where the employee uses an improper method to accomplish the employer’s objective.

Colorado’s formulation likewise asks whether a negligent act furthered the employer’s business, rather than whether the employer authorized the precise manner of performance. A delivery driver speeding to meet a company deadline is a recognized illustration of conduct serving the employer’s purpose.[5][23][21]

The company can fairly argue that any dangerous rushing was a personal choice and an impermissible departure from safe driving. That argument would be stronger if the evidence showed a substantial detour for a purely personal purpose, commonly called a *frolic*, rather than a minor deviation or *detour* from work. The stated facts instead place the courier on an active delivery route while attempting to catch up on assigned work.[21]

The company’s business benefit is correspondingly material. Completing food orders during a surge-pricing period can generate platform revenue, maintain customer service, and reduce the delivery backlog. Meeting delivery targets can also advance the platform’s operational objectives. The benefit need not be exclusive: under the Birkner/Christensen formulation, conduct qualifies when it is motivated at least partly by serving the employer’s interest. The Utah Supreme Court treated efforts to save time under work pressure as evidence from which a jury could infer an employer-serving purpose, even though the employee also received a personal benefit.[5][23]

On the stated facts, the courier’s rush to complete the assigned delivery was within the scope of delivery work if the courier was an employee. He was performing assigned work on the mandated route, not undertaking a personal excursion; completing the overdue surge-period order and reducing the delivery backlog could serve the company’s interests. An unsafe method of carrying out that assigned work does not remove the conduct from scope.[4][5][23][21] This conclusion does not entitle the pedestrian to judgment because employee status, breach, and causation remain necessary elements of derivative liability.[4][5]

The differing outcomes in the Christensen litigation demonstrate why a court should not use a rigid location rule. The security guard took a permitted short break, intended to return to her post, and collided with the plaintiffs’ motorcycle while returning from the nearby cafe.[76] The Utah Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment after treating her off-premises lunch trip as outside the ordinary spatial boundaries of her assigned worksite.

The Utah Supreme Court reversed, rejecting a bright-line premises rule and holding that reasonable minds could differ about all three scope factors.[22][23] The courier’s position is stronger on the spatial factor because travel on public roads is inherent in food delivery rather than incidental to a fixed-site job.

The collision and surgically treated fractured ankle prove that an accident occurred and that the pedestrian suffered a serious injury. They do not themselves decide breach—whether the courier failed to use reasonable care—or causation—whether that breach factually and legally caused the ankle injury. Those are required components of the underlying negligence claim and are ordinarily factual questions in vehicle-collision litigation.[4][5][19]

That distinction matters because the company’s liability is derivative. If the courier is found not negligent, the company has no respondeat-superior liability even if a jury also finds that he was an employee acting within the scope of employment. Conversely, if the courier is found negligent, employee status and scope determine whether the company must answer for that tort. No separate cause-in-fact inquiry is ordinarily required as to the employer once vicarious liability applies; the tortfeasor and vicariously liable employer are treated as a unit for that purpose.[5][87]

The governing jurisdiction may also matter if the courier is ultimately classified as an independent contractor but an agent. Colorado materials identify an unresolved split: *Daly* ordinarily rejected principal liability for physical injuries caused by a nonservant agent, whereas *Cheney* imposed vicarious liability for negligent driving without regard to independent-contractor status. The Colorado Supreme Court had not squarely resolved that issue when the judicial-instruction committee described the split.[5] This disagreement reinforces that employee classification is central under the ordinary respondeat-superior rule, while independent-contractor status is not necessarily an absolute answer in every jurisdiction or under every agency theory.[7][2]

Conclusion and recommended disposition: The pedestrian’s strongest case is that route mandates, price control, branded presentation, acceptance-related discipline, and quota pressure permit a jury to find both employee control and a delivery-related purpose to serve the company. The company’s strongest case is that self-set hours, personal vehicle ownership, personal insurance responsibility, contractor language, and the courier’s use and payment of an assistant preserve sufficient operational independence to establish contractor status. Neither side’s evidence eliminates the other’s reasonable inferences.[7][2][5][23]

1. **Deny a company motion for summary judgment** if the pedestrian offers admissible evidence supporting reasonable inferences of employee control, in-scope delivery conduct, negligent driving, and causation. [26][19][94]

2. **Deny a pedestrian motion for summary judgment** unless the company lacks evidence creating a genuine dispute on employee status, scope, breach, or causation. [26][19]

3. **Submit the factual questions to the factfinder**: the platform’s right to control delivery methods, the courier’s purpose and connection to the assigned delivery, the courier’s negligence, and causal responsibility for the pedestrian’s fractured ankle. [19][23]

Uber’s December 31, 2025 disclosure identifies legal uncertainty over liability for the actions of platform users and third parties, including uncertainty created by local law and the absence of clear precedent; it also reports personal-injury litigation and misclassification claims in multiple United States jurisdictions. Those disclosures confirm that classification and platform-liability questions arise in litigation, but they establish no fact about this courier, this collision, or the governing jurisdiction’s rule.[44]

The pedestrian bears the burden of connecting the harmful driving to the courier’s job by proving scope of employment; proof that the courier was an employee alone is insufficient. Scope is for the factfinder where the underlying facts are disputed or reasonably support more than one interpretation, while a court may decide it as a matter of law only when undisputed facts point clearly in one direction.[48][76]

The reported broker cases further show that summary-judgment outcomes may differ by claim and relationship evidence. In *McClaine v. McCloud* (2008), the driver received all dispatch instructions from the broker and had contact with the motor carrier only to collect his weekly paycheck, yet the court dismissed the vicarious-liability/agency claim against the broker at summary judgment. In *Schramm* (2004), a negligent-hiring claim survived summary judgment despite “thin” evidence; in *Jones* (2008), summary judgment was granted on agency and negligent-entrustment claims but denied on negligent hiring.[13]

Employee status is the asserted link between a worker’s relationship with a business and a third party’s effort to impose vicarious liability for the worker’s conduct. That link remains contested here because the stated evidence supports competing control inferences.[53][7][2]

The onboarding checklist does not prove that the courier actually completed the protocols, what the training contained, whether it was adequate, or whether any training deficiency caused this collision. Loss during a system migration does not itself create an adverse inference; training evidence bears more directly on a separate negligent-training or supervision theory than on respondeat superior.

### Open questions

Open issues include the governing jurisdiction’s respondeat-superior test, whether the courier drove negligently and caused the pedestrian’s injury, the actual extent and enforceability of the platform’s routing and discipline controls, and whether the courier in fact completed and received the safety training identified on the onboarding checklist. Those matters would determine whether a jury may impose vicarious liability and whether either party can obtain judgment as a matter of law.
