## Compare for Ti-6Al-4V parts

For Ti-6Al-4V, the purchase decision is not a contest between headline spindle-torque figures. The NLX 2500SY is a Y-axis turning center with a live-tool turret and counterspindle; the INTEGREX i-400S combines a B-axis milling spindle with the S-configuration second turning spindle; and the MULTUS U4000 is a B-axis milling platform for which subspindle capability depends on the selected build.[3][8][7][9] Titanium places high and fluctuating cutting loads into the spindle, machine structure, toolholder, workholding, coolant system, and cutting edge; it also retains heat at the cut, so chip control and coolant delivery directly affect tool life, surface integrity, and process stability.

The shop should therefore compare each offered machine by its exact spindle, turret/head, coolant, control, feedback, and data-connectivity configuration—not by its family name. The ±0.0005 in. requirement equals ±12.7 µm, or a 25.4 µm total tolerance band.

**Claim.** The NLX 2500SY, INTEGREX i-400S, and MULTUS U4000 can all be configured as credible turn-mill platforms for aerospace titanium. The Mazak has the highest published main-spindle peak-torque figure in this comparison, 1,400 N·m at 25% ED; the U4000 has a documented standard-spindle rating of 700/477 N·m at 30-minute/continuous duty; and the quoted NLX 2500SY|700 has a main-spindle rating of 599/505/409 N·m at 25%/50%/continuous duty. #### Ti-6Al-4V tooling and coolant on the NLX

Judgment — The baseline Ti-6Al-4V tooling route for all three machines should be carbide, not ceramic: tough uncoated or appropriate PVD-coated carbide grades for turning, sharp positive-rake cutting geometry, secure anti-pullout milling holders, internal coolant delivery, and chip-control geometry suited to the feature. Sandvik identifies H13A as an uncoated carbide grade combining abrasive-wear resistance and toughness for medium-to-rough turning of titanium alloys, and identifies titanium alloys as a typical application for uncoated cemented carbide.[42][43]

Sandvik’s titanium guidance is direct on ceramics: titanium has low machinability and, unlike nickel alloys, ceramic cannot be applied. Ceramics also have general limitations in thermal-shock resistance and fracture toughness. This matters particularly for aerospace Ti-6Al-4V because interrupted cuts, unstable walls, variable engagement, coolant interruption, and chip recutting raise the risk of brittle cutting-edge failure.[44][43]

The result is not that every non-carbide tool is prohibited. PCD with flood coolant can be used for titanium super-finishing, according to Sandvik, but that is a narrow finishing application rather than a general roughing or semi-finishing solution. The shop should not approve ceramic tools as the default titanium route on the NLX, Mazak, or Okuma.[43][44]

For titanium turning, Sandvik identifies keeping cutting temperature low as central to success and names optimized programming, insert shape, and high-pressure coolant as key factors. Sandvik also recommends abundant clean cutting fluid, filtration to 25 µm or better, coolant concentration of at least 10%, a high-pressure pump of at least 500 psi, coolant-fed tools, and aggressive chip control to prevent catastrophic chip recutting.[44][45]

The NLX documentation supports high-pressure coolant as a required procurement item rather than an assumed standard feature. One documented NLX configuration offers a maximum pump pressure of 0.8 MPa, or 116 psi, and states that it improves chip removal and tool/workpiece cooling. Another SY stock configuration lists 1.5 MPa, or 15 bar, with a 2.2 kW pump for 60 Hz operation. Those pressure figures remain below Sandvik’s cited general 500 psi titanium recommendation, so the shop should specify the desired pressure, flow, filtration, through-tool delivery, and nozzle arrangement in the request for quotation rather than treat “high-pressure coolant” as a sufficient description.[4][21][45]

Judgment — For the NLX, use a through-coolant turning holder with targeted jets at the rake and flank faces, and use internal-coolant drills and milling cutters. Select BMT live-toolholders with the shortest practical projection; use shrink-fit, hydraulic, or Safe-Lock-type retention for aggressive titanium milling, because titanium can pull end mills from holders under high load. Sandvik specifically advises keeping long-overhang modular assemblies as short and large-diameter as possible, avoiding reduction adapters where possible.[45][46]

The NLX record includes several process-support features that may reduce titanium operating risk. Easy Tool Monitor 2.0 learns load limits and can monitor turning, milling, and drilling, with limits retained for each tool and cutting edge. The product page also describes runtime monitoring for multi-channel machines, recording primary and auxiliary processing time, program pauses, and tool-change waiting time.[6]

These features are useful for monitoring overload, tool breakage, and hidden non-cut time, but they do not replace a titanium tool-life study. **The NLX decision therefore depends on buying the higher-capability coolant and thermal-feedback configuration and proving the intended carbide tooling, chip evacuation, spindle load, and surface-integrity window on representative Ti-6Al-4V stock.**

#### NLX thermal control against ±0.0005 in.

The NLX has multiple documented thermal-management features. Coolant circulation inside the castings is stated to control thermal displacement and to improve resistance to ambient-temperature changes. The spindle is described as designed to minimize thermal displacement. An optional Magnescale absolute linear measuring system has 0.01 µm resolution, and a quoted configuration uses full closed-loop X-axis feedback through a magnetic scale rather than relying on the servo pulse encoder.[13][4]

The NLX thermal-feature listing identifies MAP compensation as standard, while full closed-loop Magnescale feedback with 0.01 µm resolution and a separate coolant chiller are optional; the chiller alone does not constitute complete coolant-temperature control.[25][29][127]

The quoted full-closed-loop description is especially relevant to a tight radial or diameter-control process: DMG MORI states that the magnetic scale is not susceptible to ball-screw precision error or thermal displacement. That statement applies to X-axis position sensing on the quoted configuration; it does not remove thermal effects from the spindle, structure, fixture, tool, or workpiece.[4]

DMG MORI gives a thermal-displacement figure of 2.0 µm at 3,200 min⁻¹ and constant ambient temperature. The target ±0.0005 in. is ±12.7 µm, so the conditional published thermal displacement is numerically smaller than the tolerance band. However, its test condition is controlled speed and constant ambient temperature; a Ti-6Al-4V aerospace process adds intermittent spindle loads, coolant-temperature variation, warm-up behavior, tool wear, part deflection, fixture distortion, and possible spindle-to-spindle transfer error.[13]

The published NLX thermal-displacement example measures 2.0 µm at 3,200 min⁻¹ over a 0–9-hour test at constant ambient temperature.[25]

Judgment — Treat the ±12.7 µm requirement as a process-capability requirement. The machine needs a defined warm-up program; stabilized coolant concentration and temperature; controlled ambient conditions; documented tool offsets and wear limits; probing where geometry permits; and a capability study on the actual feature, fixture, material heat, and machining route. Axis-scale resolution and thermal compensation reduce error sources but do not certify the finished feature.

For the NLX, the purchase specification should name the desired feedback system by axis, the thermal-control options, coolant temperature management, in-machine probing or tool setting, and acceptance tests. A quoted machine’s manual tool presetter has repeatability of 0.005 mm at 2σ, while its probe specification is 0.001 mm at 2σ with a 25 mm stylus. The probe is more relevant than the manual presetter for a closed-loop correction strategy, but neither value is the finished-part tolerance.[4]

The NLX can support a controlled precision route, but **the thermal hardware makes ±0.0005 in. achievable only when the shop validates the complete machining-and-measurement process.**

That is valuable for a mixed-machine northern Mexico shop because OPC UA and MTConnect can feed manufacturing execution, historian, dashboard, or quality applications without treating the machine control as the system of record. A quoted older NLX SY configuration identifies Ethernet, RS232, USB, PCMCIA, and an EtherNet/IP interface for peripheral control signals. EtherNet/IP should not be conflated with OPC UA or MTConnect.[49][4]

AS9100D is a quality-management standard that supplements, rather than replaces, customer and regulatory requirements. It requires the shop’s quality system and customer-specific requirements to determine what records, approvals, retention periods, identification, verification, and retrieval controls apply.[50]

Judgment — OPC UA is an industrial communication framework for machine-data exchange, not a complete AS9100D part-history record.[6][26][51] For each serialized part or lot, the shop should retain and retrieve: material certification and heat/lot; traveler and operation sequence; approved NC, CAD/CAM, postprocessor, and setup revision; machine identity and control configuration; fixture and tool identity; operator and inspection authorization; tool-life or offset changes where required; process alarms and deviations; measurement results; nonconformance and rework records; and final acceptance status. The protocols help automatically capture parts of that chain, but they do not create it.

The NLX can contribute machine state, process data, spindle load, alarms, job data, and runtime information. A quoted CELOS implementation states that it can manage, document, and visualize order, process, and machine data, while JOBMANAGER supports storage of production-relevant data and documents. The i-400S spindle listing separates the 3,300 rpm main turning spindle, 4,000 rpm second turning spindle, and 12,000 rpm milling spindle; the second spindle is listed at 22/15 kW, and the milling spindle at 26/22 kW.[1]

The 1,400 N·m figure must be read correctly. It is main turning-spindle maximum torque at 25% ED. For the INTEGREX i-200, i-300, and i-400 series, the milling-spindle maximum torque is a separate 120 N·m, or 88.5 ft·lbs, figure.[1] They cannot be compared as though both represent the same spindle, speed, duration, or tool-interface condition.[1]

A used 2014 i-400S listing gives a main-spindle torque of 1,032 ft-lb for 30 minutes and a second-spindle torque of 369 ft-lb for 30 minutes. Those figures may describe that particular machine, but they use a different rating basis from the brochure’s 1,400 N·m at 25% ED. The shop should require the supplier’s torque-speed curves for the ordered main spindle, subspindle, and milling head and identify the torque available at the intended titanium cutting rpm.[19][1]

The candidate's cited Mazak torque records also give a 500 N·m main-spindle figure at 40% ED/30 minutes, versus 1,400 N·m at 25% ED; a used-machine listing gives approximately 1,400 N·m for its main spindle and 500 N·m for its second spindle. These are different stated rating and machine bases, not interchangeable continuous-torque specifications.[1][19]

**The Mazak’s principal titanium advantage is its documented 1,400 N·m peak main-spindle torque and 120 N·m milling torque, but process planning must use the applicable duty cycle and torque-speed curve rather than treating peak torque as continuous capability.**[1]

The i-400S also has a useful documented multitasking basis. It combines a 240° B-axis range, full C-axis contouring, and standard 36-tool storage; one listed i-400S-1500U milling spindle has −30° to 210° travel and a 0.0001° minimum index angle. HSK-A63 is the documented rotary-tool interface, with CAPTO C6 and KM63 options.[1][18] Individual machine listings show 72-tool or larger magazines, so magazine capacity and interface are configuration-dependent.[1][18][15][56]

For larger aerospace components, listings document up to 658 mm maximum machining diameter and roughly 1,500–1,519 mm machining length in the 1500U class. The i-400S-2500U listing reaches 2,497 mm, or 98.31 in., maximum machining length and lists a 658 mm, or 25.9 in., maximum upper-turret machining diameter. A listed automatic self-centering SMW steady rest has a 20–165 mm clamping range.[10][15][11] The customer should not compare these lengths without first deciding whether its work requires a 1500U or 2500U configuration, a steady rest, bar feed, or special workholding.[10][15][11]

The rigidity evidence is also construction-based. Mazak specifies rigid linear roller guides on all linear axes, temperature-controlled cooling oil through ball-screw cores, spindle-bearing and headstock temperature control, and a roller-gear-cam B-axis drive intended to eliminate backlash. These features directly address positioning stability, thermal drift, and B-axis cutting stiffness.[1]

The i-400S record does not state a static stiffness, dynamic stiffness, damping coefficient, or Ti-6Al-4V chatter-limit test. The shop should therefore use the stated architecture as a positive design indicator but make the decision with a representative titanium cutting demonstration—especially for deep pocketing, thin walls, long-reach milling, and interrupted turning.[1][45]

#### Ti-6Al-4V tooling and coolant on the i-400S

The same carbide-first and ceramic-exclusion logic applies to the Mazak. Sandvik says ceramics cannot be applied to titanium, and identifies low cutting temperature, insert shape, high-pressure coolant, and optimized programming as key titanium factors.[44]

The i-400S has the broadest documented coolant-pressure menu. The i-series brochure lists simultaneous through-spindle and flood-coolant options of 0.5 MPa, 1.5 MPa, 3.5 MPa, and 7 MPa, corresponding to 70, 220, 500, and 1,000 psi. This range can meet the cited general titanium recommendation of at least 500 psi if the shop orders the 3.5 MPa or 7 MPa configuration and pairs it with the required filtration, pressure control, and through-tool holders.[1][45]

A used i-400S listing includes a 70 bar, 900 L high-pressure coolant system. Another lists 213 psi through coolant. These examples demonstrate that delivered machines vary widely; 213 psi is below Sandvik’s cited 500 psi general titanium recommendation, while 70 bar is well above it. The coolant system therefore belongs in the configuration comparison, not as a generic machine-family feature.[11][19][45]

Judgment — For Ti-6Al-4V, specify the 3.5 MPa or 7 MPa Mazak coolant arrangement where the operations, tooling, seals, filtration, and chip evacuation system support it. High pressure is not valuable if it cannot reach the cutting edge consistently, if filtration damages seals and nozzles, or if the toolholder system lacks targeted internal coolant delivery.

The documented HSK-A63 interface, with CAPTO C6 and KM63 options, gives the shop a tooling choice. HSK-A63 supports high-speed milling and face/taper contact. CAPTO C6 is often advantageous for turn-mill work because of its polygonal coupling, quick-change potential, and strong bending-torque transmission. The interface should be standardized around the part mix and cutter size rather than selected solely by what a used machine happens to include.[1]

Sandvik recommends short, large-diameter, rigid modular assemblies for long-overhang titanium work and recommends avoiding reduction adapters when possible. That guidance matters for i-400S B-axis machining of deep pockets, bosses, and difficult-access aerospace features.[46]

For turning, use positive-rake carbide inserts with a tough substrate and hard, lubricious coating where the specific operation permits. Sandvik identifies built-up edge, cut-line notching, galling, and chip welding as primary titanium failure modes, and cautions that the edge should remain sharp even when a small T-land or slight hone is used for life.[45]

A TiAlN-coated CNMG120408MS PR1535 turning insert is one cited example for difficult-to-cut material; its PVD coating is described as preserving bending strength and improving coating durability.[128]

One i-400S listing includes a wireless Renishaw RMP600 probe and a motor overload detector; other listings show high-pressure coolant configurations.[11] Those features assist setup verification, tool protection, and process control but do not themselves establish a titanium-qualified process.[11]

The Mazak is the most flexible coolant-platform choice for titanium among the documented alternatives, provided the shop orders 3.5–7 MPa through-spindle coolant and qualifies carbide tooling, holders, and chip management for each feature family.

#### Mazak thermal compensation against ±0.0005 in.

Mazak’s Intelligent Thermal Shield automatically compensates for room-temperature changes. The brochure states that room-temperature changes and compensation data are displayed for the operator. Its broader thermal system includes temperature-controlled cooling oil through ball-screw cores and around spindle bearings and the headstock.[1]

The i-series brochure gives reference thermal-displacement values of 7 µm in Z, 6 µm in Y, and 8 µm in X. These figures are below the ±12.7 µm target band, but Mazak explicitly labels them as reference test results. The same brochure states that accuracy data are obtained under specific conditions and may not be replicated under different room temperatures, workpiece materials, tool materials, and cutting conditions.[1]

Mazak’s 8/6/7 µm X/Y/Z reference displacement result is stated for an 8 °C room-temperature change.[1]

This qualification is especially important for Ti-6Al-4V. A titanium component can behave differently from a steel demonstration part because heat stays close to the cutting zone, cutting forces are high, and wall deflection or residual stress can affect the measured feature. The relevant question is not whether a reference axis-displacement figure is below 12.7 µm; it is whether the completed part feature remains within tolerance after the machine, fixture, workpiece, coolant, and inspection loop have stabilized.[45]

Mazak’s i-series documentation also lists scale feedback for the B-axis and X/Y/Z axes and absolute position detection for linear axes. These options should be confirmed on the offered machine, because scale feedback can reduce sensitivity to ball-screw error and enhance control of position-dependent behavior.[1]

A 2016 SmoothX-control listing associates Intelligent Maza-Check for 5-axis calibration, Intelligent Thermal Shield, and Active Vibration Control with that control generation. Mazak’s broader Accuracy offering describes Ai Thermal Shield and Smooth Thermal Shield. These systems use spindle speed, machine temperature, machine position, coolant state, and other factors to suppress cutting-edge movement; Mazak also describes post-machining workpiece measurement and learning of thermal-displacement offset.[57][58] These functions should be confirmed for the exact i-400S control generation and option list.[58]

Smooth OMM and Smooth SET AND INSPECT are relevant where the part and probe access permit on-machine verification. Mazak says Smooth OMM can automatically update tool offsets from coordinate and workpiece measurement, determine geometric tolerances, and display graphical measurement reports. Smooth SET AND INSPECT can create inspection programs, update work coordinates and tool compensation, and provide a separate report function.[58]

Judgment — For the i-400S, configure Intelligent Thermal Shield or the applicable control-generation equivalent, ball-screw and spindle cooling, scale feedback where available, a probe, and a measurement-report path. Then validate the ±0.0005 in. feature after a representative warm-up and under normal coolant, ambient, tool-life, and material-lot variation.

The conclusion is straightforward: **the Mazak thermal package is well aligned to a tight-tolerance titanium process, but its published reference results are not a substitute for part-capability evidence.**

For connectivity, Mazak’s i-series brochure identifies MTConnect networking. Mazak also lists Operation Analysis, Tool Utilization, Maintenance Manager, and Energy Dashboard monitors. The evidence here supports MTConnect, not a confirmed OPC UA route for a specific i-400S.Judgment — If OPC UA is mandatory for the shop’s architecture, make an OPC UA server, data model, licensing, cybersecurity configuration, and acceptance test a contractual requirement. For AS9100D, the same distinction applies. Judgment — The Mazak traceability architecture should combine: machine MTConnect data; controlled NX/post/NC release; barcode or RFID traveler identification; material-lot linkage; tool identification and life records; operator and setup approval; probe/CMM results; nonconformance workflow; and an immutable retained record in the QMS or MES. #### Ti-6Al-4V tooling and coolant on the U4000

The U4000 upper-turret tooling system is listed as HSK-A63. The brochure states that commercial milling tools compliant with DIN 69893-1, HSK-A63 can be used; it also directs users to contact Okuma when using turning tools other than Okuma standard tools because some dimensions are designed for fitting accuracy.[7]

Specific U4000 listings show other interface configurations. One 2018 U4000 BB/SB-2SW listing identifies CAPTO C6 as the ATC magazine tool shank, with 80-tool storage, maximum tool mass of 10 kg, and maximum tool length of 400 mm excluding the shank. The supplier quotation must therefore identify whether the intended machine is HSK-A63, CAPTO C6, or a mixed/tooling-adapter configuration.[24]

The machine supports through coolant at the upper milling spindle, and high-pressure coolant is an optional specification. Individual listings show a 20 bar pump, a 70 bar supply pump, and a programmable 1,000 psi system with coolant-temperature-control chiller. These differences are substantial for titanium.[7][27][65][24]

Sandvik’s general titanium recommendation calls for at least 500 psi high-pressure coolant, clean fluid, filtration to 25 µm or better, coolant concentration of at least 10%, coolant-fed tools, and aggressive chip control. A 20 bar U4000 pump is about 290 psi and does not meet that cited general 500 psi threshold; a 70 bar or 1,000 psi configuration does.[45][27][65][24]

Judgment — For Ti-6Al-4V, specify the U4000 with through-spindle coolant, a high-pressure system capable of the required pressure at the tool, verified flow at the cutter, filtration compatible with live-tool and coolant-nozzle requirements, and coolant temperature regulation if the tolerance study shows coolant-temperature sensitivity. Do not buy a flood-coolant base machine expecting it to be upgraded cheaply into a titanium-production coolant system.

Velocity’s U4000 live-tooling catalogue illustrates why filtration is not merely a utility issue. It states that coolant-through live tools require 30 µm nominal and 50 µm absolute filtration, and warns that swarf can clog coolant nozzles and lines, degrade seals, and damage live-tool bearings. A separate filter example is specified at 5 µm nominal and 50 µm absolute and alarms at a 25 psi pressure differential.[66]

Judgment — The shop should reconcile tooling-supplier filtration requirements with the titanium-process filtration target and coolant-system capacity. A nominal rating, absolute rating, coolant cleanliness, pump pressure, flow, and filter-change interval should all appear in the process-control plan.

For cutters, the U4000 should use the same carbide-first strategy as the other candidates. Sandvik specifically identifies CoroMill 690 as developed for high-productivity 2D profile milling of titanium components, with secure insert seating, high-pressure internal coolant at each pocket, and a rigid cutter-insert interface. Sandvik also identifies CoroDrill 860 with -SM geometry as a robust titanium-drilling option and CoroTurn SL70 as a modular round-carbide and grooving system with high-pressure coolant.[46][44]

Ceramics should not be selected as the normal titanium route. The U4000 may be highly rigid and thermally capable, but machine rigidity does not change the titanium/ceramic limitation described by Sandvik.[44][43]

The U4000’s large envelope and optional lower turret make it attractive for large titanium forgings or prismatic/rotational parts that benefit from process consolidation. A documented case study reports that one U4000 process completed a stainless-steel valve after one clamping and reduced manual handling and work-in-process. That is not a titanium result, but it illustrates the potential value of eliminating repeated reclamping when a component can be completed in one machine.[67]

The U4000 is the strongest choice where large envelope, B-axis access, heavy continuous turning, and one-setup process consolidation outweigh the added complexity of a larger multitasking cell.

#### U4000 thermal control against ±0.0005 in.

The U4000’s key thermal technologies are TAS-C and TAS-S. TAS-C, Thermo Active Stabilizer—Construction, corrects machine-structure thermal-deformation error during shop-temperature changes. TAS-S, Thermo Active Stabilizer—Spindle, corrects milling-tool-spindle thermal deformation during spindle rotation. Okuma also says TAS-S considers spindle temperature, rotation, speed changes, and stoppage.[7][68]

Okuma’s published thermal example reports less than 10 µm thermal deformation over time. That number is smaller than the ±12.7 µm target band, but Okuma explicitly states that the data are actual data and do not represent guaranteed accuracy.[7]

The U4000 may also be configured with AbsoScale position detection on specified X and Y axes and temperature regulation for coolant and hydraulic oil. Particular listings include coolant temperature control/chiller systems and high-accuracy C-axis options.[7][24][27]

ISO 230-2 specifies a preferred 20 °C reference temperature, cites a typical minimum measurement uncertainty of 2 µm/(m·°C), and calls for agreed thermal conditions and warm-up before testing under normal operating conditions.[132]

The U4000 documentation adds an optional NC Gage system. NC Gage provides more than 20 types of geometric-tolerance and positional-dimension measurement functions, can generate measurement programs by teaching, and can store measurement-result data. This is a strong contribution to a closed-loop quality plan where probe access, cycle time, and measurement-system analysis support its use.[7]

Judgment — The U4000 can be configured for a ±0.0005 in. titanium process by combining TAS-C/TAS-S, controlled coolant temperature, warm-up, stable fixturing, on-machine probing or NC Gage where appropriate, and off-machine verification for the critical characteristics. The machine’s thermal systems correct predictable drift; they do not correct part relaxation, clamping distortion, cutter deflection, tool wear, or an unsuitable measurement method.

For parts with long tool reach, the U4000’s rigidity advantage can be consumed by toolholder compliance. Sandvik’s guidance is to keep assemblies short and rigid, choose the largest possible adapter size, and avoid reduction adapters. This is especially important on U4000 B-axis work where the machine may be stiff but the cutter assembly is not.[46]

The thermal feature set supports, rather than guarantees, the target tolerance; the deciding evidence should be a documented capability study on the real titanium feature and fixture.

## What factors drive long-term operating costs?

Long-term operating cost is not determined by the purchase price or a single energy feature. For Ti-6Al-4V aerospace work, it is driven by the cost of producing accepted parts: cutting tools and coolant, operator and programmer time, uptime, scrap avoidance, qualified process control, and the number of productive hours over which fixed ownership costs can be recovered.

**The practical comparison must use cost per productive hour and cost per accepted part, not nameplate power, calendar time, or a supplier’s efficiency claim.** A general machine-hour model divides annual depreciation, space, power, maintenance, labor, and allocated overhead by productive hours per year; a machine that is scheduled but waiting for material, setup approval, tools, inspection, or repair does not recover those costs.[80][81]

### Recurring production costs

For all three alternatives, Ti-6Al-4V makes tooling, coolant delivery, chip control, and stable unattended execution more economically important than they would be on aluminum or ordinary steel work. Titanium retains heat at the cutting edge, can work-harden locally if the cut rubs, and turns poor tool-life control into high insert, end-mill, scrap, and rework cost. Sandvik Coromant states that significant deviation from titanium’s suitable feed-and-speed range can produce melted or broken tools and expensive scrap; it recommends a positive-rake tool with a tough substrate and hard, lubricious coating for longer tool life.[45] Correct grade selection supports longer life, finish, and productivity; the wrong grade increases premature wear and cost.[82]

Judgment — For these machines, establish a titanium tooling baseline by operation rather than buying tools by machine brand: coated-carbide turning inserts for roughing and finishing, positive-rake geometries where edge strength permits, rigid boring bars with minimum practical overhang, solid-carbide or indexable carbide milling tools selected for engagement and reach, and through-tool coolant wherever the operation and toolholder permit. Ceramics are not the default titanium roughing choice: their brittleness, sensitivity to interruption and vibration, and the extreme heat generated at high cutting speeds make them a specialized solution that requires a stable continuous cut, proven parameters, containment, and disciplined chip control. Their apparent cycle-time advantage can be erased by edge failure, damaged work, or scrap in a mixed-feature aerospace part.

## Clarify utilization assumptions

Fifteen thousand annual hours is not a valid per-machine utilization assumption. A 365-day year contains 8,760 hours, so one machine cannot accumulate 15,000 elapsed operating hours in one year; the proposed figure is 6,240 hours above the calendar limit.[115][75] The purchase case must therefore identify whether 15,000 means aggregate fleet hours, routed workload demand, scheduled production time, actual operating time, or productive spindle-cutting time.

If 15,000 hours refers to one machine, it could be a multi-year cumulative figure, but not annual elapsed time; the shop should also clarify whether it means good-production-equivalent hours rather than elapsed machine hours.[1][140]

**The planning rule is: state both the resource scope and the time definition before using annual hours for capacity, machine cost, or machine selection.** A fleet-capacity formula explicitly scales hours by machine count, shift length, shifts per day, operating days, and planned uptime; it is not a per-machine runtime measure.[75]

### Fleet-hour arithmetic

A continuous single-machine calendar has a theoretical ceiling of 8,760 hours per year: 24 hours per day multiplied by 365 days. This is a theoretical limit before holidays, staffing gaps, preventive maintenance, failures, setups, first-article inspection, tool changes, and changeovers.[75][109] Therefore, 15,000 annual hours cannot mean the operating time, scheduled time, or productive time of one NLX 2500SY, Integrex i-400S, or Multus U4000.

| One machine | 1 | 15,000 h | 171.2% of 8,760 h | Physically impossible. The MULTUS U4000’s combined turning, milling, drilling, and 5-axis operations support considering multi-operation consolidation, not a single machine exceeding calendar time. [102] |

The two-machine case is the physical minimum because 15,000 divided by 8,760 equals 1.712 machines, which must be rounded up to two machines. The equal-load assumption gives 7,500 hours per machine; it does not prove that aerospace work can be split evenly between unlike work centers. Capacity must be kept by work center and time period, because unused time on another machine does not relieve the constrained turning, milling, probing, or inspection operation.[116][103][117]

The three-machine case is useful for the present purchase decision because the candidate machines could form a three-machine resource group. Its 5,000 hours per machine and 57.1% calendar-time loading are only a scenario, not a forecast. Fleet-wide utilization can hide an overloaded individual asset, while capacity decisions occur at fleet, site, and individual-machine levels.[118][77]

A 15,000-hour number can legitimately be aggregate fleet hours if it represents the summed hours of two or more work centers. Fleet-capacity methods distinguish gross hours, scheduled workload, and net available capacity after maintenance and scrap/rework buffers.[119][75] The shop should therefore provisionally label the figure as **annual fleet-hour demand or capacity** rather than per-machine utilization until its scope is confirmed.

This arithmetic rules out a single-machine interpretation and makes machine count, work-center allocation, and schedule coverage explicit inputs to the investment case.

Available machine-hours are an aggregate scheduled-time measure: machine count × hours per shift × shifts per day × working days. Calcrux calls this total scheduled machine time, before efficiency or scrap is applied; its example produces 1,200 scheduled machine-hours per month from three machines, two 8-hour shifts, and 25 working days.[96] Machestra likewise calculates theoretical machine-hours as hours per shift × shifts per day × days per week, with six machines on one 8-hour shift for five days producing 240 machine-hours per week.[110]

Capacity demand must include each routed operation’s setup time and run time. Visual South states that setup time is generally static and run time is generally variable with job quantity; the total operation hours are compared with the capacity of the required resource.[120] For aerospace parts, schedule door-to-door process time—load, deburr, travel to the tool crib, inspection queue, and setup—as well as cutting cycle time.[110]

Effective capacity is not design capacity. Rockwell Automation defines design capacity as the ideal maximum output over a period and identifies product mix, anticipated maintenance, raw-material disruptions, and labor issues as influences on effective capacity; actual output is the rate achieved.[111] Calcrux defines OEE as the composite of Availability, Performance, and Quality, accounting for downtime, speed losses, and first-pass quality, and applies the efficiency rate to reduce theoretical capacity.[96]

Utilization is active operating hours divided by total available hours, multiplied by 100; the cited example is 6 active hours in an available 8-hour shift, or 75% utilization.[118] Scheduled maintenance must not be counted as a failure in MTBF: the MTBF guidance distinguishes operating hours from elapsed calendar time and says not to count planned downtime as a failure.[103]

The illustrative finite-capacity calculation deducts setup/changeover time, planned maintenance, breaks, and shift transitions from available time. It assumes setups consume 20% of available machine time, plus one maintenance hour per machine per week and 30 minutes per machine per day for breaks and shift handoffs.[110]

Efficiency compares actual setup/run time with estimated setup/run time for an operation. When actual setup and run time exceed the estimate, the extra demand requires more capacity than originally planned; on the constrained machine, that extra demand worsens the bottleneck because capacity is determined by the most constrained machine.[120][110]

With three equal-share machines and an 80% productive fraction, 15,000 productive fleet h/year requires 6,250 scheduled h per machine-year, equal to 71.3% of each machine's 8,760 calendar h/year. This is the internally consistent three-machine planning basis, before allocating the workload to the specific turning, milling, and inspection bottlenecks.[80][75]

### Scheduled versus productive time

Scheduled Machine Hours (SMH) are all time for which a machine is allocated to work. Productive Machine Hours (PMH) are the time in which it actually performs work after mechanical and non-mechanical delays are excluded; the standard utilization expression is PMH divided by SMH, multiplied by 100.[76][77] Thus, scheduling a machine for 7,500 hours does not create 7,500 hours of productive capacity.

Good-production time adjusts output for downtime, slow running, scrap, and rejected parts, making it distinct from spindle-cutting time when planning customer deliveries and cost per conforming part.[140][144]

Machine telemetry can provide still different counters. Okuma’s OSP-P500S options list hour meters for Power ON, spindle rotation, NC operating, and NC-operation monitoring; these are distinct counters rather than annual capacity definitions.[7] A used Multus U4000 listing, for example, separately displayed power-on and cutting counters of 9,000 and 2,200 hours, with no annual reporting period.[27] Do not use power-on, NC operating, in-cycle, spindle-rotating, in-cut, or cutting hours as substitutes for one another.

Machine-monitoring systems similarly distinguish in-cycle utilization, spindle-rotating utilization, and in-cut utilization. In-cycle is standard in the cited reporting system, while spindle-rotating and in-cut measures require machine-specific connectivity.[88][97] For a titanium multitasking cell, in-cycle time may include probing, tool changes, bar-feed or handling waits, and other non-cutting activity; spindle-cutting time is narrower and is more relevant to cutting-tool consumption and material-removal capacity.[124][123]

Machine-on or automatic-mode time is not necessarily productive, and a time-in-cut measure alone can miss losses caused by slow running.[145]

A monthly capacity register can use common ISO 22400 states and actual MES/CNC data to track scheduled hours, PBT, APT, cutting time, cycle performance, quality, material yield, and maintenance loss as distinct measures.[146]

Capacity planning should start with routed required hours, derived from forecast quantities and standard operation times, and compare them with capacity by resource or work center.[125] First, calculate scheduled hours for each candidate work center from hours per shift, shifts per day, operating days, and machine count.

For each part family, capacity planning should use annual good-part demand and the approved standard cycle time, add setup and verification time, account for expected good-part yield, and compare the result with usable fleet capacity after planned downtime, availability losses, and a delivery-risk reserve.[140][147][145]

Second, deduct planned maintenance, breaks, setup/changeover time, known downtime, and first-article approval allowances. Third, apply measured availability and productive-utilization factors rather than an ideal-cycle or perfect-uptime assumption.[75][122][109]

The capacity model should separately account for maintenance, calibration, coolant service, software updates, and facility outages, and distinguish ramp-up, mature-production, and post-warranty availability. It should also record operator coverage, unattended-operation policy, achieved versus standard cycle time, first-pass yield, inspection holds, and a reserve for expedites, engineering changes, qualification, tooling failure, interrupted batches, and loss of one machine.[140][147][145]

Judgment — **For aerospace titanium work, the productive-time definition should be controlled in the routing and MES rules: classify setup, prove-out, first-article inspection, probing, tool setting, tool replacement, cutting, rework, and unattended recovery consistently. Otherwise, two apparently comparable annual-hour figures will produce misleading machine-hour rates and misleading capacity claims.**

The shop must document and consistently apply how maintenance, breaks, calibration, warm-up, and changeovers map to machine states, because that mapping changes the calculated production KPIs.[145][142]

The distinction has direct cost consequences. A machine-hour rate is annual cost divided by productive hours, not merely by the hours that the building is open; the cited guide defines productive hours as available hours less downtime, changeovers, and planned maintenance.[80] If the denominator is overstated by booking scheduled hours as productive hours, the calculated hourly ownership cost is understated and the apparent capacity of each candidate machine is overstated.

A 24/7 schedule is therefore a calendar upper bound, not a productive-hours plan. A capacity model must reserve maintenance and setup time, account for losses from breakdowns and inefficiencies, and use actual work-center history where possible.[125][117][109] This establishes the usable comparison basis for the machine, tooling, thermal-control, connectivity, and service analysis in the preceding section.

**Scheduled-hours example —** Four machines operating two 8-hour shifts for 250 days provide 16,000 scheduled machine-hours per year (4 × 2 × 8 × 250), sufficient to schedule 15,000 hours. Three machines on the same schedule provide only 12,000 scheduled machine-hours per year (3 × 2 × 8 × 250). Scheduled Machine Hours are allocated work time, not cutting hours.[75][76][77][123]

Productive and idle hours are separate, non-overlapping periods for energy costing: production energy equals average production kW multiplied by productive hours, while idle energy equals average idle kW multiplied by idle hours.[89] The annual-hour definition therefore directly changes the electricity model as well as the capacity model.

The productive-hour denominator also changes the machine-hour rate. Corposol states that fixed costs, including depreciation and space, are spread over productive hours, that higher utilization lowers the rate, and that improving OEE on expensive machines lowers effective cost per hour.[90] Its worked example starts with 4,800 available hours from two 8-hour shifts over 300 working days, deducts planned maintenance, expected breakdowns, and idle time, and applies 75% historical utilization to obtain about 3,600 productive hours.[90] Corposol directs shops to use realistic expected productive hours rather than the theoretical maximum because overestimating hours makes rates too low and jobs appear more profitable than they are.[90]

Capacity utilization, efficiency, and OEE are separate measures.Rockwell Automation defines efficiency as actual output divided by effective capacity and utilization as actual output divided by design capacity.[111] JITbase defines OEE as availability × performance × quality and states that OEE changes affect capacity directly.[95]

Total Effective Equipment Performance (TEEP) equals Loading × OEE; unlike OEE alone, it incorporates how much calendar capacity is scheduled.[144]

In the cited 8-hour-shift example, 7 hours 15 minutes of planned production and 61.5 minutes of stops yielded 22,410 seconds of operating time and 85.86% availability. At a planned 60-second cycle, 312 pieces yielded 83.54% performance; 298 first-time-good pieces yielded 95.51% quality and 68.52% OEE.[144]

Finite-capacity scheduling assigns each job only where a specific machine and operator have time available; a machine cannot run overlapping jobs.[110] Capacity is determined by the most constrained machine: Machestra’s example identifies a VMC at 90% utilization, rather than a lathe at 40%, as the bottleneck.[110]

JITbase states that a three-shift environment has higher raw capacity but can have lower utilization per shift because of handover delays and fewer support staff; it identifies 70–85% of available minutes as typical target utilization guidance for small CNC shops and directs conservative planning where setups or changeovers are frequent.[95] Machestra’s illustrative finite-capacity model subtracts setup/changeover time, planned maintenance, breaks, and shift transitions from theoretical machine-hours; its six-machine example reduces 240 theoretical weekly machine-hours to 171 practical machine-hours, or about 71%, after those deductions.[110]

When scheduled time is set, define attended versus unattended shifts and mark planned downtime, including preventive maintenance and breaks.[97] If utilization is unknown, measure it for one month before relying on an assumed rate; the cited guide gives 75–85% as a realistic utilization range for a well-run job shop and says shops still developing changeover and scheduling discipline can run well below it.[80]

The Carbon illustrative machine-hour calculation uses 4,000 available hours per year at 80% utilization to produce 3,200 productive hours per year.

Capacity planning asks whether the shop can absorb work over coming weeks or months; finite-capacity scheduling assigns a specific job to a specific machine at a specific time.[110] Planning against theoretical or practical capacity instead of effective capacity produces overloaded schedules and missed deliveries.[75]

Productive Machine Hours are Scheduled Machine Hours less mechanical and non-mechanical delays; therefore, required scheduled fleet hours = target productive fleet hours ÷ productive fraction.
**Illustrative 2026 annual fleet calculation —** At an assumed 80% productive fraction, 15,000 productive fleet hours ÷ 0.80 = **18,750 scheduled fleet hours per year**. This follows Carbon’s illustrative relationship of 4,000 available hours × 80% utilization = 3,200 productive hours per year.[80]

Fifteen thousand annual hours cannot be the operating, scheduled, or productive annual time of a single machine because one machine has only 8,760 calendar hours in a 365-day year. It may be a valid aggregate fleet-hour requirement, but only after the shop identifies the machines or work centers included and states whether the numerator is scheduled, operating, PMH, spindle-rotating, or in-cut time.[115][119][77][123] A two-machine fleet is the theoretical minimum, but its 85.6% calendar loading is substantially more demanding than the three-machine equal-load scenario at 57.1%; neither figure reserves process-specific aerospace losses.[75][109] The decision should therefore use per-work-center effective capacity rather than a fleet average or a theoretical calendar maximum.[117][75]

1. Confirm whether 15,000 means annual fleet workload, fleet scheduled hours, actual operating hours, PMH, or spindle-cutting hours.
2. Allocate the confirmed demand separately to each candidate work center and each constrained operation.
3. Build annual capacity from shift coverage and planned uptime, then deduct maintenance, changeovers, setup, first-article, and expected delay allowances.
4. Report scheduled, operating, productive, spindle-rotating, in-cut, and good-part output measures separately; do not substitute one for another.

The delivered i-400S and U4000 configurations remain to be fixed for high-pressure coolant flow and filtration, thermal-feedback and probing options, controller and NX postprocessor package, MTConnect/OPC UA licensing, and the service response and critical-spares commitment for the Nuevo León site. The intended part envelope, titanium stock form, interrupted-cut severity, workholding, required surface integrity, and the meaning of 15,000 hours also remain to be set; these determine whether the torque, rigidity, and operating-cost ranking changes.
