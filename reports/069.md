Do not choose Carrier Infinity 26 or Lennox SL28XCV for the requested whole-home heat-pump project because both are cooling-only air conditioners and have no applicable HSPF2 rating. Mitsubishi Hyper-Heat H2i is the only named heat-pump family, but an exact ducted outdoor-unit and air-handler pairing is needed before its AHRI rating, 115°F capacity, electrical demand, rebate, and 15-year cost can be compared.

Carrier publishes 55.13 MBtu/h at 115°F and 48.67 MBtu/h at 125°F for one specified 5-ton matched system, an 11.7% drop, but that establishes high-ambient performance only for that air-conditioner configuration.

Lennox publishes maximum-operation sound estimates below 65 dBA at 5 ft for its listed sizes, while property-line compliance for every option depends on the selected size and actual condenser-to-lot-line layout.

The only defensible present recommendation is to obtain a Manual J/Manual S design and bids for identified heat-pump configurations, then rank them on 115°F net delivered cooling capacity and 15-year installed cost per delivered ton rather than nominal tonnage or headline SEER2.

## Evaluate each

Carrier Infinity 26 and Lennox SL28XCV are not whole-home heat pumps. Both are variable-capacity central air conditioners that can be part of a ducted cooling system, usually alongside a gas furnace or an air handler, but neither outdoor unit reverses to provide heat. Mitsubishi Hyper-Heat H2i is a heat-pump family, but it is not one single comparable system: it can be a single-zone ducted heat pump, a centrally ducted P-Series system, or a multi-zone system with several indoor units. That distinction decides the rating, wiring, ductwork, rebate, and cost questions.

**The direct conclusion is that Carrier Infinity 26 and Lennox SL28XCV should not be selected as the requested whole-home heat-pump options.** If you want a Carrier or Lennox heat pump, the bid must substitute an identified heat-pump model and provide an AHRI certificate for its exact outdoor unit, indoor coil or air handler, furnace if retained, and controls. Carrier itself distinguishes the Infinity 26 air conditioner, model 24VNA6, from the Infinity 24 heat pump, model 25VNA4.[1][2][3] Lennox identifies SL28XCV as an air conditioner and its unit-type code C means air-conditioner condenser.[4][5][6]

### Installation

SRP’s Cool Cash program is potentially applicable if the home is in SRP territory, the homeowner is a permanent SRP residential electric customer, the system replaces or supplements an existing central AC or central heat pump, and an Arizona-licensed contractor installs the qualified system before April 30, 2027.[41] SRP says the application must be submitted within six months of installation.[41]

For variable-capacity central heat pumps and variable-capacity AC systems with SEER2 of 15.2 or higher, SRP lists a rebate of $225 per nominal cooling ton.[41] SRP separately lists $225 per ton for inverter-driven mini-splits meeting 15.2 SEER2 or higher.[41] SRP pays incentives by nominal cooling ton or half-ton as stated in manufacturer specifications.[41]

A 3-ton qualifying system would therefore imply $675, a 3.5-ton system $787.50, a 4-ton system $900, a 4.5-ton system $1,012.50, and a 5-ton system $1,125 under the stated $225-per-ton schedule.[51]

Derived — **These rebate illustrations equal selected nominal tons multiplied by SRP’s published $225 per ton; they are not confirmation that any named system qualifies.** Carrier Infinity 26 and Lennox SL28XCV may qualify in the AC category if the exact AHRI match meets SRP’s requirements, but they remain air conditioners rather than whole-home heat pumps. Mitsubishi H2i may qualify either as a central heat pump or inverter-driven mini-split depending on the final configuration and exact AHRI listing.[41]

SRP requires both the indoor air handler and outdoor condensing unit to be replaced on split central systems.[41] That requirement matters for a renovation budget because a contractor cannot generally claim the rebate while retaining an incompatible old coil or air handler. SRP states that qualifying equipment must appear in AHRI Verified Directories and meet the efficiency minimums.[41]

SRP’s generic variable-capacity example estimates 15-year energy savings of $5,500 for 3 tons, $7,350 for 4 tons, and $9,150 for 5 tons compared with an existing 11-SEER system. SRP says those estimates assume electricity at $0.11/kWh and 2,141 cooling hours.[41] These are baseline-replacement savings estimates, not annual energy costs for the Carrier, Lennox, or Mitsubishi systems under consideration.

Judgment — **A useful 15-year TCO needs an annual energy model, not a single SEER2 number.** In Phoenix, the model should apply hourly weather, solar gains, duct losses, thermostat settings, occupancy, equipment capacity modulation, and the applicable SRP rate plan. Cooling energy costs should use the final system’s AHRI match and the selected equipment’s high-ambient performance data, while heating energy should use the actual retained furnace or heat-pump configuration.

A planning-level Phoenix full heat-pump installation range is $7,000–$14,500, but that source describes a general full system, not any of the named premium products.[42] A separate Phoenix source gives $8,000–$14,500 for a 3.5- to 4-ton conversion with good ducts and no panel work, including heat pump, air handler, a 230-V heat-strip circuit, and gas cap.[93] It also reports conditional costs of $400–$1,500 for a new 230-V air-handler circuit, $1,500–$3,500 for a panel upgrade if the panel is full, and $1,000–$4,000 for duct sealing or partial replacement if ducts test leaky.[93]

Duct replacement or repair estimates are broad. One Phoenix source gives $1,500–$4,000, while a duct-retrofit guide estimates $2,310–$7,750 for 220–375 ft of ductwork in 2,000–3,500-sq-ft homes.[42][77] The renovation should carry ductwork as a separately inspected allowance rather than burying it in equipment price comparisons.

A November 2015 ACCA member survey had 44 usable responses; its installation-cost question was ambiguously worded, and responses attributing increased cost to equipment-price increases were excluded.[49] The survey gives qualitative failure modes rather than projected failure probabilities for Carrier Infinity 26, Lennox SL28XCV, or Mitsubishi H2i: contractors named motors or capacitors, improper installation or start-up, airflow problems, evaporator-coil or refrigerant leaks, and compressors.[49] Contractor comments also identified lack of maintenance, dirty filters, dirty coils, undersized ducts, undersized return air, and poor duct design.[49] Carrier, Lennox, and Mitsubishi warranty terms are useful boundaries for parts coverage, but warranty duration is not an expected failure rate.[11][5][45]

Judgment — **For a 15-year ownership model, separate planned maintenance from risk allowance.** Planned maintenance includes at least annual professional service for a heat pump, and Lennox explicitly recommends twice-yearly service.[40][57] Risk allowance should be shown as a scenario range for out-of-warranty repairs and replacement exposure, with the assumptions stated as assumptions rather than represented as technician-survey statistics.

A technically complete contractor proposal should include: exact indoor and outdoor model numbers; AHRI certificate number; rated SEER2, EER2, and HSPF2 where applicable; performance at the Phoenix design temperature; outdoor MCA/MOCP and indoor/auxiliary-heat electrical requirements; room-by-room Manual J; Manual S selection; Manual D or measured duct assessment; line-set length and reuse scope; projected property-line sound; installed price; SRP rebate eligibility; maintenance-plan scope; and labor-warranty terms.

**15-year TCO accounting equation:**

\[
\text{15-year TCO}=\text{installed equipment and required duct/electrical work}-\text{confirmed SRP rebate}+15\times(\text{annual cooling energy cost}+\text{annual heating energy cost}+\text{annual maintenance-contract cost})+\text{expected repair cost over years 1–15}.
\]

The confirmed SRP-rebate term is $225 per nominal cooling ton only when the exact variable-capacity central heat-pump or AC match has SEER2 of 15.2 or higher, appears in the AHRI Verified Directories, and meets SRP’s customer, contractor, replacement, installation-date, and application requirements. SRP pays mini-split incentives by nominal cooling capacity in tons or half-tons.[41][94][51]

For Carrier Infinity 26, the installed equipment/duct/electrical-work cost, confirmed SRP rebate, annual cooling-energy cost, annual heating-energy cost for the separate heating equipment, maintenance-contract cost, and expected 15-year repair cost are unavailable for the required matched installation. For Lennox SL28XCV, those same inputs are unavailable, including annual heating-energy cost for the separate heating equipment. For Mitsubishi H2i, the installed equipment/duct/electrical-work cost, confirmed SRP rebate, annual cooling and heating energy costs, maintenance-contract cost, and expected 15-year repair cost are unavailable until one exact whole-home configuration is selected.[1][4][31][41][2][40][79]

SRP’s stated 15-year figures of $5,500 for 3 tons, $7,350 for 4 tons, and $9,150 for 5 tons are savings estimates versus an existing 11-SEER system, assuming $0.11/kWh and 2,141 cooling hours; they are not any candidate system’s 15-year energy bill.[41]

AHRI verification method — AHRI’s Product Performance Certification Program is voluntary and certifies that participating products perform according to manufacturers’ published claims. For residential split air conditioners and air-source heat pumps below 65,000 Btu/h, AHRI Standard 210/240 establishes definitions, test and rating requirements, operating requirements, and conformance conditions. Obtain the Certified Reference Number from the installer, then confirm in the AHRI Directory the exact outdoor unit, indoor coil or air handler, furnace where applicable, model status, rated capacity, SEER2, EER2, HSPF2 where applicable, and applicable standard.[95][84][96]

SEER2 is a seasonal cooling-efficiency metric, while EER2 evaluates efficiency at 95°F outdoor temperature and is particularly relevant to hot, dry Southwest operation. SEER2 replaced SEER for equipment manufactured and sold under the new requirements beginning January 1, 2023; its updated test accounts for higher external static pressure, including ductwork resistance. Neither metric states cooling capacity at 115°F or higher.[97]

AHRI certifies equipment, not the installation. Carrier states that a higher SEER2 indicates greater efficiency under standardized test conditions and that actual energy savings depend on system size, operating hours, climate, ductwork, thermostat settings, HVAC installation quality, and electricity rates; standardized SEER and SEER2 ratings do not guarantee a specified percentage reduction in an actual electricity bill. Actual energy use and operating cost also depend on sizing, controls, maintenance, and operation.[96][97]

AHRI states that split-system efficiency claims are independently verified for matched indoor/outdoor combinations. The contractor must provide the AHRI Certified Reference Number or Certificate of Product Ratings for the proposed outdoor unit and indoor coil, furnace, or air handler; an AHRI certificate also can support a utility or local-government rebate application.[98][99]

The Florida Solar Energy Center’s 2018 study monitored HVAC energy use in 56 Florida homes from 2012 through 2016. It found cooling-related performance degradation averaging 3–7% per year, a median estimated rate of 5.2% per year, and 87% of 64 estimated degradation values above zero; it also reported substantial scatter associated with household behavior and internal heat gains.[100] The study’s robust regression estimated 3.7% annual cooling-efficiency degradation plus approximately 0.3 percentage points for each additional year of air-handler age, with the age term significant at the 90% level.[100]

Installation commissioning is a Phoenix cost and performance issue. The Florida Solar Energy Center paper cites a Phoenix-area field study in which 78% of evaluated systems were undercharged, often because long refrigerant lines were not properly augmented at installation; it also cites California-and-Southwest field work finding incorrect charge in more than 50% of examined installations.[100] In laboratory testing cited by that paper, a 20% refrigerant undercharge in a typical 3-ton machine reduced sensible capacity by 24% at 95°F outdoor conditions.[100]

The same paper cites a field study of 4,168 air conditioners in which 77% were over- or undercharged and 44% had improper airflow.[100] A separate Florida field study measured average coil airflow of 317 cfm per ton against 400 cfm per ton typically recommended.[100] Another cited study associated undersized ducts and return grilles with average external static pressure of 0.55 inches water column rather than the 0.10 inches water column used in ARI rating procedures, approximately 10% lower rated sensible capacity, and a similar increase in cooling energy use.[100]

Reported added-cost items included thermostats and wiring at $60, $200, $250, $200–500, $800, and $500–1,000; refrigerant lines at $250–1,500; start-up at $100, $250, $200–600, and $100–1,500; duct review at $200; duct redesign at $500–1,000; and installation labor at $500. The survey stated that variability and combined cost elements made averages impractical.[49]

Consumer Reports recommends negotiating a service plan that combines regular inspections, repair discounts, and a labor warranty; it states that service-plan prices vary widely. Its annual-maintenance list is coil cleaning and flushing, drain-pan and drainage-system service, blower-compartment vacuuming, refrigerant-charge and leak checks, and checks of mechanical components.[101]

For 15-year planning, AHRI gives an average properly installed and maintained central-air-conditioner life of 12–15 years and an average heat-pump life of about 14 years.[99] In Consumer Reports’ survey of more than 13,000 central-air-conditioning units bought and installed from 2009 through 2024, about 19% encountered at least one problem and 24% stopped working as well as they should or altogether. These are broad central-AC survey results, not model-specific projections.[101]

DOE also states that these metrics do not include all system energy consumption, including certain standby and off-mode auxiliary loads addressed by its newer SCORE and SHORE metrics.[48]

For an air-source heat pump that supplies both heating and cooling, DOE defines total annual operating cost as the sum of estimated annual cooling and heating operating costs. DOE’s appendix-M1 regional cooling calculation uses cooling capacity divided by SEER2, regional cooling-load hours, and representative electricity price; its heating calculation uses represented capacity divided by HSPF2, regional heating-load hours, a 1.07 adjustment factor for variable-speed heat pumps, and representative electricity price.[48]

## Lowest cost per cooling ton in extreme desert conditions

A nominal “3-ton” or “5-ton” label is not the cooling a system delivers during a Phoenix 115°F afternoon. The fair comparison is 15-year dollars per ton actually delivered at the same outdoor condition, after confirming that capacity meets the home’s Manual J cooling load. That prevents a lower upfront price, a higher seasonal rating, or a larger nominal label from being mistaken for lower extreme-heat cost.

### Comparable cost per delivered ton

Judgment — Define the Extreme-Heat Cost Index as 15-year whole-home dollars divided by independently established cooling Btu/h at the same outdoor condition of 115°F or higher and the same indoor test conditions, divided by 12,000 Btu/h per ton. The resulting unit is 15-year dollars per delivered hot-condition ton, not dollars per ton-hour.[1][37] Net ownership cost equals installed system and required modifications, plus 15 years of electricity, scheduled maintenance-contract payments, and expected out-of-warranty repairs, less confirmed rebates. Each candidate must first meet the home’s calculated design cooling load at the selected extreme condition.

The comparison therefore needs the same four inputs for every candidate: a selected indoor/outdoor match, measured or manufacturer-tabulated cooling output at 115°F or above, Phoenix installation and operating costs, and a 15-year repair assumption. A system that cannot meet the calculated load is not a lower-cost alternative merely because its dollars-per-ton figure is lower.
