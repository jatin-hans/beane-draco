Edge computing should be adopted as a hybrid edge-to-cloud architecture for industrial IoT workloads whose local action deadline or loss-of-connectivity consequence is material. For manufacturing predictive maintenance, the strongest established benefit is local continuity and preprocessing, while the cited evidence supports communications targets of 100 ms to 60 s for plant asset management and 99.99% communications availability, not a demonstrated universal edge-versus-cloud SLA gain.[1] For autonomous mobile robots, navigation, obstacle avoidance, and fleet coordination require local or near-edge decisions rather than a WAN-dependent cloud control loop, with cloud retained for fleet learning, history, and management.

For energy distribution, edge intelligence belongs close to substations and field assets where grid protection, automation, and restoration must continue through backhaul disruption, while cloud systems provide broader planning and asset analytics.

Across all three uses, edge reduces upstream traffic only when the deployed filtering, aggregation, feature extraction, or event-selection rule is measured against a defined raw-data baseline, and it trades WAN dependence for distributed hardware, patching, identity management, and OT cybersecurity responsibility.

This report covers industrial IoT edge-computing architectures for manufacturing predictive maintenance, autonomous mobile robots, and energy distribution as of 2024, using only developments, deployments, measurements, and publications made or published by 31 December 2024.

It distinguishes the date of a deployment or measurement from the publication date of the material reporting it, and labels every numerical figure as a measured outcome, engineering requirement, service-level target, vendor claim, or illustrative calculation.

## Manufacturing predictive maintenance

**Predictive maintenance** uses equipment-condition information to identify maintenance work needed to prevent an automation-device failure. **Industrial IoT (IIoT)** connects equipment data to software that uses it operationally, and **edge computing** processes data near where it is created rather than only in a cloud or data warehouse. As of 2024, manufacturing predictive maintenance was moving from centralized cloud analysis toward a hybrid **edge-to-cloud** architecture: an on-premises industrial gateway collects, cleans, extracts features from, calculates selected metrics from, and visualizes machine data locally, while cloud services provide long-term storage, cross-facility analysis, model training, and enterprise reporting.[2][3][4][5][6]

The transformation is workload partitioning, not cloud replacement: as of 2024, edge-distributed predictive maintenance moves selected data handling and operational access to the factory while retaining cloud analytics, historical retention, and cross-facility management.[4][5][7]

### a before-and-after architecture comparison (cloud-centric vs. edge-distributed)

A **cloud-centric architecture** is one in which machine and sensor data are transported from the factory to centralized services, where storage, analytics, model inference, alert evaluation, and visualization are principally performed. A sensor may still connect through a local gateway, but that gateway is primarily a communications bridge rather than the place where the predictive-maintenance decision is computed. AWS’s 2020 industrial predictive-maintenance reference architecture illustrates the general model of industrial assets feeding AWS IoT SiteWise and AWS IoT Analytics to create a predictive-maintenance machine-learning model.[8]

In a conventional cloud-centric predictive-maintenance path, the first stage is sensing: PLCs, industrial PCs, sensors, machine controllers, and historians expose values such as pressure, temperature, speed, vibration, torque, and machine state. Manufacturing-specific gateways translate these heterogeneous industrial interfaces into formats suitable for upper-layer applications and can carry information in both directions.[9] Factories commonly combine new and legacy equipment from several vendors, use many industrial protocols, and also hold relevant operational context in enterprise resource planning (ERP), manufacturing execution system (MES), and shift-scheduling systems.[10]

Second, data move through a factory network and then to a centralized platform. In the cloud-centric pattern, the platform stores and organizes time-series equipment data, associates the streams with asset models, trains or hosts machine-learning models, and presents dashboards or alerts. AWS IoT SiteWise describes its cloud function as collecting, storing, organizing, and monitoring industrial-equipment data at scale.[10] Its asset models and hierarchies can represent equipment, processes, facilities, and relations within or across facilities, which is useful when comparing similar machines, aggregating a production line’s performance, or maintaining a common asset record across sites.[5][6]

Third, cloud services evaluate the data. The cloud can compute transforms when data arrive, calculate interval-based metrics, run anomaly-detection models, retain detailed historical records, and feed enterprise analytics or machine-learning systems. AWS describes native anomaly detection as a predictive-maintenance capability and says its service manages the models while allowing customers to configure inference frequency.[5] This central placement supports a large shared analytical environment, but it makes the sensor-to-decision path dependent on the factory network, the external connection, cloud-service availability, and the latency of the cloud-processing path.

The practical limitation is not that cloud analytics are inherently unsuitable for predictive maintenance. Rather, different maintenance signals have different urgency. A trend calculated over hours or days can be centrally processed and reviewed in a cloud dashboard. A high-frequency signal that should trigger a local inspection, a process hold, or an operator warning benefits when the local plant can calculate and display the relevant metric without waiting for a wide-area round trip. Google’s industrial predictive-maintenance guidance identifies factory machinery as a setting that can require almost instantaneous inference, while also identifying inconsistent connectivity and network-transfer cost as reasons to consider edge inference.[3]

An **edge-distributed architecture** puts compute and storage nearer the generating equipment. In industrial practice, the edge is often an industrial PC, gateway, ruggedized server, or local virtualization host on the factory network. In the ETSI definition of **Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC)**, MEC applications are software entities that run on virtualization infrastructure located in or close to the network edge. A MEC host supplies compute, storage, network resources, and a data plane that can apply traffic rules and route traffic among local applications, local networks, mobile networks, and external networks.[11]

For manufacturing predictive maintenance, this means the architecture is commonly split into at least three levels:

1. **Sensor and machine-control level:** sensors, PLCs, machine controllers, and historians generate operational data. This is operational technology (OT): the hardware and software that directly monitor or control physical equipment and processes. [9]

2. **Factory edge level:** a gateway or industrial computer collects and normalizes machine data, filters or aggregates it, computes selected features and metrics, stores a bounded local history, exposes local dashboards or APIs, and may run local prediction or rule-based alerting. [5][7][12]

3. **Cloud and enterprise level:** the cloud receives selected results, aggregates, historical data, and contextual metadata for long-term retention, multi-site analysis, visualization, model development, and centralized configuration management. [5][7][10]

The edge level therefore changes the upstream data path. Rather than forwarding every raw measurement to a centralized service, the gateway can retain raw data locally for an appropriate period, discard irrelevant records, calculate a feature, calculate an equipment metric, and forward the derived result or an aggregate. AWS states that SiteWise Edge can filter streams, compute metrics including Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE), and send processed data to AWS cloud services, Amazon S3, or Amazon Timestream.[5] AWS also explicitly describes the option to process raw data locally and send only aggregated data to the cloud to optimize bandwidth use and cloud-storage cost.[7][12]

The edge does not remove cloud functions. AWS states that SiteWise metrics are sent to the cloud for long-term storage even where the customer configures their computation at the edge.[7] The management model is also hybrid: the operator configures sources, sampling, standard or custom functions, visualizations, and metrics in the cloud console and deploys the configuration to the gateway.[6] Asset models and dashboards automatically synchronize between the cloud and gateway every 10 minutes, with manual synchronization also available.[7]

The central architectural conclusion is clear. Cloud-centric predictive maintenance concentrates storage and analytics in the cloud; edge distribution moves selected operations—especially collection, signal conditioning, cleaning, filtering, feature extraction, metric calculation, and local access—to the factory. The cloud remains the system of record for long-horizon analysis and commonly remains a management plane for configuring and updating the edge estate.[4][5][6][7]

This division is compatible with the 2024 ITU-T smart-manufacturing framework. ITU-T Y.4228 describes the device layer as including sensors and gateways that can collect and process data, explicitly including edge-computing support. It recommends device-layer processing that unifies formats, parses semantics, cleans, compresses, caches, and prepares heterogeneous data before transmission to upper layers.[9] The framework also places factory internal OT and IT networks in a connected but security-separated architecture, typically using industrial gateways, firewalls, data diodes, intrusion-protection systems, and often a demilitarized zone (DMZ).[9]

The manufacturing condition-monitoring study provides a concrete example of the split. Its three-layer architecture consists of sensors, an edge layer, and centralized cloud servers. Smart sensors collect vibration signals from manufacturing machines; IIoT gateways perform signal conditioning, data acquisition, and signal preprocessing; centralized cloud servers run unsupervised-learning algorithms to monitor condition and detect faults.[4] The system calculates relative wavelet energy at the edge, forwards those features to the cloud, and uses a long short-term memory (LSTM) model—an ML model suited to sequences—to perform anomaly detection.[4]

The deployment was not a purely simulated design. The authors tested it in a real shop environment on an electronic-component insertion machine used for electronic-product assembly. The vibration-sensor nodes were wire-connected to IIoT gateways and sampled at **1,000 Hz**.[4] At that sampling rate, the useful architectural point is that the gateway can convert a dense waveform stream into a smaller set of diagnostic features before transmitting it.

The paper states that this reduces data transmission and cloud-side algorithm computing, but it does not publish the raw-versus-featured payload volumes needed to calculate a percentage reduction.[4]

The research and product examples differ in where they place the final anomaly algorithm. The manufacturing study retains LSTM anomaly detection in the cloud after edge feature extraction. AWS’s SiteWise Edge documentation permits local metrics and transforms, local visualizations, local data queries, and locally available data for applications; AWS’s generic IoT Greengrass platform also supports running local ML predictions and responding autonomously to local events.[4][5][6][7][21] This is not a contradiction.

It shows that “edge predictive maintenance” covers a continuum: from edge data preparation with cloud inference, through local metric and rule evaluation, to local model inference with cloud synchronization and model-management functions.

Judgment — place work by the task and its deadline. A maintenance trend that leads to a weekly inspection can remain cloud-first, while an immediate operator warning belongs at the plant edge. A robot can execute an already held order through its last released node, but fleet coordination requires the fleet-control view. For electrical-distribution distributed automated switching for isolation and service restoration, ETSI’s July 2024 target is **99.999 9 %** communication-service availability, end-to-end latency below **5 ms**, **1 kbit/s** in steady state, and **1.5 Mbit/s** in a fault case.[15] Southern California Edison’s earlier remedial-action schemes, before its IEC 61850-based C-RAS architecture, had a limited situational view with highly local, preselected inputs and actions; could not share information or equipment among schemes; risked uncoordinated responses and misoperation as the number of schemes increased; and were not tenable to manage and update remotely at scale.[22]

This architecture also changes integration. MQTT is a publish-and-subscribe messaging protocol: a producer publishes a message to a topic, and multiple subscribed systems can receive it without each integration being point-to-point. AWS describes MQTT-enabled SiteWise Edge gateways as enabling local publish-subscribe and hub-and-spoke integration between edge software components.[5] AWS says this open MQTT approach simplifies integration among existing equipment, middleware, and production systems without requiring proprietary APIs or deep programming expertise.[5]

AWS contrasts this with its prior edge-integration approach, in which internal components and custom edge components required point-to-point integration using proprietary AWS APIs and software. Its MQTT-enabled AWS IoT SiteWise Edge gateway acts as a local hub, collecting data through native OPC UA connectivity, certified partner integrations, and custom edge integrations.[10] A **Unified Name Space (UNS)** is a hierarchical MQTT topic structure representing operational context, such as site, area, line, machine, and data point. AWS describes it as event-driven: after data are published to a topic, subscribed systems can use them for real-time operational decision-making.[10]

The architectural shift makes local data available to local consumers, but it does not eliminate enterprise data integration. AWS’s described implementation can combine machine telemetry with MES and ERP metadata to create enriched streams, while the cloud can reflect the edge data structure through modeling for AI/ML and data-lake analytics.[10] That is the durable division of labor: the edge understands what must happen at the machine and plant; the cloud can compare, retain, train, govern, and optimize across plants.

The change also affects service continuity. AWS states that custom edge applications can use SiteWise query APIs running on the on-premises gateway to retrieve asset data and metrics without relying on cloud connectivity.[5] It also states that local metrics can be computed on premises and made available to local applications during a temporary cloud disruption.[6] Gateway caching can retain data locally for up to 30 days, constrained by disk capacity.[6] These functions improve **operational continuity**—the ability to keep reading locally available data and using local applications when the cloud path is unavailable.

They do not prove an availability percentage for the predictive-maintenance system. AWS IoT Core’s SLA is a cloud-service commitment of at least 99.9% monthly uptime in each AWS Region, calculated in five-minute intervals as the percentage of requests processed without errors; an interval with no requests is counted as 100% available.[14] That is a service-level definition for AWS IoT Core, not a measure of whether a factory gateway, local model, sensor path, PLC, machine, maintenance action, or production process remains available.

Similarly, ETSI’s 99.99% target for plant asset management is a target for communications service availability. The same profile specifies message sizes of 20–255 bytes, a mean time between failures of at least one week, transfer intervals of 100 ms to 60 s, and maximum end-to-end latency below the applicable transfer interval.[1] The target is useful in requirements engineering: it tells a manufacturer to distinguish the communications requirement from a cloud SLA, model-inference time, machine availability, and maintenance-service outcome.

The 2024 manufacturing infrastructure recommendation makes the same broad point. ITU-T Y.4228 says industrial systems typically require network-level reliability from **99.999% to 99.99999%**, because unplanned service interruption can impose major production and business losses.[9] These are requirements for industrial communications generally, not observed uptime after edge deployment. Factory and process automation requirements can also range from 99.9% to 99.999999% depending on the use case.[23]

The most defensible availability comparison is therefore architectural rather than numerical: in a cloud-centric path, cloud connectivity is in the path for cloud-only inference and alerting; in an edge-distributed path, a local gateway can continue serving locally cached data, local metrics, and local applications during a temporary cloud interruption.[13][5][6] **Edge changes the failure domain, but it does not by itself create an SLA improvement.**

**Scope as of 31 December 2024.** In manufacturing predictive maintenance, edge computing places processing near machine data sources; a Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) host provides local compute, storage, network resources, and traffic routing for applications.[3][11] In smart manufacturing, the IIoT device layer can unify heterogeneous data formats, parse semantics, clean, compress, and cache data before it is sent upward.[9] Across predictive maintenance, autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), and energy distribution, the architectural change is selective workload placement: retain centrally useful fleet, enterprise, and grid-wide coordination, while placing deadline-sensitive data handling or action close to equipment.

Electric-utility operations combine centralized control with localized autonomous decision-making.[24] The comparison below records, for each requested use, before-and-after placement, latency in milliseconds and its status, transmitted-data percentage, availability measure and comparator, and cost, security, and maintainability trade-offs.

It predicts failures days in advance, reduces unplanned downtime, reduces data-processing infrastructure, and reduces the need for data-science skills.[18] In the depicted anomaly, Amazon Lookout for Equipment detected the first anomaly **45 days** ahead of a thermal shock.[18] Toyota reports that operational availability in the focus line improved **10%** versus the previous 12-month average.[18] It also reports preventing **16 incidents** and **20 hours** of downtime, with approximately **$80,000** cost savings.[18] The solution scaled to **more than 300 assets** across TMMK Powertrain, and TMNA selected it for the region.[18]

In a Microsoft Azure IIoT case, Tetra Pak connected more than 5,000 filling machines to Azure; over six months, the system predicted five line failures and generated more than $30,000 in customer savings.[85]

The Industry IoT Consortium’s three-tier IIoT model consists of edge, platform, and enterprise tiers.[26]
The proximity network connects edge nodes such as sensors, actuators, devices, OT systems, and assets to the stack, the access network carries data and control flows between edge and platform tiers, and the service network connects platform and enterprise tiers.[26]
Edge computing is a decentralized infrastructure that distributes computing resources and application services along the path between data sources and the cloud, where it can process, analyze, and act on data rather than merely collect them for onward transmission.[26]
Industrial edge gateways can connect thousands of IIoT devices to nearby data-processing units when direct transmission of all data to the cloud is infeasible because of cost, privacy, or network constraints.[27]
The same edge-computing landscape includes high-complexity robotic applications, automated guided vehicles, and grid-system controls.[27]

Redundancy is a further edge-continuity design option. A study summarized in an edge data-reduction review proposed redundant IoT-gateway hardware and a cooperative filtering algorithm and reported a twofold increase in the availability and fault tolerance of data reduction.[20] Edge and fog systems retain partial service when some compute nodes fail; when the failed nodes’ workload is shifted to healthy nodes, they retain full service while corrective action is undertaken.[28]

Microsoft describes a model-deployment path in which a model resides in Azure Blob Storage or a web service, Azure IoT Hub synchronizes metadata through module twins, and an Azure IoT Edge module downloads, stores, and runs the model locally.[86]

In the described Dell/ifm implementation, machine sensors connect through an ifm IO-Link master block to a Dell Edge Gateway 5000, which collects, analyzes, relays, and acts on real-time data.[30] SAP edge software manages device connectivity, local storage, local analytics, data-stream analytics, and backend synchronization; it acts on perishable data immediately by generating alerts and sending only meaningful data to the cloud.[30] Decentralized analytics enable local systems to maintain normal operations when the cloud connection is temporarily lost.[30]

ZF connects OPC UA-capable devices to on-premises Azure IoT Edge, with bidirectional IoT Hub connectivity and Event Hub and Data Lake analytics upstream.[85]

### quantified impacts on latency (target milliseconds), bandwidth reduction (% data reduction), and availability/SLA improvements

Predictive maintenance requires a careful separation of four different timing measures:

- **Sampling interval:** how often a sensor measures, such as the 1,000-Hz vibration sensing in the shop-floor research system. [4]
- **Transfer interval:** how often a message is sent, such as ETSI’s 100-ms-to-60-s plant-asset-management range. [1]
- **End-to-end latency:** the time from the source event to delivery or response at the relevant destination.
- **Inference or alert latency:** the time needed to calculate a prediction and trigger or present an action.

These measures are related but cannot be substituted for one another. A 1,000-Hz sensor does not prove a 1-ms alert decision. A 10-minute cloud-to-edge configuration synchronization cadence does not define data-access or inference latency.[4][7]

For predictive maintenance specifically, the cited sources establish a timing rationale rather than a measured before-and-after millisecond result. Google states that on-premises predictive-model inference has “much lower” latency than sending new data to AWS cloud for inference and produces a more real-time evaluation.[13] It does not give an exact millisecond comparison.

In a benchmark using 100 simulated devices sending 100 device-to-cloud messages per second, direct cloud AMQP averaged 89.01 ± 21.93 ms round-trip and direct cloud MQTT averaged 90.81 ± 31.83 ms, while an Edge MQTT relay averaged 2,564.37 ± 1,547.44 ms. These are messaging-path measurements, not manufacturing sensor-to-alert times.[87]

AWS likewise says SiteWise Edge supports near-real-time factory-floor visualization when cloud connectivity is temporarily disrupted, but it does not publish a numerical latency target for that visualization.[6]

In that simulated-device messaging benchmark, the Edge MQTT path reached a maximum latency of 10.345 seconds and accumulated more than 2.5 seconds of additional delay between early and late messages, despite 98.87% CPU idle time and about 424 MB of memory use.[87]

The best source-specific numerical target available for plant asset management is ETSI’s requirement that end-to-end latency be below the transfer interval, which ranges from 100 ms to 60 s.[1] This range indicates that plant asset management includes both relatively frequent monitoring and much slower reporting.

It should not be reported as a single “100 ms predictive-maintenance edge latency,” because ETSI gives a range and defines the maximum latency relative to the selected interval.

The broader factory-automation benchmark is more stringent but applies to control rather than necessarily maintenance analytics. ETSI defines factory automation as involving determinism, low latency, reliability, redundancy, cybersecurity, and functional safety, and says low latency typically means below 10 ms delivery time.[2] ITU-T Y.4228 provides a different set of example targets: motion control should have end-to-end latency below 100 microseconds, while real-time industrial applications other than motion control should have latency below 1–100 ms.[9] Predictive maintenance often supports human maintenance decisions rather than direct closed-loop motion control; the correct target therefore depends on whether the output is a dashboard update, an operator warning, a process hold, or an automated protective action.

Judgment — a predictive-maintenance design should allocate a latency budget from sensor acquisition through preprocessing, inference, alert routing, and operator or control-system action. The system owner should then specify the only latency that matters operationally: the maximum permitted time from detectable fault signature to the action that prevents damage or unsafe operation.

The bandwidth effect is more direct architecturally than numerically. SiteWise Edge permits local filtering, aggregation, metrics, transforms, and processed-result delivery. AWS explicitly says sending only aggregated data can optimize bandwidth and cloud-storage cost.[7][12] The real-shop research system provides the corresponding analytic pattern: extract relative-wavelet-energy features from vibration signals at the edge, then transmit those features to a cloud LSTM detector.[4] In both cases, data reduction occurs because a feature vector or aggregate is usually smaller than the raw high-frequency signal stream from which it was derived.

No source in the manufacturing predictive-maintenance evidence supplies the requested percentage data reduction with a raw-data baseline and a post-edge transmitted-data measurement. A percentage should therefore not be invented or inferred from a generic edge-video paper, a historian-compression ratio, or a non-manufacturing example. The correct use-case-specific conclusion is that edge filtering and aggregation create a bandwidth-reduction mechanism, but the magnitude is plant-, signal-, model-, and retention-policy-specific.[4][7][12]

The amount can vary sharply. A condition-monitoring system may keep all raw vibration locally for forensic analysis but upload only features and anomaly events; another may upload sampled waveforms, model-training windows, or raw periods around anomalies. The relevant calculation is straightforward but must use plant measurements: **percentage reduction equals 100 multiplied by one minus edge-to-cloud transmitted bytes divided by the cloud-centric baseline transmitted bytes**, over the same machines, sampling period, and retention policy. No published values in the supplied manufacturing case provide those inputs.

This does not mean traffic is unimportant. Siemens describes one industrial scenario with 100 high-frequency variables producing **3 million values per hour**, and identifies local processing, local retention of sensitive data, and immediate decisions as the relevant response.[31] That figure is a vendor-presented scenario, not a measured predictive-maintenance bandwidth reduction. It shows why raw-data export can become an engineering issue before a plant even considers video or high-frequency vibration streams.

AWS also supports selection at the data-source boundary. Its SiteWise Edge documentation permits operators to filter OPC UA node paths so that only selected paths are uploaded to AWS IoT SiteWise.[32] Filtering at this point can reduce traffic and data exposure, but only if the asset team has determined that omitted variables are not needed for diagnosis, compliance, model retraining, or post-failure investigation.

AWS documents bounded local continuity: a SiteWise Edge gateway caches data during intermittent connectivity for up to 30 days, subject to disk capacity, and local applications can retrieve live and historical data, transforms, and metrics from the gateway.[6]

The limit matters. AWS states that if a SiteWise Edge gateway is disconnected from AWS Cloud for 30 days, its configured OPC UA source is automatically disabled.[7] An edge system therefore needs capacity planning for disk, local retention, offline duration, backfill, and reconnection. Edge continuity is bounded continuity.

Microsoft says IoT Hub can cache module-twin notifications while an edge device is offline and synchronize them after reconnection; it describes model switching with “almost zero downtime” but supplies no numerical uptime result.[86]

AWS’s Data Processing Pack has minimum requirements of a four-core CPU, 16 GB RAM, and 256 GB available disk.[6] These minimums show that local processing is not free; it requires a capable industrial computer or gateway.

They also do not establish that a device meeting those minimums will deliver a specific inference rate, latency, or availability for every vibration, acoustic, thermal, or multivariate model.

The product configuration is flexible. An asset model can calculate every property at the edge and send results to the cloud, or can allocate each property separately.[7] A transform must be computed and stored where its inputs are; therefore, when an input metric is moved to the edge, its associated transform also moves to the edge.[7] The technical implication is that computation placement propagates through data dependencies. A plant cannot decide to move one metric locally without also reviewing where the dependent calculations, data retention, visualizations, and alerts must run.

A useful availability distinction is between **service availability**, **communications availability**, and **production availability**. AWS IoT Core’s 99.9% service commitment measures cloud API request success within five-minute intervals.[14] ETSI’s 99.99% plant-asset-management target measures communications-service availability.[1] Production availability measures whether the machine can keep making acceptable product, which depends on the equipment, process, maintenance program, materials, workforce, safety systems, and many other factors. These metrics should appear in the same requirements document but should never be treated as the same metric.

The Siemens Senseye examples show why this distinction matters. Siemens reports that one global automotive manufacturer saved $45 million in labor and avoided unplanned downtime at a single site since 2019.[16] Siemens also reports that the same automaker used Senseye to monitor more than 10,000 assets in nine factories after scaling from tens of UK assets in 2016 to more than 2,500 across Europe by the beginning of the following year.[16] In a separate aluminium-producer case, Siemens reports monitoring more than 1,000 complex assets at the original site using existing machine and maintenance feeds, without installing additional sensors.[16]

BMW Regensburg uses cloud analysis of power consumption, conveyor motion, and barcode legibility across thousands of assets. Stellantis centrally shares each day’s plant problems and fixes across 28 sites.[88]

HiveMQ identifies Ford as using its platform to unify real-time data across global plants for uptime and predictive maintenance.[89]

For that aluminium producer, Siemens reports a 20% reduction in unplanned downtime, an initial return on investment in six months, and a 10x return on investment in 2019.[16] These figures are valuable evidence that predictive maintenance can improve maintenance and production outcomes. They are not latency measurements, bandwidth-reduction percentages, availability-SLA figures, or demonstrations that edge processing caused the reported outcomes.

HiveMQ’s broader manufacturing material associates $83 million in savings with a 40% downtime reduction, but does not identify the customers or period or isolate predictive maintenance’s contribution.[89]

RFC 9556 states that industrial IoT requires bounded latency and jitter as well as low latency: control packets must arrive with little variation and within a strict deadline. It states that Internet best-effort delivery makes this difficult without end-to-end guarantees for individual-message delivery and continuous data flows, and that speed-of-light limits can prevent cloud-based solutions in some cases.[33]

RFC 9556 distinguishes local storage and caching. Local storage or caching supports local processing and delayed transfer to the cloud, while caching is generally temporary storage without persistence guarantees; persistent storage is subject to retention policies.[33] In an Edge+Cloud hybrid design summarized in a data-reduction review, a full edge buffer causes raw, unreduced data to be forwarded to the cloud to avoid data loss, where reduction or machine-learning processing can occur.[20]

A systematic mapping review reports a proposed design using redundant IoT-gateway hardware and a cooperative filtering algorithm with PIP data reduction that increases the availability and fault tolerance of data reduction by a factor of two.[20]

More processing closer to the edge reduces the amount of data that must reach remote cloud servers, but distributed systems can increase total transmissions, particularly cooperative systems with heavy inter-node communication.[28] A data-reduction review states that an edge AI module can produce inaccurate data results depending on the selected architectural schema.[20]

### trade-offs in cost, security, and maintainability with cited vendor case studies or real-world examples

The primary cost shift is from recurring central data transport and cloud computation toward distributed plant hardware and operations. AWS’s published price for its SiteWise Edge Data Processing Pack was **$200 per active gateway per month**, while its Data Collection Pack was free.[34] AWS’s documentation also states that enabling the Data Processing Pack incurs additional charges.[12] Those costs sit alongside the local gateway or industrial-computer purchase, installation, ruggedization where needed, local storage, networking, spares, backup power where appropriate, monitoring, and lifecycle support.

Cloud-centric designs retain costs too. They may require continuous upstream connectivity, cloud ingestion, storage, analysis, event processing, and data egress or integration services. AWS describes differentiated cloud storage tiers, retaining recent data in hot storage for at least 30 days and moving historical data to warm storage under user-defined retention policies.[5] This can control central storage cost for historical data, but it does not remove the cost of transporting high-volume raw telemetry to the cloud in the first place.

Judgment — the economic comparison should be based on total cost of ownership per asset or per production line, not gateway price alone. The calculation should include data transmission, cloud ingestion and retention, edge hardware, local support labor, software subscriptions, cybersecurity controls, unplanned-downtime exposure, and the value of faster local diagnosis. Edge is most economically attractive where raw data are high-volume, connectivity is expensive or unreliable, and local action creates measurable operational value.

The cited manufacturing research demonstrates an implementation pattern that can reduce some integration and hardware costs. The 2022 condition-monitoring system used wired MEMS accelerometers and IIoT gateways on a real electronic-component insertion machine rather than requiring an all-new plant-wide sensing architecture.[4] The Siemens aluminium case similarly reports using existing machine and maintenance data feeds and installing no additional sensors for the original site’s more than 1,000 monitored assets.[16] These examples support an incremental rollout strategy: start with available signals and add sensing only where diagnostic coverage remains inadequate.

ZF Diepholz reported automated production transparency, faster reaction, better planning and stronger analytics-based control, with expected payback slightly above 18 months.[85]

The EMUGE case offers another shop-floor example. Siemens Industrial Edge for Machine Tools automatically records high-frequency drilling torque data for borehole monitoring. The customer stated that Analyze MyWorkpiece/Capture4Analysis allowed **95% of its measurements** to be made without an external measuring system.[31] That is a measurement-process outcome, not a 95% total cost reduction or a bandwidth figure. It nevertheless shows a plausible local value proposition: use machine-native process data to reduce dependence on separate measurement steps.

Security changes rather than simply improves or worsens. A cloud-centric architecture centralizes analytics and policy management but sends more production and condition information beyond the plant network. AWS notes industrial concern that machinery and sensor data transmitted, shared, analyzed, and modeled could be insecure or accessed by unauthorized parties.[35] The concern is legitimate because OT systems control or monitor physical processes, and OT security generally prioritizes safety, availability, and integrity ahead of confidentiality.[26]

An edge architecture can keep raw or sensitive machine data inside the factory and forward only aggregates, features, anomalies, or selected historical records. AWS identifies local processing of raw data and aggregate-only cloud delivery as a means to optimize bandwidth and cloud-storage cost.[7] Less raw-data export reduces external exposure; more distributed gateways require secured OPC UA connections, credentials, gateway certificates, access management, and OT-tested updates.[32][7][12][26] This is a security design benefit only when the local gateway, plant network, administrator identities, local storage, and update process are secured; moving data locally is not a security control by itself.

AWS provides several relevant gateway-security mechanisms. A SiteWise Edge connection to an OPC UA source can use a selected message-security mode and security policy, while source-server authentication can use credentials stored in AWS Secrets Manager.[32] Local REST API access requires credentials, an SDK endpoint directed to the gateway IP address, and registration of the gateway certificate.[7] Gateway access can use LDAP directory credentials, Linux credentials, or Microsoft Windows credentials.[12]

These controls add operational tasks. Certificates must be issued, registered, renewed, revoked, and protected. Directory groups must align with operational roles. Source-server credentials must be rotated without interrupting production. The gateway’s operating system, application runtime, local disks, brokers, containers, and local network interfaces become part of the OT attack surface.

NIST’s OT guidance explains why this must be managed differently from ordinary IT. NIST states that OT prioritizes safety, followed by availability, integrity, and confidentiality, whereas information-security programmes typically prioritize confidentiality, integrity, and availability.[26] OT systems increasingly use Ethernet, IP, wireless devices, standard computers, operating systems, and network protocols; these choices improve connectivity but reduce isolation from external systems and increase the need for security.[26] NIST says OT security controls must account for unique performance, reliability, and safety requirements.[36] A security measure that interferes with safety or time-critical operation is unacceptable.[26]

The appropriate architecture separates IT and OT zones while allowing controlled data exchange. ITU-T Y.4228 says OT and IT networks can be interconnected through industrial gateways such as firewalls, data diodes, and intrusion-protection systems, while a DMZ is common between them.[9] The same recommendation calls for security and information protection across layers, including communication security, data-management security, service-provision security, mutual authentication and authorization, and audit.[9] It also says industrial firewalls can control and filter external access and record access operations for security audit and tracing.[9]

Judgment — predictive-maintenance gateways should be treated as managed OT assets, not as ordinary unmanaged IT appliances. They need a defined asset owner, hardened baseline, least-privilege identities, segmented network placement, monitored remote administration, backups of configuration and models, tested recovery procedures, and approval-controlled updates.

Microsoft describes nested edge nodes that form daisy chains across otherwise isolated ISA-95 network levels: lower-level devices use a parent edge node for telemetry and updates without opening unrestricted communication across the plant network.[90]

Maintainability is the other major trade-off. Edge can simplify certain tasks. AWS lets an operator configure data sources, sampling, metrics, custom functions, visualizations, and deployment from the cloud console, then copy the settings to the local gateway.[6] It provides the same SiteWise query APIs locally and in the cloud, so local applications can use common interfaces for live and historical data, transforms, and metrics.[6] AWS CloudFormation templates can automate creation and management of SiteWise ingestion and asset-modeling resources and can be reused across accounts and Regions.[6]

MQTT can further reduce integration friction. AWS says SiteWise Edge’s publish-subscribe and hub-and-spoke topology uses the open MQTT standard to simplify integration between equipment, middleware, and production systems.[5] The MQTT-enabled gateway can use either its built-in MQTT broker to establish a Unified Name Space or MQTTv5 broker-to-broker bridging to connect with existing MQTT infrastructure.[10] This makes it easier to add consumers of a machine signal without building a distinct point-to-point integration for every consuming system.

But maintainability becomes distributed. A cloud-only application might involve a smaller number of centrally administered services. A fleet of gateways introduces many deployment sites, hardware configurations, disk capacities, certificates, local accounts, data-retention settings, local broker states, version dependencies, and intermittent-network scenarios. ETSI’s MEC architecture describes the corresponding management responsibilities: host-level management must manage application lifecycles, service authorizations, traffic rules, DNS configuration, and virtualized compute, storage, and networking resources.[11]

HiveMQ lists persistent-to-disk messaging, offline queuing, a masterless cluster, and zero-downtime upgrades as platform capabilities, rather than a measured customer availability SLA.[89]

Microsoft describes an AI module image as typically at least 1 GB and says replacing a model separately is easier than replacing the entire image; module twins synchronize model metadata.[86]

The need for disciplined change management is especially acute in manufacturing OT. NIST says software updates in OT often cannot be applied as quickly as in IT because vendors and end users need to test them, outages may need to be planned days or weeks in advance, and the system may require revalidation. NIST says OT change management requires OT experts working with security and IT personnel.[26] A gateway software update, broker upgrade, new ML model, or certificate change may seem minor from an IT perspective but can disrupt data acquisition or an operator’s confidence in the diagnostic system.

Azure IoT Edge 1.1.0 was a long-term-support release that received critical-security and regression fixes.[90]

AWS’s own documentation illustrates both a maintainability advantage and a boundary. For MQTT-enabled V3 gateways, OPC UA discovery settings can be changed dynamically through console settings without restarting the gateway.[32] This reduces disruption for that configuration change.

Conversely, AWS specifies that a SiteWise Edge gateway disconnected from cloud for 30 days automatically disables the configured OPC UA source.[7] A maintainable deployment must therefore monitor edge-cloud synchronization, gateway storage, source status, and offline duration rather than assuming that a local edge box can be ignored once installed.

The Edge Data Processing Pack itself is a product-lifecycle consideration. AWS documentation states that the Data Processing Pack is no longer available to new customers, although existing customers can continue using it.[7] A manufacturer designing an as-of-2024 architecture should therefore confirm product eligibility, supported platforms, feature roadmap, migration path, and long-term support before making a gateway-specific processing feature central to a multi-year maintenance program.

Vendor claims should be separated from real operational implications. Siemens describes Industrial Edge as an open software platform integrating IT with the shop floor and making data handling simple, scalable, and reliable.[31] Its architecture has three components: Siemens-operated Industrial Edge Hub, customer-operated Industrial Edge Management, and Industrial Edge Runtime on in-factory industrial devices, with global and local application repositories and local application execution.[31] This is a useful illustration of the management split: central software distribution and governance coexist with customer-operated fleet management and local execution.

Siemens also describes the environment as a secured ecosystem with hardware, runtime, and separated application containers; it supports apps from Siemens, machine builders, and third parties, plus customer-developed applications in C++, Python, and Java.[31] The flexibility can reduce dependency on a single analytics vendor and allow local adaptation. It also increases the need for application approval, dependency management, container hardening, vulnerability handling, and clear responsibility for applications created by different parties.

The factory-network design must also be managed as part of the maintenance architecture. ITU-T Y.4228 distinguishes factory-internal networks from factory-external networks. The internal network connects production resources and corporate IT systems and encompasses OT and IT networks.[9] It notes that industrial wireless technology is commonly used for non-critical applications including material transport, inventory management, patrol inspection, and maintenance, especially where cable connection is difficult or impossible.[9] The recommendation does not present wireless as a universal replacement for wired critical control.

5G and private networks can support some edge deployments, but they are not themselves edge computing. 5G-ACIA states that industrial automation communications have demanding quality-of-service requirements, especially availability and latency, which public mobile networks cannot satisfy.[23] A 5G non-public network can be administered by the user organization or operated by a contracted provider.[23] It can use separate credentials, isolation from public networks, and accountability for industrial-network availability.[23] These network decisions affect whether an edge gateway can reliably reach machines and local applications, but they do not substitute for correct data placement, local processing, or OT security engineering.

The vendor and research examples also show that predictive-maintenance return is not identical to edge value. Siemens reports maintenance and downtime improvements in its Senseye cases, but the cited presentation does not identify the precise cloud-edge allocation for those deployments.[16] The 2022 shop-floor study explicitly identifies edge feature extraction and cloud LSTM anomaly detection, but does not publish business savings, millisecond inference latency, data-reduction percentage, or an availability improvement.[4] AWS documents a configurable local-processing platform, but its materials primarily describe capabilities rather than measured plant-wide outcomes.[7]

The appropriate conclusion is not that edge has no measurable value. It is that the benefits must be measured at the actual boundary changed by the design:

- **Latency:** measure sensor-to-local-decision time and sensor-to-cloud-decision time under the same load and network conditions.
- **Bandwidth:** measure bytes sent from plant to cloud before and after edge filtering or aggregation, using identical source streams and time periods.
- **Availability:** measure local dashboard, local metric, alert, and cloud synchronization availability separately; do not use a cloud API SLA as a proxy for production availability.
- **Maintenance:** measure technician hours, deployment failures, mean time to repair, patch lead time, and configuration-drift incidents across the gateway fleet.
- **Security:** measure asset inventory completeness, identity coverage, certificate-expiry incidents, unapproved software, segmentation violations, and recovery-test results.

Judgment — these are the operating metrics that decide whether edge distribution is improving predictive maintenance rather than merely moving software from one location to another.

At AWS’s stated minimum specifications, the Data Processing Pack requires **16 times** the RAM and **25.6 times** the available disk capacity of the Data Collection Pack (16 GB versus 1 GB; 256 GB versus 10 GB). This quantifies the local-resource step-up when a gateway is sized for edge processing rather than collection alone.[6]

The RFC states that industrial data can reveal trade secrets, including production-line setups, and that indefinite cloud storage increases data-leakage risk.[33]

RFC 9556 states that an IoT gateway can operate independently in disconnected mode. It defines IoT resilience as autonomous operation during disconnected periods, possibly in a degraded mode, to preserve the integrity and safety of the controlled system.[33]

Cloud computing offers near-limitless scaling and on-demand computing resources without large up-front hardware costs, while edge infrastructure has limited workload size.[37] Cloud operation can delegate hardware, operating-system, and virtual-machine management to the platform provider.[37] These cloud advantages contrast with the distributed hardware and lifecycle responsibilities of the factory-edge estate.[37][11]

Some IoT edge systems use virtualized compute, storage, and networking resources to provide secure multi-tenancy.Open software and standards provide common languages and protocols across diverse edge devices, components, and platforms, promote interoperability, and help prevent vendor lock-in by increasing freedom to choose providers and technologies.[37]

At AWS’s stated price of $200 per active Data Processing Pack gateway per month, the recurring software charge is **$2,400 per active gateway-year** before the gateway hardware, installation, connectivity, and support costs.[34]

Predictive-maintenance alerts are normally advisory maintenance inputs, not replacements for independently engineered machine-safety or protective-shutdown functions. Edge models also need version control, validation before release, monitoring for model drift, tested rollback, and an update procedure that remains safe when the gateway is offline.

The edge gateway can acquire, clean, filter, aggregate, transform, and locally expose machine data, while cloud services retain long-term storage, enterprise context, cross-site analytics, and centralized configuration.[4][5][6][7] The demonstrated architecture supports local access through temporary cloud interruptions and can retain gateway data for up to 30 days, subject to disk capacity.[5][6]

The strongest quantified requirements are communication targets—99.99% availability and transfer intervals from 100 ms to 60 s for plant asset management—not demonstrated edge-caused SLA gains.[1] Edge provides a direct mechanism to reduce upstream traffic by sending features or aggregates rather than raw signals, but the manufacturing evidence does not publish a percentage reduction.[4][7][12] The trade-off is decisive: less dependence on cloud transport and more local operational continuity come with gateway hardware, distributed lifecycle management, certificate and identity administration, local-storage limits, and OT-specific cybersecurity obligations.[6][7][12][26]

## Autonomous mobile robots

### a before-and-after architecture comparison (cloud-centric vs. edge-distributed)

Judgment — An autonomous mobile robot (AMR) is a driverless industrial vehicle that senses its surroundings, navigates through a facility, and performs material-handling tasks. Edge computing changes the placement of fleet functions, not the robot’s inherent autonomy: onboard localization, navigation, obstacle avoidance, trajectory execution, and safe movement remain robot capabilities; a site-near fleet-control tier coordinates shared routes, traffic, zones, charging, and common resources; and central services retain fleet-wide optimization, enterprise workflow integration, reporting, and long-term analytics.[38][25][15][26]

Picking, put-away and transport between production steps are common AMR or automated guided vehicle applications.[91]

A cloud-centric AMR architecture sends robot telemetry and infrastructure sensing data over Wi‑Fi, private cellular, or another wireless network to a cloud service. In Nokia’s cloud-enhanced robotics design, data from infrastructure cameras, radio localization, and onboard sensors is sent to a cloud digital twin—a shared software representation of the fleet and its operating environment. Cloud analytics and path-planning algorithms then feed a multi-vendor fleet orchestrator, which directs the robot fleet to meet factory requirements.[39] The 2017 Rapyuta fleet-management implementation illustrates the same division: the server ran global planning, while each physical robot retained localization and autonomous navigation processes.[38]

This cloud model centralizes decisions that benefit from a common view: assigning jobs, resolving traffic conflicts and deadlocks, scheduling charging, integrating doors and conveyors, and adapting routes when the operating environment changes. Fleet control is explicitly responsible for those functions in the VDA 5050 control model.[25] It also fits cloud-managed warehouse software.

Zebra described FetchCore in 2021 as a cloud-based platform for developing and deploying workflows, and its Fetch Cloud Robotics Platform as combining AMRs with unified controls and data across intralogistics.[40][41]

The dependency is that cloud-side coordination requires wireless reachability and functioning shared services. The Rapyuta authors identify network latency, data interaction, and security as cloud-robotics challenges. In their single-master Robot Operating System (ROS) arrangement, failure of the shared ROS master stops service-based communication; already established topic connections can continue, but new topics cannot be created.[38] The evaluated cloud platform had no provision for managing communication bandwidth for different traffic conditions; the authors state that logically separating control and data traffic improves robot-to-cloud (R2C) responsiveness during remote tele-operation, and that Multi-Path TCP can use multiple interfaces to compensate for congestion on one channel.[38] They also found that making every robot topic visible to every other robot raises access-control concerns and network bandwidth requirements.[38]

An edge-distributed architecture places the time-sensitive control path nearer to the motion. The robot keeps local sensing, safety sensors, actuator control, obstacle avoidance, trajectory following, motion control, and execution of an accepted order. In the warehouse architecture described in the AMR thesis, an onboard industrial computer processes sensor data for real-time navigation: simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) builds a map while locating the robot, and local planning detects unmodelled obstacles and changes the trajectory.[42] A site-local fleet-control service and MQTT message broker coordinate traffic, job allocation, maps, charging, zones, and shared resources.[25][42] An MQTT broker is a publish/subscribe message intermediary: robots and fleet control send messages to named topics rather than maintaining a direct connection to every other participant.

Verizon Private Edge combines private 5G with AWS Outposts, which installs AWS infrastructure at the customer site.[92]

VDA 5050 is the standardized interface between that master control and the vehicles; VDA and VDMA published version 2.1.0 on 20 August 2024 for mixed in-plant robot fleets.[43][44]

ETSI’s July 2024 description states the architectural direction directly: today, AGV control intelligence is hosted inside the vehicle; in the future, centralized fleet control is to be hosted in an edge cloud, requiring reliable wireless communication with all vehicles. It also anticipates a shift from pre-described routes to more flexible target-based navigation.[15] An edge cloud is site-near compute capacity, often on premises or close to the private cellular network, rather than a distant public-cloud region.

The division must be deliberate. Robots can plan their own trajectories, while fleet control holds the complete route graph and applies restrictions over which robot may traverse each edge.[25] A robot executes its assigned route and actions locally; it decides when a node has been traversed and triggers actions attached to that node.[25] But fleet control remains necessary to manage shared traffic and permissions.

For example, a robot must request permission before entering an interactive zone and must not enter if the response is not received in time.[25]

**The transformation therefore replaces continuous remote-control dependence with distributed autonomy: robots make immediate movement decisions locally, while an edge fleet tier makes shared operational decisions close enough to the facility to support timely coordination.**[15][25]

A proposed medium-scale warehouse design makes the edge placement more concrete. It places four Edge servers inside the warehouse, assigns each server a zone of approximately 24 AMRs, and transfers SLAM and Navigation Planning—identified in the design as 70% of robot computational load—to the Edge. Control and safety, environment sensing and safety sensors, actuator control, and the communications module remain on the AMR for deterministic real-time operation.[42] The same design uses Collaborative SLAM: robots share captured information so that the Edge tier builds and updates a global map, and a later-added robot can use that existing map rather than rebuild it independently.[42]

A 2024 line-less assembly framework separates robot, edge-system, description, communication and deployment layers; the robot retains low-level motion and sensor collection while the shared edge handles point-cloud processing, localization, adaptive navigation and GPU-based object-pose estimation.[94]

The 2024 line-less assembly proof of concept used two Robotnik RB-Kairos mobile manipulators, each equipped with cameras, laser scanners, an inertial measurement unit and encoders, for pick-and-place work.[94]

A concrete edge-distributed warehouse design uses collaborative simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM): each Edge server manages robots in its zone and builds an updated local map from AMR information. Each Edge periodically sends the central server the representative keyframes required to integrate local maps, rather than the complete map; the central server distributes updated global-map information back to Edge servers when requested. The central server integrates the local maps, maintains the warehouse-wide view, calculates cross-zone routes, and assigns missions from robot position, availability, and zone state.[42] This establishes a hierarchical architecture: local Edge processing for zone operation and a central site service for decisions spanning zones.

A 2024 autonomous-driving-robot experiment provides a more aggressive offload pattern. Its Edge-Driving Robotics Platform (EDRP) placed perception, global planning, local planning, and ROS-master functions in Kubernetes pods on 5G mobile edge computing (MEC); an EMQX MQTT broker carried robot LiDAR/GPS data to the platform and returned generated control signals to the robot. The Local Dynamic Map Platform (LDMP), which did not require ultralow latency, remained in a region cloud for dynamic-object sharing and storage.[51] This is edge-distributed computation, but not elimination of the cloud: long-term telemetry storage, fleet-wide analytics, model training, software lifecycle management, reporting, enterprise integration, and historical processing remain cloud functions in the private-5G/edge/cloud continuum.[47]

Rapyuta reported on 27 September 2021 that its **70 AMRs** in production at client warehouses had completed more than **1.6 million picks**.[52] Rapyuta also states that well-defined open interfaces support third-party WMS and ERP integration, and that a hardware-maintenance company with access to operational data, hardware, and usage can perform timely maintenance and guarantee high uptime.[53]

### quantified impacts on latency (target milliseconds), bandwidth reduction (% data reduction), and availability/SLA improvements

For ordinary mobile-robot operation, the relevant latency target is not the sub-millisecond target used for tightly coupled machine motion control. The 5G-ACIA industrial-automation paper gives a maximum one-way latency of **500 ms** for periodic communication in standard mobile-robot operation.[23] ETSI’s 2024 mobile-robot use case gives a **40–500 ms** transfer interval for periodic standard operation and traffic management, with communication-service availability above **99.9999%** and mean time between failures of approximately one year.[15] A transfer interval is the time between scheduled communications, not necessarily the end-to-end response time of every command.

Safety-relevant control has stricter requirements. ETSI identifies emergency stopping and obstacle avoidance as time-critical functions for direct AGV control, whereas route updates affecting following vehicles are less time-critical.[15] This distinction is the **control-path rule**: safety sensing, local obstacle avoidance, braking, and low-level motion control should not depend on a remote cloud round trip; fleet scheduling, task allocation, utilization reporting, and longer-horizon route optimization can tolerate the communications service defined for fleet operation. Mobile robots also require time-critical interaction with storage racks, stationary robots, and moving machinery, making local integration important at hand-off points.[15]

As of 2024, calculate AMR bandwidth reduction as off-site bytes before versus after for the same workload and period; report local radio traffic separately. ETSI (2024) says robot-side pre-compression can reduce uplink data rate, and edge preprocessing can reduce the amount sent to cloud servers.[15][27] Camera-based navigation has high data-rate needs, while sensor-based navigation requires less traffic; ETSI says devices may pre-compress transport-service data to reduce uplink rate.[15] VDA-style fleet messages similarly consolidate robot state into one topic, reducing broker and fleet-control workload relative to separate status messages, though the interface provides no percentage.[25] The standard requires state updates on relevant events or at least every 30 seconds, while the integrator defines visualization-update frequency.[25]

The 2021 Runespoor study provides a useful mechanism rather than an AMR deployment result. It compressed high-definition camera video at the edge, sent it to a server for deep-learning analytics, and used analytics output to adjust the edge data rate.[46] Its controller detected and switched configurations in approximately **500 ms**, compared with approximately **10 seconds** for AWStream; its measured end-to-end per-frame latency ranges were **410–530 ms** and **330–420 ms** for the two applications reported.[46] Those values show why remote perception analytics may help fleet awareness but should not replace immediate robot safety control.

As of 2024, define the service and failure boundary before comparing availability: AMR mission completion, site-local fleet-service uptime, and remote cloud-service uptime are not interchangeable SLAs. ETSI (2024) defines communication-service availability through logical-link latency, packet-transmission reliability, and application survival time; AWS IoT Core measures successful requests by AWS Region and excludes customer equipment and internet access beyond its demarcation point.[2][14] Communication-service availability is not whole-fleet availability: ETSI says it depends on link latency, packet-transmission reliability, and the application’s survival time—the time it can remain safe or useful without successful communication.[2] The above-99.9999% ETSI figure is therefore a requirement for the communication service, not proof that edge deployment increased uptime by that amount.

AWS IoT Core’s cloud SLA, for comparison, was at least **99.9% monthly uptime per AWS Region** in its May 2022 SLA, based on successful requests in five-minute intervals and excluding, among other things, internet access beyond AWS’s demarcation point and customer equipment problems.[14]

Edge distribution improves operational resilience by defining local behavior during disconnection. A disconnected VDA 5050 robot can fulfil its order only to its last released node; if fleet control has not released the next route segment, it stops at the decision point.[25] Likewise, robots must not enter a zone or deviate into a permission-controlled corridor when authorization is absent or late; configured loss-of-release behavior is either return to the prescribed trajectory or stop for manual intervention.[25] This design preserves safety and prevents conflicting movement, but it can reduce throughput during prolonged fleet-control outages.

**The numerical record supports stringent communications targets and local fail-safe behavior, not a measured claim that edge distribution delivers a specific percentage improvement in AMR fleet availability, latency, or bandwidth.**[15][25][2]

The warehouse thesis adds deployment-sizing figures for an edge-offload design rather than a cloud-to-edge data-reduction result. Its 3–10-AMR baseline estimates Fleet Server traffic at **180 kbit/s average** and **220 kbit/s peak per robot**, or **660 kbit/s to 2.2 Mbit/s** in aggregate. Its modeled 95-AMR edge design assumes 2D LiDAR at 20 scans per second and 5 kB per scan, or **100 kB/s per robot**; it calculates **76 Mbps** for the fleet and **325 Mbps** total network capacity after assuming 30% efficiency and 69.35 Mbps of base capacity.[42] This shows that offloading SLAM and navigation can increase site-network traffic even as it reduces robot processing, rather than inherently reducing bandwidth.[42]

For resilience, the proposed four-server Edge cluster uses standby virtual-machine replicas: four primary zone virtual machines run in normal operation, backups remain powered off but are updated through state snapshots, and the node holding a replica starts it if the primary server becomes unavailable.[42] The design uses Proxmox VE High Availability and ZFS Replication, which uses server-local storage and avoids separate shared-storage infrastructure.[42] This establishes a site-edge failover pattern alongside robot-level safe degradation; it does not replace the communications availability target for mobile-robot traffic.[15][42]

Frame replication and elimination for reliability duplicates traffic across two independent 5G user-plane paths and two user-equipment devices, then removes duplicates at egress; it addresses a communications-path failure without making the robot or its safety system fault tolerant.[95]

The 2022 intralogistics feasibility assessment gives a more demanding AGV communications requirement than periodic fleet operation: **15–20 ms** latency, data rate below **10 Mbit/s**, localization accuracy below **5 cm**, device density **0.1 devices/m²**, and availability classes **5–7**. The paper defines those classes as **99.999%**, **99.9999%**, and **99.99999%** time able to provide required data within performance targets.[45] Its Release 16 assessment found none of its specified intralogistics use cases fully feasible, identifying stated **95%** availability as an obstacle for higher-safety and mobility uses; it also found that the AGV localization requirement exceeded Release 17’s stated maximum **20 cm** localization accuracy.[45] The paper therefore assigns alternative localization technology to high-accuracy cases while using 5G for data transmission.[45]

The 2024 EDRP experiment measured an average end-to-end robot-sensor-to-computation-to-control-signal round-trip time of approximately **145 ms**. A 5G-MEC offloading configuration without Kubernetes averaged approximately **142 ms**, a **3–4 ms** difference; Kafka transmission of an encoded Collective Perception Message averaged approximately **42 ms**.[51] The article cites Zhang et al.

Across 60,000 closed-loop operations in the evoBOT testbed, predictive scheduling achieved 4.1–4.9 ms mean one-way latency, versus 3.7–4.0 ms with static allocation; reactive scheduling took 6.3 ms in ideal attenuated conditions and 7.3 ms on a 3GPP TDLC channel.[93]

In Bosch’s industrial trial, tested packet sizes produced approximately 1 ms one-way uplink and downlink radio latency; this was not an end-to-end perception or motion-control measurement.[95]

Private-5G AMR traffic requires Quality of Service (QoS) classification. Emergency-related messages, vehicle state, control-related traffic, fleet coordination, telemetry, video, and bulk data have different operational importance; QoS policies assign these traffic classes different priorities and resource characteristics so that high-bandwidth sensor streams do not consume resources required by control and safety-related communication.[47]

Outdoor AMR coverage planning evaluates base-station placement, antenna configuration, frequency selection, handover behavior, and coverage overlap against intended robot routes because buildings, containers, vehicles, vegetation, terrain, and other structures create shadowed regions. As of 2024, no single cloud-centric AMR latency in milliseconds is a valid baseline across all fleets and networks: VDA 5050 leaves the visualization update rate to the integrator, 5G-ACIA specifies a maximum **500 ms** one-way latency for periodic standard mobile-robot operation, and ETSI specifies a **40–500 ms** transfer interval for standard operation and traffic management. A comparison requires a measured robot-to-service path for each case.[25][23][15]

In the 2024 EDRP experiment, Kubernetes added about **2.11%** to the 5G-MEC round-trip path: approximately 145 ms versus 142 ms without Kubernetes.[51]

This makes the site-local radio/edge link, rather than the upstream central link, the dominant listed inter-tier traffic path.[42]

In evoBOT’s attenuated and TDLC conditions, predictive scheduling used 47% and 23% fewer 5G physical resource blocks per millisecond, respectively, than optimized reactive scheduling; mean uplink latency was 34% and 33% lower, respectively.[93]

The 2024 EDRP experiment demonstrated multi-robot execution rather than an availability SLA: it managed **five robots** simultaneously, with average GPU use approximately **50%** for three robots and around **70%** for five.[51]

### trade-offs in cost, security, and maintainability with cited vendor case studies or real-world examples

Edge distribution adds onsite hardware and operational responsibility. A facility needs robot compute, a resilient wireless network, broker or fleet-control capacity, local integration with doors and conveyors, and often redundant edge nodes. Nokia’s Industrial Edge positioning makes the trade-off explicit: it keeps operational technology data on premises and supports local applications, while claiming that real-time robot control needs low-latency on-premises edge connectivity unavailable from a remote cloud.[54] The cost is therefore shifted from remote compute and WAN dependence toward local infrastructure, lifecycle management, and OT/IT integration.

Cloud robotics can reduce per-robot compute cost by offloading heavy workloads. The Rapyuta authors describe cloud robotics as augmenting robot capabilities while reducing per-unit cost through offloading computationally intensive tasks.[38] That advantage weakens when the application needs persistent high-rate perception data or must work through outages.

Verizon positions its private mobile-edge-compute model as a way to reduce AMR hardware and battery needs by placing compute locally.[92]

The same study found cloud robotics generated the least network traffic in one experiment, but did not quantify the difference; in a second image-transfer experiment, network use was almost identical across the cloud, single-master, and multi-master designs, while cloud mode consumed the most CPU at both client and server.[38] The cost decision is workload-specific: remote analytics can lower vehicle hardware requirements, but local safety and real-time functions still require capable onboard or site-near compute.

BMW provides a real industrial interoperability example. In the VDA/VDMA August 2024 release, BMW said it had already integrated transport robots from different manufacturers through VDA 5050 and was adding more AGVs; it called VDA 5050 a mandatory prerequisite for those integrations. BMW further said new AGV types could be integrated significantly faster, with less risk and lower cost.[43] The result supports lower integration friction for heterogeneous fleets, but it is not a quantified comparison of cloud and edge performance.

Nokia provides a more direct edge-AMR example. Its AAU laboratory use case offloaded industrial AMR navigation to an Edge Cloud over 5G: the robot streamed lidar and other sensory data to edge-based navigation and route planning, which returned drive-control information. Nokia said the arrangement makes data from all AMRs available for fleet control and route planning, permits compute upgrades without modifying each robot, and reduces robot power drain.[55] This is a vendor-described successful use case; it reports no millisecond latency, bandwidth percentage, or availability SLA.

At Corning’s Hickory plant, Gestalt Robotics processed AMR sensor data with edge-based vision and machine-learning models and returned autonomous-navigation and environmental-sensing commands over private 5G.[92]

Nokia also reported a factory orchestration trial in Chennai, India. Average distance travelled per robot mission fell **28%**, and average task-completion time for the first set of tasks fell **40%**; Nokia said the system had then run continuously for more than two years.[39] These are operational fleet outcomes, not measured networking latency or an uptime guarantee. They nevertheless show the economic purpose of fleet-level orchestration: fewer metres, fewer traffic jams, and more missions from the same robot fleet.[39]

As of 2024, local processing can avoid exporting raw images and limit data propagation toward public networks, but onboard computers, wireless links, and site-edge fleet servers enlarge the distributed attack surface and require secure access and updates. MQTT deployments require TLS, client authentication, and topic-level authorization; gateways can encrypt data in transit and restrict access to authorized users.[27][44][56] VDA 5050 itself is an interoperability interface, not a safety or cybersecurity standard; it leaves secure communication and data protection mechanisms outside the guideline.[25] Its recommended fleet-management controls are TLS encryption, root certificates, and a robot-specific key pair.[25] HiveMQ’s VDA 5050 architecture recommends TLS encryption for MQTT communication, client authentication during the connect phase, and topic-level authorization so that only authenticated clients can publish or subscribe.

It recommends a whitelist approach, including pattern replacement in client-specific topic filtering, to restrict each vehicle to its own topic path.[44] These controls address spoofed robots, unauthorized commands, and excessive topic access—the risks made visible in broad-topic ROS deployments.[38]

Private industrial wireless can narrow the exposure boundary. A 5G non-public network (NPN) is administered by the industrial organization or its service provider; dedicated credentials, isolation from public networks, and accountability for availability are cited reasons to use one.[23] Network slicing isolates and separates traffic types and can isolate the logical networks mandated by IEC 62443; 5G user equipment can support up to eight slices—for example, one for monitoring data and another for Industrial Ethernet control traffic.[23] These controls support separation, but they add network-design, credential, and monitoring duties.

As of 2024, central fleet software can be updated in one place, whereas distributed robots and edge servers require coordinated software and hardware versions, map activation, diagnostics, rolling upgrades, and staged firmware delivery. VDA 5050 records robot software and hardware versions and stages maps before activation; HiveMQ recommends rolling upgrades, monitoring, validation, and message tracing; Nokia’s Kalmar case manages and schedules edge-delivered firmware updates.[25][44][57] Maps must be staged on robots before activation to minimize downtime; fleet control coordinates activation, while robots report insufficient map storage rather than deleting maps themselves.[25] Robot software, certificates, broker policies, maps, and edge applications require coordinated version management.

Edge-platform tooling can coordinate virtual-machine and container deployment and updates, local failover, and monitoring of CPU, memory, disk, and connectivity.[97]

NIST notes that OT updates require vendor and end-user testing, may need planned outages weeks ahead, and can require revalidation; patches can introduce production or safety risks.[26] Conversely, distributed operations can improve maintainability when managed well: the HiveMQ design recommends dynamic broker clustering and rolling upgrades; it parses each PUBLISH message against the expected JSON Schema, provides Control Center broker-behavior monitoring and fundamental metrics, and lets operators view an MQTT client’s status and record traces of messages between that client and the broker. Nokia’s Kalmar case describes edge-delivered firmware updates that the operator can schedule to avoid interrupting vital operations.[44][57]

**The trade-off is favorable when fast local behavior and heterogeneous-fleet interoperability have greater operational value than the added burden of site compute, private wireless, credential management, testing, and multi-component lifecycle operations.**[43][55][23][26]

The proposed 95-AMR Edge model quantifies an energy-cost scenario. Assuming computation is 30% of each robot’s energy use, it calculates that offloading 50% of computation saves **15%** of total battery consumption and offloading 70% saves **21%**, for which the design assumes **20%**. For 95 AMRs operating continuously, it estimates **58.37–77.82 kWh** daily energy savings and annual electricity-cost savings of **€3,198–€4,261** at an assumed Spanish industrial electricity price of **€0.15/kWh**.[42] The same architecture adds four Edge servers, radio capacity, virtual-machine replication, and high-availability operations; its design conclusion is that offloading reduces robot processing and improves coordination while increasing dependence on the network and computing infrastructure and requiring stronger quality-of-service and redundancy arrangements.[42]

The 2024 line-less assembly proof of concept shared edge infrastructure comprising two AMD EPYC 75F3 processors, 256 GB RAM and an NVIDIA A10 GPU with 24 GB, rather than duplicating high-performance compute on each robot.[94]

Maintainability changes at both robot and fleet levels. The AMR thesis states that, when every robot independently runs navigation and control algorithms, each software update, configuration change, or correction must be applied individually and the management effort grows considerably with fleet size.[42] Its shared-map design lets a newly introduced robot use the existing global map, while the zone-server pattern concentrates part of fleet computation and recovery management in the Edge cluster.[42] Rapyuta likewise states that physically distributed, constantly updated software stacks require management infrastructure rather than an install-and-forget approach.[58]

Cloud-to-robot control expands the network security boundary as well. In The Construct Robotics Institute’s February 2023 Open-RMF training arrangement, cloud computers commanded a robot in a Barcelona cafeteria; the speaker identified establishing a VPN between cloud and edge robots as the required concern.[59]

Security operations also need signed software and firmware updates, least-privilege authorization for commands, vulnerability handling, and physical protection of robots, chargers, network equipment, and edge servers. These controls add lifecycle work but reduce the risk that a compromised device or credential can alter fleet behavior.

The 2024 EDRP experiment reports a 30% reduction in robot memory use, 80% reduction in GPU use, and 70% reduction in computation power consumption compared with traditional onboard processing. Its measured onboard/offloaded values were more than 18% versus approximately 12% memory use, greater than 60% versus less than 10% GPU use, and approximately 70–80 W versus approximately 20 W computing power. The study tested driving and latency in simple autonomous-driving applications over a stable network environment.[51]

### Synthesis — what the architecture change establishes

Cloud-centric AMR systems centralize fleet knowledge and planning, but robots still need local navigation and obstacle avoidance; a fully remote motion-control model conflicts with the different timing needs of safety functions and fleet coordination.[38][15] Edge distribution makes that division explicit: robots execute locally held routes and fail safely when shared authorization is absent, while an on-site fleet tier manages traffic, maps, and common resources.[25]

The strongest demonstrated business evidence is interoperability and fleet efficiency—BMW’s qualitative integration-cost benefit and Nokia’s reported 28% distance and 40% task-time reductions—not a universal latency, traffic, or SLA gain.[43][39] The practical decision is therefore to design a local control path and treat cloud services as fleet-optimization and enterprise layers, with measurable service objectives for every wireless and edge dependency.

1. Keep collision avoidance, braking, localization, and safe-stop logic operational without cloud reachability.
2. Put fleet traffic control and shared-resource arbitration on a resilient site-near edge tier.
3. Specify latency, transfer interval, availability, and video-uplink budgets separately for each robot flow.
4. Operate edge nodes as OT assets: authenticate robots, encrypt MQTT, segment traffic, test changes, and stage map and certificate updates.

## Energy distribution

In the central supervisory architecture, SCADA centralizes field-data acquisition, monitoring, operator control, and multi-feeder switching decisions at a control centre, while RTUs and PLCs control local processes and protective IEDs can act without control-centre instructions.[26][60] Edge computing expands this existing local capability into coordinated field automation: IEDs and local control packages execute predefined feeder protection, isolation, restoration, and voltage-control actions, while central systems retain broader coordination.[60] This matters because a distribution grid must both react safely in milliseconds to faults and coordinate many assets across a wide area.

As of 2024, the practical direction is not all-edge or all-cloud: it is a layered architecture in which local automation closes fast loops and central systems retain grid-wide optimization, records, planning, and operator oversight.

**Claim — Edge distribution can give field equipment greater local operating capability and reduce dependence on remote connectivity, but grid reliability and cyber-risk examples do not by themselves quantify an edge-caused SLA gain.**

### a before-and-after architecture comparison (cloud-centric vs. edge-distributed)

A **cloud-centric architecture**, in this context, means that telemetry is sent from field equipment to a central control room, central platform, or cloud/data-centre service where supervisory applications analyze conditions and issue commands back to the field. Strictly, many historical utility systems are control-centre-centric rather than public-cloud-hosted. **Supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA)** is the established name for the central system that gathers field data, displays it to operators, alarms abnormal conditions, and sends commands to remote equipment.

NIST describes SCADA as centralized monitoring and control for dispersed assets, including electric transmission and distribution. A control server processes information from remote terminal units (RTUs) and programmable logic controllers (PLCs), while RTUs or PLCs control the local process. Protective relays and other intelligent electronic devices (IEDs) commonly retain local programming that lets them act without waiting for a control-centre instruction.[26] That last point is essential: even the “before” architecture was not safely cloud-only. A fault relay could trip locally, while longer-horizon monitoring, dispatch, and switching decisions travelled through the supervisory system.

The conventional substation baseline connects switchyard equipment to controllers and relays in a control room, historically using copper wires that carry analogue voltage, current, and switch-control signals. RTUs act as gateways between high-voltage field equipment and a central control room; SCADA and the human–machine interface (HMI), the operator’s display and command console, sit at the supervisory layer.[61][62] Earlier RTU/SCADA systems typically updated information in several seconds to minutes, a cadence suited to supervisory visibility but not to protection-speed decision loops.[63][64] Cisco states that SCADA communications have latencies of approximately 500 milliseconds to 5 seconds.

Its IEC 61850 traffic table assigns medium-speed MMS a maximum delay below 100 ms, low-speed MMS below 500 ms, raw Sample Values below 208.3 ms, and file transfer below 1000 ms; it lists low delay for time synchronization and commands.[65]

The centralized supervisory pattern also extends over a **wide-area network (WAN)**—a network joining geographically separated substations and control centres. IEC 60870-5-style telecontrol is described as master–slave communication over serial lines or TCP/IP WANs between power centres, and the cited review notes that communications slow when transferred data are large.[62] A 2024 Department of Energy white paper similarly distinguishes the needs of metering from protection: collecting all customer-meter data can demand more bandwidth than relay-to-relay communications, while system protection is much more time-sensitive.[24]

An **edge-distributed architecture** assigns the fastest, most local decisions to field-resident IEDs, protection relays, feeder controllers, substation gateways, or a substation micro-datacentre. “Edge” therefore means compute and control close to the physical grid process—not necessarily a mobile-network edge. The upper layers still perform functions that require system-wide context: SCADA visibility, advanced distribution management system (ADMS) optimization, historian storage, planning, outage coordination, fleet configuration, and human approval.

IEC 61850 supplies a standards foundation for this placement flexibility. The standard covers utility-automation communications among devices within plants and substations, between stations, and between stations and higher-level operating and maintenance locations. It deliberately does not prescribe where application functions run: functions may be allocated across devices and control levels.[66] An **IED** is the field-grade digital protection, measurement, or control device that hosts one or more automation functions.

A local function can continue without another device if it is not dependent on external links; a distributed function spans two or more physical devices and can block or degrade gracefully if it loses a needed communication link.[67]

The locally time-critical traffic in a digital substation commonly uses IEC 61850 **GOOSE**—Generic Object-Oriented Substation Event—messages. GOOSE is multicast, event-driven relay-to-relay communication that can replace separate hard-wired exchanges. It is designed for fast protection and interlocking, whereas client–server MMS traffic used by SCADA to access IED information is described as non-time-critical.[22][68] The architecture is thus layered by control-loop speed rather than by ideology: local IED-to-IED traffic handles tripping and interlocking, while supervisory traffic moves upwards for broader operations.

In centralized fault location, isolation, and service restoration (FLISR), automated switches and reclosers isolate a damaged section and can transfer unaffected customers through tie lines; the system also draws on communications, distribution-management and outage systems, SCADA, analytics, and network models.[60]

Judgment — **The correct architectural boundary is the control-loop boundary.** Put protection, safety interlocking, and pre-approved feeder restoration at the field or substation edge when their response deadline is shorter than a reliable WAN-plus-central-processing path. Keep system-wide optimization, market-facing functions, long-term analytics, fleet administration, and operator coordination centrally managed. This avoids the false choice between “all cloud” and “all edge.”

The U.S. Department of Energy’s January 2024 grid-communications paper reaches the same operational shape: the evolved grid has distributed computing at utility control centres and consumer premises, and most utility processes combine centralized control with localized autonomous decisions.[24] This makes possible an architecture that is both locally responsive and systemically coordinated.

OpenFMB makes the field-facing part of this layered architecture more concrete. NIST’s July 2020 Technical Note 2066 states that OpenFMB, ratified by the North American Energy Standards Board (NAESB) in March 2016 as RMQ.26, is a smart-grid messaging framework for publish-and-subscribe communication among smart-grid devices.[73] The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory describes OpenFMB as a framework and reference architecture for grid-edge interoperability, ratified by NAESB in 2016, that coordinates grid-edge devices through interoperability and distributed controls, reduces the need for centralized intelligence or control, and enables circuit-level management of distribution systems.[70] EPRI’s April 2019 mapping report states that OpenFMB is a logical bus that can use Data Distribution Service (DDS), Message Queue Telemetry Transport (MQTT), or Extensible Messaging and Presence Protocol (XMPP), allowing microgrid devices to communicate through publish/subscribe integration patterns.[74] Unlike IEC 61850, DNP3, or Modbus transport protocols, OpenFMB defines the information exchanged between devices and supports distributed decision-making over underlying communications mechanisms.[75]

A centralized distribution management system (DMS) is the hub-and-spoke baseline identified in the FLISR hardware-in-the-loop paper: it offers the operator more optimized control, at the cost of time delays.[76] In the edge-distributed alternative, OpenFMB platform-independent message profiles build on portions of IEC 61850 and the common information model (CIM), can be converted to Protocol Buffers (Protobuf), and let each device publish its own data while subscribe to data relevant to its own operation, including through DNP3, Modbus, DDS, MQTT, or adapters.[76] OpenFMB categorizes communications as readings, statuses, events, and controls and uses publish/subscribe messaging; its stated default protocols are MQTT, Neural Automatic Transport System (NATS), and DDS. An example architecture permits simultaneous multiple protocols across devices: North American utilities use DNP-3 for line sensors and shunt capacitors, Modbus for most photovoltaic and battery-energy-storage-system controllers, and ANSI C12 for smart meters.[70] Open Field Message Bus (OpenFMB) enables distributed intelligence at the grid edge, supports distributed peer-to-peer (P2P) control while coordinating with a central controller, and combines centralized control with high-speed distributed P2P communications at scalable levels and future planning changes.[76]

In a field-level example, a recloser detects and isolates a line-to-ground fault, publishes its state to neighbouring devices, and prompts peer reclosers to update their protection settings.[98]

Duke Energy’s OpenFMB hierarchy places DMS, SCADA, meter-data management, outage management, and geographic information systems above substation and grid nodes and endpoint devices; legacy-protocol adapters connect older field equipment to those nodes.[83]

EPRI’s April 2019 report defines the complementary placement of a distributed energy resource management system (DERMS): it aggregates individual distributed energy resources (DERs) into virtual resources, simplifies granular settings into grid services, optimizes groups for minimal cost and maximum power quality, and translates among device protocols and information models.[74] The same DERMS capability can be deployed at the edge, within an ADMS, by a third-party cloud provider, as a substation DERMS in a box, or as a logical node in an OpenFMB microgrid.[74] Enterprise systems such as DMS, geographic information system (GIS), and outage management system (OMS) exchange messages with DERMS through IEC 61968-family interfaces, while DERMS-to-device control can use IEC 61850, DNP3, Modbus, or OpenFMB.[74]

The relevant conclusion is narrow but decisive: **for fault isolation and restoration, the <5 ms and 99.9999% requirements strongly favour local execution of the fastest automation loop over dependence on a remote cloud path.** They are targets, not measured proof that every edge deployment beats every centralized one.[15][60][24]

The data-reduction requirement in the client brief needs an equally precise answer. No cited distribution deployment reports a cloud-to-edge percentage reduction in data transmitted. The closest quantified reduction is EPRI’s report that exception and compression methods in a real-time data historian can reduce *stored* time-series data by roughly **100:1** or **1000:1**. That is storage compression, not edge-caused network-bandwidth reduction.[81] In contrast, a 2024 substation cloud–edge study calls its edge architecture “low bandwidth” and says Mode 3 occupies the platform-to-station network only for uploading inspection results, but it gives no percentage data reduction.[69]

Judgment — **A defensible engineering KPI should separately measure raw field bytes, bytes crossing the WAN, bytes retained locally, and bytes retained centrally.** “Data reduction” is otherwise ambiguous: a gateway may reduce WAN traffic while increasing local storage, replication, or event-message overhead.

Availability must also remain separated from reliability. ETSI states that communication-service availability depends on link latency, packet-transmission reliability, and the application’s survival time, while reliability has no direct one-to-one relationship with availability.[2] EPB’s SAIDI and SAIFI improvements show better delivered electricity service after distribution automation.

The ETSI figures define required communications performance. Neither is a complete end-to-end edge-computing SLA. This distinction rules out claiming a numerical edge-caused availability uplift from the evidence above.

EPB’s reported pre-automation SAIDI of 112 minutes and post-automation SAIDI of 61.8 minutes imply a reduction of **50.2 outage minutes per customer**. This expresses the reported reliability change in the underlying SAIDI unit as well as in percentage terms.[77]

The **<5 ms** automated-switching target is at least **20 times tighter** than the approximately **100 ms** distributed-voltage-control target. The calculation makes explicit that these two grid functions belong to materially different control-loop classes.[15][1]

The FLISR hardware-in-the-loop paper reports a concrete hybrid OpenFMB testbed: five SEL-651R reclosers communicated with a central SEL-3360 computer, and a packet capture showed an acknowledgement returning to the FLISR-running machine within one millisecond of the specified event.[76] The paper reports successful integration of OpenFMB into a centrally coordinated FLISR scheme with a central DMS. It also reports, citing a December 2014 U.S. Department of Energy report, that FLISR can reduce customers affected during an outage by up to 45% and customer outage time by up to 51%.[76]

Using the midpoints of ABB’s stated reverse-blocking sequences, the GOOSE implementation reduces the sequence time by **40.91%**: 65 ms versus 110 ms for the conventional approach.[68]

A 2024 distributed-FLISR simulation makes the control-path contrast explicit. In its centralized FLISR design, fault-point-indicator (FPI) and line-voltage-indicator (LVI) messages travel upstream by MQTT to a remote server, and generated control messages travel downstream to assets identified by their ASDU identifiers.[80] Across 20 identical simulated test cases on each network, centralized average operation time was 212.8 ms on 5G, 260.4 ms on 4G LTE, 267.1 ms on 3G, and 1049.6 ms on 2G. The centralized design therefore sends fault information to a remote server and returns control messages, and its simulated response changes across the tested mobile networks.[80]

In the same 2024 tests, distributed FLISR operated at or near real time at an average of two milliseconds, with network latency not a consideration for the local algorithm’s operation.[80] In the Waterford-grid simulation, one faulted switch initially affected 1,370 customers and produced 78,090 customer-minutes lost (CML) per hour; FLISR reduced these to 171 customers and 9,747 CML per hour.[80] With four faulted switches on the same grid segment, FLISR still left 844 affected customers and 48,108 CML per hour; the authors state that effectiveness depends on circumstances and lessens with numerous simultaneous faults.[80] The tested centralized and distributed algorithms generated identical operational steps for a given fault event across three simulated networks.[80] The simulation platform lacked detailed energy-flow modelling, which SCADA data could enhance.[80]

With four faulted switches on the same Waterford grid segment, FLISR left **844** affected customers and **48,108** customer minutes lost per hour after intervention.[80]

### trade-offs in cost, security, and maintainability with cited vendor case studies or real-world examples

**Cost.** Edge distribution requires more capable field hardware, local compute, hardened communications, redundant power and network paths, engineering, commissioning, and lifecycle support. Chattanooga’s EPB reported about **$48.4 million** to implement distribution automation and integrate it with other systems, excluding the cost of the fibre-optic communications infrastructure. Its deployment installed over **1,200** automated switches and sensors on **171** circuits.[77] The cost produced measurable reliability value: EPB estimated **$26.8 million per year** in customer savings from its normal-operations outage reduction.[77]

Digital substation designs can also remove physical infrastructure costs. IEC 61850 GOOSE can replace individual copper signal wires with networked exchange among IEDs, reducing wiring, trenching, ducts, conduit, relay I/O requirements, and associated engineering.[68][64] A 2023 review reported approximately **20%** project-cost reduction in cited ALSTOM transmission-substation projects in Australia and the United States, and **25%** savings at a Transgrid switching substation in New South Wales. Those are digital process-bus project outcomes, not isolated measurements of edge-compute savings.[62]

The counterweight is that local intelligence does not automatically lower total cost. A 2024 cloud–edge–end substation study characterizes direct central connection as lower construction cost but lower long-term operational efficiency and lower substation-access capacity; its tiered and edge-end alternatives carry higher construction cost.[69] Edge hardware itself has minimum resource requirements: for example, AWS SiteWise Edge’s Data Processing Pack specifies a four-core CPU, 16 GB RAM, and 256 GB disk.[6] That hardware cost is not the whole business case; network resilience, software lifecycle management, and testing often dominate over time.

Maintainability. IEC 61850 improves the prospects for standardized engineering. It defines structured logical nodes and data models, while Substation Configuration Language (SCL), an XML-based configuration format, supports sharing IED configurations among users and suppliers.[67][68] New York Power Authority selected IEC 61850 to reduce wiring, device count, and footprint and to gain centralized remote access and maintenance; it did not need RTUs or dedicated disturbance-recording devices in that implementation.[22] SCE found a centrally managed IEC 61850 remedial-action architecture more rapidly deployable and maintainable as the number of schemes grew, because it could share information and supporting equipment across the system.[22]

But the technology changes the maintenance burden rather than eliminating it. NYPA experienced configuration and field-acceptance delays from IEC 61850 Edition 1 limitations, custom logic, naming conventions, and cross-vendor integration; use of the GIO workaround added maintenance complexity.[22] SCE found that adding an IED to a many-to-many GOOSE scheme could require reconfiguring, and potentially retesting, every participating IED. SCE’s IEC 61850-based MacArthur Substation automation system in Irvine entered service in November 2013, included an IEC 61850 Substation Configuration Tool, Central Management Services, a Cyber Secured Substation Gateway, and an HMI, and was tested in 2014 and 2015.[22] Utilities adopting IEC 61850 needed changed processes, test laboratories, training, configuration tools, automated testing, and new site-testing skills.[22]

KCP&L is the cautionary real-world example. Its smart-substation demonstration used local distributed control processors, IEC 61850 communications, and redundant gigabit fibre rings. The commissioned solution reportedly exceeded its performance specifications and outperformed non-IEC-61850 equivalents.[22] Yet KCP&L chose not to operationalize the approach, planned to return high-speed GOOSE links to copper wiring, and cited an insufficiently documented business case, learning-curve concerns, and unwillingness to support multiple designs with their training, documentation, and process changes.[22] **This establishes that technical performance is necessary but not sufficient: a utility must fund the operating model that keeps distributed automation correct over decades.**

Security. Edge can reduce the amount of raw data sent outside the operational site and permit local autonomy during WAN disruption. It also expands the number of devices, interfaces, remote-update paths, credentials, and software images that require protection. NIST warns that OT adoption of Ethernet, IP, wireless devices, standard computers, and operating systems makes systems less isolated and increases cybersecurity exposure.[26] The security objective in power distribution is not merely confidentiality. Loss of integrity or availability can directly affect protection actions, equipment, public safety, and electric service.[26]

The Ukrainian incidents demonstrate this physical consequence. NIST records that the December 23, 2015 cyberattack affected more than **225,000** customers; attackers remotely operated breakers at more than **50** regional substations and corrupted firmware in serial-to-Ethernet devices.[82] A December 17, 2016 malware attack at a substation outside Kyiv caused an approximately **one-hour** outage for customers of that substation.[82] These incidents do not show that edge computing caused the failures or that edge would have prevented them. They show why local IEDs, gateways, remote access, firmware, and control channels must all be treated as critical assets.

Security architecture therefore needs segmentation, authentication, monitoring, and tested recovery paths. NIST’s IIoT example separates network segments and routes platform-tier communications through a demilitarized zone (DMZ) firewall so that security services can monitor controlled flows to enterprise or internet systems.[26] SCE’s MacArthur Substation deployment included a Cyber Secured Substation Gateway and its Common Cybersecurity Services platform for field-device and back-office communications.[22] IEC 61850-related cybersecurity standards named by NIST cover IED cybersecurity capabilities, secure SCADA communications, automation and control cybersecurity requirements, and electric-power cybersecurity testing.[82]

Judgment — **The safest edge architecture uses local autonomy only for bounded, prevalidated actions; it does not let every gateway become an independent grid optimiser.** It separates protection networks from enterprise analytics, signs and verifies software, applies least-privilege access, records configuration changes, and defines fail-safe or graceful-degradation behaviour for lost communication.

Connectivity is a related cost-security trade-off. A 5G non-public network can provide dedicated credentials, public-network isolation, and accountability for availability; the industrial organization may administer it itself or use a contracted provider.[23] This can improve control over critical traffic, but it also creates a network-operation responsibility that a cloud-only design may shift to a provider.

FLISR networks must remain resilient when the grid itself is damaged, while also providing wide bandwidth and low latency; utilities reported that these requirements raise cost and constrain technology choices.[60] This makes possible dependable local operations, but only where the communications and operations budget matches the protection duty.

Using EPB’s reported $48.4 million implementation cost and 171 circuits, the distribution-automation investment was about **$283,041 per circuit**, excluding fibre infrastructure. This is a simple deployment-scale cost benchmark, not a marginal cost for an additional circuit.[77]

Dividing EPB’s reported $48.4 million implementation cost by its estimated $26.8 million annual customer savings gives a simple recovery period of about **1.81 years**. This uses the reported annual savings estimate and excludes fibre infrastructure from the cost input.[77]

EPB’s deployment of more than 1,200 automated switches and sensors across 171 circuits works out to **more than 7.02 devices per circuit**. This indicates that automated restoration is a distributed field-infrastructure investment rather than a control-centre software change alone.[77]

OpenFMB security and performance trade-off. NIST’s July 2020 OpenFMB proof of concept assigned simulated recloser, battery, solar-photovoltaic-array, and load-generation roles to separate Raspberry Pi 2 Model B nodes and ran the MQTT broker on a separate Raspberry Pi; all simulated microgrid activity and measurement data used OpenFMB-formatted publish-subscribe communications.[73] NIST requires compensating controls beyond those specified in NAESB RMQ.26, including operating-system and MQTT controls.[73] NIST identifies unauthorized device or data access, changes to device settings, service interruptions or outages, and equipment damage as risks from inadequate controls.[73]

NIST specifies certificates for authentication and encryption, restricted administrator accounts and concurrent sessions, session locks, logging and log review, and a comprehensive cybersecurity program covering distribution-network devices, systems, data, and applications.[73] At the MQTT layer, NIST specifies TLS authentication and encryption, unique usernames, limited concurrent topic users, access-control lists, and synchronized system and device time for self-signed and SSL certificates.[73] Duke Energy’s 2016 OpenFMB design adds deny-by-default or whitelisting, traffic engineering, intrusion detection and behavior analysis, authentication, public-key infrastructure (PKI), certificates, confidentiality, authorization, logging, auditing, configuration management, security management, and patch management.[83] These controls make field messaging secure and deterministic, while adding certificate, identity, time-synchronization, logging, patching, and audit operations to the lifecycle burden.[73][83]

The mapping lacks a direct representation for CIM CorrelationID and several optional CIM header fields; it also requires multiple SolarControlProfile messages when a CIM EndDeviceGroup contains multiple solar inverters.[74] OpenFMB has no DER-group versioning in the described profile, leaving DERMS to track additions and removals from a group over time.[74] For DER-group disconnection, the IEC 61968-5 illustrated exchange has the server acknowledge receipt of an EndDeviceControls message with an OK result, then send asynchronous messages notifying the client of each disconnected meter; the illustrated disconnect is successful.[74] These are specific configuration, state-management, and test obligations when utility enterprise control is integrated with device-level edge messaging.[74]

OpenFMB Protocol Buffers are described by EPRI as smaller and 20 to 100 times faster to process than the XML payloads used in the IEC 61968-5 test-script example.[74] This is a payload-processing comparison, not a percentage of WAN bandwidth saved.[74] EPRI states that standards help hold down maintenance costs and promote innovation, and that mapping supports interoperability while minimizing data loss across enterprise and microgrid domains.[74]

Duke Energy’s February 2016 OpenFMB demonstration distributed a microgrid application across 12 conference booths connected by 4G LTE, 900 MHz, wired Ethernet, wireless Ethernet, and fibre. The partners ran near-real-time grid optimization, islanding, and resynchronization functions, with each booth operating part of the application.[84] Duke’s Mount Holly, North Carolina test site included a 250 kW/250 kWh battery energy-storage system, a 100 kW photovoltaic system, a 10 kW rooftop photovoltaic system, and a 500 kW load bank.[83] The example demonstrates that OpenFMB can coordinate heterogeneous grid-edge communications and resources while retaining utility head ends.[83][84]

Security design must also address supply-chain compromise, cloud-identity failures, and insecure remote access, because each can expose operational commands or telemetry. Maintainability requires an accurate asset inventory and patch windows that are scheduled around reliability constraints for long-lived field equipment.

### Cross-use-case verdict

The three use cases in this report share a general transformation: edge computing moves time-sensitive sensing, inference, and actuation nearer to the physical process, while cloud or central platforms retain fleet-scale coordination, historical analysis, and lifecycle management. Energy distribution has the strongest safety case for hard local control boundaries because protection failures can cascade through electrical infrastructure and because its formal communications targets reach the sub-5-ms range.[15][24]

| Use case | Edge-distributed architectural priority | Evidence-supported preference criterion | What would change the verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manufacturing predictive maintenance** | Analyze condition signals near equipment; send selected events, features, and history to central systems. | Preference follows where local analysis prevents unnecessary data transfer or keeps monitoring useful during intermittent connectivity; see Manufacturing predictive maintenance. | A measured comparison showing that centralized analysis meets the required detection time, cost, and connectivity-resilience targets at lower lifecycle cost. |
| **Autonomous mobile robots** | Keep immediate navigation, perception, and safety behaviour on the robot or site edge; coordinate fleet tasks over local and central services. | Preference follows where motion safety and mobility require operation through variable wireless connectivity; see Autonomous mobile robots. | A demonstrated communications service that preserves robot safety and task continuity across the whole operating area, including mobility handovers. |
| **Energy distribution** | Keep protective relaying, interlocking, and predetermined isolation/restoration at substations or feeders; retain central coordination for multi-feeder optimization and system-wide awareness. | The decisive criterion is the **<5 ms** and **99.9999%** target for automated switching, versus WAN-dependent supervisory paths and larger-scale coordination needs. [15][60][24] | A field deployment showing a remote-central architecture meeting the same fault-loop deadline, availability definition, failure conditions, and safety requirements at lower whole-life cost. |

The verdict is not that energy distribution should be “more edge” in every function. SCE’s earlier isolated local remedial-action schemes had limited situational awareness, could not share information or equipment, risked uncoordinated responses, and became difficult to manage at scale; its standards-based centralized system addressed those limits.[22] Conversely, the centralized system depended on careful traffic engineering, redundant infrastructure, and a wide-area optical network.[22] The preference is therefore **hybrid and function-specific**: local execution is stronger for protection-speed, bounded actions; central coordination is stronger where the action requires a broader network model and more switching options.

OpenFMB sharpens the energy-distribution verdict: it distributes device-level information exchange and bounded local control while preserving central DMS, DERMS, ADMS, and control-centre functions for aggregation, grid-wide awareness, optimization, and dispatch.[76][74] The deciding architecture criterion remains the action’s control-loop deadline and required scope: local peer-to-peer or device control serves rapid field response and resilience to delayed or lost DMS communication, while central systems supply the whole-grid view and coordinate distributed devices through controls such as ramp-rate curves.[76][74]

Energy distribution shows edge computing most clearly as a control-placement change. The legacy supervisory architecture concentrates monitoring and higher-level decisions in control centres, but field relays already retain essential local protection. IEC 61850 and OpenFMB extend that local capability into interoperable, distributed automation while retaining central SCADA, ADMS, planning, and maintenance roles.[26][66][70]

The quantitative case is strongest for timing requirements: automated isolation and restoration requires <5 ms and 99.9999% communication-service availability, whereas real deployments such as EPB show large outage-duration improvements without proving a specific edge SLA uplift.[15][77] Edge therefore strengthens resilience to remote-path loss, but it adds field compute, cyberattack surface, configuration, testing, training, and lifecycle obligations. The durable architecture as of 2024 is not cloud replacement; it is controlled distribution of responsibility.[22][82][26]

1. Place protection, interlocking, and pre-approved restoration logic locally where the fault-loop deadline is below credible WAN-plus-central-processing delay.
2. Keep ADMS, SCADA, historians, planning, and cross-feeder optimization centrally coordinated, with explicit fallback rules for lost communications.
3. Report latency, WAN-traffic reduction, communication-service availability, and customer reliability indices separately; they are different measures.
4. Fund IEC 61850/OpenFMB configuration management, test laboratories, workforce training, redundancy, patch testing, and cybersecurity as operating requirements, not optional integration work.

Use-case-specific before-and-after bandwidth-reduction percentages and availability or SLA improvements remain open where the published examples establish local processing capability or a design target but do not report comparable production baselines and outcomes. They would be settled by operational measurements that define the traffic boundary, time period, payloads retained locally and transmitted upstream, availability scope, and outage conditions for the same cloud-centric and edge-distributed service.
