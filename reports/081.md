### Rode RodeCaster Pro II

RØDE’s RØDECaster Pro II is natively configured for four in-room XLR microphone users. It has four balanced analogue Neutrik combo inputs, and its default assignment places inputs 1–4 on faders 1–4. Multitrack mode can retain each track individually alongside the programme stereo mix.[7][23][5]

The four-input architecture is important because its six faders and nine assignable channels are mixer-control assignments, not nine equivalent microphone preamps. RØDE separately identifies four analogue microphone-capable inputs.[27][7]

A five-person local production can use the four wired mic inputs plus one RØDE Series IV wireless transmitter, or add a RØDE USB microphone through USB 2. A six-person production can use four wired microphones plus two compatible wireless transmitters, because the built-in wireless receiver supports up to two transmitters.[27][7][60] This is a workable connection plan, but it is not equivalent to a six-XLR studio: the local sources use mixed wired and wireless signal paths, the USB microphone does not receive RØDECaster channel processing or effects, wireless transmitters need battery and pairing discipline, and the documentation’s USB multitrack layout expressly identifies four individual XLR/TRS tracks.[60][5]

The monitoring constraint is just as material. The RØDECaster Pro II has four direct headphone outputs, each with independent level control. A five- or six-person panel therefore needs a headphone distribution amplifier, shared monitoring, or some contributors without headphones.[6]

**For a conventional studio in which every local contributor uses a wired XLR dynamic microphone and wears headphones, the RØDECaster Pro II is a native four-person recorder and monitor.** A fifth or sixth participant requires a verified input-and-track plan and headphone distribution.[7][6][5][28][61] The supplied listings differ on whether wireless use expands the RØDECaster Pro II beyond four microphones: one describes four wired microphones plus up to two RØDE Series IV transmitters, while Voosestore describes up to four microphones including two wireless microphones.[27][7][62]

#### Preamp noise floor and measured SNR

Signal-to-noise ratio requires both a declared signal and a declared noise measurement reference. EIN—equivalent input noise—describes the input-referred noise contribution of the preamp under stated source and gain conditions. It is valuable for engineering comparison, but it is not the same measurement as dynamic-microphone recording SNR.

RØDE specifies Revolution preamp EIN at −131.5 dBV, A-weighted, with a gain range of 0–76 dB. RØDE also says the preamps can supply demanding dynamic microphones without an inline lifter or booster.[10][6]

RØDE also publishes 113 dB of input dynamic range; this is not a measured dynamic-microphone recording SNR.[153]

A third-party bench observation used a 150-ohm resistor between pins 2 and 3 of input 1, set the preamp to 76 dB maximum gain, and measured noise at −69.47 dB RMS maximum.[40] That is a stated noise-floor observation under a resistor-load test; it does not provide the signal reference needed to calculate SNR.

The RØDECaster Pro II has a strong published gain-and-EIN specification for dynamic-mic work, but it has no comparable measured SNR result using a named dynamic microphone under declared test conditions. The 76 dB gain figure supports use with low-output microphones such as an SM7B without a Cloudlifter-style booster, but it does not justify a numeric SNR ranking against the Zoom or TASCAM.[10][40][52]

#### Onboard processing quality and post-production flexibility

The RØDECaster Pro II has the most extensive dedicated voice-processing set of the three. Its channel chain includes high-pass filtering, de-essing, a noise gate, compressor, three-band EQ, Aural Exciter, Big Bottom and pan; channel reverb and echo are also available as always-on effects, in addition to on-the-fly voice effects accessible using the SMART pads.[4][42] The master Compellor acts on the Main Mix and is intended to make the combined programme more balanced.[42]

VoxLab gives the operator three high-level controls—Depth, Sparkle and Punch—that alter several processing parameters. Advanced mode exposes individual APHEX processors and their settings, while Neutral, Podcast Studio and Broadcast presets can be recalled or used as bases for saved show-specific presets.[42]

For a multi-guest programme, a modest channel compressor helps reduce loudness differences between speakers, and the master Compellor can manage peaks when two guests speak or laugh together.[65] In a practitioner demonstration, Judd recommended fairly transparent dialogue compression with a ratio around 2.5:1 as a starting point; stronger compression sounded extreme, unpleasant and stuffy.[65]

Sound On Sound described the processors as decent. It found the de-esser effective, with threshold, ratio, attack, release, frequency and gain controls; the high-pass filter has 6, 12 or 24 dB/octave slopes and a 20–200 Hz range; and the noise gate offers range and hysteresis control. It found the channel compressor clean and capable, with a maximum 4.5:1 ratio that it considered suitable for dialogue, and noted that the internal processor order is fixed.[31] MusicTech called the DSP effects studio-quality and useful in its review tests.[66] These are positive qualitative assessments, not a measured processor-quality ranking.

The recorder’s post-production design is unusually good for a live-production unit. Multitrack can be recorded pre-fader or post-fader, and the user can exclude channel processing from individual recordings while retaining a processed stereo mix.[5] Pre-fader means later fader moves do not alter the isolated recording; post-fader commits the live level ride. A fully faded-down channel cannot be recovered from a post-fader recording at that point.[5][67]

For a four-microphone show that will be professionally edited, select multitrack, pre-fader capture and processing exclusion for the isolated channels, while keeping the processed stereo mix as a fast reference or immediate delivery file. This gives the producer a finished-sounding live programme and dry source tracks for later EQ, noise repair, edits and level correction.[5]

### Zoom PodTrak P8

The PodTrak P8 is purpose-built for a six-person podcast table. Zoom specifies six mono mic inputs, six independent headphone outputs, and up to six microphones and six pairs of headphones connected simultaneously.[11][2][1] This directly matches four-, five- and six-person in-room panels: each person can have a dynamic XLR microphone, a fader and a personal headphone level.

The P8 can record individual tracks for each input plus the stereo mix, and it is specified for up to 13 simultaneous SD-card tracks.[2][3] At four or five people, spare channels remain for another guest, utility source or changing format. At six local mic users, all local mic channels are occupied.

A remote guest changes that picture. Channels 1–5 accept mic sources; channel 6 can be either a mic input or USB audio return.[11] If channel 6 is assigned to a USB call, it cannot simultaneously take the sixth local microphone.[29] The P8 also has a dedicated phone-call channel with mix-minus, so a phone guest can be handled separately from the six mic inputs.[44][30]

**The P8 is the cleanest direct hardware fit for a fully local six-person roundtable because six microphones, six faders, six headphone feeds and separate tracks are all available without an external mixer or input-expansion scheme.**[1][2][3]

#### Preamp noise floor and measured SNR

Zoom specifies P8 equivalent input noise of −124 dBu or less, IHF-A, at +70 dB gain with a 150 Ω input.[11] This is an input-referred preamp noise specification. It does not state a recorded signal level or a signal-to-noise ratio with a dynamic microphone.

The P8’s maximum gain is 70 dB, and Zoom explicitly says it can handle demanding microphones including the Shure SM7B and Heil PR 40.[44] A reviewer also reported satisfactory results with dynamic and condenser microphones.[41] These statements describe gain capability and listening outcome, not SNR.

Zoom specifies a 3 kΩ microphone-input impedance and a maximum input of +5 dBu at 0 dBFS for the P8.[11][158][159]

A reported 102 dB signal-to-noise ratio applies to the P8’s AKM AK5720 analogue-to-digital converters.[41] ADC SNR and microphone-preamp SNR describe different stages. A low-noise ADC cannot convert a preamp-noise figure into a dynamic-microphone recording-chain SNR.

For the requested dynamic-mic SNR comparison, the P8 must be recorded as measured SNR unavailable; its relevant published engineering value is −124 dBu EIN at +70 dB gain with a 150 Ω source. Its 70 dB gain remains practically useful for low-output broadcast dynamic microphones.[11][41][44]

For a studio that intends to do full post-production, SD-card multitrack capture is therefore the primary recording path; USB is appropriate for a stereo feed to streaming, communication software or a separate reference recording.

Zoom’s manual gives practical mitigation steps for skips: increase software buffer size where possible, connect directly to the computer rather than through a USB hub, and disable computer power-saving and sleep functions.[11] It also instructs users to confirm the selected interface, close connected software and restart the P8 if necessary.[11] A review found all channels and USB mix-minus operating as expected, but did not identify Windows version or test duration.[45] A user-report summary describes occasional computer-recognition problems, often resolved by using the correct USB port or cable.[85]

The P8’s six local mic channels and six headphone outputs feed individually editable SD-card tracks, whereas its documented Windows USB interface carries a stereo 2-in/2-out feed. Use SD-card recording as the primary multitrack path for a six-person production. This is the decisive workflow distinction from the Model 12’s 12 channels to the computer, not a Windows endurance result.[1][2][3][11][30][32]

One review calls the P8 a 13-in/2-out USB interface, while Zoom’s operation manual specifies 2-in/2-out at 44.1 kHz/16-bit. The manufacturer manual’s 2-in/2-out figure is the appropriate basis for configuring the Windows audio device.[84][11]

#### Onboard processing quality and post-production flexibility

The P8 gives each mic channel a limiter, low-cut filter, Tone control and linked Comp/DeEsser control. It also offers Noise Reduction that automatically reduces levels of microphone inputs not in use when multiple mics are open.[11][1] The limiter targets sudden overloads; Low Cut reduces low-frequency rumble from sources such as air conditioning, street noise and vibration; the linked Comp/DeEsser adds vocal density while controlling sibilance.[44]

This is useful for a live panel. In a six-mic room, inactive microphones collect air-conditioning, HVAC, computer-fan and room noise. The P8’s Noise Reduction is designed to lower those inactive mics in the stereo programme mix.[44][1] It is a live-mix management function, not restoration processing that removes noise from a speaker’s active voice.

The P8’s processing controls are intentionally simple. The Tone function is a broad bright-to-warm adjustment, and compression/de-essing are linked rather than separately parameterised.[29] Review views differ: Sound On Sound considered the minimal processing well judged for a quick podcast workflow, while Audio Mentor considered the audio enhancement limiting.[45][30] One reviewer found that increasing the linked Comp/DeEss could make speech somewhat squashed, while another judged the processing capable of professional sound.[30][41]

The P8’s processing is practical for a fast live podcast, especially its limiter, low-cut and inactive-channel noise management, but it offers less per-voice precision than the RØDECaster Pro II or a full mixer workflow. Use it lightly in a professional studio, especially where individual tracks will be mixed later.[11][44][30]

The P8 retains useful multitrack files. It can store a stereo mix plus separate INPUT 1–6, PHONE and SOUND PAD files. Individual files are pre-fader, and the user can choose Original Data before Tone and Comp/DeEsser or With Tone & Comp after that treatment.[11][45][24] Original Data is the correct option for an archive intended for later DAW work.

The recorder’s own editor can trim, split, combine, fade, add background music, normalise and export MP3 from the stereo podcast mix.[86][29] It does not replace DAW editing of isolated contributors. The raw/individual tracks remain the material for participant-specific level repair, edit decisions, EQ, compression and revised mixes.

Zoom’s manual specifies WAV recording at 44.1 kHz/16-bit.[11] One retailer claims 32-bit float at 44.1 or 48 kHz, but that claim conflicts with the manufacturer-manual format statement.[21][11] Configure the studio on the manufacturer-documented 44.1 kHz/16-bit basis unless Zoom’s current official firmware documentation for the specific unit states otherwise.

### Tascam Model 12

The Model 12 is an integrated mixer, recorder, USB interface and DAW controller.[98][99] It has eight XLR microphone inputs with phantom power and can record up to 12 tracks simultaneously to SD card: ten input channels plus MAIN MIX L/R.[25][100][8]

For four-, five- or six-person podcasting, channels 1–6 are the clearest configuration. TASCAM identifies them as channels with Ultra-HDDA mic preamps, and the recorder captures inputs 1–6, stereo inputs 7/8 and 9/10, plus MAIN MIX L/R. A six-person panel can therefore use six individual dynamic microphones on channels 1–6, retain isolated tracks and simultaneously create a stereo programme mix.[8]

The wording around input counts varies because several different things are being counted: microphone jacks, mixer channels, line inputs and recordable tracks. The relevant microphone-per-participant figure is eight XLR-3-31 mic inputs—channels 1–6, 7 and 9; the relevant recorder figure is ten input channels plus stereo master, not twelve microphones.[12][25][8]

After six microphones occupy six of the Model 12’s eight XLR inputs, two XLR inputs remain available for other local sources, such as a producer or an audience microphone.[161][22]

The Model 12 has two headphone amplifiers with independent level control.[9] A four-to-six-person table therefore needs an external headphone amplifier or distribution system if all participants must monitor independently. This is a normal studio addition, but it should be part of the physical system design and cost plan.

**The Model 12 exceeds the six-mic recording requirement and gives the greatest input headroom of the three, but it is not a six-headphone-output podcast appliance.** It suits a studio comfortable with a small analogue console and an external headphone-distribution solution.[25][8][9]

#### Preamp noise floor and measured SNR

TASCAM specifies equivalent input noise of −128 dBu, A-weighted, with a 150 Ω source, MIC IN to INSERT SEND, and gain at maximum.[12] This is a microphone-input EIN figure. It measures input-referred preamp noise under a resistor-source test and is not a dynamic-microphone SNR result.

TASCAM’s mic-stage specifications give 0–50 dB gain, 1.8 kΩ input impedance and −58 dBu minimum input level.[161] A third-party review instead gives 56 dB of gain.[137]

TASCAM also specifies 103 dB A-weighted S/N measured at MAIN MIX and channel 1 at 0 dB over 22 kHz.[12] This is an overall signal-path specification, not a microphone-preamp SNR measurement with a dynamic microphone.

A Sound On Sound reviewer recorded voice with a dynamic microphone under heavy compression and treble boost, and reported little hiss.[9] That is a useful subjective indication of practical quietness under demanding voice treatment, but it is not a reproducible numeric SNR result.

For the requested dynamic-microphone SNR comparison, the Model 12 is also unrateable: −128 dBu EIN is the preamp input-noise specification, while 103 dB is a stated main-mix S/N figure under different conditions. The Model 12’s published EIN is lower numerically than the P8’s stated EIN, but their weighting, units and complete test conditions differ and do not establish a direct recorded-SNR ranking.[12]

#### Onboard processing quality and post-production flexibility

The Model 12 uses a traditional small-console approach. Inputs 1–6 and stereo inputs 7/8 and 9/10 provide digital compression and three-band semi-parametric EQ, and each channel has a switchable 100 Hz low-cut filter.[32][8] This gives an operator practical control over rumble, plosives, proximity build-up, uneven speech levels and tonal differences between guests.

A Model 12 product listing describes its input-channel compression as one-knob control.[162]

Its shared FX engine provides 16 reverb, delay, chorus, flanger and combination presets through the AUX2/FX bus, and can store up to ten recallable effect settings. Master EQ provides programme-bus shaping.[17][8] The built-in effect return can be routed to the MAIN MIX L/R, SOLO L/R, AUX1 and SUB L/R buses. The built-in effects cannot be used when the AUX OUTPUT AUX 2 jack is in use.[8]

GuitarPlayer called the semi-parametric EQ and the effects great-sounding.[106] That supports a favourable usability and sound impression, but it is not an objective processor comparison against RØDE APHEX or Zoom processing.

The Model 12 preserves extensive post-production options. For each song, it creates ten individual track WAV files and a stereo master; recordings are WAV/BWF at 44.1 or 48 kHz and 16 or 24 bit.[8] Channel recording can be set pre-compressor, post-compressor or post-EQ. PRE COMP is the best archival choice, because later DAW work can apply compression and EQ non-destructively.[8][9]

The Model 12 also provides DAW control, overdubbing and punch-in/out alongside its multitrack recording and USB interface.[103][17][162]

Some front-end decisions are necessarily committed. The 100 Hz low-cut filter and insert-point processing occur before conversion and cannot be undone from the recorded track.[9] This makes conservative use of high-pass filtering advisable for speech: remove obvious rumble, but do not over-filter voices during capture.

For an edited multi-guest programme, set the Model 12 to PRE COMP, record all participant channels plus MAIN MIX L/R, and use the stereo mix as the immediate reference rather than as the only post-production source. PRE COMP preserves the Model 12’s individual channels for later compression and EQ.[8]
