### robotics penetration rate (number of installed industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers or equivalent metric)

The appropriate penetration metric is industrial robot density: operational industrial robots in manufacturing per 10,000 manufacturing employees. IFR describes robot density as a measure of automation and defines the manufacturing metric as robots in operation per 10,000 employees. Operational stock means robots currently in use, not annual purchases.[4]

IFR’s industrial-robot scope is narrower than factory automation. Its definition requires an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in at least three axes, fixed or mobile, and intended for industrial automation. IFR counts multipurpose industrial robots, excludes robotic devices, and generally excludes dedicated industrial robots such as dedicated machine-loading equipment and automated storage-and-retrieval systems.[28]

For countries other than Japan, IFR estimates operational stock as installations over a 12-year assumed service life, withdrawing robots after that period. It prefers point-of-installation data because shipment data can misstate both the actual installation date and geographic destination, particularly where system integrators export completed systems. IFR also notes that robot-density values can be revised because employment data arrive with a lag.[28]

Saudi workforce data provide possible denominators but not the necessary robot-stock numerator. SIDF reports that factory employment was 841,367 workers in 2018, 894,198 in 2019, 952,215 in 2020, 990,076 in 2021 and 1,031,284 in 2022; factory counts were 8,521, 9,027, 9,922, 9,968 and 10,562, respectively.[12] For 2022, SIDF lists cement, plaster, concrete, clay and semi-finished steel products at 1,557 factories and 159,594 employees; basic chemicals at 645 factories and 94,590 employees; and refined petroleum, plastics and synthetic-rubber products at 528 factories and 75,124 employees.[12]

GAStat’s Register-based Labour Market Statistics, as republished by GLMM, report 1,576,431 manufacturing employed persons in Q1 2025, versus 626,216 in Q1 2021.[5] These figures use the Saudi Classification of Economic Activities based on ISIC4, but they exclude categories outside the specified administrative records, including some unregistered workers and non-Saudi employees whose work normally lasts under three months.[5]

The denominator choices therefore differ: SIDF’s 2022 factory workforce is not the same observation as GAStat’s Q1 2025 administrative-record manufacturing employment. IFR’s industry classes are derived from, but do not exactly match, ISIC Rev.4; IFR identifies class D as manufacturing.[12][28][29][5]

IFR identifies Saudi Arabia as survey item SA and provides year-end operational-stock estimates and manufacturing density analyses across more than 40 countries. The cited IFR material states no Saudi industrial-robot stock, Saudi manufacturing density, or Saudi 2015–2025 density series. IFR suppresses data points with fewer than four non-zero observations and may suppress territory-industry data.[4][28]

The valid international benchmark is IFR’s 2024 global manufacturing average of 177 robots per 10,000 employees; regional averages were 204 in Asia, 148 in Europe and 131 in the Americas. Those figures are benchmarks, not Saudi results.[30]

**Saudi manufacturing’s robot penetration rate for 2015–2025 remains unquantified: a rate cannot be calculated without a Saudi operational-stock numerator matched to a manufacturing-employment denominator for the same date and scope.**[12][31][4][5]

Reported administrative-record manufacturing employment grew at a **26.0% CAGR from Q1 2021 to Q1 2025**, from 626,216 to 1,576,431 persons. This quantifies the rapid expansion of the potential manufacturing-worker denominator over that four-year interval.[5]

SIDF’s factory series shows that the factory workforce increased by 189,917 workers between 2018 and 2022, a **5.22% CAGR**, while the number of factories rose by 2,041, a **5.51% CAGR**.[12]

The three listed 2022 sectors—cement/plaster/concrete/clay and semi-finished steel products, basic chemicals, and refined petroleum/plastics/synthetic rubber products—employed 329,308 workers, equal to **31.9% of the reported 2022 factory workforce**.[12]

Mordor Intelligence uses annual robot installations per 10,000 manufacturing workers as a market-model variable. This is an annual-installation metric, whereas IFR robot density uses operational industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees.[10][4]

Result — No numerical Saudi manufacturing robot-density rate is established for any year from 2015 through 2025. The IFR-style calculation is operational industrial-robot stock ÷ same-year manufacturing employment × 10,000.[4][28]

## How Saudi Vision 2030 has driven robotics demand

Vision 2030 has created a policy environment in which manufacturers have stronger reasons to invest in automation: more local production, higher-value industrial sectors, localisation requirements, factory-modernisation programmes, and large project demand. It has not imposed a national requirement to buy industrial robots. The practical effect is therefore a demand mechanism—more greenfield plants, retrofits and supply-chain investment in which robotics can compete—rather than a published count of robot orders or installations.

**Vision 2030 drives potential robotics demand by expanding and localising higher-value manufacturing; its published measures create factory-transformation, greenfield and retrofit opportunities rather than a national robot-purchase requirement.[35][21][6]** The most direct factory-level mechanism is the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources’ Future Factories Program, which targets a transition from low-wage and low-skilled labour toward operational efficiency, automation and advanced industrial solutions.[6] Its stated scale is 4,000 factories.[6]

### specific policy measures, investment targets, or regulatory changes

The original Vision 2030 document established the economic rationale for industrial automation: diversify beyond oil, develop domestic manufacturing capability, and draw private capital into strategic sectors. It says diversification is vital for sustainability and that Saudi Arabia had begun expanding investment into additional sectors.[35] The policy matters to an automation supplier because a larger and more sophisticated domestic production base creates both greenfield engineering demand and retrofit demand.

Vision 2030 linked renewable-energy development to domestic industrial capability. It set an initial target of 9.5 gigawatts of renewable-energy generation and committed to localising a significant part of the renewable-energy value chain, including manufacturing and research and development.[35] It also proposed reviewing the legal and regulatory framework through which private-sector companies can buy and invest in renewable energy, while encouraging public-private partnerships to localise the industry and develop skills.[35] Renewable-energy localisation does not require robotics, but manufacturing of components, electrical equipment and associated process equipment creates addressable applications in assembly, material handling, testing, traceability and quality control.

Vision 2030 also targeted localisation in sectors with complex industrial supply chains. It aimed to localise more than 50% of military-equipment spending by 2030, using direct investment and strategic partnerships to transfer technology and build national expertise in manufacturing, maintenance, repair, research and development, training, and specialised industrial complexes.[35] It separately targeted an increase in oil-and-gas localisation from 40% to 75% by 2030.[35] These are localisation targets, not automation targets, but they raise the commercial value of locally capable suppliers and can justify investments that improve repeatability, throughput and inspection quality.

The original programme also set out financial and site-enablement measures. Vision 2030 said financial institutions would be enabled to tailor products to sector needs, ranging from capital funding for large projects to short-term working capital for small businesses, and that licensing procedures would be facilitated and expedited according to national economic priorities.[35] It committed to allocate appropriate land for industrial projects, facilitate private investment and pursue public-private partnerships.[35] It further proposed special zones—including industrial zones—with special commercial regulations designed to improve investment potential.[35]

For smaller manufacturers, Vision 2030 states that SMEs contribute 20% of GDP, compared with up to 70% in advanced economies, and that financial institutions should be encouraged to allocate up to 20% of overall funding to SMEs by 2030.[35] It also said the SME Authority would review laws and regulations, remove obstacles, facilitate funding access, and support entrepreneurs through incubators, specialised training institutions and venture-capital funds.[35] Automation suppliers should not assume that these policy commitments equal approved project finance. They do make financing, localisation status, licensing and site selection part of qualification rather than afterthoughts.

The institutional architecture was strengthened when Royal Decree No. A/472 established the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources on 30 August 2019, transferring industrial and mineral-resources responsibilities to it; the Ministry began duties on 1 January 2020.[36] The Ministry defines its vision as making Saudi Arabia a magnet for high-quality industrial and mining investment and its mission as promoting industrial development toward a diversified, globally competitive economy.[36] This is the institutional channel through which industrial modernisation programmes, incentives and factory-transformation initiatives have become more visible to manufacturers.

The National Industrial Development and Logistics Program (NIDLP), a Vision Realization Program, provides another policy channel linking industrial development, logistics and advanced technology.[37] The Saudi Industrial Development Fund’s manufacturing-digitalisation report says NIDLP, launched in 2019, aims to make Saudi Arabia an industrial powerhouse and global logistics hub and promotes adoption of IoT, AI and robotics to improve productivity and competitiveness.[12] The same SIDF report defines digitalisation in manufacturing as the integration of IIoT, AI, robotics, big-data analytics, machine learning and cloud computing into the manufacturing ecosystem.[12]

The National Industrial Strategy (NIS) translated the diversification objective into a specific industrial-expansion agenda.[21] The Vision 2030 Annual Report 2024 describes the NIS as expanding the manufacturing base, investing in advanced technologies and strengthening supply-chain resilience, backed by targeted regulation, financing tools and industrial infrastructure.[21] The 2025 Annual Report similarly states that policy has shifted toward expanding industrial capacity, strengthening local content and attracting investment into higher-value industries.[9] These statements establish the industrial direction; they do not quantify the portion of capital expenditure allocated to robots.

The National Industrial Strategy states three objectives for a private-sector-led industrial economy: an integrating regional manufacturing hub, industrial resilience and global leadership in selected segments. It identifies advanced manufacturing in special economic zones as a means to increase productivity and reduce investor costs, and regional machinery-and-equipment demand serving high-growth industries as an investment opportunity.[7]

The NIS describes the implications of automation unusually explicitly. It states that advanced automated systems will have profound implications for Saudi factories and could affect most industrial sectors.[7] It identifies drones, self-driving vehicles, industrial robots and service robots as primary areas of growth, and describes industrial robots as moving out of cages to work with people.[7] In manufacturing terms, that refers to the growing relevance of collaborative and flexible robotic applications alongside conventional fenced robot cells.

The NIS also identifies where digital industrial effects may emerge first. It says the most immediate impacts from IoT and emerging connectivity ecosystems are expected in Machinery and Equipment, Medical Devices and Renewables, and that Saudi manufacturers need support to adopt these technologies to remain competitive and enter high-value-added segments.[7] Its priority-sector list includes Metal; Building Materials; Food Processing; Military; Machinery and Equipment; Aerospace; Pharmaceutical Products; Renewables; Medical Devices; Chemicals; Maritime; and Automotive.[8] These sectors are a policy-based account map for industrial-automation sales, rather than evidence that every sector has the same robot intensity.

The NIS’s investment ambition is substantial. It says the strategy seeks investment close to SAR 1 trillion through more than 814 industrial opportunities.[7] Its interim plans specify 814 opportunities with investment reaching SAR 979 billion by 2035, taking account of current capabilities, required technologies and workforce qualification.[7] The strategy states that the number of industrial establishments increased by nearly 50% in five years, from 7,206 plants in 2016 to 10,293 in 2021, with a stated 2035 target of 36,000 plants.[7] This is industrial-base expansion, not a robotics forecast.

There is a dating discrepancy in official and non-official descriptions of the NIS. Vision 2030’s 2024 Annual Report presents 2020 as the launch year, while a separate commercial page dates the launch to 18 October 2022.[21][38] For a sales strategy, the key point is not to treat the two dates as interchangeable; the official annual-report chronology should govern when describing the Vision 2030 record.

Giga-projects connect industrial policy to potential demand from project supply chains. The NIS says NEOM, the Red Sea and the Saudi Green Initiative will create demand for goods and services, creating an opportunity to build local manufacturing capabilities and attract modern industrial technologies.[7] NEOM, Oxagon and The Red Sea are also listed on the official Vision 2030 transformation map, and both NEOM and The Red Sea are presented as Vision 2030 projects.[37] NEOM and The Red Sea are Vision 2030 projects.

These statements indicate the intended technological character of the development, rather than realised robot penetration or a confirmed factory-robot procurement pipeline.[40]

Oxagon describes its core area as 48 square kilometres and its planned port capacity as 1.5 million TEU by 2030.[83]

NEOM is a greenfield, multi-sector development in Tabuk that identifies advanced manufacturing among its nine target sectors.[90]

The policy also contains procurement and market-access measures relevant to suppliers. The NIS calls for government entities and state-owned companies to estimate demand for targeted segments over the next five to ten years.[7] It proposes standardising supplier qualification for state-owned companies, including use of the Jadeer platform and memoranda of understanding.[7] It also proposes improving government-tender regulations by dividing tenders to support SME participation, and simplifying foreign-investment and business-management regulations through measures including international arbitration, intellectual-property protection and reduced bureaucracy.[7] These are enabling measures for industrial participation, not proof that a particular automation vendor will qualify or win.

The NIS identifies localization and local content as mechanisms to increase demand for local products, facilitate access to industrial finance, create clear and flexible systems and procedures for investors, and support SMEs.[7] For automation sales, this makes local manufacturing, local engineering support and a credible Saudi supply-chain plan more valuable in public and quasi-public procurement.[7]

The PIF’s real-estate supply-chain localization initiatives emphasize long-term relationships with local suppliers, domestic capability and employment.[91]

The 2025 report also identifies automotive, renewable energy, pharmaceuticals and advanced manufacturing as sectors developing projects within integrated industrial and logistics ecosystems.[9] Automotive receives particular emphasis: Vision reporting describes it as a priority area for establishing a full-scale industrial ecosystem, including investment in local vehicle production and development of advanced technologies.[21] These sectors usually have more structured requirements for traceability, quality assurance, safety, process control and production consistency than low-complexity manufacturing, strengthening the business case for automation.

PIF’s 2021–2025 program identifies automotive initiatives in research and development, new-age materials and ecosystem development, including a partnership project with a global lithium-ion battery-cell producer. It also describes aerospace and defence localization across systems, equipment, parts, components and maintenance, repair and overhaul, and identifies foreign-OEM partnerships as a route for Saudi Arabian Military Industries to transfer know-how.[91]

**The policy measures make Saudi Arabia more attractive for automation investment because they enlarge the number and sophistication of industrial projects, but they do not turn announced industrial investment into a measured robot-order book.**[35][21][7]

### Factory transformation and financing mechanisms

The Future Factories Program is the clearest direct policy mechanism for factory automation. The Ministry states that it aims to shift 4,000 factories from dependence on low-wage and low-skilled labour toward operational efficiency, automation, and advanced industrial solutions and practices.[6] It is not framed merely as a technology showcase; its objectives include higher industrial competitiveness, reduced dependence on unskilled labour through alternatives, and creation of quality jobs aligned with educational outputs.[6]

The Future Factories Program also supports digitization, production-line controls, material handling and packaging.[6][92][93][54]

The programme has two distinct paths. First, it targets new factories, which should be designed and constructed to high standards of manufacturing and production efficiency. Second, it targets existing factories, which should convert to factories adopting operational-excellence standards and advanced technologies.[6] For a supplier, these are different sales motions.

A greenfield factory permits architecture-level decisions on robot cells, controls, safety, data infrastructure and material flow. A retrofit requires proof of compatibility, phased downtime planning, operator adoption and a return on investment against the installed base.

The Ministry’s December 2025 communication describes the Factories of the Future program as aiming to transform more than 4,000 factories into smart and automated facilities.[50]

The Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources’ Main Digitization Track supports foundational digitization and automation, generally without requiring large capital expenditure. Its covered applications include digital procedures and planning, communications, production-line control, material handling, and packaging systems and machines; the Ministry may provide solutions or cover part of implementation costs.[92]

SIDF offers mechanisms that can convert a technical discussion into a financeable industrial-modernisation project. Its Tanafusia Program supports financing for automation, digitisation and Industry 4.0 solution projects intended to improve Saudi manufacturers’ efficiency.[12] SIDF also offers Smart Industry Readiness Index (SIRI) assessments, advisory services for SMEs, industrial benchmarking and partnerships with service providers to support Industry 4.0 implementation.[12] SIRI is a structured Industry 4.0 maturity assessment: commercially, it can help a factory move from a generic interest in “smart manufacturing” to a prioritised gap list and investment sequence.

At a February 2023 Saudi Advanced Manufacturing Hub workshop for SMEs, factories and providers including Siemens identified factory problems, selected solutions and developed software-as-a-service proof-of-concept cases. SIDF described these low-capital-cost tools as a way to control operating costs and improve performance.[94]

The NIS proposes expanded soft financial products, including government support covering all or part of interest costs for local investors and manufacturers in targeted segments taking local commercial-bank loans.[7] It also identifies localisation, local content, industrial finance, flexible investor systems and SME support as levers for expanding the industrial business base.[7] This proposal should be treated as a qualification topic, not an assumed subsidy: the account must establish whether its sector, investment structure and local-bank financing meet actual programme conditions.

Implementation activity is visible beyond generic policy language. Vision 2030’s 2025 Annual Report says ALAT signed a strategic agreement with SoftBank Group involving a $150 million joint investment to develop a fully automated industrial robotics manufacturing centre.[9] The report separately describes ALAT as building domestic capacity in semiconductors, smart devices and AI infrastructure across nine core business units.[9] This is a specific robotics-manufacturing investment; it is not a national total for industrial-robot purchases.

The Ministry also used grants and ecosystem-building instruments in 2025. It reported that nine winning Innovative Factories Grant Program projects collectively received SAR 15 million.[50] It said it had supported research and development projects exceeding SAR 15 million through the Innovative Factories Grant Program and allocated 50 seats for factories beginning their transformation under the Fourth Industrial Revolution Grant initiative.[50] Industrial Transformation Saudi Arabia 2025, held in Riyadh from 1 to 3 December 2025, showcased automation, AI, smart manufacturing, sustainable energy and Fourth Industrial Revolution applications.[50] The Ministry described the exhibition as a platform connecting Saudi manufacturers with global solution providers, widening access to advanced industrial solutions, improving operating efficiency and reducing digital-transformation costs.[50]

The nine Innovative Factories Grant winners announced in 2025 covered large-scale 3D-printing lines, diagnostics, advanced materials, alternative proteins and industrial antiscalants.[50]

The industrial base expanded during the period. The NIS records manufacturing at 13% of GDP by 2016 and says it grew at an annual rate of 5.3% to SAR 258 billion in 2020.[7] The Vision 2030 Annual Report 2024 says more than 12,000 factories were operating across the Kingdom in 2024; the 2025 report puts the count at more than 12,900 factories in 2025, compared with 7,200 in 2016.[21][9] DataSaudi separately reported 10,434 operating industrial units in 2022.[51] The differing figures are not necessarily contradictory because the documents use different labels—factories, industrial establishments and operating industrial units—and do not set out a common classification or counting method.

A 2024 academic study of Saudi manufacturing respondents provides directional evidence of adoption and investment intent. Across 301 responses, automation being integral to the company’s manufacturing process had a mean score of 4.1196, with observed values from 2.00 to 5.00, and plans to invest in business-automation technology in the next year had a score of 4.0698.[52] The study’s average across seven automation items was 3.8946.[52] These scores describe the survey participants’ views; they are not a national robotics-penetration rate.

**The Future Factories Program is the strongest factory-level demand driver because it explicitly separates greenfield production-efficiency projects from existing-factory automation retrofits, while SIDF and proposed NIS finance mechanisms give a route to structure the business case.**[6][12][7]

In March 2023, the Future Factories Program’s second phase began with a first batch of 217 facilities, whose average SIRI self-assessment score was 2.4 out of 5.[53]

On 10 May 2024, the Ministry said the Future Factories Program included financial grants covering audited factory evaluations and the development of transformation plans.[54] It also included grants covering part of the transformation costs for machinery and equipment needed for automation and improvements in production and operational efficiency, and grants covering software costs for ERP systems, facilities and building-management systems, and other digital solutions.[54] As of that communication, over 700 factories had undergone audited evaluations, 370 had developed transformation plans, approximately 150 had achieved the program’s targeted level, and the Kingdom ranked first in the Middle East in maturity-level assessments for adopting advanced technologies.[54]

The Future Factories Main Digitization Track identifies controls, communications, handling and packaging among its supported categories; it also addresses performance follow-up and operational data.[92]

As of the Ministry’s May 2024 update, more than 700 factories had completed audited evaluations, 370 had transformation plans and about 150 had reached the targeted level—equivalent to 17.5%, 9.2% and 3.8%, respectively, of the 4,000-factory Future Factories target. This identifies a sizeable assessed and planned pipeline ahead of full transformation.[6][54]

In February 2024, ALAT announced four global partnerships and said it would invest USD 100 billion by 2030, focusing on clean energy and Industry 4.0.[2]

On 16 December 2022, the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources, MODON and OXAGON signed a Memorandum of Understanding to cooperate in implementing the Future Factories Program.[55] The parties agreed to advance clean-manufacturing technologies including artificial intelligence, 3D printing, the Internet of Things and smart robots.[55] The MoU provides for legislation, incentives and enablers supporting the Future Factories Program and removing barriers to industrial innovation.[55] MODON will provide sites and selected factories to pilot the assessment.[55] The MoU is expected to enhance local content, localise strategic goods, promote national industries and adopt a circular-economy approach to modernise manufacturing.[55]

### official Vision 2030 documents

The official Vision 2030 record shows industrial policy moving from broad diversification toward industrial expansion, localisation and factory transformation.[21] The 2025 Annual Report lists the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources, the National Industrial Strategy, Made in Saudi, Shareek, Future Factories and Special Economic Zones as industrial-development milestones.[9] The 2024 report dates Made in Saudi to 2021, Factories of the Future, Leading Factories and Shareek to 2022, and Special Economic Zones to 2023.[21] This sequence matters because it shows policy moving from broad diversification objectives toward factory transformation, localisation and specialised industrial locations.

The reports also establish broader investment capacity. The 2025 Annual Report gives $381 billion of non-government investment in economic cities, industrial cities and special zones, and $3.4 billion of government financing for emerging industries.[9] It says industrial infrastructure and connectivity improved access to ports, transport networks and industrial zones, allowing specialised clusters to combine infrastructure, incentives and market proximity.[9] These are aggregate industrial-environment indicators, not automation expenditure.

Industrial performance indicators point in the same direction, although they should not be used as automation proxies. The 2025 Vision report presents the Industrial Production Index at 104.3 in 2024 and 113.6 in 2025, with 8.9% shown alongside those values.[9] A Ministry release reported that the index rose 6.5% in July 2025 compared with July 2024, led by manufacturing activities.[44] Manufacturing-sector operating revenues reached SAR 1.7 trillion in 2025, while manufacturing-sector FDI stock rose from SAR 277.2 billion in 2024 to SAR 285.4 billion in 2025.[51] These indicators describe a larger manufacturing economy; they do not identify what share of investment was spent on robots, drives, controls, software or systems integration.

The official documents explicitly recognise that factory modernisation is broader than robots. Future Factories refers to automation and advanced industrial solutions and practices.[6] The SIDF definition includes IIoT, AI, robotics, analytics, machine learning and cloud computing.[12]Judgment — A robot sale is usually contingent on this broader stack: a stable process, safety design, fixtures or end effectors, material presentation, controls integration, maintenance capability and a measurable operational problem. Therefore, a factory’s Industry 4.0 status is a useful lead indicator, but it is not equivalent to a robot opportunity.

The NIS creates a strong policy-to-demand rationale for robotics in selected manufacturing applications. It calls for adoption of advanced industrial technologies to increase productivity and reduce costs, alongside industrial infrastructure, logistics services and special economic zones.[7] It states that advanced industry can raise supply-chain capability and productivity.[7] Combined with the NIS recognition of industrial robots and the Future Factories target, this supports a practical conclusion: Saudi industrial policy is designed to make automation investments easier to justify where they improve competitiveness, local content, quality or labour productivity.[7][6]

It does not establish that Vision 2030 caused a quantified national increase in robot purchases. IFR’s industrial-robot statistics define an industrial robot narrowly as an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in at least three axes and used in industrial automation.[28] IFR excludes robotic devices and generally excludes dedicated industrial robots such as dedicated machine-loading equipment and automated storage-and-retrieval systems.[28] That distinction matters because Vision 2030 policy frequently uses automation, digitalisation and Industry 4.0 in a broader sense than IFR industrial robots.

Vision 2030 supports robotics demand through industrial expansion, local manufacturing, priority-sector localisation, industrial infrastructure, financing pathways and factory modernisation.[35][21][7][6] The Future Factories programme provides a direct implementation mechanism for factory transformation.[6] But targets for factories, investment, local content or industrial output do not constitute measured robotics sales, installed-robot counts or robot density.

For a manufacturing seller, the policy implication is operational. First, identify whether the customer is a Future Factories candidate, a new plant, an existing plant pursuing operational excellence, or a supplier seeking local-content status. Second, map the proposal to a priority-sector operating problem: quality, throughput, labour dependency, traceability, energy use, safety or supply-chain resilience. Third, qualify capital structure early through SIDF, local-bank financing and potential targeted-segment support rather than presenting automation solely as an equipment purchase.[6][12][7]

The 207 products on the mandatory local-industrial-content list represented 78.4% of the 264 products identified in the 2023 Vision 2030 Annual Report. This indicates that mandatory local-content procurement coverage was already broad across the reported product set.[46]

Manufacturing-sector FDI stock increased by SAR 8.2 billion, or 3.0%, from SAR 277.2 billion in 2024 to SAR 285.4 billion in 2025. This quantifies the year-on-year expansion of the manufacturing investment base reported in the Vision 2030 record.[51]

Using the reported SAR 1.7 trillion of 2025 manufacturing operating revenue and 0.8% year-on-year growth, the implied increase from 2024 is approximately SAR 13.5 billion. This puts the reported 2025 revenue growth into Saudi-riyal terms.[51]

Vision 2030’s effect on robotics demand is strongest as an industrial-development mechanism, centred on higher-value manufacturing, localisation, industrial zones, infrastructure, priority supply chains and factory transformation.[21][7][6] The policy expands higher-value manufacturing, industrial zones, infrastructure and priority supply chains; the NIS explicitly identifies industrial robots as relevant to the Saudi factory of the future, while giga-project demand is intended to build local manufacturing capability and attract modern technology.[21][7] The Future Factories Program is the most direct factory-level instrument because it targets 4,000 factories for automation-oriented transformation and distinguishes greenfield design from retrofit modernisation.[6] Across 2015–2024, official Vision 2030 and NIS documents show policy favouring advanced industrial capacity and automation.[35][21][7][6] Its relative strength is highest for factory transformation and localisation; its limit is that these measures do not publish a national robot-order or installed-robot outcome.

## Three practical implications for a sales rep

Saudi manufacturing automation sales is not a single “robotics market.” It spans process control, connected equipment, material handling, machine vision, industrial robots, energy management, software and the engineering services that make these systems work in an operating plant. The practical task is therefore to identify plants with a specific operational problem, a credible modernization path and a buyer able to implement it.

The evidence points to three actions: focus account coverage on industrial clusters and priority manufacturing segments; sell phased, policy-linked operational improvement rather than standalone equipment; and treat NEOM and Red Sea announcements as ecosystem intelligence that must be qualified by actual scope. **The strongest sales proposition combines application engineering, integration, measurable operational outcomes and workforce enablement—not hardware alone.**[12][30][28]

### Prioritize factory clusters

Saudi Arabia has a large, diverse manufacturing account universe, but it is fragmented. DataSaudi recorded 10,434 operating industrial units in 2022, while its enterprise measure counted 78,031 active manufacturing enterprises in 2024. These are different statistical units and should not be added together: an industrial unit, establishment and active enterprise are not necessarily the same commercial account.[51]

The account base also has a long small-business tail. In 2018, 78.2% of manufacturing establishments had fewer than five employees, 16.5% had five to 19 employees and 5.3% had more than 20 employees. That does not mean only 5.3% are viable automation prospects; it means a territory plan needs separate motions for small workshops, medium plants and large multi-line sites rather than one enterprise-sales model.[51]

The first named-account screen should be location. A market report identifies Riyadh, Jeddah and Dammam as focal points for manufacturing-automation investment because of industrial infrastructure and proximity to manufacturing hubs. The 2025 Vision 2030 Annual Report adds that industrial infrastructure and connectivity have improved access to ports, transport networks and industrial zones and enabled specialized clusters.[1][9]

The second screen should be industrial process and strategic fit. The National Industrial Strategy identifies 12 priority sectors: metal, building materials, food processing, military, machinery and equipment, aerospace, pharmaceutical products, renewables, medical devices, chemicals, maritime and automotive. These are a better prospecting starting point than a broad list of “Industry 4.0 companies,” because each has identifiable production, quality, traceability, safety or material-flow problems.[8]

A task-level discovery is more useful than a technology-led pitch. The Saudi Industrial Development Fund (SIDF) identifies welding, painting and assembly as robot applications, and computer vision for defect inspection, sorting and inventory tracking. Ask the plant manager where these tasks create the most scrap, rework, safety exposure, downtime, queueing or labor variability; then specify the cell, controls, vision, safety system, integration and service package needed to address that task.[12]

This approach fits the observed state of adoption. The Saudi manufacturing study published in 2024 reported that many companies remain in the automation process and have not reached full automation, even as its 301-respondent survey found strong agreement that automation is integral to respondents’ manufacturing processes. The coexistence of these findings supports staged upgrades: some plants need a first connected-control layer, while others need a robot cell, machine vision or line-wide integration.[52]

Connectivity is an essential qualification question, not proof of automation readiness. In the 2022 Digital Economy Survey, 61.5% of Saudi manufacturing establishments reported using systems or devices connected to the internet. That supports asking about networked equipment, data ownership, cybersecurity, enterprise-resource-planning (ERP) and manufacturing-execution-system (MES) interfaces early in discovery; it does not identify robot users.[51]

Judgment — Build the first 50–100 named accounts by combining geography, priority sector, production complexity, Future Factories Program stage and a visible plant event: new capacity, an expansion, obsolete controls, quality losses, safety incidents, localization requirements or a planned digital project. Use task-level discovery for assembly, inspection and material handling in automotive, petrochemicals and packaging, and for welding, painting, assembly, defect inspection, sorting and inventory tracking where those tasks are material.[54][12][70] Give small firms a repeatable entry offer and reserve full-site transformation resources for plants with multiple production lines, defined data ownership and an internal technical sponsor.[19]

The result is a territory that targets factory buying conditions rather than simply chasing the largest number of registered manufacturers.

**1. Target factories by Future Factories Program stage and priority manufacturing task.** Build named accounts in the National Industrial Strategy’s 12 priority sectors—Metal, Building Materials, Food Processing, Military, Machinery and Equipment, Aerospace, Pharmaceutical Products, Renewables, Medical Devices, Chemicals, Maritime and Automotive—and identify whether each prospect is at self-assessment, audited evaluation, transformation-plan or implementation stage. Match the opportunity to a defined production task, such as welding, painting, assembly, inspection, sorting or inventory tracking. The Future Factories Program aims to shift 4,000 factories toward operational efficiency, automation and advanced industrial solutions and practices, with grants for audited evaluations, transformation plans, ERP and other digital solutions, and part of the machinery-and-equipment cost for automation and production- and operational-efficiency improvement.[8][6][54][12]

2. Baseline the plant and qualify integration, training and financing for a phased business case. Separate new-factory design-in opportunities from existing-factory modernization under the Future Factories Program’s two paths. Use SIDF SIRI assessments and advisory support to assess Process, Technology and Organization, including automation, connectivity, intelligence, and training and education; investigate Tanafusia financing for automation, digitization and Industry 4.0 projects; and scope application engineering, integration, commissioning and workforce training with the equipment.[6][12]

3. Qualify megaproject-linked suppliers by awardee, asset and actual factory-production scope. Siemens’ reported ENOWA work concerned water-transmission-pipeline construction and automation; ABB’s Red Sea work concerned power systems, digital solutions and EV-charging infrastructure; and ZPMC—not Siemens—was awarded the Port of NEOM crane package, with Siemens Europe delivering automation components. Pursue these as infrastructure or supplier-ecosystem opportunities only when the defined package contains a factory-production requirement.[57][66][64][60]

UNIDO reported that Saudi manufacturing value added rose from USD 79,584 million in 2015 to USD 89,060 million in 2018, in constant 2010 prices: a change of 11.9% from 2015–2018 and 112.1% from 2005–2018. UNIDO also reported government investment to build six new industrial cities with modern infrastructure; the Saudi Industrial Property Authority (MODON), established in 2001, oversees development of industrial cities with integrated infrastructure and services, which are expected to act as industrial clusters and boost productivity. This makes industrial-city and cluster coverage a practical starting point for territory design.[18]

SIDF’s 2022 industrial-loan approvals provide an additional account-screening signal. Engineering industries received SAR 7,665 million across 26 loans; chemical industries SAR 5,411 million across 34 loans; consumer industries SAR 393 million across 37 loans; building-materials industries SAR 32 million across four loans; and other industries SAR 697 million across 10 loans.[71]

Software should be qualified alongside equipment. Grand View Research recorded software as the largest component of Saudi Arabia’s smart-manufacturing market in 2025, with a 58.03% revenue share, and identified it as the fastest-growing component during its forecast period. This supports account discovery covering plant data, digital work instructions, quality management and asset-performance workflows as well as controls, robots and drives.[14][72]

The Future Factories Program provides a sharper account-selection signal than registration counts. In May 2024, the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources said the program aimed to transform 4,000 factories into advanced, automated factories; its sequence is self-assessment, audited evaluation by a certified assessor, and development and implementation of a transformation plan. By that point, more than 700 factories had undergone audited evaluations, 370 had developed transformation plans, approximately 150 had achieved the program’s targeted level, more than 490 had received incentives, and more than 1,200 trainees had been connected to modern-manufacturing training.[54]

Make program stage a named-account field: identify factories preparing self-assessment, undergoing an audited evaluation, holding a transformation plan, or already at the targeted level. The Ministry’s incentives include grants for audited evaluations and transformation plans, ERP, facilities- and building-management software and other digital solutions, and part of the machinery-and-equipment cost needed for automation and production- and operational-efficiency improvement.[54]

Prioritize sectors where industrial policy creates a defined localization or capacity agenda. The National Industrial Development and Logistics Program’s 2030 aspirations include a globally competitive localized machinery-and-equipment industry; 50% localization of military and security spending; 70% localization of the future supply chain for basic and intermediate chemicals; 40% localization of pharmaceutical market value; 15% localization of medical devices and supplies; and food industries meeting 85% of domestic demand. It also names renewable energy, aquaculture, specialty chemicals, plastics and rubber as industrial themes.[42]

Industrial-city prospecting should include Jazan, Yanbu and Jeddah. NIDLP’s 2023 annual report states that a SAR 129 million commercial agreement was signed with several companies to develop public and commercial facilities in Jazan City for Basic and Transformational Industries; Yanbu Industrial City had completed 572,835 metres of fibre-optic installation across industrial and commercial areas and public parks by end-2023; and MODON signed an “A 750 million” contract with Desert Technologies to localize solar-panel and cell manufacturing at Jeddah Third Industrial City, with 5 GW production capacity on 170,000 square metres.[73][74]

The Vision 2030 Annual Report 2025 states that the Future Factories Program supports factories to adopt automation, artificial intelligence and advanced production systems; its Future Factories Program graphic lists 4K and 3.9K alongside Factories, Enabled and Target.[9]

Saudi manufacturing also has structural reasons to concentrate account research on localization-sensitive segments. UNIDO reported in 2019 that manufacturing production and exports relied heavily on a few activities and products, with relatively strong dependence on oil, while the domestic market relied strongly on imported manufactured products and manufacturing relied strongly on foreign workers.[18] UNIDO defines the import-to-apparent-consumption ratio as an indicator of domestic manufacturing competitiveness: a high ratio indicates low domestic-market competitiveness.

Saudi Arabia’s ratio was 0.8 in 2015 and 0.67 in 2016. Its domestic-market analysis assumes imports are primarily for domestic consumption rather than re-export; a high ratio can indicate either weak domestic-product competitiveness or domestic production insufficient to meet demand.[18]

Engineering and chemical industries together received SAR 13.076 billion of SIDF’s SAR 14.198 billion in 2022 approved NIDLP loans: 92.1% of approved value across 60 of 111 loans (54.1%). This makes those two industries a high-value starting point for finance-aware manufacturing account coverage in that approval year.[71]

Use market-size and robot-density findings to prioritize accounts, not as evidence that any individual factory is ready for a robot cell.

### Sell against policy-linked needs

Vision 2030 and the National Industrial Strategy create a favorable modernization environment, but they do not turn policy targets into an equipment order book. The National Industrial Strategy says advanced automated systems may have profound implications for the Saudi factory of the future and explicitly identifies industrial robots, including robots working alongside people, as a growth area. It also seeks nearly SAR 1 trillion of investment through more than 814 industrial opportunities, while a separate statement specifies SAR 979 billion by 2035. These are broad industrial-investment ambitions, not a published automation or robot-spending forecast.[7]

The practical implication is to connect an automation proposal to the customer’s industrial objective. The Future Factories Program has two paths: new factories are to be designed and built to high manufacturing and production-efficiency standards, while existing factories are to adopt operational-excellence standards and advanced technologies. This gives the salesperson a simple branch in qualification: greenfield design-in opportunity or brownfield modernization opportunity.[6]

For greenfield plants, engage before process design, electrical architecture, utility sizing, data architecture and machinery procurement have been frozen. A proposal can then specify the control layer, safety design, robot interfaces, industrial network, historian, MES/ERP connection, spares strategy and commissioning plan. For existing plants, begin with baseline performance and a limited retrofit scope that proves operational value without forcing a full shutdown. **A greenfield factory is a design-in sale; an existing plant is an outcome-led modernization sale.**[6][12]

SIDF provides a useful customer-conversation framework. Its Smart Industry Readiness Index (SIRI) assessments cover Process, Technology and Organization across 16 dimensions including automation, connectivity, intelligence, training and education. SIDF also offers advisory services to SMEs, industrial benchmarking and partnerships with service providers, while its Tanafusia Program supports financing for automation, digitization and Industry 4.0 projects intended to improve Saudi manufacturers’ efficiency.[12]

A salesperson should therefore structure the commercial conversation in four stages:

1. **Baseline the operation:** establish the customer’s current overall equipment effectiveness (OEE), unplanned downtime, throughput, changeover time, defect/rework rate, energy use, safety incidents, labor allocation and maintenance backlog. The point is not to promise a generic productivity gain; it is to identify the current loss mechanism.

2. **Prioritize a first use case:** SIDF recommends low-complexity projects capable of significant returns, including repetitive-task automation, cloud-based data management and machine-learning predictive maintenance. It advises prioritization by feasibility, impact and alignment with business objectives. [12]

3. **Build the deployment scope:** include hardware, controls, software, safety, cyber controls, process engineering, factory acceptance testing, site acceptance testing, commissioning, operator training and maintenance support. Purchase, installation, software updates and worker training are all relevant cost categories in automation investment. [52]

4. **Create the scale-up roadmap:** use the first deployment’s measured result to decide whether to repeat the cell, connect adjacent equipment, add vision, automate material flow or integrate plant data. SIDF describes digital transformation as an ongoing journey and makes workforce training central to effective use of digital tools. [12]

A Saudi case illustrates why operational measures matter. SIDF reports that Zamil Steel began shop-floor automation in 2021 to improve OEE and profitability, track manufactured components and assembly, and reduce waste. It reports OEE increasing from 52% to 65%, return on investment within 24 months and an annual revenue increase of SR 24,000,000 after digital transformation. These are Zamil Steel’s reported, plant-specific results—not a sector benchmark or robotics-only result—but they provide a locally intelligible template for a business case.[12]

Cost and return expectations should be handled with the same discipline. SIDF estimates Saudi digitalization investments of SAR 500,000–2 million for small factories, covering basic IoT devices, simple automation tools and data-management systems; SAR 2–10 million for medium factories, including comprehensive IoT connectivity, semi-autonomous robots and advanced data analytics; and SAR 10–50 million or more for large factories, including AI-driven technologies, advanced robotics, digital twins and predictive maintenance. It gives estimated ROI ranges of two to four, three to five and four to seven years respectively. These are planning ranges for digitalization, not automation package prices or guaranteed returns, so use them to establish budget bands and then cost the customer’s actual scope.[12]

Workforce capability must be a line item in both technical and commercial scope. SIDF identifies limited digital skills and expertise, resistance to change and conventional operating practices as barriers to adoption. IFR similarly identifies the engineering needed for peripherals such as vision and process design, and the availability of system integrators, as key adoption constraints.[12][30]

The policy-linked proposition should also make localization concrete. Vision 2030’s 2025 Annual Report says the Local Content and Government Procurement Authority introduced a mandatory list of locally manufactured products, from which government entities and their contractors are required to procure. Red Sea Global likewise says it prioritizes Saudi-based suppliers and embeds local-content requirements in key processes. Ask whether the prospect sells into government, state-owned enterprises, giga-project supply chains or local-content-sensitive tenders; if so, bring local assembly, service, training, spares and integrator partners into the account plan early.[9][48]

This sales model makes policy relevant to the customer’s business case while avoiding the false claim that a national target guarantees a purchase order.

In SIDF’s Zamil Steel case, OEE rose from 52% to 65%: a **13-percentage-point increase**, equivalent to a **25.0% relative increase** from the starting OEE.[12]

**2. Qualify a phased plant-level business case and financing.** Separate new-factory design-in opportunities from existing-factory modernization opportunities under the Future Factories Program’s two paths. Use SIDF SIRI assessments and advisory support to assess process, technology and organization, investigate Tanafusia financing for automation, digitization and Industry 4.0 projects, and include integration and workforce training in the commercial scope.[6][12]

The National Industrial Development and Logistics Program (NIDLP) 2021–2025 Delivery Plan identifies improving communication and digitalization-technology infrastructure as a key driver of the growth and spread of Fourth Industrial Revolution applications; building high-skilled Fourth Industrial Revolution human capabilities to occupy newly generated jobs; and supporting entrepreneurs and domestic technology companies, attracting foreign companies specialized in Fourth Industrial Revolution applications, and localizing research-and-development centers of excellence. These NIDLP measures make connectivity, skills transfer and local technical capability part of the customer proposition.[45]

Financing qualification should be an early sales step. In 2022, SIDF approved SAR 14,198 million across 111 National Industrial Development and Logistics Program loans, including SAR 13,396 million across 102 industrial-project loans; industrial SMEs received 78% of SIDF loans, with SAR 1.1 billion in loan value.[71]

On 5 September 2023, SIDF stated that its Tanafusiya Accelerator Track offered financing of up to 75%, a grace period of up to 24 months, a minimum tenor of seven years and an eight-week application turnaround time for applicants meeting program requirements. SIDF also stated that its soft-loan offer could finance up to 50% of project cost, with a tenor of up to 10 years, a grace period of up to 24 months and a 20% advance disbursement. Investors seeking SIDF incentives within the Industrial Sector Support Initiative (ISSI) apply electronically through the ISSI page on the Ministry of Industry and Mineral Resources website; ISSI includes sub-initiatives and programs supporting industrial investment through cooperation among entities in the industrial ecosystem, mineral resources, and the Local Content and Government Procurement Authority. Build the capex case, financing timetable and customer eligibility into the opportunity plan.[56]

Cybersecurity and legacy integration belong in the first technical workshop. The Saudi operational-technology framework spans four domains and 23 subdomains, and is described as containing 47 controls across those 23 subdomains. Require the plant’s OT owner to define network segmentation, access control, documentation and audit responsibilities before finalizing the controls or connected-equipment scope.[72]

The Future Factories Program’s financial measures make the funding conversation operational. The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development’s Manufacturing Sector Skills Framework states that the program aims to automate 4,000 factories and end reliance on low-skilled workers using AI, IoT and robotics. The framework also describes an approximately USD 800 million Saudi Industrial Development Fund (SIDF) financing program to convert 100 factories in Saudi Arabia to Industry 4.0, Industry 4.0 stimuli for 43 facilities and five centres focused on Industry 4.0 applications in Saudi Arabia.[43]

Use the SIRI transformation process as a joint sales plan. NIDLP’s June 2022 partnership with INCIT aimed to adapt SIRI at several thousand Saudi production facilities, establish a SIRI training and certification centre and appoint local champions to scale the index. SIRI covers Process, Technology and Organization and is designed to help manufacturers start, scale and sustain transformation.[75] In parallel, the Ministry, MODON and Oxagon’s 16 December 2022 MoU covered AI, 3D printing, IoT and smart robots, with an Advanced and Clean Manufacturing Improvement Tool to assess readiness for advanced manufacturing systems and processes; the program aims to build a technology ecosystem for sustainable digital transformation and remodel 4,000 existing factories to enhance the Kingdom’s global competitiveness, while MODON was to provide sites and selected factories for pilots.[55]

Make the local-content case specific to the customer’s supply chain. NIDLP’s 2024 annual report calls for increased local-manufacturer participation in government procurement and state-owned industrial enterprises’ capital and operational expenditure, alongside workforce training and investment attraction to localize strategic industries. The Ministry–MODON–Oxagon MoU was expected to enhance local content, localize strategic goods, promote national industries and adopt a circular-economy approach.[74][55]

For brownfield plants, offer wireless sensing for assets without native connectivity and modular solutions that fit limited shutdown windows. Make compatibility with installed controls, plant safety and cybersecurity processes, baseline measurement and post-deployment measurement explicit gates in the proposal. Local engineering capacity, open integration and post-deployment support belong in the commercial scope.[19]

Workforce development is a sellable delivery requirement. Saudi manufacturers are reconfiguring supply chains and production processes and require workers trained in current technological and digital skills; manufacturers are focusing on upskilling and retraining to address skills shortages.[43] Include training, commissioning and support because the Ministry’s program already connects factory personnel with modern-manufacturing training, while the national skills framework identifies industrial IoT, augmented and virtual reality, robotics, e-commerce, artificial intelligence, data analytics and modeling, real-world evidence, and patient-centricity and omnichannel marketing as technology transformations affecting manufacturing and its workforce.[54][43]

Structure the value case around real-time data, predictive maintenance, asset management, energy, downtime and resource use. Industrial IoT embeds sensors in machines for real-time data collection and analysis to optimize production processes, improve quality and reduce operating cost; the skills framework identifies advanced and collaborative robotics as technologies that increase productivity, reduce operating cost, improve safety and improve product quality.[43] Siemens Financial Services, Siemens’ B2B financing arm, describes flexible leasing, working-capital products, project-related and structured financing, corporate lending, equity investments, finance advisory, and trade and receivables financing; it states that combining digitalization and automation technologies with smart finance makes the shift to Industry 4.0 more financially sustainable, with increased productivity, faster time to market, higher flexibility and greater environmental sustainability. Assess which of these structures can support the individual account’s investment case.[76]

The Vision 2030 Annual Report 2025 lists the Mandatory Product List at 1.6K+ in 2025 and factories benefiting at 3.9K+ in 2020 and 6.7K+ in 2025.[9]

Include Arabic and English documentation where needed, alongside local integrator coverage, service support, operator training, spares, cybersecurity and interoperability with the plant’s existing PLC, MES or ERP environment.

### Qualify awards by scope

NEOM and Red Sea Global are commercially important, but their project brands can conceal very different buying categories. The practical rule is the **scope test**: identify the end user, asset type, contract package, equipment boundary, decision maker, local-content requirement and whether the scope concerns a manufacturing process, enabling infrastructure or visitor services before allocating pursuit resources.

Siemens’ reported NEOM-linked work illustrates the distinction. In September 2023, Mutlaq Al Ghowairi Co. and Siemens secured two ENOWA projects to construct and automate water-transmission pipelines. This is genuine automation-related work at NEOM, but it is water infrastructure rather than a manufacturing-factory or industrial-robot award. A separate Siemens memorandum of understanding with Saudi Coffee Co. concerned a smart factory in Jazan, using electrification, automation and digitalization technologies; it is a manufacturing lead but is neither a NEOM nor Red Sea award.[57]

ABB’s Red Sea work is similarly relevant but distinct from factory robotics. ABB said it was working with The Red Sea Development Company on power systems and digital solutions for a resort intended to operate on 100% renewable energy when completed in 2030. Its described scope included 10 low-voltage synchronization panels rated 2,500–6,300 amps, associated with 36 MW from 69 generators, plus ABB Ability Energy and Asset Manager. ABB also announced AC and DC charging infrastructure at 13 Red Sea and AMAALA locations, including local manufacture of an EV-charging pedestal.[65][64][66]

Those projects offer valid adjacent leads for electrification, energy monitoring, power quality, asset management, charging infrastructure, local assembly and service. They do not establish industrial-robot demand inside Saudi manufacturing plants. The same distinction applies to NEOM’s HVDC infrastructure: on 30 May 2023, Hitachi Energy announced agreements with ENOWA and Saudi Electricity Company (SEC) for three high-voltage direct-current links totaling up to 9 GW. An SEC order, awarded to Hitachi Energy and its consortium partner Saudi Services for Electro Mechanical Works (SSEM), covers a 3 GW, 525 kV HVDC Light transmission system connecting Oxagon with the Yanbu area more than 650 kilometres away; these are power-transmission systems rather than factory automation awards.[77]

A project lead should be scored as follows:

- **Manufacturing-process lead:** the end user operates a plant, and the scope includes production equipment, controls, robotics, machine vision, material handling, quality systems or plant data. Pursue through the factory’s operations, engineering, maintenance, quality and finance stakeholders.

- **Industrial-infrastructure lead:** the scope covers utilities, pipelines, substations, ports, energy systems, water, logistics or construction. Pursue only if the offered technology fits the asset package, and do not use the project value as a proxy for automation spend. Siemens’ ENOWA pipeline work and ABB’s Red Sea energy systems fall in this category. [57][64][65]

- **Ecosystem lead:** the announcement signals a future industrial environment but lacks a defined equipment package. Oxagon is described as NEOM’s industrial city, advancing a manufacturing and logistics ecosystem, with technology and digital manufacturing among seven industrial sectors; this supports stakeholder mapping, not a forecast of robot orders. [41][40]

System integrators are central to this qualification process. IFR defines them as companies that buy robots and other automation equipment to build standard or customized solutions, including turnkey delivery to end users. For robot cells, identify the integrator at the same time as the manufacturer, because application engineering, safety, vision, end-of-arm tooling, process knowledge and commissioning are often decisive.[28]

Supplier registration is a route to opportunity, not an award. NEOM says registration is free but does not guarantee tender invitations; suppliers are selected from its database for suitable tender activity. Red Sea Global says qualified suppliers receive tenders for their category and can view its planned procurement pipeline, but explicitly states that pre-qualification does not guarantee future business.[78][48]

The result is a disciplined pursuit model: engage NEOM and Red Sea Global where the scope matches the portfolio, but build the core manufacturing pipeline from operating factories with a defined production problem and investment case.

**3. Qualify megaproject leads by awardee, asset and manufacturing scope.** Siemens’ reported ENOWA work concerned water-transmission-pipeline construction and automation, while ABB’s Red Sea work covers power systems, digital solutions and EV charging infrastructure. These are infrastructure opportunities, not manufacturing-robot demand.[57][66][64]

At the Port of NEOM, ZPMC—not Siemens—was awarded contracts valued at over SAR 1 billion for ship-to-shore, electric rubber-tyred gantry and automated rail-mounted gantry cranes; ZPMC was to work with Siemens Europe on the automation components.[60]

Red Sea Global’s procurement structure reinforces the scope test. It stated that it procures individual trade-works packages itself rather than awarding projects to a series of main contractors, with typically about 20 packages per project. Map the relevant package and developer procurement route rather than pursuing only a presumed main-contractor channel.[79]

ENOWA provides a separate route into NEOM utility opportunities: supplier registration requires a profile with a signed and stamped NDA and code of conduct, followed by completion of a supplier questionnaire through Ariba credentials. ENOWA states that it seeks multinational and local companies across industry sectors.[80]

Oxagon is an ecosystem lead with identifiable industrial attributes. Oxagon describes its planned ecosystem as serving renewable-energy manufacturing and AI-driven industries through pre-zoned industrial land, scalable infrastructure, competitive energy resources and a digital backbone; ENOWA states that scalable smart utility infrastructure and renewable-energy generation are intended to enable industry in Oxagon to advance. Use these signals for stakeholder and future-factory mapping.[61][81]

The Saudi Coffee Co.–Siemens memorandum provides a non-giga-project manufacturing reference for the sales motion. The planned Jazan smart factory was described as using one integrated platform to give farmers visibility and control, and as incorporating carbon capture, renewable energy, responsible waste management and eco-friendly packaging. This is a manufacturing digitalization and sustainability use case rather than a NEOM or Red Sea award.[58]

Oxagon is an ecosystem lead with concrete industrial and logistics signals. Its 2021 plan named sustainable energy, autonomous mobility, water innovation, sustainable food production, health and well-being, technology and digital manufacturing—including telecommunications, space technology and robotics—and modern methods of construction as its seven industrial sectors. The plan couples robotics, IoT, human-machine fusion and artificial and predictive intelligence with fully automated distribution centres and autonomous last-mile delivery assets; it also describes an integrated port and logistics hub and a net-zero city powered by 100% clean energy.[82]

Pursue an Oxagon opportunity only after identifying the tenant, package and operating asset. Oxagon says it is building renewable-energy-manufacturing and AI-driven-industry ecosystems with pre-zoned industrial land, scalable infrastructure, competitive energy resources and a digital backbone; its operational port and multimodal logistics are intended to streamline import and export flows. AHG’s industrial-gases facility is scheduled to begin operations in late 2026 and is intended to give tenants localized, more affordable and reliable access to industrial gases, while its phased plan includes green oxygen, nitrogen, argon and hydrogen.[83][84]

NEOM Logistics Park is a separate project-linked manufacturing and logistics setting: as of February 2022, permanent-facility construction had begun for a hub supporting construction operations, a working population of 30,000, asphalt production plants and waste-treatment facilities. The planned next-generation port was described as automated and integrated, with capacity for 3.5 million standard shipping containers.[47]

IFR states that online B2B quota systems are common between robot producers and large system integrators or resellers: the vendor negotiates a quota that the customer can call forward using a digital interface, and the sales representative participates in contract negotiation rather than processing call-offs.[28]

Use verified giga-project awards to map EPCs, utilities, developers, electrical and digital standards, and local partnership routes; then seek introductions to suppliers, fabrication yards, modular-construction firms and manufacturers serving the projects’ supply chains.

3. Approach relevant Saudi manufacturers, system integrators and suppliers connected to verified NEOM or Red Sea procurement, but commit sales-engineering resources only after confirming that the awarded package requires factory automation rather than infrastructure alone. [86][87][88]
