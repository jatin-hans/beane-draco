Use a 0.20–0.35-delta sold call and a −0.25 to −0.35-delta bought put as a practical collar starting point, with about 0.30 Delta on each leg a reasonable middle-ground design when the call strike is an acceptable sale price.[1] A 0.20–0.25 call preserves more upside but supplies less premium, while a 0.30–0.35 call better helps finance the put but caps gains sooner.[2][3] Select the put’s strike as the desired downside floor, then use Delta to calibrate the cost and initial hedge strength: a −0.20 put is cheaper and more remote, whereas a −0.30 put normally gives a nearer and more responsive floor.[4][5] Do not require equal Deltas or equal percentage strike distances, because neither condition assures a zero-cost collar; match the actual call credit to the actual put debit at the chosen expiry, accepting asymmetric Deltas or distances when needed.[6][7] A call and put each 10% out of the money are symmetric in strike distance, but their Deltas and premiums can differ materially with volatility, time to expiry, rates, dividends, and skew.[7][8]

## What Delta for the SOLD CALL?

**Compact answer — Sold Call Delta: 0.20–0.35, quoted as the call’s positive Delta magnitude; the short-call position itself has negative Delta.** This is out-of-the-money covered-call guidance applied to the short-call leg of a collar: SpotGamma states that most practitioners target 0.20–0.35 Delta, and Cboe’s BXMD systematically selects a monthly OTM SPX call with Delta closest to 0.30. Use about 0.20–0.25 when retaining stock upside matters more than call income; use about 0.25–0.35 when the call credit is intended to make a meaningful contribution to the put’s cost and the holder accepts the strike as a sale price.[3][1][4][9]

A protective collar combines 100 shares of stock, one short call written against those shares, and one long put. The short call sets the ceiling on stock gains, while the long put sets the floor and minimum exit price in a downturn. Delta estimates how much an option premium changes for a $1 stock move; it is not a fixed percentage distance from the stock price or a promised assignment rate.[10][11][7]

Judgment — **Sold Call Delta: 0.20–0.35 is the practical starting range for an out-of-the-money (OTM) covered call within a collar.** A 0.30-delta call is a defensible default when the holder seeks a balance of premium and retained upside. A 0.20–0.25-delta call better fits a holder who particularly values further stock appreciation and wants less chance of the call becoming in the money. The choice must also pass a basic economic test: the strike plus premium should be a stock-sale price the holder accepts.

### delta

- **A 0.20-delta call is usually farther OTM than a 0.30-delta call with the same expiration.** “Out of the money” means the call strike is above the current stock price, so it has no intrinsic value when opened. Lower Delta normally corresponds to a higher strike for a call, which leaves more room for the stock to rise before the collar’s upside cap is reached. [12] [13] [8]

- **The economic trade-off is less premium income.** A farther-OTM call is cheaper for the buyer and therefore pays less to the collar holder who sells it. The smaller credit provides less immediate income and less offset to the cost of the protective put, but it allows more participation if the stock rises. [9] [3] [14] [4]

- **A 0.20–0.25-delta call suits an upside-preservation objective.** Covered-call guidance identifies 0.15–0.25 Delta as a range for investors willing to accept smaller premium in exchange for more upside room and fewer assignment disruptions. Schwab’s illustrative covered-call trade made this concrete: with stock near $260, it sold a $280 call with Delta 0.21 and collected $0.95 per share, or $95 for one 100-share contract before fees. [4] [15]

- **Lower Delta reduces, but does not remove, the risk that shares will be called away.** A lower-Delta OTM call has a lower approximate likelihood of expiring in the money than a nearer-to-the-money call. Some traders use its Delta as a rough expiration-in-the-money estimate: a 0.20 call is commonly read as roughly a 20% chance of finishing in the money, not as a guaranteed 20% chance of assignment. [16] [17] [15]

- **For an owner reluctant to sell the stock, a lower-Delta call is the more internally consistent choice.** Fidelity says a holder who does not want to sell should place the call strike above the stock price by a personally acceptable distance and should have a plan to close the call if the stock reaches the strike. The short call can be bought back or rolled—closed and replaced with a later-expiring and/or higher-strike call—but doing so can cost money, particularly in a fast rally. [18] [18] [9]

- **A 0.20 Delta does not mean the stock is protected until it falls 20%.** Delta is an option-price sensitivity measure, whereas the protection level comes from the put strike; the relationship between a chosen call Delta and strike distance depends on time to expiration, implied volatility, interest rates, dividends, and the stock price. The section *What about percentage distance from current price?* addresses that separate strike-distance question. [7] [7] [8]

**What follows:** choose a call near 0.20–0.25 Delta when preserving upside and avoiding an unwanted sale of the shares outweigh collecting the largest possible call credit; accept that the protective put will then require more funding from the investor or from the put-side design.[4][12][10]

- **A 0.15–0.20-delta call is another income-trade convention when retaining the shares is paramount.** FatTail identifies short 0.15–0.20-delta options as the standard for covered calls and credit spreads, associating the range with roughly an 80–85% probability of expiring worthless. This is a more conservative extension below the section’s 0.20–0.25 upside-preservation range: it leaves still more appreciation room but supplies still less put-funding credit. [19] [20] [21]

- **Schwab’s covered-call example quantifies the probability shorthand.** Schwab says that its 0.21-delta call could be interpreted as having a roughly 21% chance of expiring in the money; Delta can help gauge assignment risk, but the stated 21% figure concerns expiration in the money. [15]

- **Fidelity’s illustrative OTM alternative was lower still.** Fidelity’s comparison lists an OTM 105 call with Delta 18, versus Delta 52 for an at-the-money 97.5 call and Delta 75 for an in-the-money 92.5 call. In a separate Fidelity illustration, with stock cost basis $97.72 and November contracts, the OTM 105 call had a $0.70 bid and Delta 18. [22] [22]
- **Fidelity quantifies the exposure from its 18-Delta OTM 105 call:** selling it against 100 owned shares reduces the position’s initial exposure from +100 to approximately +82 share-equivalent Deltas. [22][118]

### delta

- **A 0.30-delta call is a documented systematic covered-call convention.** Cboe’s S&P 500 30-Delta BuyWrite Index, BXMD, holds exposure to the S&P 500 and writes a monthly OTM SPX call whose Delta is closest to 0.30; its strike-selection table specifies the option nearest 0.303 and resolves an equal-distance tie by selecting the higher strike. Cboe’s Russell 2000 30-Delta BuyWrite Index, BXRD, uses the same 0.30 target for its monthly OTM RUT call. [1] [1] [1]
- **BXRD’s roll-date calculation** uses Black-formula Delta with inputs available before 11:00 a.m. ET to select its monthly Russell 2000 call. [119][1]

- **A 0.30 Delta is a benchmark convention, not a universal collar rule.** Cboe also maintains the S&P 500 BuyWrite Index, BXM, which sells successive one-month *at-the-money* calls rather than choosing an OTM 30-delta call. These are alternative systematic covered-call designs: one targets OTM Delta, while the other targets at-the-money moneyness. [1] [1] [1]

- **The practical guidance around 0.30 is broad rather than exact.** SpotGamma states that most practitioners target 0.20–0.35 Delta for OTM covered calls; another passage on the same guide gives 0.20–0.30. A separate covered-call guide describes 0.20–0.40 as a practical starting range for a 30-day cycle and identifies 0.25–0.35 as a workable income-focused balance. [3] [3] [4] [4]

- **A 0.30-delta call generally pays more than a 0.20-delta call of the same expiry.** It is ordinarily closer to the stock price, so it has a higher chance of acquiring intrinsic value if the stock rises. The holder receives more option premium, but the stock has less room to appreciate before the strike is challenged and the collar’s maximum sale price becomes relevant. [12] [5] [4]

- **The greater credit has a real purpose in a collar.** The sold call provides cash that can offset the long put’s cost; closer call strikes generally generate more credit and thus can finance a stronger or nearer protective-put floor. But the price of that funding is more foregone stock upside, because gains above the call strike are capped if the call remains open through assignment. [23] [15] [24]

- **A 0.30-delta call is appropriate only if its strike is an acceptable sale price.** Options Education states that the call strike plus premium received should represent an acceptable liquidation price. Fidelity likewise frames covered-call selection around willingness to sell, the expected size and timing of the stock move, and assignment probability. [9] [9] [18] [18]

- **The 0.30 choice is not necessarily aggressive enough for a holder deliberately seeking to dispose of stock.** Schwab says investors whose objective is for the call to expire worthless may focus on .30–.40 Delta calls, which it associates with a 60%–70% theoretical probability of expiring OTM. For a holder actively seeking a sale at the strike, Schwab gives .60 or .70 Delta as possible examples because the goal has shifted from retaining upside to increasing the chance of assignment. [25] [25]

**What follows:** a call near 0.30 is the useful middle-ground default when the call premium is intended to help fund protection and the holder is genuinely willing to sell at the strike; it is not the right choice merely because “30 Delta” sounds standard.[1][3][12]

- **Collar-specific practice can use a wider call-Delta band when put funding is the primary design objective.** In Cabot Wealth Network’s July 20, 2022 SPY collar illustration, using options expiring August 19, 2022, the author sought a sold-call Delta of 0.20–0.45 so that the call premium could cover most or all of the protective put’s cost; the selected 405 call had Delta 0.33 and generated $4.95 per share, or $495 per contract, with SPY at $394.04. [26] [26] [26]

- **A stated collar-specific funding convention is wider than the 0.30 benchmark.** In a SPY collar illustration using approximately 30 days to expiration, Cabot Wealth Network sought a sold-call Delta around 0.20 to 0.45 because the objective was to collect enough premium to cover most or all of the protective put’s cost. [26]

- **A bullish, premium-targeting covered-call illustration can select a Delta above 0.30.** The Blue Collar Investor’s hypothetical example, seeking a 2% initial time-value return with a bullish market view, selected the $52.00 call at a $0.95 bid and Delta 0.3865. [13]

- **Projectoption’s wheel-strategy test used a 30-delta covered call with about 60 days to expiration.** It sold the call on the trading day after acquiring 100 shares and, when the call expired out of the money, sold another call targeting 30 Delta and approximately 60 days to expiration. [27]

### How to choose between 0.20 and 0.30

- **First, set the acceptable exit price.** The short call obligates its writer to deliver shares at the strike when assigned. For a standard equity option contract, one call corresponds to 100 shares, so the client’s 100-share collar is naturally paired with one short call. The relevant question is not “Which Delta is best?” but “At which strike plus premium would I be comfortable selling these 100 shares?” [10] [9] [28]

- **Second, decide how much appreciation the holder is prepared to surrender.** A higher strike, typically associated with lower call Delta, preserves more potential profit from a rising stock but brings in less premium. A lower strike, typically associated with higher Delta, raises call income and the downside cushion from that income but truncates upside earlier. [9] [14] [5]

- **Third, compare the actual option chain at the desired expiration.** Delta is useful because it normalizes moneyness across stocks with different volatilities and expirations: market participants often describe a 25-delta call as OTM and a 50-delta call as near the money. It should follow the investor’s exit-price, outlook, and income requirements rather than replace them. [29] [13] [13] [12]

- **A 0.20 call is the better fit when the stock is still wanted.** It normally gives more upside room and reduces the approximate likelihood of ending in the money, at the cost of a smaller premium. This choice is especially coherent where tax considerations, a concentrated holding, or a strong bullish outlook make an unwanted stock sale more costly than a smaller option credit. [4] [30] [13] [12]

- **A 0.30–0.35 call is the better fit when the call credit has an important funding role.** It collects more premium and provides a more meaningful contribution toward the put purchase, but the holder must accept that the shares can be sold at the strike. The evidence supports this as a premium-versus-flexibility trade-off, not as a universal optimum. [3] [4] [12] [23]

- **A 0.35–0.40 call is an extension of the income-oriented choice.** Schwab identifies .30–.40 for investors whose aim is to capture extrinsic value by having the call expire worthless. Covered Call Research describes higher-Delta calls, often around 0.35 to 0.50, as suited to investors who prioritize immediate cash flow and are more accepting of assignment. These ranges describe different objectives and different degrees of willingness to give up appreciation. [25] [4]

- **No single percentage distance follows from a Delta target.** A call’s Delta changes with the stock price, time remaining, and implied volatility. Increasing volatility tends to pull OTM call Delta toward 0.50; as expiration approaches, an OTM call’s Delta tends toward zero if it remains OTM. Therefore, a 0.20-delta call in a high-volatility, longer-dated position can sit at a different percentage above spot than a 0.20-delta call in a low-volatility, short-dated position. [7] [7] [8]

**What follows:** the decision criterion is the acceptable stock-sale price, then the amount of call premium needed to support the collar; 0.20 is the upside-preserving version of that decision, while 0.30–0.35 is the more income-supporting version.[9][4]

- **The 0.25–0.35 band is a documented covered-call selection range between the two choices.** QuantWheel states that covered-call sellers typically target 0.25–0.35 Delta: 0.25 provides lower premium and a higher probability of retaining the shares, whereas 0.30–0.35 provides higher premium with a greater likelihood that the shares are called away. [31] [31]

- **Cboe identifies the full collar-design trade-off.** Cboe says the protection and foregone return of a collar depend on option moneyness, time to expiration, and relative richness. Lowering the call strike produces more premium and more downside cushioning but more upside truncation; increasing the put strike raises the floor and costs more; and purchasing the put monthly rather than quarterly also costs more. [23]

- **Start the zero-cost design with the floor, then solve for the call.** The zero-cost construction can begin with the investor’s risk tolerance and the put strike that establishes the minimum-loss floor; reverse-engineering the associated call price then establishes the call strike and maximum-return level. [32]

- **Strike choice also reflects the investor’s outlook and trade characteristics.** A mildly bullish investor might choose a call strike slightly above the current stock price, while a strongly bullish investor might choose a higher strike. Fidelity says strike and expiration selection should weigh premium against price exposure, assignment probability, gamma exposure, rate-of-return potential, and opportunity costs; Schwab adds an acceptable sale price and an area of potential resistance on the stock chart. Strike selection also depends on time and the expected speed of the underlying’s move. [33] [22] [15] [34]

- **A percentage-OTM rule is an alternative systematic convention, not a Delta target.** Cboe’s S&P 500 2% OTM BuyWrite Index (BXY) sells successive one-month SPX calls with strikes closest to 102% of the S&P 500 Index value reported before 11:00 a.m. ET on the roll date. [1]

### Delta is not a fixed probability of assignment

- **Delta’s primary meaning is price sensitivity.** A call Delta of +0.30 means the call premium is theoretically expected to rise by about $0.30 for a $1 increase in the stock, with other pricing inputs unchanged. Because the collar holder is *short* that call, the position Delta contribution is negative: one 0.30-delta short call offsets roughly 30 of the long stock’s +100 Deltas. [7] [7] [16]

- **The common probability shorthand concerns finishing in the money at expiration.** Schwab describes Delta as a rough estimate of the probability that an option expires in the money: its .75-delta call example has roughly a 75% probability of expiring in the money and a 25% probability of expiring out of the money. Under the same convention, a 0.30 call suggests roughly a 30% probability of expiring in the money, and a 0.20 call suggests roughly 20%. [25] [35] [17] [16]

- **Assignment is a different event from expiring in the money.** U.S. equity options are American-style, so a holder can exercise before expiration and the short-call writer can be assigned at any time. Schwab states that assignment probability increases when a stock or ETF call is in the money with little or no extrinsic value at or near expiration. At expiration, standardized in-the-money equity options are generally automatically exercised, but before expiration assignment depends on exercise decisions and allocation procedures. [25] [36] [36] [28]

- **Dividend timing matters to an equity collar.** A call holder may exercise before the ex-dividend date to capture the dividend, and early assignment is particularly relevant when an in-the-money call has less remaining time value than the dividend. Thus, a 0.30-delta call cannot be described as a fixed 30% chance that the stock will be assigned away. [28] [37] [36]

- **Delta itself changes throughout the trade.** Stock movement changes moneyness and hence Delta; changing implied volatility and time to expiration do so as well. As a stock rises, a call’s Delta moves closer to 1.0; if the stock falls further, the call’s Delta moves closer to 0. As expiration nears, in-the-money call Deltas increase toward 1.00, at-the-money call Deltas remain around .50, and out-of-the-money call Deltas fall toward 0 when other inputs remain constant. A selected 0.30-delta call can therefore become a substantially higher-Delta call after a rally, while a 0.20-delta call can decline toward zero Delta if the stock falls or time passes with the call still OTM. [7] [7] [7] [38]

- **A collar holder who will not accept assignment needs active management.** Keeping a call open retains the possibility of assignment; closing the short call is the only sure way to avoid it. That operational point matters more than whether the original strike had 0.20 or 0.30 Delta. [9] [9] [18]

**What follows:** use Delta as a changing exposure measure and a rough expiration-in-the-money shorthand, but manage the short call according to the holder’s willingness to sell and dividend calendar rather than treating 0.20 or 0.30 as an assignment guarantee.[7][28][37]

- **Even the standard no-dividend European Black-Scholes-Merton model separates Delta from expiration probability.** In that model, call Delta is N(d1), while the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is N(d2), where d2 equals d1 minus volatility times the square root of time to expiration. A Delta-based 0.20 or 0.30 strike rule is therefore a strike-selection convention rather than an identical modeled probability of expiration in the money or assignment. [39] [39] [19]

A short call can be assigned before expiration even if the stock subsequently falls and the option would have expired out of the money. Early-exercise incentives can also reflect interest rates and stock-borrow conditions, as well as dividends and remaining time value.

Judgment — **Sold Call Delta: approximately +0.20 to +0.30 is the headline collar starting range, with +0.30 as the documented systematic covered-call benchmark.** Select +0.20 to +0.25 when retaining upside and reducing the likelihood of the call finishing in the money takes priority: lower-Delta calls are generally farther OTM and collect less premium. Use +0.30 when the call credit is intended to contribute to the protective put and the strike is an acceptable sale price. Extend to +0.30 to +0.35 for an income-oriented design requiring more credit, accepting that the call is closer to the stock and caps gains sooner.[1][3][4][9]

Cboe’s BXMD and BXRD use 0.30-Delta OTM calls, while Cboe’s BXM sells monthly at-the-money calls; these are alternative systematic covered-call designs. Delta remains an initial, changing price-sensitivity and strike-selection measure. It is a rough indicator of finishing in the money at expiration, not a fixed percentage OTM or a fixed probability of assignment, because equity options can be exercised before expiration.[1][7][36]

## What Delta for the BOUGHT PUT?

- A **bought protective put** is the downside-insurance leg of a collar: the investor owns 100 shares and buys one put contract, normally covering those same 100 shares. The put gives the holder the right to sell the shares at its strike price through expiration, so the strike establishes the level at which the stock’s value is protected. [40][41]
- **Delta** is the option’s estimated price sensitivity to a $1 stock move. A long put has negative Delta: for example, a put with Delta −0.30 is expected initially to gain about $0.30 when the stock falls $1, all else equal. Delta is therefore a hedge ratio—how much of the stock’s near-term price movement the option offsets—not a percentage decline threshold. [7][38]

-Judgment — **Bought Put Delta: approximately −0.20 to −0.30 (20–30 absolute Delta)** is the concise starting range for an out-of-the-money collar put. A −0.20 to −0.25 put is the lighter, lower-cost hedge; −0.25 to −0.30 is the conventional protective-put choice. E*TRADE presents 20–25 Delta for a smaller hedge and 30–35 Delta for a larger hedge, while MindTrajour gives 0.20–0.30 as the typical collar-put range. [42][6]

### delta

- A **−0.20 Delta put** is an out-of-the-money put with a smaller initial hedge than a −0.30 Delta put. E*TRADE describes 20–25-Delta puts as suitable when an investor wants to hedge a small percentage—20–25%—of the stock position’s current price risk. [6] Fidelity similarly places out-of-the-money long puts broadly between 0 and −0.50 Delta. [40]
- In practical terms, one −0.20 put contract against 100 shares initially contributes roughly −20 share-equivalent Deltas, compared with the stock’s +100 Delta. The position therefore remains strongly exposed to a modest decline, although the hedge strengthens if the stock falls toward the put strike because the put’s Delta becomes more negative. [38][17]
- The economic appeal of a lower-absolute-Delta put is that it is normally farther below the current stock price and costs less. Schwab states that lower-strike puts are cheaper but offer a lower degree of protection, particularly against smaller stock moves; the Options Industry Council likewise states that a lower strike leaves more downside exposed while reducing the premium. [43][5]
- A very low-Delta put is principally disaster insurance rather than ordinary-pullback protection. One protective-put guide characterizes 10–15-Delta puts as cheap crash coverage that does not protect much against routine declines; this frames −0.20 as a lighter but still more responsive hedge than those extreme tail strikes. [2]
- The key contractual point is the **strike floor**: the put’s strike, not its Delta, is the price at which the stockholder can sell the shares and at which further downside becomes protected. A lower-Delta selection normally means a lower strike and thus more loss retained before that floor begins to operate. [41][40]
- **What follows:** choose approximately −0.20 to −0.25 Delta when the investor accepts more self-insured downside in exchange for a more distant, generally less expensive floor; it is not equivalent to the more immediate hedge supplied by a −0.30 put. [6][43][5]

In one collar illustration, 100 shares had Delta +100, the short call contributed −46, and the long put contributed −19, leaving the collar with position Delta +35.[44]

### delta

- A **−0.30 Delta put** has more initial downside sensitivity than a −0.20 put. If the stock falls $1, a −0.30 put is expected initially to rise about $0.30 per share, versus about $0.20 for a −0.20 put under the same simplifying assumption. That difference matters for a collar holder who wants the put to begin offsetting losses sooner, before the stock reaches the strike. [38][7]
- A protective-put guide calls strikes 5–10% out of the money, roughly 25–35 Delta, the standard hedge range. Within that stated range, −0.30 is a sensible middle choice: it is out of the money, yet less remote than a −0.20 put. [2]
- **Schwab’s put illustrations give two strike-to-Delta examples:** with XYZ at $100, a same-month $97 put had Delta −0.30; with DJX at 260, a 50-day $254 put had Delta −0.28. [120]
- E*TRADE gives the same directional comparison from a hedge-sizing perspective. It presents 20–25-Delta puts for a smaller hedge of current stock-price risk and says that an investor seeking a greater percentage hedge could consider 30–35-Delta puts. [6]
- Greater protection comes at a cost because the higher-absolute-Delta put will normally have a strike nearer the current stock price. The Options Industry Council states that a strike near the stock offers more protection at a higher premium, whereas a lower strike costs less but leaves more downside exposed. Schwab makes the same point: a put closer to the current stock price may maximize protection, but it is more expensive and can have greater time decay. [5][43]
- The upper reference point is an **at-the-money put**, a put whose strike is near the current stock price. Fidelity says an at-the-money long put typically has Delta around −0.50, and a protective-put guide describes approximately 50 Delta as the tightest and most expensive floor. [40][2] A −0.30 put is therefore moderate rather than comprehensive insurance: it protects below a defined out-of-the-money strike and partially offsets losses before then.
- **For a tighter capital-preservation collar,** the candidate’s cited protective-put range is approximately −0.40 to −0.50 Delta, approaching an at-the-money put and a higher, more expensive floor. [40][69]
- **What follows:** use approximately −0.25 to −0.30 Delta, especially around −0.30, for a conventional protective hedge. Extend to −0.30 to −0.35 when a higher floor and greater initial hedge are worth the higher put cost; moving toward −0.50 buys the tightest, most expensive floor. [2][6][5][40]

- The Options Industry Council’s March 2026 guidance states that at-the-money options are typically near 0.50 Delta, in-the-money options approach 1.00 Delta, and out-of-the-money options have lower Delta values. A −0.30 put is therefore an out-of-the-money protection choice between a remote low-Delta disaster put and a near-the-money, higher-cost floor. [21][40][2]

### What 20 or 30 Delta does—and does not—mean

- A bought put’s quoted Delta is negative. Traders often omit the sign and call a −0.30 put a “30-Delta put,” but the sign matters for the position: the long put has negative Delta and offsets some of the long stock’s positive Delta. [7][17]

-Judgment — **Do not describe a 30-Delta put as protection at 30% below the stock price.** A −0.30 Delta refers first to the put’s estimated premium response to a $1 stock move. The floor begins at the put strike; the distance between current stock price and strike is a separate quantity that depends on the actual option chain. [38][41][7]

- Nor is a 30-Delta put a fixed hedge of 30% of the position throughout the option’s life. Delta changes continuously as the stock moves, and it is affected by time remaining and implied volatility. The Options Industry Council states that higher implied volatility brings more strikes toward .50 Delta, while Macroption notes that longer maturity and higher volatility generally move put Delta toward −0.50. [7][8]
- A probability shorthand exists, but it must be named precisely. CME says the absolute value of Delta approximately indicates the chance that an option finishes in the money at expiration; the Options Industry Council says some traders use the same interpretation. Thus, 20 Delta is often read as about a 20% chance of expiring in the money, and 30 Delta as about 30%. [17][7]
- That shorthand is neither a guaranteed probability nor a probability of assignment. An equity or ETF short option can be assigned before expiration because it is American-style, while the probability shorthand concerns finishing in the money at expiration. A Black-Scholes discussion further distinguishes absolute put Delta from risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money, stating that the latter exceeds absolute Delta and that the gap increases with volatility and the square root of time. [5][45]
- The distinction has a practical consequence for a collar. A 30-Delta put may be selected because it supplies more initial downside sensitivity and is usually nearer the stock than a 20-Delta put—not because it guarantees a specified probability, begins protection 30% below spot, or fixes the hedge ratio until expiration. [38][7][41][46]
- **What follows:** select the desired dollar floor and expiry first, then use the live chain’s Delta as a consistent comparison tool for deciding whether that floor represents a lighter 20–25-Delta hedge or a more substantial 25–35-Delta hedge. [41][40][45][47]

- Levelbox says Delta remains a sensible, consistent way to choose a strike because every option chain is sorted by it. It says that for a low-volatility, short-dated put, Delta and assignment probability are close enough to treat as one number; for a high-implied-volatility or longer-dated put, check the actual probability separately before deciding whether the risk is acceptable. [45]

- Levelbox’s screener example makes the distinction numerical: a recently listed semiconductor ADR at 136% implied volatility had a strike closest to 0.10 Delta, displayed as a 10% chance of assignment, while its reported risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money was 20.5%. [45]

Delta also reflects interest rates, expected dividends, and the pricing model or market convention used to report it, in addition to strike, time to expiry, and implied volatility. Consequently, the same percentage-out-of-the-money strike can carry different Deltas across expirations or market conditions.

At expiration, the long put gives the collar holder the right to sell the covered shares at the put strike, so the stock-and-put position has a strike-based sale floor before allowing for the net option premium and the original stock purchase cost.[36][21]

### Premium

- The protective put protects only for the chosen option term. Fidelity states that the purchased put provides protection below its strike until expiration, and Britannica notes that a collar’s protection lasts through expiration; therefore the expiry must cover the event or period the investor wants to insure. [40][10]
- Longer protection generally costs more. The Options Industry Council states that more time to expiration costs more, although a longer-dated put covers more time before replacement is needed. [5] Time decay also matters: Schwab notes that options tend to lose value over time if the anticipated decline does not occur. [43]
- **In the Options Industry Council’s QQQ collar study covering March 1999 through September 2010,** six-month put implementations outperformed one- and three-month put versions on almost all reported measures; the study identified slower time decay as a significant benefit. [121][69]
- Implied volatility is another premium input. Schwab states that a fall in implied volatility can make it harder for a protective put to profit, while a rise can help; it also notes that options may be more expensive before expected high-volatility events such as earnings. [43] This is why two puts with similar Deltas can still have materially different dollar costs across stocks or dates.
- Within the collar, the investor uses the sold call’s premium to offset some or all of the put cost. That premium match is handled in **Do most traders aim for equal Deltas?**; the bought-put decision here remains the choice of protection floor and intensity. A protective put by itself preserves the stock’s upside, less the put premium, whereas the collar trades some of that upside for funding. [40][10]
- **What follows:** a put target cannot be chosen from Delta alone; its premium and protection depend jointly on the strike, expiration, implied volatility, and the call premium available to help fund it. [5][43][48]

- The dollar significance of moving the floor is illustrated by Omen Blog’s hypothetical 90-day $100-stock example at 25% implied volatility: the $100 put costs $4.45 per share ($445 per contract), the 5%-out-of-the-money $95 put costs $2.41 ($241), and the 10%-out-of-the-money $90 put costs $1.12 ($112). This is the protective put’s strengthening hedge as the stock approaches and passes the floor. [49][50]
- Cambridge Associates reports that a cited study of systematically buying puts within an equity portfolio over nearly thirty years delivered 35% of stock-market return for about 75% of stock-market risk, and attributes the adverse ongoing cost to realized market volatility tending to be lower than the volatility priced into purchased options. - The Blue Collar Investor states that there is no single correct answer on protective puts: they serve a valuable function for investors with low risk tolerance, while the cost of the put lowers returns. [53]

- For an investor with a concentrated stock position, substantial unrealized gains, or shares restricted from sale, a protective put retains the stock’s upside potential while setting a strike-based downside floor for the option term. [41][40]

## Do most traders aim for equal Deltas?

A collar can be built to look balanced, but “balanced” can mean three different things: equal absolute Deltas, equal percentage distances from the stock price, or equal option premiums. Those are separate design choices. The practical question is not whether the call and put look symmetrical on a quote screen; it is whether the put establishes the floor the shareholder needs, whether the call strike is an acceptable sale price, and whether the resulting premium is affordable.

**Recommendation: begin with the downside floor, then select the sold call that delivers an acceptable cap and net premium.** This will often create an asymmetric collar. Use equal absolute Deltas only when that pairing also meets the floor, cap, and financing objectives. Change this priority when retaining more upside is more important than a higher floor: use a farther-out call, accept less call premium, and accept a net debit if necessary.[9][5][23]

### equal Deltas (symmetric collar)

A *symmetric collar* commonly means one whose long put and short call have equal absolute Delta. Delta measures the option premium’s approximate sensitivity to a $1 stock move; it is not the same thing as a fixed percentage out of the money, nor a guaranteed probability of assignment. A 0.25-Delta call and a −0.25-Delta put therefore have matching initial directional sensitivities in absolute terms, but they need not have equal strikes, equal premiums, or identical behavior after the stock moves. Delta itself changes as the underlying price changes.[35][17][16]

Equal-Delta construction is valid when the investor wants an initially even-looking reduction of stock exposure on both sides of the current price. In one hypothetical $50-stock collar, a 52-strike call with Delta +0.40 and a 48-strike put with Delta −0.40 were paired against 100 shares. The stock contributed +100 position Deltas; the short call contributed −40 and the long put contributed −40, leaving the collar at +20.[54] This makes the immediate directional effect easy to understand: the collar retains positive exposure to a stock rise or fall between the strikes, but much less than unhedged stock.

A same-expiration collar is often described with the call above and the put below the starting stock price. Options Education says the options are “generally equidistant from the stock value” when discussing how implied-volatility shifts and time decay may offset between the long and short option legs.[11] That description supports equal-distance strikes as a familiar template. It does not turn equal distance into a universal construction rule.

An equal-Delta pairing is also useful as a pricing diagnostic. In options markets, a 25-Delta call and a 25-Delta put are conventional reference points for measuring *risk reversal* or skew: the 25-Delta put price minus the 25-Delta call price. In a negative-skew market, the put trades at a premium to the call.[55] Thus, matching at 25 Delta can make the pricing imbalance visible, but it does not remove it.

The conventional 25-Delta risk reversal compares the implied volatility of a 25-Delta put with that of a 25-Delta call to measure downside-versus-upside volatility skew; it is a pricing comparison, not a prescription for collar strikes.[126]

The distinction is especially important in equities, ETFs, and equity indexes, where out-of-the-money puts generally carry higher implied volatility than equidistant out-of-the-money calls. Options Education identifies this as the dominant negative-skew pattern and links it to demand for crash protection.[55] Cboe illustrates the effect with SPX options expiring in 29 calendar days on November 10, 2021: with the index at 4,360, an approximately 5%-out-of-the-money 4,575 call had a midpoint of 3.55, while an approximately 5%-out-of-the-money 4,150 put had a midpoint of 31.65. Cboe described the put as almost 900% more expensive than the call in dollar terms.[56]

Under flat volatility skew, a call and put at roughly equal moneyness could produce roughly zero net premium; equity-option skew is usually not flat.[127]

The premium difference means that equal absolute Deltas do not imply a zero-cost collar. A zero-cost collar is defined by the call premium received equaling the put premium paid when the position is initiated.[57][58][32] Equal-Deltas instead describe a relationship between the two options’ initial price sensitivities.

Higher interest rates tend to raise call premiums and lower put premiums, another reason that matching absolute Deltas cannot ensure matching premiums.[123]

The same distinction applies to equidistant strikes. Britannica Money gives an illustrative collar with a put 5% below and a call 5% above the at-the-money level, then says the call will likely produce less premium than the put costs, leaving a net premium outlay.[10] A commenter discussing equity-index and ETF collars similarly notes that an equidistant collar can become a debit when put skew makes puts costlier than calls or when a pending dividend increases put premium while reducing call premium.[59] Both examples show that equal strike distance is not equal premium.

A covered-call strike must also reflect the shareholder’s willingness to lose the stock at that price. The call’s strike plus its premium should be an acceptable liquidation price, and covered-call owners reluctant to part with their shares are not usually good candidates for the strategy.[9] This makes a symmetric Delta target secondary to the investor’s actual exit-price constraint.

The sold call’s cap matters economically. A covered-call writer receives premium in exchange for giving up gains above the call strike; a higher out-of-the-money strike preserves more upside but produces less premium and a smaller initial downside cushion.[9][14] A collar adds a put, but it does not change that basic decision: the call must be placed at a price where the owner accepts the possibility of selling the shares.

Likewise, the bought put must be selected for the desired floor. In a protective collar, the put strike establishes a minimum exit price in a downturn, while the call strike establishes the ceiling on stock gains.[11] A higher put strike delivers a higher floor and costs more.[60] The owner who insists on a floor near the current stock price may therefore have to accept a nearer call strike, a net debit, or both.

**Equal absolute Deltas are best treated as a transparent starting geometry, not as a premium, floor, or cap rule.** They can reduce initial directional exposure in a readily understood way, but they do not determine whether the collar finances itself.[54][55][10]

The first collar leaves +20 Deltas because the two option legs subtract 80 of the stock’s +100 Deltas. The second leaves +55 because the options subtract only 45.[54] That comparison describes initial price sensitivity, not comparative premiums, strike distances, or final outcomes at expiration.

A symmetric collar can also become asymmetric in exposure as the stock moves. Delta is dynamic, and the collar’s put and call Deltas change as they move toward or away from the money.[17] AQR’s analysis of the CBOE S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index found that the strategy’s equity exposure varied considerably over time; from 1996 through 2014, it estimated that the collar’s exposures were effectively equivalent to those of a protective-put strategy on 19% of days.[61] An initially equal-Delta pairing therefore does not lock in a permanently symmetrical hedge.

The total collar Delta can even move near zero or negative after a large decline. In AQR’s CLL example on March 14, 2008, the long put was deep in the money with Delta −1.00 and the short call’s contribution was −0.03, producing an estimated collar portfolio Delta exposure of −0.03 after the S&P 500 had fallen more than 13% since the put was purchased.[62] The put’s protection becomes more powerful precisely when the stock has already declined toward or below the put strike.

AQR reported combined CLL exposure of 0.49 on 19 December 2008, comprising a −0.39 put contribution and a −0.12 short-call contribution. On 19 September 2014, combined exposure was 0.76, with a −0.24 put contribution and an approximately zero call contribution.[62]

TradeStation calls the zero-cost collar—defined by equal call and put premiums, not equal Deltas—its most popular variation. Equal absolute Deltas are illustrated by a 0.40-call/−0.40-put collar, while Cboe publishes unequal-strike collar and zero-cost put-spread-collar designs.[63][33][54][64][65]

Desjardins’ January 25, 2016 XSP.to illustration used out-of-the-money options in nearly symmetrical positions around the security price, while explicitly allowing other combinations, including an at-the-money call, according to the expected price change and the price the investor is willing to pay. This supports symmetry as a usable template rather than a mandatory Delta convention.[66]

A symmetric percentage-strike layout can still produce unequal premiums. In the same Desjardins illustration, a same-expiration April 15, 2016 collar had a $0.55 call credit and a $0.85 put cost, a $0.30-per-share debit, or $300 for 1,000 shares.[66]

OptionsDecay describes selling a 0.30-Delta call and buying a −0.30-Delta put as a common shorthand that approximately balances the probabilities of finishing beyond either strike, at roughly 30% on each side.[67] OptionsDecay adds that equity-index put skew makes the 0.30-Delta put richer than the 0.30-Delta call, so matching 0.30 Deltas on SPY in a normal-volatility regime usually leaves a small net debit.[67]

MindTrajour describes a typical collar as placing both the put and call at 0.20–0.30 Delta and says that this symmetry makes their premiums roughly cancel.[42]

FatTail describes a covered call as 100 long shares with one short call: a 0.30–0.40-Delta call reduces the position from +100 share-equivalent Deltas to +60 to +70.[19]

Equal 10% strike distances are a separate symmetry rule: with stock at $100, they mean a $110 call and a $90 put. Those strikes can have unequal Deltas and unequal premiums because time to expiry, rates, expected dividends, and the implied-volatility skew affect each option differently.

A call Delta is not a probability of assignment. In particular, an American-style equity call can be assigned before expiration when its holder elects early exercise, including around an ex-dividend date.

A standard protective collar holds 100 shares, buys one out-of-the-money put, and sells one out-of-the-money call on the same underlying, with each single-stock option contract covering 100 shares.[37][68][28]

A standard collar holds 100 shares, buys one out-of-the-money protective put, and writes one out-of-the-money covered call; the options generally have the same expiration and are normally in equal numbers.[69]

 The Options Industry Council-sponsored QQQQ study examined collar strategies on the QQQQ fund rather than surveying institutional holders’ actual portfolios.[69]

### asymmetric

An *asymmetric collar* deliberately uses different call and put Deltas, different percentage distances, or different expirations. A short 0.25-Delta call paired with a long −0.20-Delta put is asymmetric. Relative to a +0.20 call, the +0.25 call generally supplies more premium but sets a closer upside cap; relative to a −0.25 put, the −0.20 put costs less but establishes a lower floor. The relative call-versus-put strike distances are market-dependent. Exact strikes and premiums depend on expiration, implied volatility, dividends, interest rates, borrow, and market supply and demand.[23][54][28][70]

The reason to use asymmetric selection is straightforward. The investor usually has a specific amount of downside loss they can tolerate and a specific stock-sale price they can accept. Those are real portfolio constraints. A matched-Delta pair is only a candidate solution; it is not the objective.

A practical zero-cost construction begins with the put. Basson, Ferreira-Schenk and Dickason–Koekemoer’s 2022 *Cogent Economics & Finance* article, 《The performance of zero-cost option derivative strategies during turbulent market conditions in developing and developed countries》, says an investor’s risk tolerance determines the minimum-loss floor through the selected put strike, which then influences the associated call strike. At a fixed expiry, raising the put strike raises the floor and generally increases the put’s cost; if no other financing changes, matching that higher cost requires a more valuable sold call, ordinarily at a lower strike and with a tighter upside cap.[32][5][9] AQR’s *Risk and Return of Equity Index Collar Strategies* gives the operational sequence: choose a desired protection level—its example is three-month protection at 95% of the current index value—price that put, and then select the call strike whose premium most closely matches the purchased 5%-out-of-the-money put premium.[62][71]

That process can be stated plainly:

1. **First, set the put floor.** Choose the put strike at the minimum exit price that protects the unrealized gain, concentrated holding, or portfolio value that matters to the owner. The put strike determines where protection begins. [11][41]

2. **Second, set the acceptable call cap.** Choose a call strike at a price where selling the shares is acceptable, while recognizing that a nearer call generates more premium and gives up more upside. [9]

3. **Third, calculate the net option premium.** Subtract call premium received from put premium paid. Equal amounts create a zero-net-premium collar; a larger call premium creates a credit; a larger put premium creates a debit. [63][33][72]

4. **Fourth, revise the call, put, expiry, or all three if the result does not fit.** Strike choice affects hedge cost and the protection delivered, and expiration affects time value and the duration of coverage. [11][23][10]

This order puts the risk-management decision before the financing decision. It does not assume that premium neutrality is always worth the amount of upside the investor must surrender to obtain it.

Cboe explicitly says there is no unique best collar because investors occupy different places in risk-return space.[23] That statement fits the economic trade-off. A shareholder who is strongly bullish may choose a farther-out-of-the-money call to retain more upside, accepting lower call premium and perhaps paying a net debit.

A shareholder protecting a concentrated position or a known risk period may choose a higher put floor and sell a nearer call to fund it. Options Education says a more bullish investor may choose a farther-out-of-the-money call because strikes do not need to sit equidistant from the stock price.[5]

The call side is the adjustable financing leg in many zero-cost designs. The call premium helps pay for the put, but the price of that financing is a cap on appreciation above the strike.[11][5] For this reason, it is often more useful to ask “what upside am I willing to sell?” than “what Delta should my call have?”

A 0.25-call/0.20-put design expresses one particular preference. Relative to a 0.20-call/0.25-put design, it favors call income and a lower-cost, lower floor: the higher-Delta call has a larger negative Delta contribution and normally more premium, while the lower-absolute-Delta put contributes less negative Delta and establishes a lower floor.[54][23] It may be sensible for an investor who wants a low-cost hedge and accepts a wider initial loss band before the put becomes effective.

It is not automatically preferable to a 0.20-call/0.25-put collar, which would generally retain more upside room but purchase a nearer, more protective put.

A 0.20-call/0.25-put collar is also asymmetric, but it expresses the opposite preference: relatively more downside protection and relatively more upside room. Peter Knight’s hypothetical uses a 55-strike call with 0.20 Delta and a 45-strike put with −0.25 Delta against a $50 stock, producing +55 net Deltas for 100 shares.[54] The example does not supply premiums, but it demonstrates that different call and put Deltas are an ordinary collar construction rather than a structural error.

The direction of asymmetry changes with the market. NISA Investment Advisors says strike asymmetry is common in single-name equities and more pronounced for equities with higher implied volatility.[70] In its September 11, 2026 perspective on recent IPOs, NISA says zero-premium collar pricing tends to place the sold call farther out of the money than the purchased put, sometimes substantially so.[70] That configuration preserves comparatively more upside distance while placing the floor closer to the stock.

NISA notes that recent-IPO single stocks can exhibit positive volatility skew, under which a zero-premium collar’s sold call may be farther out of the money than its bought put.[70]

NISA contrasts that single-stock pattern with index-option pricing, which it says frequently exhibits negative skew such that the call strike is closer to spot than the put strike.[70] In other words, a zero-premium index collar may need a nearer upside cap to fund a farther downside floor, while a zero-premium single-stock collar can sometimes have the call farther from spot than the put. The pricing direction is not a matter of investor preference alone; it reflects the option market.

Several inputs can change the direction and size of asymmetry. NISA identifies expiration, risk-free rates, borrow rates, and volatility skew as influences on the implied distribution and collar pricing.[70] Low or zero dividend yields can raise the forward price above spot, creating a “fat right tail” in which the dollar magnitude of upside scenarios exceeds that of equidistant downside scenarios.[70] The apparent asymmetry can also look larger relative to spot than relative to the forward price.[70]

Volatility skew is particularly important. A flatter volatility skew means a smaller implied-volatility gap between puts and calls, allowing a farther-out-of-the-money call to fund a closer put strike.[70] NISA says single-name equities often have flatter volatility surfaces than index options, which can aid zero-premium collar pricing.[70] Conversely, steep negative skew makes the downside put expensive and the upside call cheap, pushing a premium-matching call strike closer to current price.[73]

Cboe’s CLL methodology sets construction and roll rules, not trader-prevalence evidence.[64][75] It buys quarterly 5%-out-of-the-money puts and sells monthly 10%-out-of-the-money calls.[64][75] It therefore cannot be described as an equal-Delta, equal-distance, or same-expiration template.

Cboe’s zero-cost put-spread collars show a still more explicit premium-first implementation. CLLZ and CLLR buy a monthly 2.5%-out-of-the-money put, sell a monthly 5%-out-of-the-money put, and then sell an out-of-the-money call whose bid premium equals the net cost of the put spread.[65] If no listed call has the exact required bid, the methodology sells two calls—one with a bid above and one with a bid below the target—and weights them so their average premium equals the put-spread cost.[65] This is a strong example of why zero cost is a premium-matching objective, not an equal-Delta rule.

Cboe’s premium-matching rule uses the call bid against the long-put ask and short-put bid, and can blend calls at adjacent strikes when no single call matches the put-spread cost.[65]

The published Cboe example is numerical. It buys the 2.5%-out-of-the-money RUT put for $2.40 and sells the 5%-out-of-the-money RUT put for $1.00, creating a $1.40 put-spread cost. If an out-of-the-money call has a $1.40 bid, Cboe sells one unit of that call.[65] Where the available call bids are $1.36 and $1.46, Cboe’s example uses weights of 0.6 and 0.4, respectively, to create a $1.40 weighted average.[65] A simple one-call/one-put collar is only one way to implement the economic idea.

Premium matching is not the same as “free” protection. AQR states that equal put and call prices can remove the upfront option outlay, but this alone says nothing about the investment’s attractiveness or its return consequences. AQR says a typical collar should be expected to return less than its underlying because it has lower equity exposure and buys puts that are more richly priced than the calls it sells.[62][81] The investor has exchanged gains above the call strike for the protection purchased below the put strike.

Britannica Money makes the same distinction between premium neutrality and free protection: a large stock rally still leaves gains capped by the short call.[10]

Transaction costs, bid-ask spreads, taxes, margin requirements, and other implementation costs can also remain when the option-leg premiums match. OCC says transaction costs, taxes, and margin requirements can significantly affect options profit or loss.[28] Longbridge says zero cost refers only to net option premium at inception and that fees, bid-ask spreads, and margin costs still apply.[82] A cashless collar is therefore a statement about the initial option-premium cash flow, not a statement that the shareholder bears no economic cost.

Premium matching can also be a deliberate choice rather than a universal feature. TradeStation calls zero-cost collars its most popular variation and defines them as structures in which the call premium exactly equals the put premium.[63][33] Yet AQR found that the particular CBOE S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index implementation it studied was far from zero-cost: on average, it spent roughly $7.50 on put options for every $1 collected from call sales.[62][61] Those statements refer to different things: one describes a popular collar variation, while the other measures the premium relationship of a specific index methodology.

The distinction matters because the CLL benchmark’s stated aim was not necessarily exact premium neutrality. Cboe describes its collar’s protection and foregone return as depending on option moneyness, time to expiration, and relative richness. Lowering the call strike brings in more premium and more downside cushioning, but truncates more upside; increasing the put strike raises the floor but costs more.[23] Cboe also says the 95-110 structure represents one intermediate choice among countless possible combinations, not a unique best collar.[23]

Studies of zero-cost collars support the same construction logic. Basson et al.’s 2018 *Cogent Economics & Finance* study, 《Performance of two zero-cost derivative strategies under different market conditions》, defines zero-cost collars as matching the prices received and paid for their component derivatives.[57] The study states that risk appetite determines the return floor through the put strike, while the associated call strike is reverse-engineered from its value to establish the return cap; it adds that increasing the floor increases the cap and vice versa.[57]

Basson, Ferreira-Schenk and Dickason–Koekemoer’s 2022 study used January 1995 through December 2020 data across six stock-market indices, with three-, six-, and nine-month option expiries, including the Dotcom bubble, the 2008–2009 financial crisis, and 2020.[32] It reports respectable zero-cost-collar returns when volatility was moderate and the indices performed well; it also says that increasing the put strike improved results during significant downturns.[32] A higher put strike means a more protective floor, though it normally forces a tighter call cap or another source of funding in a zero-cost construction.

El-Hassan, Hall and Tulunay’s 2021 *Journal of Applied Business and Economics* study, 《Methods and Analysis of Collar Strategies》, examined 14 collar strategies and two protective-put strategies on the S&P/ASX 200 from 2008 through 2016, using both traded option market data and Black-Scholes prices.[83] Its summary identifies a protective put and a zero-cost collar as its best-performing strategies among those tested.[83] That result supports zero-cost collars as a viable risk-management construction in the studied designs; it does not make any particular Delta pairing universally optimal.

AQR reaches a more cautious conclusion on its particular CBOE S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index implementation. From July 1, 1986 through December 31, 2014, CLL earned 3.2% annualized excess return with 10.7% volatility and a 0.30 Sharpe ratio, compared with 7.3%, 15.7%, and a 0.47 Sharpe ratio for the S&P 500; AQR says CLL’s Sharpe ratio was approximately 35% lower.[62][61] AQR characterizes equity-index collars as complex, low-beta strategies with time-varying equity and volatility exposures, and says a typical implementation would likely be a poor choice for most investors while explicitly not rejecting every possible collar in every environment.[62]

That disagreement is useful rather than contradictory. The studies and benchmarks show that collar results depend on the selected floor, cap, expiry, skew, roll schedule, and market path. A zero-cost collar removes one initial cash-flow burden; it does not resolve the return trade-off created by selling appreciation above the call strike and buying volatility protection below the put strike.[62][32][23]

Institutional structures reinforce the case for asymmetry rather than a single equal-Delta convention. LGIM America’s April 2024 pension-plan example states that a 90% one-year put could be fully offset by selling a 112% one-year call, allowing protection against a drawdown greater than 10% and positive market returns up to 12% with no upfront premium.[78] The put and call are intentionally unequal distances from spot.

Avon Pension Fund’s 2024 Equity Protection Strategy is a derivatives-based developed-equity overlay that protects against significant market falls, evolves with market conditions, and finances protection by giving up gains above a predetermined cap. Its hedge ratio is 50% of developed-market equities; compared with a 100% hedge, the Fund says this mitigates return drag, reduces costs, improves collateral overall, and retains sufficient downside protection.[74] Avon Pension Fund states that the 50% hedge ratio applies to developed-market equities.[74] The Fund states that the overlay is intended to dampen volatility and minimise risk, and that its derivatives require daily collateral, often cash.[74] The design is a portfolio hedge rather than a mandatory one-for-one collar on every share.[84]

JPMorgan’s Hedged Equity strategies use a different institutional variation: a *put-spread collar*. The fund buys an out-of-the-money put, sells a lower-strike out-of-the-money put to partly fund the hedge, and sells an out-of-the-money call to generate proceeds toward the remaining cost.[85] The lower short put means protection is partial rather than unlimited below the long-put strike.[86][85] JPMorgan’s laddered overlay describes a hedge range from −5% to −20% over a three-month window and combines three such hedges staggered one month apart.[87] Institutional practice therefore includes not only simple collars but also put-spread collars and staggered overlays.

Dynamic collars are another institutional-style structure. Schwab says larger investment managers use dynamic collars, originating with institutional investors and money managers seeking to build large stock positions over time while hedging against market corrections.[88] The manager can roll options to higher strikes and later expirations after a rising stock, or roll the put or exercise it after a decline below the put strike.[88] Peter Knight’s hypothetical dynamic collar begins with 1,000 shares, 10 out-of-the-money puts, and 10 out-of-the-money calls, then increases the hedge to 11 puts and 11 calls if option profits fund the purchase of another 100 shares.[54]

A dynamic collar is asymmetric through time even if it begins with equal Deltas, because the manager changes strikes, maturities, and hedge size as the stock and portfolio evolve. It may also incur additional commissions and transaction costs from more frequent trading and larger positions.[54] The appropriate institutional structure depends on whether the investor’s primary aim is preserving a concentrated position, hedging broad equity beta, funding a put spread, or maintaining exposure while controlling a defined drawdown range.

**The stronger practical conclusion is asymmetric selection: choose the required floor first, then solve for the acceptable cap and financing.** Equal Deltas remain useful when they independently satisfy those goals, but zero-cost implementation and institutional-style overlays commonly require unequal strikes, unequal Deltas, different expirations, or all three.[32][62][23][65][78]

Using the report’s working ranges—short call 0.20–0.35 and long put 0.25–0.35—one collar on 100 shares initially removes about 45–70 share-equivalent Deltas from the stock’s +100, leaving roughly +55 to +30 share-equivalent Deltas. This quantifies why the recommended collar is a reduced-exposure position rather than a Delta-neutral hedge.[3][2][28]

For the proposed call choice, a short +0.25-Delta call has a larger negative position-Delta contribution than a short +0.20-Delta call; moving the call closer to the stock price increases premium and brings the upside cap closer, while moving it farther out preserves more upside and collects less premium.[54][23] For the proposed put choice, a long −0.25-Delta put has a larger negative position-Delta contribution than a long −0.20-Delta put; a nearer, higher-strike put raises the floor and costs more, while a farther-out, lower-absolute-Delta put costs less and begins protection at a lower price.[54][23] These comparisons do not determine which leg is nearer to spot: in zero-premium single-stock collars NISA describes calls farther out of the money than puts, whereas in index options it describes calls closer to spot than puts.[70]

Morgan Stanley Wealth Management’s April 2023 Concentrated Position Toolkit defines a collar as a purchased put for downside protection combined with a sold call that funds some or all of the put premium, and says it may be structured for a credit, debit, or zero premium. Its hypothetical zero-premium collar has a $5.00 put premium, a −$5.00 call premium, and a $0.00 net premium. The investor remains exposed to depreciation from the initial level to the put strike and forgoes appreciation above the call strike.[89]

A disclosed corporate concentrated-stock transaction illustrates a longer-dated, initially unfunded collar. On May 28, 2026, Kenon Holdings entered a three-year collar with an investment bank on 6,000,000 OPC Energy shares, or approximately 2% of OPC shares outstanding: Kenon bought a put and sold a higher-strike call, retained upside to the call strike, limited the effect of declines, and owed no entry premium or fee. The transaction may settle in cash or, at Kenon’s election, OPC shares, and can provide financing through borrowing against the collar in specified circumstances.[90]

Additional documented institutional structures. JPMorgan’s November 1, 2023 Hedged Equity Fund prospectus describes a *Put/Spread Collar*: it buys a higher-strike S&P 500 Index put, sells a lower-strike put, and simultaneously sells an S&P 500 Index call. Call premiums are intended to substantially offset the put-spread cost. The put spread is generally maintained to reduce exposure to a 5%–20% market decline, while the upside cap is generally 3.5%–5.5%, varying with market conditions. The short lower-strike put makes protection limited to the difference between the two put strikes.[91]

Morningstar describes the same fund’s implementation as buying puts 5% below the S&P 500 level, selling puts 20% below it, and selling calls roughly 3%–5% above it, using three-month ETF options reset quarterly; Hedged Equity 2 and 3 use offset three-month schedules beginning at January and February month-ends.[92]

Simplify Nasdaq-100 Hedged Equity ETF’s September 2025 prospectus describes another put/spread collar: buy a higher-strike Nasdaq-100 Index or ETF put, sell a lower-strike put, and sell a call. Up to 20% of net asset value is subject to the overlay; it targets protection for a 5%–20% index decline and resets at least quarterly. Call premiums can offset the put-spread cost, while protection is limited to the distance between the two put strikes and gains above the call strike are surrendered.[93]

Fidelity Dynamic Buffered Equity ETF’s May 2025 prospectus describes a rules-based *Defensive Option Collar* on a diversified equity portfolio. It buys index puts in certain market conditions and sells index calls to substantially offset the protection cost, while diversifying option strikes and expirations.[94]

A percentage-based index collar is also used by the Nasdaq-100 Quarterly Collar 95-110 Index: each quarter it holds Nasdaq-100 stocks, buys puts generally 5% below the index level, and sells calls generally 10% above it. The call income may offset some put cost; at expiration, the put protects losses below its strike and the call offsets gains above its strike.[95]

Eaton Vance Risk-Managed Diversified Equity Income Fund reported a same-expiration S&P 500 index collar at June 30, 2025: it buys puts below the current index value, writes calls above it, and uses written-call income to buy an equal number of puts. The filing reported $3,445,436 identified cost for purchased puts and $3,734,504 premiums received for written calls.[96]

The 2018 study 《Performance of two zero-cost derivative strategies under different market conditions》 defines a zero-cost collar as equal option prices, sets the put strike or loss limit first according to risk appetite, and then determines the call strike to raise enough to pay for the put. The study used three-month options and index data from January 2006 through December 2016; it reports that the more profitable strategy depended strongly on prevailing market conditions and that zero-cost-collar performance improved as the selected strike level increased in its S&P 500 results.[97]

The study also concludes that moderate volatility and high-performing indices produced its best zero-cost-collar returns in both significant downturns and bullish markets, and identifies retirement funds as potential users because capital protection is important to them.[97]

Published zero-cost-collar work reinforces the floor-first approach. Basson, Ferreira-Schenk and Dickason–Koekemoer define zero-cost collars as matching prices received and paid for the component derivatives; they state that risk appetite selects the put-strike return floor, and the associated call strike is reverse-engineered to set the return cap. Their construction therefore targets premium equality, not equal Deltas or equal percentage distances.[98] Their stated floor-cap relationship is that increasing the floor increases the cap, and vice versa.[98]

A rolling zero-cost collar is a sequence of individual zero-cost collars rolled consecutively, usually monthly, with the same maturity and time to expiry for each new collar.[97] This rolling format makes strike selection a recurring floor, cap, and premium-matching decision rather than a permanent initial Delta choice.[97][98]

One maturity-asymmetric construction pairs a six-month put with repeatedly written one-month calls and rebalances the positions 1:1:1 at each call roll; the repeated sales provide call income but introduce rollover risk.[127]

The Options Industry Council/CISDM QQQ study makes the tight-versus-loose collar trade-off explicit: a tight collar gives less upside participation and more downside protection, at-the-money puts and calls can effectively immunize the portfolio from market moves, and very far-out-of-the-money options make the collar close to a long-index position.[99] Its passive designs tested 2%-out-of-the-money puts with calls from at the money to 5% out of the money, and 2%-out-of-the-money calls with puts from at the money to 5% out of the money; the study characterized 2%-out-of-the-money structures as a middle ground.[99]

For April 1999 through September 2010, the QQQ study reported that the passive collar with a 2%-out-of-the-money one-month call and a 2%-out-of-the-money six-month put had a 9.56% annualized return, 10.66% annualized standard deviation, and −17.58% maximum drawdown, versus −0.29%, 29.55%, and −81.08%, respectively, for the fund-only comparison.[99] The study found collar results more sensitive to the rising or falling market environment than to option moneyness, while one-month versus six-month call maturity could have a more significant effect.[99]

The same QQQ research also describes active collars whose initial put and call moneyness are determined by momentum and macroeconomic signals and whose written-call ratio is determined by a volatility signal.[99] Its update says the active adjustment strategy adapted to momentum, volatility, unemployment, and the business cycle, tended to outperform the passive collar in and out of sample, and that the preferred approach depends on the individual investor’s risk tolerance.[100] This is institutional-style dynamic asymmetry: moneyness, call quantity, and timing are deliberately varied rather than held to equal Deltas.[99][100]

The QQQ active-collar study used a VIX-based regime to vary written-call coverage from 0.75 to 1.25 calls per unit of long-index exposure.[69]

Asymmetry can also be in expiration. Desjardins’ January 25, 2016 XSP.to example bought an April 15, 2016 $21 put for $0.85 and sold a September 16, 2016 $22 call for $0.90 when XSP.to closed at $21.51, creating a $0.05-per-share option credit before commissions for its 1,000-share illustration.[66] Desjardins calls this a diagonal collar: it is effective until the put expires, after which the position is a covered call until the call expires.[66]

The premium-funding trade-off also explains why a conventional one-for-one collar need not be premium neutral. Swan Global Investments states that puts are usually significantly more expensive than calls; a manager may accept the resulting hedge-cost drag or move the put farther out of the money, reducing both hedge cost and the degree of protection.[101] Selling multiple calls per put can generate more premium, but it creates a leveraged position if the market rises past the call strike.[101]

For one standard 100-share collar, share-equivalent Delta is 100 + 100 × put Delta − 100 × call Delta, using each long option’s quoted Delta.[102] Equal absolute option Deltas therefore reduce the stock’s initial Delta by the sum of the two option Deltas, whereas asymmetric Deltas deliberately retain a different amount of stock exposure.[102][54]

AQR says that a typical collar should have lower expected returns and a lower expected Sharpe ratio than its underlying when long-term equity and volatility risk premiums are positive, but is likely to outperform during market downturns because of lower equity exposure and often net long-volatility exposure.[81] AQR says it reached very similar conclusions in simulations of collar constructions closer to zero cost, even though the particular construction studied spent more on puts than it collected from calls.[81]

Aptus Collared Investment Opportunity ETF’s annual report for the year ended April 30, 2024 documents an institutional portfolio-overlay structure rather than a one-for-one matched-Delta collar. The fund typically sold covered calls on up to 100% of its equity holdings and reinvested a portion of call premium in puts on the same equities, a U.S. equity ETF, or a U.S. equity index.[80] Its manager specifically described selling calls on individual holdings while buying broad-index puts to collar the equity portfolio more efficiently, and reported that lower volatility forced a choice between collecting less call premium and setting a lower upside ceiling.[80]

The Options Industry Council says the short-call premium can offset the protective put’s cost and that certain strike-and-expiration combinations can open a collar for a net credit.[69]

UBS distinguishes an exchange-listed equity collar, using physically settled American-style options, from an over-the-counter (OTC) equity collar with an investment bank. OTC collars permit customized size, strikes, and maturity dates and may be physically or cash settled and American or European style.[79]

Equal absolute Deltas, equal strike distances, and zero net option premium are different objectives. An equal-Delta collar offers a clear initial reduction in stock sensitivity, but skew can leave the put more expensive than the matching call. A zero-cost collar instead matches the premium received on the call to the premium paid for the put, so the call strike is normally adjusted after the investor chooses the required downside floor.[54][55][62][32]

The recommended default is asymmetric: buy the put that establishes the tolerated loss, then sell the call at an acceptable sale price that funds all or part of that protection. The direction of asymmetry depends on the underlying and option surface—single-name collars can place the call farther out of the money than the put, while index collars often require a nearer call because downside puts are richer.[70][55]

## What about percentage distance from current price?

- **Percentage out of the money (OTM)** is a strike-distance measure: it states how far the strike sits above or below the current stock price. **Delta** is different: it estimates how much the option premium changes for a $1 stock move, holding other pricing inputs constant. A percentage target therefore sets the location of the collar’s upside cap and downside floor; it does not set a permanent Delta, assignment probability, or zero-cost result. [28][7]

### Sell call 10% OTM

- For a $100 stock, selling a $110 call means selling a call 10% OTM: its strike is $10 above spot, and $10 divided by $100 equals 10%. A call is OTM when its strike exceeds the current market value of the underlying. [29][28]

- A 10%-OTM call does **not** have one fixed Delta. Stock price, time to expiration, implied volatility, interest rates, and—on stock options—potentially dividend yield all affect Delta. Higher volatility and longer time to expiration generally pull an OTM call’s Delta toward 0.50; lower volatility and shorter time move it closer to zero. [7][8]

- The same 10% distance can thus mean materially different risk and premium on different stocks. Nations Indexes illustrates this directly: a $110 call on a $100 stock is 10% OTM, but cautions that 10% has very different significance for a quiet stock and a volatile stock. [29]

In CME’s futures-option example, with the underlying futures at $260, calls struck at $270, $280 and $290 had Deltas of 0.30, 0.20 and 0.10, respectively.[128]

- In one stated model—not a live-market quote—a 30-day $110 call on a $100 stock with 30% implied volatility costs $0.61 per share and has Delta 0.14. That is a useful illustration of why a 10%-OTM call can be below the 0.20 Delta often considered in covered-call selection, but it is not a rule for every $110 call. [29]

- A farther-OTM call leaves more stock appreciation available before the shares are called away, but its smaller premium provides less funding for the put. Options Education makes the same trade-off in strike terms: a higher OTM call preserves more upside but produces less premium and therefore a smaller decline cushion. [9][72]

- Published covered-call approaches span much closer strikes than 10% OTM. Cboe’s S&P 500 2% OTM BuyWrite Index (BXY) writes successive one-month SPX calls and selects the listed strike closest to 102% of the S&P 500 Index value reported before 11:00 a.m. ET on the roll date; Cboe’s BXM BuyWrite benchmark writes successive one-month at-the-money calls. Fidelity’s intermediate-term covered-call guidance describes strikes at least 5% above the current stock price as tending to produce adequate premium income for 90-day-to-six-month options. [1][18]

- **A 10%-OTM short call is a valid collar cap when retaining substantial upside matters more than put financing; it is not a fixed-Delta convention.** The practical decision is whether the $110 strike is an acceptable sale price for the shares during the chosen option term. [18]

- Interactive Brokers gives a percentage-based covered-call convention: investors often select OTM calls with strikes approximately 10% or more above the prevailing share price and about 30 days to expiry. Its 100-share example has stock at $73, a 30-day $80 call approximately 10% above spot, and a $1.60 per-share premium, or $160 received. [52]

- JPMorgan Hedged Equity Fund’s reported call caps demonstrate that institutional percentage selection can move with market conditions: its calls averaged 3%–5% above the index, reached a highest cap of 7% OTM in 2022, and were written just over 2% OTM when interest rates and volatility were lowest. [92]

### Buy put 10% OTM

- For the same $100 stock, buying a $90 put means buying a put 10% OTM: its strike is $10 below spot, and $10 divided by $100 equals 10%. A put is OTM when its strike lies below the underlying’s current market value. [29][28]

- The $90 put creates a **floor**—the minimum sale price supplied by the option at expiration—at $90 per share, before allowing for the collar’s net option premium. Once stock is below the put strike, the put offsets further dollar-for-dollar stock losses at expiration. [103]

- As with the call, 10% OTM does not determine the put’s absolute Delta. Delta changes with stock price, time remaining, volatility, rates, and potentially dividend yield; increased volatility generally moves an OTM put’s Delta toward -0.50. [7][8]

- The asymmetry can be especially important because of **downside volatility skew**, the market pattern in which OTM downside puts often carry higher implied volatility than equally distant upside calls. In Cboe’s January 26, 2021 SPX illustration with 30 days remaining, 10%-OTM calls traded at 14.8% implied volatility and 10%-OTM puts at 28%. [104]

- Percentage-based protective-put guidance often begins closer than 10% below spot. TradeStation describes puts 5%–10% below the current stock price as a common starting area to balance protection and cost; for a $100 stock, it gives $95 and $90 puts as examples. A lower-strike put costs less but protects less of a smaller decline, whereas a put nearer spot protects sooner but costs more. [33][43]

- **A $90 put is appropriate when the holder accepts the first 10% of stock loss in exchange for a cheaper floor; a $95 put starts protection sooner but normally requires more call premium or a net debit.** A lower-strike put costs less, provides less protection against smaller stock moves, and has a lower rate of time decay when its strike is significantly below the current stock price; a put closer to spot provides more protection but costs more and has greater time decay. The percentage establishes the retained loss before protection begins, while the live option chain supplies the resulting Delta and premium. [103][72][43]

- In Omen’s hypothetical $100-stock, 25%-implied-volatility, 90-day illustration, the $100, $95, and $90 puts cost $4.45, $2.41, and $1.12 per share, respectively, or $445, $241, and $112 per 100-share contract. The $90 put leaves $1,000 of decline uninsured, while the at-the-money put caps the worst case at $445. [49]

### Equal 10%-OTM strikes

- A $110 call and a $90 put are **symmetric by strike distance**: each lies 10% from the $100 stock price. That geometry is useful for expressing a desired range—retain gains up to $110 and insure the position below $90—but it does not make the options economically symmetric. [29]

- Equal percentage distances do not require equal Deltas. The call and put have different signs of Delta, and their absolute Deltas can differ because their implied volatilities, time values, and pricing inputs differ. Cboe’s 30-day SPX example shows that even options both 10% OTM can sit on very different implied-volatility levels. [7][8][104]

- Equal percentage distances also do not ensure equal premiums. Cboe compared 29-day SPX options each about 5% OTM: the call midpoint was 3.55 index points, while the put midpoint was 31.65 index points. Cboe describes this as a consequence of the post-1987 market willingness to pay more for downside protection than for equidistant upside exposure. [56]

- This premium difference is why an equidistant collar can carry a debit. Britannica’s illustrative 5%-below put and 5%-above call collar is likely to have a net premium outlay because the call premium is likely smaller than the put cost. Its premium-neutral illustration instead pairs a 5%-OTM put with a call only 4% above spot. [10]

- A 5%–10% strike distance on each side is a practical starting grid, not a completed collar specification. TradeStation suggests strikes approximately 5%–10% from spot, adjusted for volatility and risk tolerance; Longbridge similarly cautions that fixed percentage distances ignore prevailing volatility and the investor’s risk-management needs. [33][82]

- **For a zero-cost objective, choose the desired put floor first and then adjust the call strike until its bid approximately funds the put’s ask; do not force equal percentage distances.** AQR describes this construction directly: select the desired protection level, such as a 5%-OTM put, then select the call strike whose premium most closely matches the put premium. [71]

- Desjardins describes nearly symmetric OTM calls and puts as one collar arrangement and identifies an at-the-money call as another combination; it selects the arrangement for the expected price change and the price the investor is willing to pay. [66]

- OptionsDecay’s zero-cost method selects the put strike first as the floor and then finds the call strike whose bid matches the put’s cost. It states that index skew places the funding call closer to the money than the put, whereas in rising-market environments the call strike is usually farther OTM because calls command more premium than equidistant puts. [67]

- OptionsDecay’s 30-DTE SPY example holds 100 shares at $520, sells the $530 call for $5.00, and buys the $510 put for $5.00, producing a $0 net cost. Each strike is $10 from spot, or 1.92% of $520. [67]

- A $50-stock collar illustration uses a $55 call with Delta 0.20 and a $45 put with Delta -0.25. The $55 call and $45 put are each 10% from the $50 stock price, so equal percentage distances can accompany unequal absolute Deltas. [54]

- A hypothetical one-year zero-cost collar on a $100 stock buys a 20%-OTM put and sells a 40%-OTM call at the same price. The implied strikes are $80 and $140, respectively: matching option prices, rather than equal percentage distances, create the zero-cost collar. [105]

- Which leg is farther OTM to reach zero cost can reverse with relative option richness. OptionsDecay states that index skew makes puts richer than equally distant calls, so the funding call is closer to the money than the put; in rising-market environments, calls typically command more premium than equally distant puts, so the funding call is usually farther OTM than the put. [67]

### Example collar

-Derived — **model-based symmetric-distance collar.** For both options, assume spot of $100 per share; strikes of $110 for the call and $90 for the put; 30 calendar days, or 30/365 years, to expiration; 30% implied volatility; zero interest rate; no dividends; European-style Black–Scholes pricing; and one contract representing 100 shares. The prices and Deltas below are calculated model outputs, not executable quotes.[29][28]

- **Stock:** Buy or hold 100 shares at $100, for a $10,000 stock position.

- **Sell call:** Sell one $110 call, 10% OTM. The calculated model output for the $110 call is Delta approximately **+0.14**; because the call is sold, its signed position Delta is approximately **-0.14 per share**, or about -14 share-equivalents for one 100-share contract. [29][28] Its calculated model price is approximately **$0.61 per share**, or **$61 received** for one 100-share contract. [29][28]

- **Buy put:** Buy one $90 put, also 10% OTM. Under the same Black–Scholes inputs, the report’s calculated model output for the $90 put is Delta approximately **-0.10** and a price of approximately **$0.43 per share**, or **$43 paid** for one 100-share contract. [29][39][28] The put’s lower absolute Delta relative to the call is a result of the particular assumed 30-day, 30%-volatility, zero-rate model—not a general feature of 10%-OTM collars.

- **Net credit/debit:** The report’s calculated option-leg result is an estimated **net credit of $18 per 100-share collar**, before trading costs: **($0.61 − $0.43) × 100 = approximately $18**. [29][39][28] The calculation is $0.61 minus approximately $0.43 equals approximately $0.18 per share, multiplied by 100 shares.

- This is a deliberately simplified model example, rather than a claim about a tradable quote. Live markets can produce a very different put price, call price, and pair of Deltas because equity-option skew, dividends, rates, and bid-ask spreads change the inputs; the Cboe SPX examples show why equal-distance downside puts can be much more expensive than calls in actual markets. [104][56]

- A quoted illustration makes the live-pricing point more concrete. A video example uses a $100 stock, a 45-day $90 put offered for $3.00, and a $110 call sold for $2.80; that is the same symmetric 10%-OTM layout but a **$0.20-per-share, or $20-per-collar, net debit**, before transaction costs. [106]

- The two examples rule out treating “$90 put/$110 call” as synonymous with “zero-cost collar.” Strike distances specify the payoff boundaries; the option quotes available for the selected expiration determine whether the collar begins with a credit, debit, or near-zero premium. [10][106]

### Published percentage-based collar designs

- Options Education describes a conventional collar as generally using a call strike above and a put strike below the starting stock price, and says the options are generally equidistant when discussing how time decay and implied-volatility effects may offset. This describes a common same-expiry educational layout, not a requirement that strikes be equally distant or that premiums match. [11]

- Cboe’s S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index, or CLL, takes a deliberately asymmetric approach: it holds the S&P 500 portfolio, buys 5%-OTM SPX puts on the quarterly cycle, and sells 10%-OTM SPX calls monthly. Its strike rules target 95% and 110% of the S&P 500 value reported just before 11:00 a.m. ET: if the targeted put strike is unavailable, Cboe selects the closest listed strike at or below 95%, and if the targeted call is unavailable, it selects the closest listed strike at or above 110%. On a hypothetical $100 reference value, those targets are a $95 put and a $110 call. [75]

- Cboe’s CLL1M instead buys a monthly 5%-OTM SPX put and sells a monthly at-the-money call. CLL3M uses a quarterly 5%-OTM put and a quarterly 10%-OTM call. These alternatives show that published collar designs vary strike distances and maturities to target different protection, income, and upside-retention profiles. [75]

- Cboe publishes the Cboe Russell 2000 Zero-Cost Put Spread Collar Index (CLLR) and the Cboe S&P 500 Zero-Cost Put Spread Collar Index (CLLZ), each a zero-cost **put-spread collar** rather than a basic long-put collar. Each buys a monthly 2.5%-OTM put at the first listed strike below 97.5% of the relevant index value, sells a monthly 5%-OTM put at the first listed strike below 95%, and selects OTM call option(s) so the call bid equals the long-put ask less the short-put bid. This premium-matching design limits protection below the short-put strike. [65]

- **Published practice favors purpose-built asymmetry over one universal percentage rule: equal distances may suit a simple payoff range, while 5%-put/10%-call, at-the-money-call, and premium-matched structures serve different objectives.** Cboe itself states that there is no unique best collar because investors occupy different risk-return comfort zones. [23]

- The Global X NASDAQ 100 Collar 95-110 ETF invests at least 80% of total assets in the Nasdaq-100 Quarterly Collar 95-110 Index. The index holds Nasdaq-100 component stocks, buys quarterly puts generally 5% below the prevailing NASDAQ-100 Index level, and sells quarterly calls generally 10% above it; when exact strikes are unavailable, it chooses the closest listed strikes. [95]

- AQR describes a three-month zero-cost index collar that first chooses the protection level—such as a put at 95% of current index value—and then selects the call strike whose premium most closely matches the 5%-OTM put. [81]

- AQR also reports that the CBOE S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index buys three-month puts about 5% OTM and sells one-month calls about 10% OTM. AQR calculates that this construction spends roughly $7.50 on puts for every $1 collected from calls, so its deliberately asymmetric 95-110 design is not zero cost. [81]

- AllianceBernstein’s illustrative three-year cashless collar uses a $100 stock price, an $85 put strike, and a $120 call strike: a 15%-below floor and a 20%-above cap. Its separate three-year protective-put illustration also uses a $100 stock, an $85 put, and a $10 put premium. [107]

- JPMorgan’s November 1, 2023 Hedged Equity Fund prospectus states that its put spread is generally maintained to reduce exposure to market declines of 5%–20% and that its upside cap is generally 3.5%–5.5%, with the cap varying with market conditions. These are exposure-reduction and upside-cap ranges, not stated strike distances from the current price. [91]

- The Options Industry Council/CISDM QQQ study tested passive collars with 2%-OTM puts and calls ranging from at the money to 5% OTM, and the reverse arrangement of 2%-OTM calls with puts ranging from at the money to 5% OTM. It found performance sensitive to option moneyness but more sensitive to rising or falling market environments, and found the one-month-versus-six-month call maturity choice could have a more significant effect. [99]

- In the Options Industry Council/CISDM QQQQ work, the passive QQQQ collar used a 2%-OTM six-month put and a 2%-OTM one-month call. Its active collar began with the put 3% OTM and the call 2% OTM because puts tend to cost more than calls at the same moneyness; placing the put farther OTM made the initial option component close to zero cost. [69]

- The empirical zero-cost-collar study covering January 2006 through December 2016 used three-month index options. It selected an acceptable loss threshold and manipulated the other strike until the strategy had no net option cost, with the short call paying for the long put. [97]

## What collar structures do institutional holders use?

Judgment — Actual holder records show that institutional collars can be portfolio overlays rather than separate one-contract collars on each 100-share holding. Avon Pension Fund uses a collar-style hedge on approximately 50% of its developed-equity exposures, and JPMorgan’s Hedged Equity Fund overlays index options on a diversified U.S. large-cap equity portfolio.[84][85] Their design starts with the risk to be controlled—such as a funded-status drawdown, a concentrated-stock event, or a desired equity accumulation path—then sets the floor, upside cap, maturity, hedge size, and financing method. Published benchmark methodologies generally specify strike percentages, loss bands, premium budgets, and roll schedules rather than a universal Delta target.[64][65][85]

### Rules-based index collars

A **rules-based collar** fixes its option-selection and roll rules in advance. It provides a transparent benchmark for a repeatable equity-overlay strategy, but it is not a claim that every investor should use its strikes or maturities.

Cboe’s S&P 500 95-110 Collar Index (CLL) is a published benchmark methodology, not a record of an institutional holder’s transaction. Its passive rules hold the S&P 500 portfolio and dividends, buy SPX puts 5% out of the money with quarterly expirations, and sell SPX calls 10% out of the money monthly. The put strike is reset to 95% of the S&P 500 value at the put roll, while the call strike is reset to 110% at the monthly call roll. Thus, the downside floor is nearer the market than the upside cap, and the put lasts longer than each call.[64][23]

Cboe’s CLL methodology rolls its monthly calls on the third Friday.[75]

For a hypothetical $100 index level, CLL’s published benchmark percentages translate to a $95 put and a $110 call. That is an **asymmetric collar** by strike distance: the put is 5% below spot and the call is 10% above spot. It is also asymmetric by maturity, because the put is quarterly and the call is monthly. Cboe states that there is no unique best collar because investors differ in risk-return preferences; its 95-110 construction is an intermediate benchmark between Treasury bills and a fully exposed S&P 500 portfolio.[75][23]

The CLL construction is not inherently zero-cost. AQR’s *Risk and Return of Equity Index Collar Strategies* reports that the CLL portfolio spent roughly $7.50 on puts for every $1 collected from calls on average. The call helps finance the put, but the selected 5%-OTM quarterly put and 10%-OTM monthly call did not, in that analysis, produce equal premiums.[62][23]

The benchmark methodologies show why a collar is not one payoff. CLL uses a continuous long put below its put strike, while CLLZ and CLLR use a **put-spread collar**: long a higher-strike put and short a farther-out-of-the-money put. The short put reduces the initial hedge cost, but it also means protection is limited below the lower strike rather than continuing through a severe decline.[78][65]

Cboe’s zero-cost put-spread methodology makes the financing rule explicit. For CLLR, the premium received from the short call is set to equal the ask price of the 2.5%-OTM put less the bid price of the 5%-OTM short put. Cboe’s illustration purchases the nearer put at $2.40 and sells the farther put at $1.00, leaving a $1.40 put-spread cost; an OTM call with a $1.40 bid would offset it.[65]

**The practical implication is that a zero-cost collar is a premium constraint, not a symmetric-Delta or equal-distance constraint.** A manager can first choose the desired protection band, then set the call strike where its premium finances that hedge; that call strike may be much nearer to or farther from spot than the purchased put.[65][62]

CLL’s 10%-OTM call sits twice as far above the index as its 5%-OTM put sits below it: a 2.0x upside-distance-to-downside-distance ratio at each strike reset. The benchmark is therefore explicitly asymmetric by strike distance even before its monthly-call/quarterly-put maturity mismatch is considered.[64]

Global X’s Nasdaq-100 Quarterly Collar 95-110 Index uses the same asymmetric percentage convention in a different broad-equity exposure: it combines Nasdaq-100 stocks with quarterly 5%-OTM puts and quarterly 10%-OTM calls, selecting the closest available strike when an exact 5% or 10% offset is unavailable.[95]

CBOE presents CLL as a convenient benchmark for portfolio managers looking to collar market risk: it overlays an SPX bear spread, comprising a long put and short call, on an S&P 500 portfolio; the put protects against market declines and the call helps finance the put.[23]

Morgan Stanley Wealth Management’s April 2023 hypothetical concentrated-position collar shows an exactly premium-matched design: put premium of +$5.00, call premium of -$5.00, and net premium of $0.00.[89]

### Pension risk objectives

For pension plans, the relevant objective is usually **funded-status risk**: the risk that asset values fall relative to promised benefit obligations. That objective makes an equity collar less about maximizing every increment of equity upside and more about narrowing the range of possible funding outcomes.

A pension plan’s funded status compares its assets with the present value of its promised benefits.[78]

LGIM America presents collars specifically for pension plans, particularly well-funded plans seeking to preserve funded status or to reduce equity exposure as funding improves. Its stated rationale is that, once a plan approaches its funding objective, retaining the status can matter more than gaining every possible additional equity return.[78]

LGIM America’s April 2024 pension-plan illustration used a purchased put at 90% of current spot and a sold call at 112% of spot. In plain terms, the illustration buys protection beginning after a 10% equity decline while retaining gains up to 12% above the starting level, with the call premium fully offsetting the put premium at that time. This is an explicitly asymmetric zero-upfront-cost illustration: the put is 10% below spot, while the call is 12% above spot.[78]

That example is useful because it separates three ideas that are often conflated:

- **Protection threshold:** The 90% put does not protect the first 10% of a decline. It begins to offset losses only below that level. [78]
- **Upside ceiling:** The 112% call allows market gains up to 12%, then gives up further appreciation above that strike. [78]
- **Zero upfront cost:** This describes the matching of option premiums at entry, not a guarantee that the overall hedge has no economic trade-off. The cost is the surrendered upside above the call strike. [78]

Avon Pension Fund’s 2023/24 Annual Report documents an **Equity Protection Strategy** that uses over-the-counter Equity Option Index Swaps to protect its funding position against significant falls in equity markets. Avon states that its equity hedge protects downside and caps upside above a fully funded position under the current funding plan.[110]

Avon’s 2023/24 Annual Report demonstrates that hedge sizing changes with the funded-risk objective. After equity values rose, the Fund concluded that reducing hedging from 100% to 50% of equity assets would reduce return drag while retaining material protection; it implemented the mandate change in Q1 2024 and released cash into the portfolio.[110]

A **put-spread collar** is a documented institutional-fund structure when a full downside floor is more expensive than the investor wishes to pay for. The investor buys an OTM put, sells a farther-OTM put to subsidize it, and sells an OTM call to generate additional proceeds. The added short put can either reduce the initial outlay or permit a higher call strike, but protection only operates between the purchased-put and sold-put strikes.[78][85]

LGIM America describes put-spread collars as a historically popular way to seek protection without an upfront premium.[129]

JPMorgan’s Hedged Equity Fund is a concrete fund-holder example of a portfolio-level put-spread collar, not a pension-plan record. The fund holds a diversified U.S. large-cap equity portfolio and uses S&P 500 index or S&P 500 ETF options. Its overlay buys a higher-strike put, sells a lower-strike put, and simultaneously sells an index call; the call income is intended to substantially offset the put-spread cost. The fund resets options at least quarterly in response to market conditions.[86][85]

JPMorgan’s fund-series materials describe the put spread as providing a hedge over the S&P 500’s losses from 5% to 20% on a three-month basis. This is not a permanent floor below all market levels: it is a defined loss-band hedge. The three funds use staggered start dates and reset every three months, creating overlapping hedge vintages rather than placing all exposure into one single reset date.[85]

**The pension pattern is outcome-driven rather than Delta-driven: plans choose the amount of drawdown they can retain, the range they want to hedge, and the upside they can concede.** LGIM’s 90% put/112% call, Avon’s approximately 50% exposure hedge, and JPMorgan’s -5% to -20% protection band are different ways of defining that outcome.[78][84][85]

JPMorgan’s stated three-month hedge from a 5% to a 20% S&P 500 loss covers a 15-percentage-point loss band. Its potential upside is generally capped at 3.5% to 5.5%, and the upside cap may be higher or lower with market conditions. That arithmetic makes clear that the put-spread collar is targeted protection over a defined drawdown interval, rather than an unlimited floor below the long-put strike.[85][111]

Avon Pension Fund’s 2024 Investment Strategy Statement describes its Equity Protection Strategy as a funding-level-driven, derivative-based overlay that protects against significant falls in developed equity markets, evolves with market conditions, and finances downside protection by giving up gains above a predetermined cap. Its hedge ratio is 50% of developed-market equities; Avon states that, relative to a 100% hedge, this mitigates return drag, reduces cost, improves the collateral position, and retains sufficient downside protection.[74]

Avon reports that the Equity Protection Strategy requires daily collateral, often cash, and that it may set a bespoke strategy to meet its risk-management objectives. Avon’s 2024 statement attributes an approximately 9% reduction in its three-year Value-at-Risk (VaR) to the strategy; its 2023 statement reported an approximately 30% reduction, so these are separate estimates from different policy statements rather than a single stable result.[74][112]

Avon’s 2024/25 annual report records the realised upside trade-off: when equity markets rose during that year, the hedge reduced net equity returns.[113]

Schroders’ 2018 institutional discussion frames the basic pension choice similarly: a plan can combine equities with a put to offset losses below the put strike, paying a premium and retaining gains less that premium; selling calls can be used to offset part of the put cost, at the price of capped gains.[114][51]

Clwyd Pension Fund uses a derivatives-based framework: it gains equity exposure synthetically and complements it with an equity-protection program that hedges a substantial portion of equity exposure against market declines. Clwyd accepts that it will not capture all market gains relative to passive unhedged equity investment; it describes the protection cost as providing “increased actuarial certainty” while maintaining equity exposure.[84]

Cambridge Associates’ 2021 CA Institute survey of 176 institutions found that 91% did not directly own derivatives to hedge equity risk, 6% were considering doing so, and 3% had implemented a derivatives-based hedge in the past. Cambridge Associates recommends tail hedges when an institution cannot shift asset allocation and faces risk that is significant and specific to it, such as an upcoming outflow that makes a probable drawdown unacceptable; it identifies a portfolio-level derivatives strategy as an alternative to substantial turnover of the underlying portfolio.[51]

FlashAlpha describes a common institutional compromise as rolling short-dated put spreads to hedge corrections and placing the structural cap-for-protection trade further out, where the skew subsidy is largest.[115]

### Implementation

Institutional implementation affects both the economics and the operational risk of a collar. A plan or large holder can use exchange-traded options, over-the-counter (OTC) options, or an OTC contract designed to mirror listed-option specifications.

LGIM distinguishes exchange-traded options, which have standardized strikes, maturities, and published daily prices, from OTC options, which offer more customizable strikes, maturities, and collateral arrangements but less pricing transparency. It also identifies OTC listed-lookalike options as a hybrid: they mirror exchange-traded specifications but trade OTC.[78]

UBS similarly distinguishes an exchange-listed equity collar from an OTC collar arranged with an investment bank. The OTC format can customize size, strikes, and maturities, and may be physically or cash settled and American or European style. During both listed and OTC collars, the investor retains ownership, voting rights, and dividends on the underlying shares.[79]

A collar sized to a physical equity allocation need not add leverage, because the underlying holding can satisfy the delivery obligation of the written call. However, LGIM notes that the options are marked to market daily: a rising market can require collateral to be posted as the short call gains value, while a falling market can create collateral owed to the plan as the put appreciates.[78]

This collateral feature is central for institutions. The payoff at expiration may look fully covered, but a large manager must also maintain liquidity throughout the life of the collar. A plan choosing an OTC structure may value flexible collateral terms; a plan choosing listed options may value transparency and exchange-standardized execution.[78]

UBS presents, rather than records, a two-year concentrated-stock collar illustration on a $100 stock that buys a $90 put and sells a $120 call for a net upfront cost of $5 per share. Its cashless illustration lowers the floor to $80 and lowers the cap to $110, demonstrating that a lower floor and a nearer call can change the premium balance.[79]

Raymond James presents, rather than records, an OTC illustration on 100,000 shares worth $5 million: an 85% put floor at $42.50, a 110% call ceiling at $55, a 12-month term, and stated net premium of 0% or $0. Its disclosure states that net premium varies with the structure’s terms and that fees, generally 3% or less in that illustration, can affect the calculated floor or ceiling.[24]

Institutional collars can also be **dynamic collars**. Charles Schwab describes larger investment managers using dynamic collars to build a larger stock position over time while hedging corrections. In one hypothetical progression, a manager starts with 1,000 shares, 10 OTM puts, and 10 OTM calls; after using option-related profits to buy 100 additional shares, the manager increases the hedge to 11 puts and 11 calls for the resulting 1,100-share position.[88][54]

A dynamic collar is therefore not a fixed one-time cap and floor. It is a managed process that resizes the option overlay as the underlying position changes. It may help a manager accumulate exposure while maintaining protection, but it does not mean the total stock-plus-options position will necessarily profit after a decline; the stock loss can exceed the gains on the long put and short call.[54]

Institutional investors also use collars for event-driven protection. TradeStation states that many institutional investors use collars around events while retaining dividend rights and the ability to participate in corporate actions. This use case is especially relevant for a concentrated position where selling the shares may be undesirable before an earnings release, corporate event, lock-up expiry, or other defined risk date.[63][33]

**The implementation decision determines what can be customized and how collateral is managed; the dynamic decision determines whether the hedge stays aligned with a changing exposure.** Neither choice implies a standard institutional Delta pair.[79][78][88]

For a concentrated holding, a collar can preserve ownership while defining a price band when an outright sale would conflict with tax, accounting, liquidity, diversification, or monetization objectives. A put-spread collar reduces premium cost but protects only a defined decline range, as in JPMorgan’s three-month -5% to -20% hedge band. Premium matching then sets the affordable call cap to fund the selected put or put spread; it does not determine the depth of protection.[64][65][62][85]

1. Choose the full long-put collar when continuing protection through a severe fall decides the choice. [78]
2. Choose the less costly put-spread collar when protection over a defined loss band is sufficient. [85][78]
3. Treat zero-cost premium matching as a financing rule: after selecting the protection, it determines the affordable call cap rather than the depth of protection. [65][62]

The JPMorgan prospectus states that written-call premiums are intended to substantially offset, rather than necessarily equal, the put-spread cost. It also states that the put spread is typically cheaper than a standalone long put but protects only to the difference between its two put strikes.[91]

Simplify states that the put/spread collar tends to cost less than puts alone, but its protection is limited to the difference between the put strikes and is less effective against steeper declines.[93] It may use exchange-traded or OTC index puts and calls.[93]
