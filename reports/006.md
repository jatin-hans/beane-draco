## Account and grant rules

This section sets out the account rules that determine which dollars are truly available for retirement, education and emergencies. The key distinction is simple: RRSPs provide a deduction now but taxable withdrawals later; TFSAs use after-tax money but provide tax-free access; RESPs do not provide a deduction but can earn education grants. The account label also affects who ultimately pays tax and whether money can safely be used in an emergency.

### What RRSP and spousal RRSP rules are needed to understand

An RRSP is a registered retirement savings plan. You can contribute cash or eligible investments, claim a deduction within your personal deduction limit, and generally pay no tax on income earned while the money remains in the plan. When money is withdrawn or paid out, it is generally taxable income. The CRA describes an RRSP as a plan to which you or your spouse or common-law partner may contribute, with income usually exempt while it remains inside and payments generally taxable when received.[1]

The immediate tax benefit is an RRSP deduction. The amount claimed is reported on line 20800 of the income-tax return and cannot exceed the taxpayer’s RRSP deduction limit. A contribution and a deduction are related but separate decisions: you may contribute in one year, report the contribution, and defer all or part of the deduction to a later year. Undeducted contributions can be carried forward, subject to the deduction limit in the year you claim them.[1]

For a freelancer, the amount of RRSP room is not simply 18% of the current year’s invoicing or gross revenue. The CRA’s earned-income calculation includes self-employment earnings but subtracts specified expenses and business or rental losses. For the 2026 RRSP deduction-limit calculation, the CRA subtracts the 2025 pension adjustment, adds pension adjustment reversals and pension adjustment corrections, and includes unused 2025 RRSP deduction room.[1]

For 2026, the CRA’s published RRSP dollar limit is CAD 33,810. The formula uses the lesser of 18% of 2025 earned income and CAD 33,810 before the applicable adjustments. If CAD 95,000 were the client’s 2025 RRSP earned income, with no pension adjustment and ignoring carried-forward room, 18% would be CAD 17,100. That is an illustration of new room created by the formula, not the client’s actual available contribution room; their notice of assessment may show unused room, previously made unused contributions, pension adjustments or other changes.[1]

Derived — **At CAD 95,000 of prior-year RRSP earned income, 18% equals CAD 17,100 for the following year before pension adjustments and carried-forward room. This is calculated as CAD 95,000 × 0.18, using the CRA’s 18% rule.**

The CRA determines the personal RRSP deduction limit from information on the taxpayer’s prior tax returns and its own records. The limit can be checked on the latest notice of assessment or reassessment, through CRA My Account, via CRA automated phone services, or in Form T1028 where CRA issues it. A later reassessment can change the amount shown on an earlier statement.[9][1]

For a self-employed designer, this means the annual income estimate is useful for budgeting but is not permission to deposit that amount. Business profit after deductible expenses, prior unused room, any pension adjustment and prior RRSP deposits all matter. The safe working rule is to use the CRA-reported available room, then maintain a running record of contributions made after that notice.[1]

The distinction matters because a person can have room to contribute but decide not to claim the deduction immediately. The CRA specifically notes that a person expecting higher taxable income in a future year may find it more beneficial to defer some of the deduction. That can suit irregular income, but only when the cash can be committed to retirement despite the possibility of leaner months.[1][46]

RRSP room is not restored by an ordinary withdrawal. A withdrawal is included in income in the year received, tax is generally withheld at source, and the lost contribution room is generally not recreated. This makes an RRSP a poor primary cash-flow reserve for a freelancer with two dependants.[1][47][48]

The contribution deadline is also relevant to a variable-income business. For the 2026 tax year, the CRA states that March 1, 2027 is the deadline for an eligible contribution to be claimed for 2026. A taxpayer can therefore wait until after the calendar year closes to assess business income, cash on hand and tax payable before choosing how much of their available room to use.[43][1]

That timing flexibility does not mean waiting always improves the investment result. A contribution made earlier has more time invested, while a later contribution provides more certainty about taxable income and affordability. For a freelancer, the useful approach is to set a ceiling based on CRA room and make deposits after strong cash-flow periods, rather than committing to an inflexible annual lump sum before taxes and living expenses are covered. The CRA’s rules permit unused room to carry forward; a delayed contribution does not forfeit the room.[1][46]

RRSP excess contributions are expensive enough to avoid deliberately. Generally, excess contributions arise when unused contributions from prior years plus current-year contributions exceed the deduction limit by more than CAD 2,000. The amount over that threshold can attract a 1% tax per month while it remains in the RRSP, PRPP, SPP or RRIF. The CAD 2,000 amount is not additional deductible room; it is only a limited excess-contribution buffer, and the CRA says it is available only if the person was age 18 or older at some time in 2025.[1]

The CAD 2,000 buffer should therefore not be treated as an invitation to contribute CAD 2,000 above available room. It can leave money trapped as an undeducted contribution, produce a recurring monthly tax and require corrective filing or withdrawal. Before any large year-end deposit, check both the CRA-reported room and all deposits made since the date of the notice.[43][42][30]

A regular individual RRSP has the simplest ownership and tax pattern: the same person contributes, claims the deduction, owns the plan and reports withdrawals as income. The account holder can withdraw money before retirement income begins, but the withdrawal is taxable and permanently uses the room, apart from special programs such as the Home Buyers’ Plan or Lifelong Learning Plan.[49][50][1][47]

A spousal or common-law partner RRSP changes ownership but not the contributor’s current deduction. In this arrangement, one partner is the contributor and the other partner is the annuitant, meaning the account owner. The contributor receives the receipt and may claim the eligible deduction; the annuitant owns the plan and is generally the person entitled to withdraw from it.[1][51][49][52]

A spousal RRSP requires an eligible spouse or common-law partner. It is not an account that can be opened for a child, another relative, a business partner or a friend. The comparison between a personal RRSP and a spousal RRSP is therefore conditional on this client having a spouse or common-law partner and on the couple’s relative income now and in retirement.[2][13]

The spousal contribution uses the contributor’s RRSP room, not the annuitant spouse’s room. Contributions to the contributor’s own RRSP and to a spousal RRSP share one combined deduction limit. The spouse can still use their own RRSP room independently, but the contributor does not receive an extra deduction or a second contribution limit merely by directing money to a spousal plan.[1][2][53][54][55]

The following comparison separates the current tax result from the eventual ownership result. It compares account structures, not investment performance; the same investment held in either RRSP structure has the same tax-deferred treatment while inside the plan.

| Feature | Individual RRSP | Spousal RRSP |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributor and owner** | The individual is both contributor and annuitant/owner. [49][14] | One spouse or common-law partner contributes; the other is the annuitant and owner. [49][53][52] |
| **Who claims the current deduction** | The contributor/account owner claims the deduction up to their deduction limit. [1][49] | The contributor claims the deduction, not the annuitant. [1][49][53] |
| **Which RRSP room is used** | The account holder’s own RRSP room is used. [43][1] | The contributor’s own room is used; it shares the contributor’s personal-RRSP limit. [1][2][56] |
| **Tax inside the account** | Income is usually exempt while it remains in the RRSP. [1] | Income is generally tax-deferred on the same RRSP basis while it remains in the plan. [1] |
| **Who normally reports a withdrawal** | The account holder reports the withdrawal as taxable income. [49][1] | The annuitant generally reports the withdrawal, subject to the attribution rule for recent spousal contributions. [49][57][14] |

**The immediate deduction is generally identical whether the CAD 95,000 Ontario earner contributes to their own RRSP or to a spousal RRSP: both use that contributor’s single available RRSP deduction limit.**[1][2][14]

The current-year tax deduction follows the contributor, not the account. If this client contributes CAD 10,000 to their own RRSP, they can claim a deduction up to their available limit. If they instead contribute CAD 10,000 to a qualifying spouse’s spousal RRSP, they can claim the same CAD 10,000 deduction up to that same available limit. The receiving spouse’s own RRSP room is unaffected in either case.[2][53][54]

The meaningful difference is later taxation. With an individual RRSP, the client’s withdrawals add to the client’s taxable income. With a spousal RRSP, the annuitant spouse generally pays tax on withdrawals after the attribution rules no longer apply. This can reduce total household tax if the spouse who receives the withdrawals is in a lower marginal bracket than the contributor would otherwise be in at the time of withdrawal.[49][53][58]

That is the income-splitting mechanism, but it is not automatic tax savings. It matters only if retirement incomes would otherwise be uneven enough that shifting some taxable withdrawals to the annuitant lowers the couple’s total tax. The eventual result depends on both partners’ retirement income sources, pensions, CPP, OAS exposure, age difference, province of residence and planned withdrawals.[13][57][54]

A spousal RRSP can also provide more control over pre-age-65 income splitting than relying exclusively on pension-income splitting. The cited guidance notes that pension-income splitting generally requires RRIF income and age 65, while a spousal RRSP can place post-attribution withdrawals in the annuitant’s income at other ages. After age 65, up to 50% of RRIF income can be split under pension-income-splitting rules, reducing but not necessarily eliminating the value of earlier spousal RRSP planning.[59][60]

The spousal structure does not work as a quick way to transfer taxable income to a lower-income partner. The CRA’s attribution rule looks to contributions made in the withdrawal year and either of the two preceding calendar years. If the contributor made any such contributions to any of the annuitant’s spousal RRSPs, the contributor will probably have to include some or all of the withdrawal in income.[1][51]

The timing rule is based on calendar years, not a rolling 36-month count. For example, a contribution made in December 2026 can generally be withdrawn by the annuitant and taxed to the annuitant starting January 1, 2029 if there are no later spousal contributions; a contribution made in January or February 2027, even if deducted on the 2026 return, generally pushes that first clean withdrawal year to 2030.[59][14][61]

The attribution rule applies across the contributor’s spousal RRSP arrangements. Opening several spousal RRSPs at different financial institutions does not isolate one account from contributions made to another. Continued annual spousal contributions can therefore keep the attribution window active for all spousal RRSP withdrawals.[14][61]

There are specified exceptions and nuances. The cited guidance identifies exceptions for relationship breakdown while spouses are living separate and apart, the contributor’s death in the withdrawal year, certain non-residency circumstances, eligible Home Buyers’ Plan and Lifelong Learning Plan withdrawals, and minimum RRIF payments. Withdrawals above a spousal RRIF’s annual minimum can still be attributed if recent contributions trigger the rule.[56][57][14][61]

The attribution rule makes a spousal RRSP unsuitable for money that may be needed soon to smooth freelance income, fund an emergency or meet a large family expense. A personal RRSP is not ideal for those purposes either because withdrawals are taxable and room is lost, but the spousal RRSP adds uncertainty about which spouse must report the income.[1][46]

The annuitant, not the contributor, controls the spousal RRSP. The annuitant makes investment decisions and is generally the person entitled to withdraw. This has practical implications beyond tax: money placed in a spousal RRSP becomes retirement property owned by the spouse or common-law partner, not a pool the contributing freelancer can treat as their own business reserve.[51][62][56]

A spousal RRSP cannot be created by simply moving an existing personal RRSP balance to a spouse’s RRSP. CRA guidance says RRSP funds cannot be transferred to an RRSP with a different annuitant. A future contribution can be directed to a spousal RRSP, subject to the contributor’s room, but existing personal-RRSP assets normally remain with their original annuitant.[2][53]

At age 42, the age limits are distant but relevant to long-term planning. A personal RRSP must generally mature by the end of the year the annuitant turns 71; it must then be withdrawn, converted to a RRIF or used to buy an annuity. A contributor may continue contributing to a spouse’s RRSP until December 31 of the year the spouse turns 71, provided the contributor has available RRSP room.[1][2][13]

Judgment — **For this client, the spousal RRSP decision should be treated as a retirement-income allocation choice, not a current tax-saving choice. Use a spousal RRSP only if there is an eligible spouse or common-law partner who is expected to have materially lower taxable retirement income, and only with money neither partner expects to need for at least three calendar years.**

The required information for this decision is specific. The client needs to know whether they have an eligible spouse or common-law partner; that partner’s current income; each partner’s existing RRSP, pension and TFSA assets; expected CPP, OAS and pension income; relative ages; and whether either partner expects withdrawals before retirement. Without those inputs, a precise split between personal and spousal RRSPs would only be a guess.[13][57]

This subsection therefore makes two things possible. First, it allows the later comparison to treat the individual and spousal RRSP as equal for the immediate deduction at the same contributor income and contribution amount. Second, it identifies the only reason to prefer the spousal form: a credible expectation that it will reduce combined household tax on later withdrawals after the attribution period.[1][49][58]

Tax withheld by the financial institution on an RRSP withdrawal is a prepayment, not the final tax bill. The withdrawal is included in income for the year, and the final tax depends on total income reported on that year’s return.

When spousal-RRSP attribution applies, the amount attributed to the contributor is the lesser of the annuitant’s withdrawal and all contributions the contributor made to any of the annuitant’s spousal RRSPs in the withdrawal year and the two preceding calendar years.[63]

### What TFSA contribution rules and accessible savings matter for this

A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is not an RRSP and does not produce a deduction. It is funded with after-tax dollars. In exchange, investment income, capital gains and withdrawals are generally tax-free. A TFSA can hold cash, GICs, bonds, mutual funds and publicly listed securities, much like an RRSP, but the account’s tax treatment differs.[64][65][66]

For this family, the most important TFSA feature is access. A TFSA withdrawal is not taxable income and does not generally affect eligibility for federal income-tested benefits and credits. That is different from an RRSP withdrawal, which is taxable income and can affect income-tested benefits.[64][66][47]

A TFSA is therefore not merely a retirement account. It can hold the accessible part of a freelancer’s reserve while also serving long-term investing goals. The same account can do either job, but the investment held inside it must match the job: money needed quickly should be in cash or another low-volatility, readily redeemable product, while money not needed for years may be invested for longer-term growth.[67][8]

The annual TFSA dollar limit is set on January 1. The Canada Revenue Agency states that the limit was CAD 7,000 in 2024, 2025 and 2026, and that the CAD 7,000 for 2026 was added to room on January 1, 2026. This annual limit is not necessarily the client’s available room, because unused room from earlier years carries forward and previous withdrawals are added back in the following calendar year.[68][69]

TFSA room begins accumulating when a Canadian resident reaches age 18, or when Canadian residency begins for someone who becomes resident after age 18. It continues to accumulate even if the person does not open a TFSA or file a tax return, provided the residency requirements are met. For a 42-year-old who has been a Canadian resident since 2009 and has not used all room, the cumulative room may be substantial; it must still be calculated from their own contribution and withdrawal history.[68][67][64]

The room is personal and shared across all of the individual’s TFSAs. Opening two or three accounts does not create separate room limits. A contribution to any TFSA uses the same pool immediately, even though CRA My Account may not immediately display the transaction.[69][68]

| **Contribution made** | A contribution reduces available room immediately by the same amount, across all TFSAs. [69] | Track deposits yourself, especially irregular lump sums after strong client payments. |
| **Withdrawal made** | A withdrawal is generally tax-free and restores equivalent room on January 1 of the following calendar year. [69][3] | You can access cash in an emergency without taxable income, but cannot assume the money can be put back in the same year. |
| **Same-year recontribution** | It is permitted only to the extent of independently available room; otherwise it is an excess contribution. [3][64] | Rebuilding an emergency fund after a weak month may require a non-registered savings account if TFSA room is already fully used. |
| **Excess contribution** | The excess is generally taxed at 1% per month for as long as it remains in the account; the monthly amount is based on the highest excess in the month. [3][64] | A deposit and withdrawal in the same month can still trigger the monthly tax, so do not “temporarily” overcontribute. |

The withdrawal-restoration rule is particularly important for a freelancer. Suppose the emergency reserve is in a TFSA and a slow month requires a withdrawal. The withdrawal itself is tax-free, but the withdrawn amount does not become new room until the following January 1. If the client receives a large project payment later in that same year, they can replace the money only to the extent they still had unused TFSA room before the emergency withdrawal.[69][3]

This is called the **next-January rule** in this plan: a TFSA withdrawal restores room next January, not immediately. It preserves long-term contribution capacity, but only after a delay. The rule means that a fully maxed TFSA is accessible in an emergency, but it is not automatically refillable in the same calendar year.[68][67][70]

A TFSA excess contribution can arise from a simple mistake: checking CRA My Account early in the year, seeing a figure that does not yet reflect prior-year transactions, then making a deposit that exceeds actual room. CRA states that issuer information for a calendar year is reported by the end of February of the following year and CRA account data are updated in the spring. The taxpayer should reconcile CRA information against their own bank and investment records before contributing.[69][3][68][71]

CRA may notify some people of an apparent excess only after receiving information from the issuer, often in late spring. That notification is not a safe control system; the account holder is responsible for keeping records, withdrawing an excess promptly and filing a TFSA return if an excess exists.[71]

**Keep a TFSA ledger that records, for every account, opening-year room, each contribution, each withdrawal and remaining room before making an irregular deposit.**[69][64]

A useful ledger should be updated when the transaction occurs, not when CRA’s site eventually refreshes. The client should retain financial-institution confirmations and reconcile the ledger to CRA My Account after annual processing. This is particularly important if the client has transferred accounts, uses more than one provider, or makes deposits in response to unpredictable project payments.[69][3][64][72]

The client can generally withdraw TFSA funds at any time, but the investment held matters. A high-interest savings account is usually accessible. A cashable GIC may be appropriate if its specific redemption terms fit the reserve. A non-cashable GIC may restrict access, and stocks, ETFs and mutual funds may be worth less when an emergency forces a sale.[3][8][73][46]

An emergency fund should therefore not be invested as though it were retirement money. RBC’s guidance says an emergency reserve should be separate from day-to-day spending and liquid, identifying a high-interest savings account or cashable GIC as possible options. Market investments may suit long-term money but are unsuitable for cash that must be reliably available at a particular time.[8][73]

Judgment — **For this client, the first layer of the six-month reserve should be immediately accessible cash in a high-interest savings account, whether inside a TFSA or outside it. A cashable GIC can be a second layer only if its redemption terms and timeline are acceptable; equities, bond funds and non-cashable GICs do not belong in the emergency layer.**

There is a trade-off in using a TFSA for the reserve. The interest can grow tax-free, and a withdrawal will not create taxable income. But TFSA room used for low-yield emergency cash cannot simultaneously be used for higher-return long-term investing. A non-registered high-interest savings account can be simpler for a reserve that exceeds available TFSA room or must be replenished after a same-year withdrawal.[7][67][46]

This is not an argument against using the TFSA. It is an allocation choice: use available TFSA room for the reserve if the tax-free interest and tax-free access are valuable, but do not put emergency money into volatile investments simply to maximize expected return. If TFSA room is limited, preserve enough cash outside registered accounts to ensure that an emergency withdrawal does not force an RRSP withdrawal or an RESP contribution withdrawal.[67][7][8][73]

A TFSA can also complement an RRSP. The cited guidance notes that an RRSP refund may be contributed to a TFSA, and that a TFSA can provide education savings in addition to an RESP. That flexibility is valuable because a TFSA withdrawal can later be directed to a child’s education or to the client’s own retirement without a tax consequence or an educational-use requirement.[67][74]

If the client has a spouse or common-law partner, each person has separate TFSA room. A higher-income spouse can give funds to the other spouse to contribute to that spouse’s TFSA without the investment income and capital gains being attributed back to the giver, provided the receiving spouse has room and does not overcontribute. This differs from ordinary non-registered investment gifts between spouses.[67][60][64]

That spousal-TFSA option is not the same as a spousal RRSP. There is no “spousal TFSA” account type, no RRSP deduction and no three-calendar-year RRSP attribution rule. Each partner owns their own TFSA, makes contributions to their own account and must remain within their own room.[67][64]

This subsection therefore establishes that the TFSA is the principal registered account for accessible, tax-free cash. It supports an emergency reserve if the holdings are genuinely liquid, but the next-January rule and the 1%-per-month excess tax require careful contribution tracking.[69][3][8]

### What RESP and Canada Education Savings Grant rules are needed

A Registered Education Savings Plan (RESP) is a long-term education savings plan. It can help pay for post-secondary education, including university, college, CEGEP, trade school and apprenticeships. A parent can use a family RESP for more than one child if the relationship requirements are met, or separate individual plans; in either case, contribution limits and CESG history are tracked per beneficiary.[75][76][77][4]

RESP contributions are made with after-tax dollars and are not deductible. That differs from an RRSP contribution, which can reduce taxable income. The RESP’s central advantage is not a deduction; it is tax-deferred growth plus potential government incentives, especially the Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG).[76][77][78]

An RESP has no annual contribution limit. It does have a lifetime contribution limit of CAD 50,000 for each beneficiary across all RESPs. Contributions from parents, grandparents and other subscribers all count toward the same child’s CAD 50,000 limit; government grants and investment growth do not count toward it.[76][79][12]

A CAD 50,000 limit is a contribution ceiling, not a recommended target. A family can deposit more than CAD 2,500 in a year, but contributions above the amount eligible for CESG do not automatically receive a grant. The distinction is essential to the later front-loading comparison.[80][5][12]

An excess RESP contribution occurs when total contributions for a beneficiary across all plans exceed CAD 50,000 at month-end. Each subscriber is liable for a 1% monthly tax on their share of the excess until it is withdrawn. Withdrawing an overcontribution does not restore lifetime contribution room.[76][4][79]

The basic CESG is a federal grant equal to 20% of eligible RESP contributions. In an ordinary year, it applies to the first CAD 2,500 contributed for a beneficiary and is therefore worth up to CAD 500 per child for that calendar year. It is available to eligible children regardless of adjusted family net income.[76][81][82]

The Canada Education Savings Grant is deposited directly into the child’s RESP.[81][11][82]

Unused basic CESG room carries forward. If a child has unused grant room, a contributor can generally contribute up to CAD 5,000 in a year and receive up to CAD 1,000 of basic CESG: CAD 500 for the current year and up to CAD 500 from one earlier unused year. The annual catch-up rule allows only one additional year of basic grant room to be used in a calendar year, even where the child has accumulated several missed years.[77][82][35][5]

The lifetime CESG maximum is CAD 7,200 per beneficiary. This maximum includes both basic CESG and Additional CESG. Once the beneficiary reaches CAD 7,200 of CESG across all RESPs, further contributions for that child cannot earn more CESG.[76][4]

The basic CESG rule creates a **grant ceiling**. Contributing CAD 2,500 for an eligible child in a standard year earns the full ordinary CAD 500 basic grant. Contributing CAD 10,000 in that same year does not turn the ordinary basic grant into CAD 2,000. With carry-forward room, the grant can rise to CAD 1,000 on CAD 5,000, but the excess over the grant-eligible amount earns no additional basic CESG that year.[76][77][5][12]

This is why an RESP front-load should be divided into two conceptual pieces. The first piece is the amount that earns available current-year and catch-up CESG. The second piece is an additional no-grant deposit, which may still gain from earlier tax-deferred investment growth but does not receive another immediate 20% grant. The later RESP comparison will assess whether that early growth compensates for giving up future annual grant opportunities.[76][77][80]

The basic CESG is distinct from Additional CESG. Additional CESG can add 10% or 20% on the first CAD 500 contributed for a beneficiary in a year. It depends on the adjusted income of the individual primary caregiver, and the CRA’s calculation can also take into account a cohabiting spouse or common-law partner’s information. The client’s stated CAD 95,000 freelance income alone does not establish eligibility.[81][4]

Canada.ca’s supplied 2026 tables do not state a fully consistent set of Additional CESG income brackets. One 2026 chart gives less than CAD 59,523, CAD 59,523 to CAD 117,045, and more than CAD 117,045; a separate Canada.ca CESG page prints less than CAD 58,523, CAD 59,523 to CAD 114,750, and more than CAD 117,045. Both indicate a maximum yearly CESG of CAD 600, CAD 550 or CAD 500 depending on the income category and contribution amount, but the overlapping or missing threshold ranges require confirmation through the RESP promoter or CRA before relying on a specific Additional CESG estimate.[76][81][82]

Unlike unused Basic CESG, Additional CESG cannot be carried forward. If the household qualifies for Additional CESG in a year but contributes too little to receive it, that additional grant opportunity is not later recovered through a catch-up contribution. This makes annual deposits of at least CAD 500 per eligible child more valuable in any year where the family qualifies for an additional rate.[6][84]

The Canada Learning Bond (CLB) is separate from CESG. It is for eligible lower-income families and does not require a personal RESP contribution. It can provide an initial CAD 500, followed by CAD 100 for eligible years through the year the child turns 15, to a lifetime maximum of CAD 2,000 per child.[76][4][82]

For July 1, 2026 to June 30, 2027, the CLB adjusted-income threshold for families with one to three children is CAD 58,523 or less. Because this household has two children and the stated income is CAD 95,000, CLB eligibility seems unlikely if adjusted family income is near that amount, but net income, spouse information and primary-caregiver status must be checked rather than assumed.[82][4]

RESP assets receive different tax treatment depending on what is withdrawn. The subscriber’s original contributions can be returned tax-free. Investment income accumulates tax-deferred inside the plan. When a child is enrolled in qualifying post-secondary education, grants and earnings are generally paid as Educational Assistance Payments (EAPs) and are taxable to the student beneficiary. The student may pay little or no tax if their income is low.[76][77]

An EAP is not the same as a return of contribution. The grant and earnings component is taxable to the student; the parent’s own contributed principal is not taxable when returned. This distinction is why RESP proceeds can be tax-efficient for education, but it also means the account should be used for the intended educational purpose rather than as a general household savings account.[76][77][81]

If the beneficiary does not pursue post-secondary education, CESG must generally be returned to the federal government. The subscriber can withdraw their contributed principal tax-free, but accumulated income paid as an Accumulated Income Payment (AIP) is generally taxable at the subscriber’s ordinary rate plus an additional 20% for an Ontario resident. In specified circumstances and with RRSP room, up to CAD 50,000 of AIP can be contributed to the subscriber’s own or a spousal RRSP to reduce or eliminate that additional tax.[76][85][4][86]

If a child’s education plans change, CESG may have to be repaid unless it can be transferred to an eligible sibling.[118][87]

That AIP rollover is a contingency rule, not a reason to overfund an RESP. It requires available RRSP deduction room and other conditions, including the conditions governing AIP eligibility. It also does not convert ordinary RESP contributions into RRSP contributions or make RESP withdrawals generally tax-free.[85][77][4]

RESP contributions should not be counted as emergency savings. Although a subscriber can generally withdraw contributed capital tax-free, a withdrawal of assisted contributions before the child is eligible for an EAP will normally require CESG repayment, except in limited situations such as correcting an overcontribution of CAD 4,000 or less. Repayment does not restore the child’s CESG room.[4][87]

An assisted-contribution withdrawal made after March 22, 2004 can make all beneficiaries named in the RESP ineligible for Additional CESG rates for the balance of that calendar year and the next two calendar years. This anti-churning rule applies across all plans and does not affect eligibility for Basic CESG. The practical conclusion is the same: do not plan to use RESP money as a cash-flow cushion.[4][77]

RESPs can accommodate irregular contributions. Individual and family plans generally allow the subscriber to choose when and how much to contribute, subject to lifetime limits and any plan minimums. A subscriber may pause contributions. This flexibility is useful for a designer whose strong project months do not arrive evenly through the year.[77][84]

A family RESP can also offer flexibility between siblings at the education-withdrawal stage. CESG and earnings may be shared among beneficiaries in a family plan within the relevant per-beneficiary restrictions, although CLB cannot be shared in the same way. The contribution history and CESG room still remain child-specific, so each child’s grant record must be tracked independently.[4][11][12]

The older child’s actual birthday matters. The request describes the child as age 11 and expects university in about seven years, but CESG rules use calendar years, not an approximate seven-year education estimate. If the child turns 15 before sufficient RESP contributions have been made, the CAD 2,000-or-four-CAD-100-years test must be satisfied by the end of that calendar year to preserve CESG eligibility at ages 16 and 17.[4][82][35]

**For the 11-year-old, the immediate administrative priority is to confirm whether the age-16/17 test has already been met; if not, ensure at least CAD 2,000 of contributions remain in an RESP before the end of the calendar year the child turns 15.**[4][5]

For the 8-year-old, there is more time to use annual grants and catch-up room. For both children, the exact amount of available CESG depends on prior RESP contributions, grants already received, whether other relatives have contributed, and the children’s Canadian-residency and beneficiary history. A promoter can provide plan-specific records, and the subscriber should coordinate with grandparents or other contributors before a large deposit.[75][36][12]

This subsection makes the contribution-timing comparison possible. It establishes that front-loading can accelerate investment growth but cannot bypass the annual CESG ceiling; that unused Basic CESG can be recovered only one prior year at a time; and that the older child’s age-16/17 eligibility test may be more urgent than maximizing investment growth.[77][35][4]

Basic CESG requires a registered RESP naming the beneficiary, a valid beneficiary SIN, Canadian residence when the contribution is made, and a contribution made before the end of the calendar year in which the beneficiary turns 17.[4]

For each year from 2007 onward that an eligible child under age 18 is a Canadian resident, CAD 500 of unused Basic CESG room accumulates if it is not used.[76][81]

### Synthesis —

RRSPs, TFSAs and RESPs solve different problems. An individual and spousal RRSP provide the same current deduction to the contributor and use the same contributor room; the spousal version matters only because it can place later taxable withdrawals with a lower-income spouse after the attribution period.[1][2][49] A TFSA provides tax-free access but requires careful tracking because withdrawn room returns only next January and excess deposits are taxed monthly.[69][3] An RESP has no current deduction but adds tax-deferred growth and CESG opportunities; annual grant limits, carry-forward rules and age-16/17 conditions constrain the benefit of a large early deposit.[76][77][4] Read together, the rules support keeping emergency money separate and liquid, using RRSPs for retirement money that can stay invested, and directing RESP deposits first to the portions that earn available grants.

## Individual versus spousal RRSP

Judgment — The immediate tax question is simpler than the ownership question. At your income level, an individual RRSP and a spousal RRSP give the same deduction to the person who contributes; the meaningful difference is whose future income is taxed when money comes out. A spousal RRSP is therefore a retirement-income-balancing tool for an eligible couple, not a second source of RRSP room or a way to create a larger refund today.

### When could a spousal RRSP change eventual household after-tax proceeds?

A spousal RRSP can permanently improve household after-tax proceeds when the annuitant spouse has a lower marginal tax rate than the contributor in the year of withdrawal. The contributor gets the same current deduction as for an individual RRSP, but—once attribution does not apply—the annuitant’s withdrawal is taxed to that annuitant instead.[49][58][13]

Canada’s graduated tax system means that a household generally pays less tax when taxable retirement income is more evenly divided rather than concentrated in one person’s return. A spousal RRSP can direct part of the higher earner’s available RRSP room into the lower earner’s retirement account, helping balance future withdrawals; the larger the projected income gap, the greater the potential benefit.[60][62][14]

For example, the financial effect is not a special spousal-RRSP deduction but the tax rate applied at withdrawal. Manulife’s illustration compares a CAD 12,000 withdrawal taxed at 40% with the same withdrawal taxed at 20% to a lower-bracket spouse: tax payable is CAD 4,800 versus CAD 2,400, after-tax income is CAD 7,200 versus CAD 9,600, and the annual difference is CAD 2,400. That illustration shows the mechanism precisely.[59]

**A spousal RRSP is preferable for part of your RRSP saving only if you have an eligible spouse or common-law partner who is expected to have materially lower retirement taxable income than you; otherwise an individual RRSP gives the same deduction with less complexity.** Current earnings alone do not settle this: the relevant comparison includes both partners’ expected work income, CPP, employer pensions, rental income, existing RRSP and TFSA assets, and planned withdrawals.[13][62][14]

A partner who earns less today may not be the lower-taxed spouse in retirement. A defined-benefit pension, a large existing RRSP or RRIF, business income, investment income, or an age difference can reverse the expected result. One planning guideline is to seek broadly balanced retirement income, not to send every RRSP dollar to the spouse’s account and create the opposite imbalance.[57][14]

The principal limitation is the **three-calendar-year attribution rule**. If the annuitant withdraws in the year you contribute or either of the two preceding calendar years contains a spousal contribution by you, the amount attributed to you is the lesser of withdrawals and contributions to any spousal RRSP in the withdrawal year and the two preceding calendar years. CRA’s rule applies across the spouse’s RRSPs, not merely the account from which money comes out.[1][51][14][63]

CRA’s 2026 example shows the rule in action: contributions made in 2024, 2025, and 2026 to a spouse’s RRSP can cause a 2026 withdrawal to be included in the contributor’s income. Form T2205 determines the amount each spouse reports; the attributed amount is generally limited to the lesser of withdrawals and relevant contributions in the withdrawal year plus the preceding two years.[1][57]

CRA also gives a 2025 withdrawal example in which spousal contributions made in 2023, 2024 or 2025 can affect which spouse reports the withdrawal. Form T2205 calculates how a spousal RRSP, RRIF or SPP withdrawal is allocated between spouses.[51]

The timing is by calendar years, not a rolling 36-month period. A December 2026 spousal contribution can first be withdrawn and taxed to the annuitant from January 1, 2029 if there are no later relevant contributions; a January 2027 contribution, even if deducted on the 2026 return, generally delays that clean withdrawal year to 2030.[59][61]

Judgment — Do not treat a spousal RRSP as part of your emergency reserve or as money available for irregular-income gaps. It can legally be withdrawn, but the withdrawal is taxable, uses RRSP room permanently, and may be attributed back to you during the very period in which an income interruption is most likely. The Emergency Savings and Variable-Income Allocation section sets the appropriate liquid reserve.

A spousal RRSP retains value even though pension-income splitting reduces some of its later-life advantage. After age 65, eligible couples may generally elect to allocate up to 50% of qualifying RRIF income to a spouse; direct RRSP withdrawals do not qualify. A spousal RRSP can still allow the annuitant to receive taxable withdrawals before age 65, provided the attribution rule has ceased to apply.[13][14][58]

Eligible spouses can choose the percentage of eligible pension income to split each year, up to 50%, and change that percentage annually.[133]

There are important exceptions and retirement-stage details. Minimum payments from a spousal RRIF are not subject to the usual attribution rule, but amounts above the minimum can be; a spousal RRSP contribution also becomes the annuitant spouse’s property, who controls investment decisions and withdrawals.[13][59][56] Balancing income can also reduce one spouse’s exposure to OAS recovery tax, but shifting too much taxable income to the lower-income spouse can reduce that spouse’s credits or benefits, so projected household income—not only marginal brackets—must drive the split.[13][60]

Pension-income splitting lowers the transferring spouse’s net income and raises the receiving spouse’s, potentially changing each person’s age amount, spouse amount or Old Age Security recovery tax.[133]

The unresolved fact is whether you have an eligible spouse or common-law partner, and whether that person’s projected retirement income will be lower than yours after allowing for pensions, registered assets, work, and government benefits. Those facts determine the dollar value of using a spousal RRSP; the CAD 95,000 income figure does not.

What this decides: a spousal RRSP can improve the household’s eventual after-tax result only through lower-taxed future withdrawals after attribution ends, not through a larger present deduction.

## RESP contributions for both children

For RESP purposes, the two children are not one combined savings target. Each child has separate Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) room, age deadlines, lifetime limits and contribution history. The central choice is therefore not simply lump sum versus monthly deposits: it is whether to put each available dollar into the RESP early enough to earn a grant without sacrificing the cash reserve a freelance household needs.

An RESP contribution does not reduce your taxable income. Your contributions are made from after-tax income, grow tax-sheltered while inside the plan, and can later be returned tax-free; the CESG and investment earnings are normally paid as an Educational Assistance Payment (EAP) and taxed to the student when withdrawn for qualifying post-secondary education.[4][87][98] The grant is valuable, but RESP money should be treated as education-directed capital, not as the family’s emergency account.

### Does front-loading RESP contributions or spreading them evenly make more

“Front-loading” can mean two very different strategies. The first is a **full lump sum**, such as depositing CAD 35,000 or CAD 50,000 per child immediately. The second is **grant-efficient acceleration**: contribute only up to the amount that earns available current-year and one-year catch-up Basic CESG, normally CAD 5,000 per child, then repeat in later calendar years while carry-forward room remains. CAD 5,000 per child in an eligible year is catch-up acceleration, not an unrestricted upfront lump sum. With equal available cash, adequate TFSA room, comparable investment returns and sufficient grant eligibility, annual grant-eligible RESP deposits are preferable to putting the entire seven-year budget into the RESP immediately because the annual schedule captures Basic CESG in each available year.[77][4][78][74][97][107]

A full lump sum puts more money into the tax-sheltered RESP earlier, so it can have more time to earn investment returns. That timing benefit is real in principle: money invested sooner has more time in the market.[36][84] But the full lump sum normally sacrifices future CESG. A published comparison illustrates the trade-off: an annual strategy received CAD 7,200 of CESG while a CAD 50,000 first-year lump sum received CAD 500, although the lump-sum example had a higher account value under its assumed 4% annual return because all capital was invested earlier.[74] That illustration is not a forecast for either child and is not a complete household wealth comparison, because it does not account for the alternative use of the upfront capital.

A full upfront deposit also creates a liquidity problem. Contributions can generally be returned tax-free, but withdrawing assisted contributions before a beneficiary is eligible for an EAP can require CESG repayment.[4][87][11] This is especially unsuitable for a Toronto freelancer with irregular income who must first retain six months of essential expenses, as addressed in **Emergency savings and variable-income allocation**.

The Canada Education Savings Program’s anti-churning rule is specific: if assisted contributions are withdrawn before a beneficiary is eligible for an EAP, all beneficiaries under the RESP lose Additional CESG eligibility for the rest of that year and the next two calendar years, but remain eligible for Basic CESG. The rule applies across all plans for each affected beneficiary. Do not use an RESP as a cash-flow buffer, because withdrawing assisted contributions before EAP eligibility requires CESG repayment and can eliminate future Additional CESG.[108][109][99]

Judgment — **Grant-efficient acceleration is the better front-loading strategy for both children: use verified unused Basic CESG room through annual CAD 5,000 contributions, then revert to CAD 2,500 annually once catch-up room is exhausted.** It captures the 20% Basic CESG earlier than an even CAD 2,500 schedule, places both the contribution and grant into the tax-sheltered RESP sooner, and avoids wasting capital above the annual grant-eligible level. It only applies after the six-month emergency reserve is complete and only while the money is genuinely committed to education.[76][77][4][11]

The eldest should receive priority in this accelerated schedule because the grant window ends sooner and the age-16/17 contribution-history test is approaching. The youngest can still use unused Basic CESG room over more future years, while the oldest has only a limited number of calendar years before CESG stops.[76][82][11] This does not mean ignoring the younger child: it means first preserving the oldest child’s age-16/17 eligibility, then allocating the remaining annual RESP budget to whichever child has the more time-sensitive unused grant room.

A practical seven-year contribution rule is:

1. **First, verify each child’s records.** Obtain RESP statements or provider confirmation showing total contributions, CESG received, available grant room and contributions made in any other RESP. [87][5][36]
2. **Second, protect the eldest’s age-16/17 eligibility.** If necessary, deposit enough before the end of the age-15 calendar year to meet the CAD 2,000 test. [4][82]
3. **Third, capture annual Additional CESG if eligible.** Put at least CAD 500 per child into the RESP in each qualifying calendar year, because that grant cannot be recovered later. [4]
4. **Fourth, use Basic CESG catch-up efficiently.** In strong income years, contribute up to CAD 5,000 per child only where verified grant room supports it; that can generate up to CAD 1,000 of Basic CESG per child. [82][11]
5. **Fifth, once catch-up room is used or cash flow is weaker, contribute CAD 2,500 per child for the ordinary CAD 500 Basic CESG.** Make the final contribution early enough to be received by December 31 of the calendar year. [82][35]

An even CAD 2,500-per-year schedule remains a sound fallback. It captures the ordinary CESG without requiring a large upfront cash commitment, supports steady budgeting, and reduces the chance of missing a grant year. Monthly contributions can be adjusted or paused, and a year-end top-up can bring the annual amount to the target.[74][36] For example, CAD 208 to CAD 209 per month per child is approximately CAD 2,500 over a year.[74][35]

Judgment — **For irregular freelance income, do not set an inflexible monthly RESP obligation at the CAD 5,000 catch-up level. Set a low affordable automatic amount, then make top-ups after strong project months or quarterly tax-and-expense reviews.** This preserves the ability to capture grants without forcing a contribution withdrawal later.

A family RESP can be appropriate for the two siblings because it permits multiple beneficiaries, and Additional CESG may be paid into a family plan where all beneficiaries are siblings.[76][4][11] Its convenience does not pool contribution room: deposits still need to be assigned separately to the 8-year-old or 11-year-old, and each child’s CAD 50,000 lifetime limit and CESG history remain separate.[4][87][101]

The decision is therefore not full front-load versus perfectly even deposits. Putting the entire seven-year RESP budget into the account immediately gives up future annual Basic CESG opportunities when that budget would otherwise fund later grant-eligible deposits, while annual CAD 5,000 catch-up contributions use no more than the current year and one carried-forward year of Basic CESG room. Extra early RESP funding is a separate conditional choice after preserving the emergency reserve, maximizing TFSA and RRSP contributions, and preserving RESP contribution room for later grants. Returns that money could earn in a TFSA or RRSP can negate the benefit of front-loading an RESP.[77][4][78][74][97][107]

In the cited comparison, the CAD 50,000 lump-sum approach received CAD 500 of CESG versus CAD 7,200 for the annual strategy: a **CAD 6,700 gap**, meaning the lump sum received **93.1% less CESG**.[74]

A third timing method is a grant-preserving partial front-load: put extra non-grant-eligible capital into the RESP early only after preserving the emergency reserve and enough RESP contribution room for future annual grant-eligible deposits. This is distinct from both a CAD 50,000 unrestricted lump sum and a CAD 2,500-per-year schedule.[76][78][74][107]

Published long-horizon illustrations reach different account-balance results because they use different funding patterns and return assumptions. Invested MD’s 18-year illustration, using CAD 36,000 of subscriber contributions in either case and a 4% annual return compounded annually, reports CAD 73,942 for a CAD 36,000 first-year contribution with CAD 500 CESG, versus CAD 64,010 for CAD 2,000 annual contributions with CAD 7,200 CESG—a CAD 9,932 higher RESP balance for the early-funded account.[97] In contrast, a 2025 Yahoo Finance illustration treats the CAD 33,500 not placed in the RESP under a CAD 16,500 initial-contribution strategy as personally invested capital and concludes that the grant-maximizing strategy beats a CAD 50,000 upfront RESP contribution under its stated return assumptions.[107] The combined household result therefore depends on both the RESP balance and the value of money retained outside the RESP, not on the RESP balance alone.[107]

A withdrawal of original contributions before post-secondary education can require CESG repayment equal to 20% of the amount withdrawn where the plan has received CESG.[24]

As the eldest approaches post-secondary education, progressively reduce the RESP’s exposure to large market declines so that money needed for near-term withdrawals is not dependent on stock-market performance.

The RESP and TFSA both use after-tax contributions and shelter investment income while invested. On withdrawal, RESP growth is taxed in the beneficiary’s hands, whereas TFSA growth is never taxed.[39]

After contributing CAD 2,500 per child per year to receive the ordinary CESG, direct excess cash to available TFSA and RRSP room until those accounts are filled. Further RESP contributions can then be considered while the child remains below the CAD 50,000 lifetime RESP contribution maximum.[39]

### Synthesis

**The winning RESP approach is staged acceleration, not a one-time maximum deposit.** For each child, contribute CAD 5,000 in a calendar year only when verified unused Basic CESG room exists; this can earn up to CAD 1,000 of Basic CESG, versus CAD 500 on a CAD 2,500 even annual contribution. A CAD 35,000 or CAD 50,000 lump sum cannot claim future CESG years at once, even though RESP contributions have no annual limit.[4][82][11][74]

Put the eldest first: confirm the age-16/17 eligibility test before the end of the year they turn 15, then use available catch-up room while grant years remain. Preserve annual CAD 500-per-child contributions if cash flow is constrained, and contribute at least CAD 500 per child in any year of Additional CESG eligibility because that component cannot be carried forward. Keep RESP deposits outside the six-month emergency reserve because an early withdrawal can trigger CESG repayment.[4][87]

For the requested seven-year period, the grant target must be set separately for each child after confirming the exact calendar years remaining before the child turns 18, each child’s CESG received to date, unused Basic CESG room, and RESP contributions made by all contributors.[105][106]

## Emergency savings and variable-income allocation

### How should six months of emergency fund liquidity be maintained?

Irregular freelance income makes liquidity part of the investment plan, not an afterthought. The first job of the emergency reserve is to keep the household running through a client-payment delay, a gap between projects, or a sudden necessary expense without cashing out retirement or education savings.

Judgment — **Set a six-month liquidity floor equal to six times essential monthly household expenses, not six months of CAD 95,000 gross income.** Let monthly essential expenses be **E**; the target is **6 × E**. Build E from housing, utilities, groceries and household basics, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments, necessary childcare or school costs, and uninsured medical costs. Review recent bank and credit-card statements to capture variable costs and irregular essentials rather than guessing from an average month.[7][8][73]

### Synthesis

**The recommended allocation is feasible with one condition: do not invest money needed to maintain the six-month emergency floor.** Use an expense-based target, separate it from planned-cost sinking funds and business cash, and hold it in a liquid low-risk account. Do not treat RESP assets as emergency money because a pre-enrolment withdrawal can trigger CESG repayment, and do not rely on RRSP assets because withdrawals are taxable and erode retirement capacity.[8][4][48]

For variable income, the reserve-first waterfall is stronger than equal monthly commitments: weak months fund essentials and liquidity; strong months restore the reserve and fund RESP targets, then compare the current RRSP deduction rate with the expected withdrawal rate and the need for flexible cash before allocating the remaining surplus between TFSA and RRSP. Use the RRSP when the deduction rate is higher than the expected withdrawal rate, and use the TFSA when rates are the same or flexibility is needed. A six-month reserve meets the requested floor, while the self-employed and irregular-income guidance supports considering an eventual nine-month-or-more buffer.[112][101][70][95][8][7]

Judgment — No unconditional 2032 winner can be named: the RRSP-versus-TFSA result depends on the tax rate at deduction relative to the eventual withdrawal rate, and the RESP front-load result depends on returns and on the use of money retained outside the RESP. CIBC’s January 2026 report says RRIF withdrawals after age 65 can be split with a spouse or partner, so projected retirement income sources and the availability of RRIF pension splitting must be considered before treating a spousal RRSP as necessary for future income splitting.[37]

### Synthesis

The winning plan in the illustration is annual grant-eligible RESP funding plus a spousal RRSP where an eligible lower-tax spouse or common-law partner exists; otherwise it is annual grant-eligible RESP funding plus an individual RRSP. This is not the strategy with the largest RESP balance; it produces the highest stated total after retaining the TFSA balance and reflecting eventual RRSP withdrawal tax.[77][4][1][49][59] TFSA withdrawals are tax-free, while RRSP withdrawals are fully taxable.[67][70] All activity from 2033 onward remains outside the December 31, 2032 totals, even though the separate education-funding schedule continues for approximately seven elapsed years.[101][82][11]

In that separate CESG-first model, projected after-tax household wealth was CAD 179,700 by December 31, 2032, while maintaining a separate CAD 34,200 liquid emergency reserve.[128][127][11][82][140][141]

1. **First:** calculate the December 31, 2032 account-value projection separately from the seven-year education schedule.
2. **Second:** verify each child’s CESG history and age-16/17 eligibility before setting RESP deposits.
3. **Third:** use the hybrid RESP approach rather than a full superfund unless TFSA room, RRSP room and the six-month reserve are already fully funded.
4. **Fourth:** compare RRSP and TFSA using your expected retirement withdrawal tax rate, and use a spousal RRSP only if the household’s future income split supports it.

**Implementation checklist**

1. **Emergency-reserve risk — record and decision:** Record monthly essential spending, including housing, utilities, communications, insurance, transportation, childcare or education, groceries, uncovered medical costs, and irregular costs such as camps, holidays and tax bills; record current liquid balances; then set the reserve target at six times average monthly essential expenses and keep that amount accessible before making education or retirement deposits. [8]
2. **Contribution-room risk — record and decision:** Record the 2026 RRSP deduction limit from the latest CRA notice of assessment, notice of reassessment or Form T1028, and reconcile TFSA room in CRA My Account with financial-institution records; then limit RRSP and TFSA deposits to verified available room. [1][3]
3. **RESP grant and over-contribution risk — record and decision:** For each child, record the birth date, total contributions across all RESPs, CESG received and remaining CESG room; then choose the contribution schedule that preserves age-16-and-17 CESG eligibility and does not exceed the $50,000 lifetime contribution limit per beneficiary. [4][5][36]
4. **Spousal-RRSP attribution and income-splitting risk — record and decision:** Record whether there is an eligible spouse or common-law partner, each partner’s expected retirement income and planned withdrawal dates, and all spousal-RRSP contributions; then use a spousal RRSP only where future withdrawals support income splitting and no withdrawal is planned within the three-year attribution look-back period. [14]

5. **Education-use and withdrawal-tax risk — record and decision:** Record expected years of study for each child, existing education savings, future saving ability, an investment return suited to risk comfort, expected student income, and expected eligible credits or deductions; then set the front-loaded amount only to education funding the family expects to use and plan EAP withdrawals while student income is low. [125][87][126][100][120][38]

6. **Household retirement-tax risk — record and decision:** Record both partners’ current and projected earned income, retirement ages, pension eligibility and expected pension income, RRSP/RRIF balances, and liquidity needs; then compare year-by-year household tax payable, marginal tax rates, OAS recovery-tax exposure, and total after-tax retirement income before selecting an individual or spousal RRSP. [29]

### Open questions

The open dollar decisions are the six-month emergency-reserve amount, the monthly TFSA, RRSP and RESP allocation, the value of front-loading versus even RESP deposits for each child, and the personal-versus-spousal RRSP split. These are settled by actual essential expenses and liquid savings, CRA TFSA and RRSP room, each child’s RESP contributions and CESG history, and an eligible spouse or partner’s current and expected retirement income, pension assets and planned withdrawal timing.
