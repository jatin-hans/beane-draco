# Comparative Analysis of Grounded Theory’s Methodological Divergence

## Executive summary

The defensible current verdict is that constructivist grounded theory, especially Charmaz’s reflexive and interpretive position, is the dominant cited orientation in major qualitative-methodology journals in 2020–2024, but journal citations do not directly establish what most training programmes teach.

Glaser’s 1978 classic grounded theory gives priority to emergence, delays substantive-area framing, rejects forcing, and treats checking as continuous assessment of fit, relevance, workability, saturation, integration, and modifiability. Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 version makes analytic procedures, a coding paradigm, and explicit validation more prominent, thereby risking the framework-led forcing that Glaser condemned while not necessarily requiring it when used heuristically.

Charmaz’s 2006 constructivist reformulation retains close comparative coding and rejects prefabricated explanations, but replaces discovery of an external theory with a reflexive account co-constructed by researcher, participants, data, and interpretive context.

The 1967 formulation already contained the tension that later divided them: it opposed preconception and hypothesis-testing primacy while also requiring theoretical sensitivity and systematic comparison.

## Grounded theory: the 1967 formulation and later approaches

Grounded theory is a family of approaches for building an explanatory account from systematic engagement with empirical material. Its central dispute is not whether researchers should code data, but how they should use concepts while coding: should categories emerge from comparison with data, may a prior framework guide the analysis, and what counts as checking a developing theory? The answers given by Glaser and Strauss in 1967 were internally tense; Glaser, Strauss and Corbin, and Charmaz each made a different part of that tension central.

### What did Glaser and Strauss’s original formulation (1967) say about

Glaser and Strauss presented grounded theory as “the discovery of theory from data.” Their target was the dominant hypothetico-deductive model, in which researchers begin with a theory, derive hypotheses from it, and collect data to test them. In its place, they proposed an inductive, iterative, and systematic process in which data collection, coding, comparison, and conceptual development proceed together. The objective was not simply rich description. It was a substantive or formal theory: an abstract explanation of patterns in a particular empirical area or across several areas.[1][2][3]

Glaser and Strauss presented the constant comparative method as applicable to social units ranging from persons and roles to nations.[4][10][96]

A **category** in grounded theory is a conceptual label that captures a recurrent pattern in data, rather than a descriptive label attached to one isolated statement. A **code** is the analytic name given to an incident, action, process, or meaning in data; codes may develop into categories as repeated comparison identifies their properties, variation, conditions, and consequences. The 1967 approach therefore joined coding to theory development. Glaser and Strauss wrote that the constant comparative method combined explicit coding with a flexible style of theory generation, rather than using coding only to produce evidence for provisional hypothesis testing.[4][5]

**The baseline position was disciplined openness: theory should fit the data, but researchers could not work without concepts.** This is the premise from which the later disagreement over frameworks, forcing, and verification followed.[1][6]

The original method arose from Glaser and Strauss’s hospital research on dying and the social organization of care. The 1967 book was written partly to explain how the authors had developed their analyses, after readers showed strong interest in the empirical study. It challenged a methodological environment in which qualitative work was often treated as preliminary to quantitative research and in which theory testing appeared more prestigious than theory generation.[6][7][8][9]

#### Pre-existing theoretical frameworks in coding

A **pre-existing theoretical framework** is a set of concepts and propositions adopted before analysis that directs what researchers expect to find and how they interpret observations. Glaser and Strauss warned strongly against beginning with such a framework when it dictated in advance which concepts, hypotheses, or “relevancies” mattered. Their initial decisions for theoretical data collection were not to be based on a preconceived theoretical framework, because the relevant concepts should be discovered through the research process.[10][6]

Their well-known injunction was to ignore, initially, theoretical and factual literature in the specific substantive area being studied. The purpose was to prevent extant accounts from contaminating the emergence of categories. They feared that prior immersion in the literature would sway researchers toward what established theories said was important, rather than toward what was happening in the field.[6][8]

This was not, however, an instruction to become theoretically empty. Glaser and Strauss explicitly rejected the image of the researcher as a blank slate. Glaser and Strauss (1967) wrote that the researcher does not approach reality as a *tabula rasa* but needs a perspective to see relevant data and abstract significant categories. They defined **theoretical sensitivity** as “theoretical insight into [one’s] area of research” combined with an ability to make use of those insights.[6][11][12]

The 1967 formulation thus allowed a researcher to bring an “armamentarium” of substantive and formal categories and hypotheses to the inquiry. Existing concepts could be used if they proved useful, but exclusive commitment to one prior theory—especially a “pet theory” or grand theory—was held to reduce sensitivity because it prevents the analyst from seeing beyond that theory’s terms. A grounded theory would normally combine mostly data-emergent concepts with some existing concepts that earned their place through clear usefulness and fit.[6][10]

This is the founding ambiguity. On one side, Glaser and Strauss (1967) instructed researchers to set aside theory and factual literature in the study area so categories would not be contaminated. On the other, they named theoretical sensitivity as the ability to “see relevant data,” allowed a grounded theory to combine data-emergent concepts and hypotheses with “some existing ones that are clearly useful,” and acknowledged that theoretical insight can derive from one’s own or another person’s theory.[6][11]

The tension can be stated plainly. The founders rejected starting with one framework that controls interpretation; they did not reject all prior knowledge. Their proposed safeguard was breadth and provisionality: engage with a wide range of ideas, including ideas outside the immediate substantive field, but do not let one framework decide in advance what the data must mean.[6]

#### Forcing data into categories

Forcing means making data fit a category, hypothesis, or explanatory model selected in advance, rather than revising the category in response to what comparisons reveal. The 1967 book rejected forcing directly. Glaser and Strauss contrasted an approach in which data must fit theory with their own position that theory should fit data.[1][2][13]

Their alternative was **constant comparison**: repeatedly comparing incidents with other incidents, then comparing incidents with developing categories, so that categories acquire properties and dimensions from variation in the data. The analyst began by coding each incident in as many ways as seemed possible rather than applying a complete coding scheme prepared before fieldwork. This generated provisional categories, which were then repeatedly altered, elaborated, combined, or abandoned through further comparisons.[6]

Their account of constant comparison also specified integrating categories, delimiting the emerging theory, and writing the theoretical account.[4][10][96]

The original formulation treated **theoretical sampling** as part of this safeguard. Theoretical sampling means deciding what data to collect next, and where to find them, according to the needs of the developing analysis. Researchers did not fix all comparison groups in advance because emerging categories determined what variation was analytically useful.[6][14]

In the 1967 formulation, emerging theory directed later theoretical sampling rather than statistical representativeness.[4][10][64]

The procedure did not mean that every apparent contradiction invalidated a developing explanation. A negative or deviant case became another datum to analyze. It could generate a new property of a category, require a qualification to an assertion, or become a variable that the theory had to account for. The method therefore treated discrepancy as a source of theoretical refinement rather than as a nuisance to discard.[4][6]

The original position also did not equate categories with raw participant language. Coding moved from empirical incidents to conceptual categories. Glaser and Strauss sought theories that would be sufficiently abstract to explain processes, but still grounded enough to fit the substantive area and be useful to those involved in it. Their concepts were intended to bridge sociological theorizing and the practical understanding of people in the field.[6]

The original approach was therefore not a license for analysts to claim that any category “emerged” merely because it seemed plausible. #### Verification procedures

Verification means checking whether an explanation is supported, challenged, or modified by evidence. In conventional hypothetico-deductive research, verification ordinarily concerns data collected to test hypotheses specified before the test. Glaser and Strauss objected to making that form of verification the dominant purpose of social research. Their distinctive goal was discovering concepts and hypotheses relevant to an empirical area before treating them as objects of testing.[6][11][14]

They did not deny that comparisons can verify facts or theories. They wrote that comparative analysis “subsumes and assumes verification and accurate description,” but only insofar as these serve theory generation. The priority was not to demonstrate that an established theory was correct. It was to generate a theory capable of fitting and explaining the field being studied.[23][6][10]

Glaser and Strauss held that qualitative and quantitative materials could both generate theory and mutually verify one another, while keeping verification subordinate to theory generation.[4][10]

Glaser and Strauss’s 1967 account allowed qualitative and quantitative materials to complement one another in theory generation and mutual verification; comparative analysis could range across social units from persons and roles to nations.[4][10]

Their preferred form of checking was internal to the analytic process. The developing theory was continuously refined and checked against data as the analyst compared incidents, selected new comparison groups, and sampled further cases theoretically. Theoretical sampling was exploratory: it sought settings and cases that could illuminate properties and relationships in emergent categories.[6]

Glaser and Strauss (1967) articulated four credibility criteria: sufficient detail and description for readers to feel “vicariously in the field”; sufficient information about data gathering and analysis for readers to assess how findings were reached; multiple comparison groups; and specification of the kinds of data supporting the interpretation. They also gave four applicability criteria: a theory must closely fit its substantive area; be understandable to laypeople concerned with that area as well as professionals; be general enough for diverse daily situations and populations within it; and allow users partial control over changing situations.[17]

The founders questioned whether quantitative standards of rigor were appropriate for flexible theory-generating research. They proposed judging credibility partly from the actual strategies of collecting, coding, analyzing, and presenting data, and partly from the way people read and use the resulting theory. This was an argument for qualitative standards, not an argument against scrutiny.[4][8][20]

Yet the 1967 text contains a genuine unresolved issue. Glaser and Strauss occasionally implied that a theory generated in close interaction with data may be sufficiently plausible and useful without further validation. That proposition differs from an independent test. If the data that generated a theory are also used as the sole evidence for validating it, they cannot provide the independent evidence required for a confirmatory test of pre-specified claims.[6]

The founding position therefore treated verification as legitimate but subordinate: comparison and further sampling checked and revised a developing theory, whereas independent testing of a specified theory was a distinct later task.[6][23]

This distinction also explains why theoretical saturation is not identical to verification. **Theoretical saturation** is reached when continued comparison yields no new properties or dimensions of a category. It tells the analyst that categories have become sufficiently developed for the current theoretical purpose. It does not prove the theory true in all settings or close the possibility of revision when new data are encountered.[6]

#### The founding conditions of the later split

The founders’ later disagreement did not arise because one author favored data and the other favored theory. Both accepted constant comparison, simultaneous analysis and data collection, theoretical sampling, coding, and theory generation. After Glaser and Strauss ended their methodological collaboration in the late 1970s, Glaser developed theoretical coding and coding families, while Strauss and Corbin developed the coding paradigm. The disagreement arose because the original formulation left underspecified how analysts could use prior concepts without treating them as a governing frame.[6][24][25]

The 1967 formulation contained two claims that pull in different directions. First, categories should emerge if researchers avoid prior theoretical commitments. Second, researchers need theoretical sensitivity and cannot approach social reality without concepts. The later versions of grounded theory can be read as different institutionalizations of these two claims.[6][11]

Glaser developed the emergence side. Strauss, and later Strauss with Corbin, developed the theoretical-sensitivity and action-analysis side. Both retained the core requirement that the developing explanation remain responsive to empirical observation, but they disagreed about whether an explicit analytic framework helps that responsiveness or threatens it.[6][12]

The subsequent methods cannot simply be treated as interchangeable variants of a single procedure. Classic, Straussian, and constructivist grounded theory share a common origin and several techniques, but differ in coding conventions, philosophical commitments, and the use of literature.[26][27] (Kenny and Fourie, 2015)[27]

The baseline therefore decides the terms of the comparison that follows: later approaches can be judged as grounded theory only insofar as they preserve theory generation through sustained empirical comparison, while their central disagreement concerns the proper role of theoretical concepts in that work.[1][6][25]

Charmaz (2006) describes the founders’ methodological objective in the same terms: their 1967 book advocated developing theories from research grounded in data rather than deducing testable hypotheses from existing theories. She also describes grounded-theory guidelines as systematic but flexible general principles and heuristic devices, rather than formulaic rules.[28]

The founding method also included theoretical memo writing: researchers recorded analytic ideas, questions, comparisons, and possible relationships among categories as these developed. Memos linked ongoing coding and theoretical sampling to the eventual explanatory account rather than treating writing as a task that begins only after analysis ends.

Strauss and Corbin’s own 1990 definition states that a grounded theory is inductively derived, discovered, developed, and “provisionally verified through systematic data collection and analysis of data pertaining to that phenomenon.” Their stated purpose was to build theory faithful to and illuminating the area under study.[21]

### How did Glaser’s Theoretical Sensitivity (1978) treat pre-existing theoretical frameworks

Glaser’s *Theoretical Sensitivity: Advances in the Methodology of Grounded Theory* (1978) was the first book to clarify and further explain grounded-theory methods. It was Glaser’s major independent elaboration after the shared formulation and retained the language of discovery, emergence, and theory generation from data.[29][9][6]

In *Theoretical Sensitivity* (1978), Glaser described **theoretical sensitivity** as understanding theories and how they are constructed without imposing their concepts on the theory emerging from the data. It is the analyst’s capacity to recognize theoretically significant patterns while staying open to what the data reveal, not an instruction to apply a favorite theory to the data.[3][6][11]

#### Pre-existing theoretical frameworks in Glaser’s coding

Glaser retained the 1967 warning against beginning a grounded theory study with a framework that predefines the categories to be found. He advised researchers to enter the setting with as few predetermined ideas as possible, particularly hypotheses logically deduced in advance. This posture was intended to prevent analysts from filtering observed events through prior hypotheses and biases.[6][25][30]

His guidance on literature follows from the same concern. Glaser advised postponing a focused review of literature in the substantive area until the emerging theory was sufficiently grounded and developed. At that later point, researchers could integrate the literature with the theory, rather than use it as a template for initial coding.[6][30][31]

This is often misrepresented as a demand that researchers never read. Glaser’s position was narrower: a focused substantive-area review should not organize the early conceptualization of the study. Existing theory and literature could become data for comparison later, and wider reading could increase a researcher’s knowledge of possible theoretical codes.[6][23][31]

Glaser made this balance operational through a distinction between **substantive coding** and **theoretical coding**. Substantive coding develops concepts that relate directly to the empirical research area during open coding. Theoretical coding then conceptualizes possible relationships among those substantive codes, allowing them to be integrated into hypotheses and a more general theory.[6][11][30]

This distinction matters because Glaser was not arguing that theory only appears in the final written account. Researchers have conceptual resources available throughout the inquiry. The difference is temporal and evidential: empirical substantive categories are developed first through open comparison; theoretical codes may then help explain how those categories relate, but they must fit the emerging analysis.[23][6]

Glaser supplied **coding families** as broad repertoires of theoretical relations. These included the Six Cs—causes, contexts, contingencies, consequences, covariances, and conditions—as well as process, degree, type, strategy, interaction, identity/self, cutting-point, cultural, and consensus families. These were not meant to be a single mandatory grid for every study; they were potential ways to think about relations among categories.[6][32][3]

The Glaserian distinction can be expressed through sequence:

1. **First, open coding:** code incidents line by line and in as many ways as possible, without selectively sorting them into a predetermined framework. [30][6]

2. **Second, emergence of a core category:** compare incidents and categories until a central, recurrent category begins to account for the participants’ main concern or a major pattern of action. Selective coding begins only after that core category has been identified. [30][25]

3. **Third, theoretical coding:** use broad conceptual resources to relate substantive categories as hypotheses that form an integrated theory. The theoretical code must display emergent fit; it cannot be installed because it is intellectually attractive or professionally fashionable. [11][6][23]

4. **Fourth, theoretical sampling, memoing, and sorting:** seek further data to develop the emerging categories, write conceptual memos, and sort those memos to integrate the theory. [6]

The sequence rules out a framework-first analysis, but it does not rule out theory-informed analysis. Glaser described coding as combining the analyst’s scholarly knowledge and research knowledge of the substantive field in the ongoing coding process. His approach thus preserved the original tension but tried to discipline it through the order of analytic operations.[6][11]

#### Glaser’s account of forcing

For Glaser, forcing was the primary methodological danger. It occurred when a researcher began with a received concept and tried to make incidents conform to that concept’s established meaning. A category had to earn its relevance by repeated comparison, not be accepted because it came from influential literature, professional common sense, or a previous study.[33]

His example of prostitution illustrates the point. If a researcher begins by treating prostitution as deviance, then “deviance” controls what the researcher notices. Yet the participants’ principal concern might instead concern effective client service, safety, income, organizational practices, or some other process. Deviance should enter the theory only if comparison shows that it has explanatory relevance.[6]

Glaser applied the same warning to formal theory. A formal theory should not be extended to a new substantive area merely because it sounds relevant. The analyst must establish relevance through comparative evidence and avoid **logical elaboration**—building attractive theoretical extensions that go beyond the data that substantiate them.[6]

The practical implication is that theoretical resources are legitimate only as possible analytic tools, not as directives. A coding family can prompt questions about conditions, processes, or consequences. It cannot establish in advance that a given incident is a condition, a consequence, a strategic action, or a contextual factor.[6][11]

Glaser’s approach has an internal tension here. Theoretical codes are concepts already available to the analyst independently of the present data collection. Thus the rhetoric that all codes emerge directly from data is too simple. The more precise Glaserian position is that substantive codes should emerge from open coding, while theoretical codes may be brought to the analysis if their relevance emerges through their fit with the substantive categories.[11][6]

**The decisive Glaserian boundary is not whether the researcher has concepts, but whether a concept earns emergent fit through constant comparison rather than determining the analysis in advance.**[6][23]

This also identifies the trade-off in Glaser’s method. Coding families offer a broad set of possible integrative ideas and can prevent analysts from becoming trapped inside one disciplinary lens. But the range of possible coding families can be demanding for novice researchers, because they must decide which conceptual relation is appropriate without a single prescribed template.[6][11][3]

#### Glaser’s developmental account of verification

Glaser did not treat verification as a separate, final stage in which a completed theory is formally tested against an independent dataset. His approach embedded checking in the analytic work of generating the theory. Constant comparison asks repeatedly whether incidents support a category, whether the category’s properties are sufficiently developed, whether counterinstances require revision, and whether categories fit together as an integrated explanation.[6]

Glaser described deduction and verification as “servants of emergence”: they check, correct, and refit developing categories against old and new incidents rather than govern theory generation.[97]

In Glaser’s account, **open coding carries verification, correction, and saturation** because line-by-line analysis makes the analyst return repeatedly to the data rather than relying on a first impression. The proposed category is elaborated only as further incidents display a pattern, and continued comparison tests whether apparent regularities recur.[34][33][6]

Glaser warned that easy, persistent confirmation could itself signal that an analyst was forcing the data.[97]

This is not verification in the sense of proof. Glaser’s completed grounded theory was assessed through fit, work, relevance, and modifiability. **Fit** asks whether categories fit the substantive data; **workability** asks whether the theory explains the relevant behavior or process; **relevance** asks whether it addresses a genuine concern in the substantive field; and **modifiability** requires that it can be changed when new data warrant change.[35][8][7]

Memos are central to this developmental verification. Glaser called theoretical memo writing the core stage of grounded theory. Memos slow the analyst down, make emerging relationships explicit, and require the researcher to reason through whether categories, their relationships, and their integration have fit, relevance, and workability.[6][30]

Theoretical sorting performs a related function. Sorting memos is conceptual rather than descriptive data sorting: it helps integrate categories into a coherent account. Without it, Glaser held, the resulting theory remains linear and thin rather than fully integrated.[6]

Theoretically sampled data continue this checking process. The analyst seeks cases, groups, settings, or events that can elaborate a category, reveal a dimension of variation, or challenge the emerging account. The question is not “How many participants represent the population?” but “What data are needed next to develop this concept or relationship?”[6][17]

Glaser’s approach therefore treats category saturation as one condition for confidence in the theory. A core category is not accepted simply because it looks central after a few interviews. It requires sustained coding, saturation, relevance, and workability.[30][6][33]

Glaser also identified parsimony and scope, alongside saturation and modifiability, as marks of a workable theory rather than demanding an elaborate final proof procedure.[97]

This position remains distinct from independent confirmatory testing. Glaser’s quality criteria assess whether the emerging theory is grounded and useful in the substantive area. Independent testing would require specifying propositions before gathering new evidence designed to refute or support them. The 1967 distinction between discovery and testing remains decisive in Glaser’s 1978 development.[8][6][36]

Glaser’s 1978 verification model is iterative scrutiny of an emerging conceptual account, not a procedure for proving a pre-specified proposition.[6][8]

#### What Glaser changed, and what he retained

Glaser retained the original emphasis on discovery, direct engagement with data, emergence, conceptual abstraction, and resistance to framework-driven coding. He strengthened the 1967 formulation by providing more explicit coding language: substantive versus theoretical codes, coding families, core categories, theoretical memos, sorting, and a more systematic account of saturation and integration.[6]

He did not resolve the original ambiguity by eliminating theory from the analysis. Rather, he converted it into a rule of analytic discipline. Prior theoretical knowledge belongs in the researcher’s sensitivity and may later help integrate the theory, but it must not control open coding or determine what counts as relevant before patterns have been established comparatively.[3][6][11][23]

The result is a method with a strong anti-forcing ethic and a relatively weakly specified account of how analysts choose among multiple theoretical codes. That is a strength when the main risk is premature closure around a fashionable theory; it is a cost when inexperienced researchers need explicit support for relating categories into an explanatory structure.[11][3]

Strauss and Corbin defined open coding as “breaking down, examining, comparing, conceptualizing, and categorizing data” in terms of properties and dimensions. They then defined axial coding as making connections between a category and its subcategories developed in open coding. Thus their structured relational analysis was intended to follow, rather than substitute for, initial category development.[19]

Charmaz’s initial coding examines actions in each data segment rather than applying pre-existing categories. Focused coding then uses the most significant or frequent initial codes to analyze larger amounts of data; the researcher decides which data cohere into a category, guided by theoretical sensitivity.[37]

The later 1990 Strauss–Corbin account confirms the baseline contrast: investigators may enter the field with questions or areas for observation, but analysis can show these questions to be irrelevant. Thus an initial focus is permitted, whereas a framework that settles relevance in advance is not.[38]

Glaser’s resistance to a governing framework is consistent with the later characterization that he rejected assigning grounded theory to a specific philosophical setting because such a setting would narrow the method’s potential.[39]

In *Theoretical Sensitivity* (1978), Glaser stated that line-by-line coding verifies and saturates categories, grounds them in data, and corrects preferred themes unless those themes achieve emergent fit.[30]
Glaser treated the fit and relevance of concepts and hypotheses as verification within grounded theory rather than proof.[6]
Glaser maintained that constant comparison incorporates verification within the primary inductive work of generating theoretical ideas rather than as an external frame imposed on analysis.[23]

Glaser’s stated quality criteria were fit and relevance, workability, and modifiability: categories must derive from and relate to data through constant comparison, integrate into the emergent core category, and remain alterable when new relevant data are compared with existing data.[40]

Glaser’s 1978 book is *Theoretical Sensitivity: Advances in the Methodology of Grounded Theory*, published by Sociology Press. It set out a concept–indicator model of theorizing, line-by-line coding, basic social processes, and loosely related theoretical codes.[41][42]

Glaser’s 1978 book set out a concept–indicator model of theorizing, line-by-line coding, basic social processes, and loosely related theoretical codes.[41][42]

### How did Strauss and Corbin’s Basics of Qualitative Research (1990)

Strauss and Juliet Corbin’s *Basics of Qualitative Research: Grounded Theory Procedures and Techniques* made grounded theory more explicit, more teachable, and more procedural. The book became influential partly because it appeared to offer a practical manual: recognizable ingredients, defined stages, and clear instructions for conducting analysis. It made grounded theory accessible to graduate students and newcomers, although critics argued that this procedural clarity could become a cookbook approach that limited theory generation.[6][29]

SAGE published Strauss and Corbin’s *Basics of Qualitative Research: Grounded Theory Procedures and Techniques* in 1990.[98][99]

The 1990 book retained core grounded theory commitments. It described concepts as derived from data collected during the research process rather than chosen before research begins; it linked analysis and data collection in an ongoing cycle; and it retained constant comparison as a standard analytic practice.[17][43]

The divergence lay in how Strauss and Corbin handled the researcher’s prior knowledge, how explicitly they structured relational analysis, and how visibly they incorporated checking and evaluative criteria.

#### Pre-existing theoretical frameworks in Strauss and Corbin’s coding

Strauss and Corbin rejected the fantasy that a researcher enters inquiry without any conceptual resources. They acknowledged that researchers bring life experience, professional knowledge, and disciplinary literature to their investigations. Strauss and Corbin (1990) acknowledged that researchers bring life experience and professional and disciplinary-literature knowledge to inquiry. Their account treats concepts placed on data as abstractions of what researchers understand the data to mean; categories therefore carry something of both researcher and participant. Strauss (1987) held that delaying scrutiny of related literature applied fully to inexperienced researchers but less strongly to experienced researchers.[6][17]

Strauss and Corbin allowed open codes to use participants’ own words or to be developed with reference to technical literature; personal and professional experience could also inform the questions researchers asked of data.[12]

This differs from the strongest reading of Glaserian emergence. Glaser stressed entering the field with as few predetermined ideas as possible and delaying engagement with substantive literature. Strauss and Corbin made the researcher’s knowledge more explicit as a resource for interpretation, while retaining a requirement that concepts be examined against the data.[6][17]

Their central device was the **coding paradigm**. In Straussian grounded theory, a coding paradigm is a structured set of questions for relating a category to its causal conditions, context, intervening conditions, action or interaction strategies, and consequences. It is rooted in pragmatist and symbolic-interactionist attention to action, interaction, process, and the conditions in which people act.[3][6][24]

**Axial coding** is the stage in which analysts relate categories to subcategories and investigate their conditions, actions or interactions, and consequences. Charmaz (2006) identifies it as a coding type Strauss introduced in 1987 for relating categories to subcategories. It follows open coding rather than replacing it: the intended sequence is to identify and develop categories, then examine how the conditions and consequences of action connect them. The chapter author judges that axial coding adds a needless level of complexity for novices and narrows the options for theorizing.[6][12][23]

Strauss and Corbin’s coding paradigm was partly derived from conceptual resources that Glaser himself had presented in coding families, especially the Six Cs and strategy family. The later disagreement was not therefore simply between a theory-free Glaser and a theory-using Strauss. It concerned whether an explicit, specified analytic framework should guide relational coding, or whether analysts should select theoretical codes more freely and only when they emerge as fitting.[3][6][12]

Strauss and Corbin also allowed borrowed concepts to be used provisionally. A borrowed concept had to fit the study area, be sufficiently general, be clearly defined in terms of properties and dimensions, and be checked against the present data. Their position thus did not authorize importing concepts as settled answers. It treated extant concepts as tentative analytic resources subject to revision or rejection.[17]

The contrast with Glaser can be stated narrowly. Glaser’s theoretical coding gives researchers a broad repertoire of possible conceptual relations and requires that the fit of any relation emerge. Strauss and Corbin’s coding paradigm supplies a more specific action-oriented framework that tells researchers what kinds of relationships to examine.[6][11][3]

The decisive Strauss–Corbin move was not abandonment of data-grounded analysis; it was making an action-oriented interpretive framework explicit and procedural.[6][17]

This move helped novice researchers. A coding paradigm gives a practical route from a collection of open codes to an account of what conditions shape action, what strategies actors use, and what consequences follow. Where Glaser’s coding families require the analyst to select and combine theoretical relations, axial coding provides a more direct instructional structure.[11][12][23]

But that instructional advantage has a cost. The coding paradigm is itself a theoretical framing. It directs attention toward an action-oriented, micro-sociological account of conditions, strategies, and consequences. It may therefore make other possible forms of theorizing less visible, such as historical, discursive, institutional, affective, or material accounts that do not fit easily within that relational axis.[11][12]

#### The forcing dispute: axial coding and the coding paradigm

Glaser’s later criticism of Strauss and Corbin was uncompromising. In *Emergence vs. Forcing* (1992), he accused them of replacing grounded theory with a different method because axial coding and the coding paradigm encouraged researchers to force categories onto data rather than allow categories to emerge. He asked Strauss to withdraw the 1990 book, arguing that it distorted and misconceived grounded theory.[6][44][45]

The core of Glaser’s charge was methodological. If analysts are instructed to find causal conditions, contexts, intervening conditions, action strategies, and consequences, they may begin to organize every category under those headings whether or not that organization emerges from the data. In Glaser’s terms, the framework becomes a Procrustean bed: data are cut or stretched to fit the analytic template.[6][11]

Glaser’s criticism also identified Strauss and Corbin’s conditional matrix as a device that could make researchers fit data into a preconceived mould.[3][9]

Strauss and Corbin presented the axial-coding paradigm as necessary for a theory to have “density and precision,” giving Glaser a specific basis for objecting that its relational structure could become obligatory.[97][12][3]

Strauss and Corbin’s own anti-forcing rule is explicit in their separate 1990 coauthored article, *Grounded Theory Research: Procedures, Canons, and Evaluative Criteria*: every introduced or discovered concept is provisional, must demonstrate relevance through continued scrutiny, and must be discarded if it does not do so. They add that systematic comparison can expose data placed in an analytically unsuitable category and correct the classification. In *Basics*, the coding paradigm does not supply substantive categories in advance; it supplies broad questions for exploring relationships among categories developed through analysis.[38][12]

Kelle’s analysis supports this more qualified reading. He argues that Glaser identified genuine weaknesses in the Straussian framework but exaggerated the risk of forcing. The paradigm’s terms have limited empirical content: conditions, actions, and consequences do not dictate a particular empirical explanation in the way a highly specific theory would. Yet Kelle also notes that the paradigm remains linked to a particular micro-sociological perspective.[45][11]

This qualification matters. The risk is not that axial coding inevitably produces fabricated findings. The risk is that its patterned questions can become prescriptive when applied mechanically. A novice who treats the paradigm as a mandatory sequence may transform an exploratory inquiry into a search for slots in a pre-existing matrix.[6][35][11]

The strongest judgment supported by the comparison is therefore conditional. Axial coding is a legitimate device for exploring relations among categories when used as a flexible heuristic—an open guide for asking questions. It becomes forcing when analysts treat the paradigm as a compulsory classification scheme or use it to disregard data that point toward relationships outside the action model.[11][12][35]

This differs from Glaser’s position but not as absolutely as the polemic suggests. Glaser’s coding families also provide theoretical relations, including causes, contexts, and consequences. The key contrast is degree of specification and timing. Glaser offered multiple coding families for ad hoc theoretical integration after substantive categories emerged; Strauss and Corbin provided a named, more structured paradigm for relating categories during axial coding.[6][11][12][3]

The forcing disagreement is thus not whether analysts may relate categories, but whether a specified relation-model assists discovery or silently determines the shape of the discovered theory.[6][11][12]

#### Strauss and Corbin on verification procedures

Strauss and Corbin made evaluation and checking more explicit than Glaser’s 1978 presentation. Their work acknowledged that conventional canons of “good science” require redefinition for qualitative research rather than direct transfer from quantitative inquiry.[20]

Their analytic procedures nonetheless included recurrent checking. Analysts compare one data segment against another, seek contradictions as well as confirming instances, examine whether borrowed concepts fit current data, and return to data as categories are developed. This is a form of validation through comparison and disconfirmation, not mere acceptance of the first plausible interpretation.[17]

Their account of qualitative analysis also emphasizes the interplay of data and researcher. Strauss and Corbin argued that grounded theory had been misunderstood as wholly or primarily inductive. They interpreted the original 1967 book as having rhetorically overplayed induction in order to oppose speculative, ungrounded theory, while still recognizing the interaction between analyst and data.[17][6]

That stance permits a more visibly abductive movement: researchers formulate possible interpretations, explore their implications, and return to data to assess whether they fit. **Abduction** is reasoning that develops plausible explanatory hypotheses from surprising observations and checks them through further empirical inquiry. Although the founders did not use the term, later accounts identify abductive strands in grounded theory, especially in Strauss’s pragmatist lineage.[6][17][24]

Strauss and Corbin’s approach also gave more attention to evaluation criteria. The supplied text records that they believed evaluation was necessary but that no single uncontroversial set of criteria could apply across all forms of qualitative research. This position resists both extremes: it rejects the claim that qualitative work needs no standards and the claim that a fixed universal test can assess every qualitative study.[17]

They accepted that concepts from previous grounded theories could help verify, expand, and develop a theory, provided those concepts were used provisionally and tested against present data. This is more hospitable than Glaser’s strongest formulations to the deliberate use of prior theoretical work as an analytic resource.[17]

Their use of verification should not be conflated with conventional hypothesis testing. Strauss and Corbin did not advocate collecting a fixed dataset solely to prove a pre-specified theory. Their approach retained simultaneous data collection and analysis, theoretical sampling, constant comparison, and iterative refinement.[17][43]

However, verification acquired greater procedural prominence. The researcher was encouraged to examine data for confirming and contradictory evidence, test emerging interpretations against further data, make analytic processes inspectable, and use explicit canons for judging the quality of the final study. These procedures made Straussian grounded theory look more structured and more deductively self-conscious than Glaserian grounded theory.[17][20][6]

Strauss and Corbin combined theory generation with explicit recurrent checking; Glaser treated the same comparative work more exclusively as the ongoing emergence and integration of theory.[17][6][8]

That distinction is relative, not absolute. The original formulation, Glaser’s work, and Strauss and Corbin’s work all used constant comparison, theoretical sampling, and return to data. Lempert reports that Glaser and Strauss (1967), Glaser (1978, 1994), Strauss (1987), and Strauss and Corbin (1990, 1998) all advocated delaying comprehensive use of literature until the analytical story had emerged and stabilized. These works also cautioned against selecting data for a category established by another theory, because that singular selection hinders the generation of new categories and theories.[6]

The difference concerns what each side considered dangerous. Glaser feared that prescribed procedures would force analysis and turn grounded theory into qualitative data analysis organized by a preconceived frame. Strauss and Corbin feared that an unqualified rhetoric of emergence could conceal the analyst’s interpretive work and leave researchers without adequate tools for developing relational, explanatory theory.[6][17][11]

#### What the 1990 position changed

Strauss and Corbin shifted grounded theory from an approach that foregrounded discovery and delayed formalization toward an approach that made analytic structuring overt. They taught researchers to identify categories, define their properties and dimensions, explore relationships through axial coding, and integrate the result into a theoretical explanation.[6][12]

This changed the status of theoretical knowledge. Rather than treating it principally as background sensitivity to be held in reserve until categories developed, Strauss and Corbin treated prior knowledge, action theory, and analytic questions as resources that could assist interpretation, provided they remained subject to comparison with present data.[6][17]

It also changed the status of verification. Rather than allowing the term to remain largely embedded in saturation, fit, comparison, and modifiability, Strauss and Corbin made the checking of interpretations and the articulation of quality criteria more visible parts of method.[17][20]

The practical result is a different balance. Glaser’s position prioritizes freedom from a single framework and risks under-specifying how analysts should integrate theory. Strauss and Corbin’s position provides a more teachable account of relational analysis and risks treating an action-oriented paradigm as a compulsory template. The deciding criterion is whether the framework remains revisable and responsive to the categories generated from data.[11][12][17]

The Straussian position combines theory generation with recurrent comparison and explicit checking of emerging interpretations. Its separate 1990 Corbin–Strauss article frames verification as one scientific canon requiring redefinition for qualitative research, rather than as unmodified quantitative hypothesis testing.[17][38]

Strauss and Corbin defined selective coding as selecting the central or core category, systematically relating it to other categories, validating those relationships, and filling in categories requiring further refinement and development. Validation was thus part of category integration, not only a final check.[46]

Strauss and Corbin stated that well-performed grounded theory meets the requirements of “good Science”: significance, theory-observation, compatibility, generalisability, reproducibility, precision, rigor, and verification.[46]

In Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 coding paradigm, axial coding examines an open-coded category as a phenomenon in relation to causal conditions, context, intervening conditions, action or interaction strategies, and consequences.[47]

Strauss and Corbin (1990) defined grounded theory as theory inductively derived and “provisionally verified” through systematic data collection and analysis.[21]
In *Basics of Qualitative Research* (1990), axial coding relates categories to subcategories and tests those relationships against data.[38]
Strauss and Corbin (1990) held that relationships proposed deductively from prior experience remain provisional until repeatedly checked against incoming data.[38]
Strauss and Corbin (1990) held that a hypothesis becomes increasingly plausible when data repeatedly indicate it; unsupported hypotheses may be revised or rejected.[38]
Strauss and Corbin (1990) included searching for negative and qualifying evidence within verification.[38]

Strauss and Corbin cautioned that rigid application of the axial-coding paradigm may obstruct analysis of complex relationships and changing events.[48]

Strauss and Corbin (1990) cautioned against applying theories developed in one cultural setting to other societies, because imposed theories may not fit and may mislead; they argued that countries should develop theories reflecting their own cultures and behaviors.[49]

Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 quality criteria adapted scientific canons associated with quantitative research to qualitative contexts and emphasized consistency and verification.[7]

Strauss and Corbin stated that researchers make statements about relationships between concepts and validate those hypotheses through subsequent data collection.[23]

*Basics of Qualitative Research* includes “Validating the Theory” in Chapter 10 and “Validating the Scheme” in Chapter 16.[17]
Strauss and Corbin recommend reviewing memos and selected field notes to test whether a theory withstands scrutiny, identify underdeveloped categories, and locate gaps in its logic.[17]

Later editions set out checks that included reviewing internal consistency, filling weak categories, trimming theory, validating theory, addressing outliers, and examining variation.[96][17]

### How did Kathy Charmaz’s constructivist reformulation (2006) attempt to resolve

Kathy Charmaz’s constructivist grounded theory retained the major analytic practices of grounded theory—coding, constant comparison, theoretical sampling, memo writing, and theory development—but repositioned their epistemology. **Epistemology** concerns what counts as knowledge and how researchers can claim to know. Charmaz did not present her approach as merely a different coding recipe. She changed the underlying account of what data, categories, researchers, and theories are.[24][50]

Charmaz first explicitly labeled constructivist grounded theory in 2000 and elaborated methods and procedures in her 2006 book. Her approach developed in response to the limits of both objectivist discovery rhetoric and procedural coding schemes that could obscure the researcher’s interpretive role.[24][35]

#### Charmaz on pre-existing theoretical frameworks in coding

Charmaz retained the grounded theory commitment to initial openness. She wrote that initial coding should remain open to theoretical possibilities discerned in the data and should stick closely to data. She agreed with Glaser that early coding should be open-ended, but explicitly acknowledged that researchers enter research with prior ideas and skills.[18]

This is a direct response to the founding contradiction. Charmaz did not ask researchers to pretend to have no prior knowledge, because that would misrepresent the interpretive conditions of qualitative research. Instead, she asked them to recognize their prior knowledge and prevent it from becoming an unexamined analytic authority.[18][6]

Charmaz’s coding logic differs from quantitative logic that applies preconceived categories or codes to data. She describes grounded theory coding as creating codes by defining what the researcher sees in the data. The phrase “what we see” is important: it preserves close engagement with data while acknowledging that coding is an act of interpretation rather than a neutral extraction of categories hidden inside the material.[18][6]

She permitted researchers to use existing theoretical codes or special analytic procedures, but only if the emerging analysis indicated that doing so was useful. This is the constructivist version of the earned-fit rule. Existing theory is neither prohibited as contamination nor granted authority as a framework that determines the answer.[18][6][24]

A **sensitizing concept** helps clarify this position. A sensitizing concept gives the researcher a general direction for looking at empirical cases, but it does not prescribe exactly what will be seen. It differs from a definitive concept, which specifies fixed attributes or benchmarks. In Charmaz’s constructivist approach, prior concepts are most defensible when used sensitively: as provisional prompts for inquiry that can be altered, set aside, or redefined through the study.[43][18][6]

This position mediates between Glaser and Strauss–Corbin. It accepts Glaser’s warning that early coding should not be controlled by preconceived categories. It accepts Strauss and Corbin’s point that researchers inevitably bring conceptual resources to analysis. But it changes the question from “Can prior theory be avoided?” to “How is prior theory used, disclosed, and made answerable to the developing analysis?”[18][6][17]

**Charmaz permits prior concepts as conditional resources, not as governing frames: they enter when the analysis warrants them and remain open to revision.**[18][6]

#### Charmaz on forcing data into categories

Charmaz rejected the idea that categories simply reveal themselves to a detached observer. Coding is constructive and interpretive: researchers name, compare, and develop categories in interaction with data, participants, prior knowledge, and disciplinary language. This does not license forcing. It makes the possibility of forcing more visible because researchers must examine how their standpoint and analytic decisions shape the resulting theory.[6][24][35]

Her approach distinguishes **construction** from arbitrary invention. A construction is an interpretive account produced through research relationships and analytic work. It remains constrained by the empirical materials, by sustained comparison, by the adequacy of the data-to-argument link, and by whether participants’ meanings and circumstances have been represented plausibly.[25][18][17][51]

The key safeguard against forcing is therefore not a claim of theoretical innocence. It is reflexive, comparative, data-near analysis. **Reflexivity** means examining how the researcher’s position, assumptions, relationships, and decisions shape the research process and the account produced. The original 1967 formulation did not make reflexivity central: it emphasized objectivity and generality more than the researcher’s role in producing data and representations.[35][24][18]

Charmaz’s approach makes this shift explicit. Researchers should use line-by-line and initial coding to stay close to the data, compare data with data and codes with further data, write memos about their developing interpretations, and examine how they move from particular statements or incidents to more abstract claims.[25][18][9]

In Charmaz’s illustrative case, line-by-line coding and further data collection refined an initial concept of participation; social capital and empowerment guided attention but were discarded as final theoretical categories.[100]

This is not a weaker standard than emergence. It is a different standard. In an objectivist version, the central risk is the analyst imposing concepts that prevent discovery of the real categories in data. In Charmaz’s constructivist version, the central risk is the analyst treating an interpretation as though it were an unmediated fact. Reflexive comparison and transparency about interpretive decisions are therefore the safeguards against forcing.[35][18]

Her critique also applies to rigid use of Straussian procedures. Charmaz observed that *Basics of Qualitative Research* became a kind of bible for novices and that narrow, concrete application of its procedures could mute the social-constructionist elements in the method and produce superficial studies. The problem was not that Strauss and Corbin supplied analytic tools; it was treating those tools as a fixed sequence that eliminates judgment, creativity, and contextual interpretation.[35][6]

At the same time, Charmaz did not simply return to Glaser’s account of emergence. She explicitly acknowledged that researchers hold prior ideas and skills, and her epistemology does not imply that data speak for themselves. This is a direct break with objectivist assumptions that a neutral observer discovers a singular reality through value-free inquiry.[18][35][50]

Charmaz changes the forcing dispute from “emergence versus construction” to “accountable construction versus unexamined imposition.”[24][18][35]

#### Charmaz on verification procedures

Charmaz retained rigorous assessment, but she resisted treating grounded theory as a verification method in a technical sense. She distinguished checking data and evaluating an analysis from verification understood as a procedure aimed at establishing a final, singularly correct theory.[22][17]

Her constructivist criteria were credibility, originality, resonance, and usefulness.

- **Credibility:** the analysis should be sufficiently grounded in data, adequately supported by evidence, and capable of showing readers how the researcher moved from data to claims. [17]

- **Originality:** the analysis should offer a fresh conceptual rendering, rather than merely repeat established categories or obvious descriptions. [17]

- **Resonance:** the interpretation should make sense in relation to participants’ lives, meanings, and circumstances. It should have interpretive reach for those whose experiences it addresses. [17][52]

- **Usefulness:** the theory should contribute to understanding, practical action, further inquiry, or social change. [17]

These criteria preserve parts of the original concern with fit, understandability, relevance, and practical usefulness. But they displace the implication that data can validate a theory as a final representation of an external reality. A constructivist account can be credible and useful without claiming to be the only plausible interpretation.[17][52]

Charmaz’s use of checking is therefore iterative and provisional. Researchers return to data, gather further data to refine emerging categories, compare interpretations with new observations, and assess whether their account has breadth, empirical grounding, resonance, and explanatory power.[9][25][17]

Charmaz’s procedures for refining claims also included useful returns to participants, alongside comparison, memoing, additional sampling, and category revision.[101][62]

This does not mean that all interpretations are equally defensible. An analysis that ignores contradictory data, relies on thin evidence, conceals its conceptual leaps, or imposes categories that participants’ accounts cannot support is weaker on Charmaz’s own constructivist terms. Constructivism rejects final proof, not disciplined inquiry.[17][6][24]

The difference from Glaser is precise. Glaser’s internal verification concerns whether categories fit, work, remain relevant, reach saturation, and integrate coherently. Charmaz retains such scrutiny but rejects the objectivist implication that rigorous comparison allows a neutral observer to discover the one theory already present in the data.[8][6][35][22]

The difference from Strauss and Corbin is also precise. Strauss and Corbin gave explicit prominence to validating interpretations, contradictory evidence, borrowed concepts tested against present data, and qualitative evaluation criteria. Charmaz accepts careful checking but refuses to let “verification” frame the epistemological objective, because that term can imply a technical procedure for approaching a singular truth.[17][20][22]

Charmaz preserves evaluation but redefines its purpose: checking strengthens a situated interpretation; it does not verify one final account of reality.[17][22][51]

#### Charmaz’s epistemological shift

The deepest difference in constructivist grounded theory concerns the status of reality, data, and the researcher. Glaserian and early Straussian accounts were often interpreted as retaining objectivist or postpositivist assumptions: an external reality exists, the researcher can observe it with sufficient neutrality, and categories may be discovered through careful procedure.[29][35][8][53]

Charmaz argued that the original formulation had a limited social constructionist element but did not attend adequately to how researchers affect the research process, produce data, represent participants, and position their analyses. The founders emphasized generality and objectivity rather than relativity and reflexivity.[35]

Her constructivist reformulation adopts a different view. Researchers do not simply collect data like objects. Interviews, observations, documents, and field interactions are produced in particular social, historical, and relational circumstances. Researchers and participants co-construct the data and the subsequent theory, although this construction occurs in relation to empirical realities that constrain what can plausibly be claimed.[24][52][51]

This means that a grounded theory is not a timeless extraction from a dataset. It is an interpretive rendering of a phenomenon, developed through particular questions, relationships, comparisons, and analytic decisions. It may be credible, resonant, useful, and theoretically powerful without being universal or final.[51][17]

The shift should not be overstated as unrestricted relativism. Charmaz did not argue that researchers may construct any account they prefer. Her method still requires close coding, constant comparison, theoretical sampling, memo writing, and return to data. Constructivism changes the status of those procedures: they no longer promise neutral discovery, but they still discipline interpretation and expose it to challenge.[24][25][9][18]

This also changes the role of participants. In an objectivist account, participant statements are often treated as observations from which the analyst extracts concepts. In a constructivist account, participants’ accounts are situated interpretations, and the researcher’s own interpretation of those accounts must be examined as part of the theory-building process.[35][24][52]

Charmaz’s central epistemological shift is from discovering a theory presumed to reside in an external reality to constructing a situated theory through researcher–participant interaction and reflexive interpretation.[24][35][51]

That shift resolves some tensions while creating a new one. It resolves the impossible demand that researchers enter the field with no assumptions. It also supplies a principled reason to use prior concepts provisionally and transparently. But it asks researchers to demonstrate reflexive accountability: they must show how their interpretive position shaped the categories and why the resulting construction remains credible and useful.[18][17][35]

Charmaz’s constructivist position also places theory in time, place, and culture and gives the researcher a reflexive position. It attends to the author’s place in the research text, the researcher’s relationship with participants, and writing as part of constructing a final text grounded in data.[19][54]

Glaser and Strauss (1967) and Corbin and Strauss (2015) preferred **credibility** rather than validity and reliability as the term for evaluating qualitative findings. Corbin and Strauss (2008) defined credibility as findings that are trustworthy and believable reflections of participants’, researchers’, and readers’ experiences while remaining one of many plausible interpretations from the data.[52]

Charmaz also permits in-vivo codes—codes using participants’ own words—during initial coding when these terms capture a significant meaning or action. Such codes help keep early analysis close to participants’ language, but they are not a requirement to preserve every participant term as a final theoretical category.

### How did grounded theory’s founding commitment to generating theory from

The disagreement among Glaser, Strauss–Corbin, and Charmaz was enabled by the success of the original project. Glaser and Strauss established that qualitative inquiry could be systematic, conceptually ambitious, and capable of generating theory rather than merely supplying preliminary observations for quantitative tests. The method spread across disciplines and professions precisely because it supplied recognizable procedures for moving from empirical material to theoretical explanation.[9][35][7]

Yet the method’s founding statements were not philosophically complete. The 1967 book described methods for conducting research but did not openly situate them within a fully articulated ontology or epistemology. **Ontology** concerns what reality is; epistemology concerns what can count as knowledge of that reality. This left later users room to interpret the same procedures in objectivist, pragmatist, interactionist, constructivist, and other ways.[29][55][51]

The 1967 formulation was also historically hybrid. It combined empirical grounding and systematic procedure associated with quantitative research with the process-oriented, meaning-centered concerns of Chicago School sociology, pragmatism, and symbolic interactionism. This mix made grounded theory powerful but also made its epistemological direction contestable.[6][51][56][52]

Guba and Lincoln described grounded theory as a qualitative strategy for eliciting *emic* meanings—participants’ insider understandings—and generating theory through empirical engagement, rather than as a paradigm in its own right.[68]

Glaser’s later work emphasizes the objectivist-discovery side of this inheritance. He stresses a neutral or disinterested analyst, emergent categories, direct empirical grounding, conceptual abstraction, and the avoidance of imposed frameworks.[35][50][31][9]

Strauss and Corbin’s work emphasizes the pragmatist and interactionist side. It foregrounds action, interaction, conditions, processes, and the researcher’s use of conceptual tools to build an explanatory scheme. Their coding paradigm makes the relational analysis of social action visible.[6][24][3][17]

Charmaz foregrounds the constructivist implication that neither data nor categories can be separated from the circumstances of their production and interpretation. She retains grounded theory’s practical analytic tools while making researcher reflexivity and co-construction explicit.[24][35][18]

The divergence can therefore be understood as a sequence of responses to the same problem:

- **First, the 1967 problem:** how can qualitative researchers generate theory from data without allowing a prior theory to dictate what they find? The founders answered with constant comparison, theoretical sampling, theoretical sensitivity, and caution about preconceptions. [6]

- **Second, Glaser’s answer:** keep early coding open, let substantive categories emerge, use theoretical knowledge later and only when it earns fit, and treat ongoing comparison as the internal checking mechanism. [6][11]

- **Third, Strauss and Corbin’s answer:** acknowledge that analysts use concepts, make the action-oriented relations among categories explicit through axial coding, and subject interpretations to continuing comparison and evaluation. [6][17][3][20]

- **Fourth, Charmaz’s answer:** acknowledge that the researcher’s interpretive role cannot be eliminated, use prior ideas reflexively and conditionally, and assess the constructed theory through credibility, originality, resonance, and usefulness rather than technical verification. [18][17][22]

The methodological conflict is thus inseparable from an epistemological conflict. Glaser’s language of emergence can imply that categories are already present in data and await discovery by a sufficiently careful analyst. Strauss and Corbin’s procedures imply that analysts actively construct explanatory relationships. Charmaz makes that construction explicit and asks researchers to account for it reflexively.[35][57][24]

The distinction also clarifies why the term **grounded theory** can cause confusion. It may name a shared set of practices—coding, comparison, memoing, sampling, saturation, and theory development—or it may name distinct methodologies with incompatible assumptions about objectivity, prior theory, the role of the researcher, and the status of the resulting theory.[24][26][50][31]

A researcher cannot resolve this simply by combining favored elements from every strand. A study that claims Glaserian emergence while imposing the Straussian coding paradigm from the outset, or that claims constructivist co-construction while presenting categories as objectively discovered, contains a methodological contradiction unless the researcher explicitly explains how the elements have been reconciled.[50][6][18][12]

The practical decision is therefore not “Which coding technique is easiest?” It is “What claim about knowledge does the research design make, and do its coding and evaluation procedures support that claim?” A classic study should make emergence, conceptual fit, and delayed framework use central. A Straussian study should make its action-oriented analytic framework explicit and demonstrate that it remained open to revision. A constructivist study should disclose interpretive positioning, show close engagement with data, and assess credibility, resonance, originality, and usefulness.[6][17][35]

A later synthesis names the resulting major approaches Classic, Straussian, and Constructivist grounded theory. Rather than a simple binary split, these variants can be understood as points on a methodological spiral: each depends on a stated account of the researcher–participant relationship and of what can be known.[58][48][37]

These disagreements became especially consequential during the qualitative-methodology paradigm wars of the 1990s and 2000s. Those debates set postpositivist, interpretivist, constructivist, critical, feminist, postmodern, and participatory paradigms against one another by asking what reality is, how knowledge can be warranted, what role values play, and which methods suit those commitments. In that setting, Strauss and Corbin’s procedural rigor and validation were often read as pragmatic or postpositivist, Glaser’s discovery language was criticized for objectivist implications, and Charmaz aligned grounded theory with the interpretive and constructivist turn through co-construction and reflexivity.

Glaser and Strauss developed their approaches independently partly because the 1967 method was perceived as too ill-defined.[59]

### Synthesis

The 1967 formulation made grounded theory a method for generating theory through constant comparison rather than verifying pre-specified hypotheses. It opposed forcing data into a favored framework, yet acknowledged that researchers need theoretical sensitivity and existing conceptual knowledge. Glaser’s 1978 development strengthened the emergence pole: prior concepts could assist theoretical coding only after substantive categories earned fit through comparison. Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 development made the theory-guided pole explicit through axial coding and an action-oriented paradigm, improving procedural guidance but creating a risk of template-driven analysis. Charmaz’s 2006 constructivist reformulation rejected both the fiction of a theory-free analyst and the idea of technical verification of one final truth; it retained close coding and comparison while treating theory as a reflexive, situated construction. The decisive issue across all four positions is whether concepts remain answerable to the developing data rather than becoming categories that data are required to fit.[6][18][17]

The founders stated the relation between generation and checking directly: “We would all agree that in social research generating theory goes hand in hand with verifying it; but many sociologists have been diverted from this truism in their zeal to test either existing theories or a theory that they have barely started to generate.” Concurrent data collection and analysis distinguished their design both from designs that collect all data before analysis and from designs that first construct a proposition and then collect data to test it.[54]

The later divergence also reflected different intellectual inheritances. Glaser’s Columbia training included quantitative research with Paul Lazarsfeld and Robert K. Merton, whereas Strauss’s intellectual formation drew on Chicago School symbolic interactionism and pragmatism. These differing epistemological and ontological traditions helped produce their divergent methodological paths.[19][37]

Charmaz treats axial coding as optional rather than compulsory. Researchers who want a preset structure may use a frame, whereas researchers who prefer flexible guidelines may follow leads defined in their empirical materials without axial coding.[37]

The development of the three approaches also altered the visible coding repertoire: Glaserian grounded theory uses substantive and theoretical coding; Strauss and Corbin use open, axial, and selective coding; and constructivist grounded theory uses initial, focused, and theoretical coding, with axial coding available but not required.[37]

The anti-forcing commitment also requires that comparison be capable of correcting classification. Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 account states that systematic comparisons can locate data inadvertently placed in an analytically unsuitable category and rearrange both data and concepts into appropriate classifications.[38]

The subsequent Strauss–Corbin interpretation of the founding text made the issue explicit: Corbin and Strauss (1990) said that readers had too readily understood *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* as unconcerned with verification because verification had been associated with the kinds of research the founders opposed.[38]

Corbin (2009) characterizes the later field as a set of grounded-theory methods sharing the purpose of constructing theory from data while differing in philosophical foundations and approaches to data gathering and analysis. She identifies comparative analysis, questioning the data, theoretical sampling, memo writing, concepts with properties and dimensions, and saturation as threads shared across these approaches.[60]

Corbin and Strauss’s separate 1990 article supplies the direct anti-forcing safeguard: open coding is an interpretive process for generating new insights and breaking through standard interpretations; every introduced or discovered concept remains provisional until continued scrutiny proves its relevance; and an analyst’s attachment to a concept is no reason to retain it. This safeguard differs from Glaser’s objection that the prescribed relational coding paradigm itself risks forcing.[38][6]

Hood characterizes the 1967 process as “a spiral of cycles of data collection, coding, analysis, writing, design, theoretical categorization, and data collection.” This recurring design joined category development to further empirical engagement rather than treating coding as a one-way stage.[6]

A comparison of coding purposes describes axial coding as similar to theoretical coding because both identify concepts associated with emergent themes rather than ground core concepts.[61]

Glaser’s later 2002a criticism of Strauss and Corbin’s 1998 edition was that they “force descriptions on the theory, irrespective of emergence,” in order to locate conditions and contextualize theory, thereby losing its abstraction and generalisability.[46]

Strauss and Corbin treat the coding paradigm as an analytic tool rather than a set of directives: it gives analysts questions for sorting concepts and considering possible links, while rigid attention to its specified features can rigidify analysis.[17]

## Charmaz’s epistemological shift and the paradigm wars

Grounded theory’s later divisions concerned what researchers can know, whether data are discovered or produced in research encounters, and what counts as a defensible theoretical claim. Kathy Charmaz’s 2006 constructivist reformulation retained coding, memo-writing, comparison, and theoretical sampling while changing grounded theory’s epistemological foundations and the researcher’s role.[24][9]

### What epistemological shift did Kathy Charmaz’s constructivist reformulation (2006) introduce?

**Charmaz shifted grounded theory from discovery by a detached observer to interpretation and construction by situated participants and researchers.** In *Constructing Grounded Theory* (2006), she rejects the classic image that theory emerges from data independently of the researcher: “neither data nor theories are discovered.” Researchers are part of the world they investigate and construct grounded theories through their past and present involvements, interactions, perspectives, and research practices.[9] Constructivist grounded theory therefore treats the data not as a transparent record of a pre-existing social reality but as material produced through observations, interactions, interviews, and collected documents; analysis then generates concepts from these constructed data.[9][23]

This is an epistemological shift. *Epistemology* means a theory of knowledge: an account of what can be known and how researchers can claim to know it. In the objectivist position against which Charmaz defines her approach, systematic procedures are assumed to allow a researcher to discover reality and build a provisionally true, testable, ultimately verifiable theory.[6] Constructivist grounded theory instead holds that theoretical understanding develops through the researcher’s interaction with data, and that one body of material may sustain more than one theoretically plausible interpretation. A grounded theory thus constructs an image of a reality, rather than reproducing the one reality.[23]

Charmaz makes this shift explicit at the level of coding. A *code* is a concise analytic label attached to a segment of data. Researchers actively construct codes by naming data: their chosen words identify what they consider significant and what they think is occurring. Codes arise from the languages, meanings, and perspectives of participants and researchers alike, so coding examines hidden assumptions in each.[18]

Charmaz’s *reflexivity*—systematic examination of how one’s standpoint, choices, relations, and assumptions shape inquiry—requires constructionist researchers to identify their value positions and assess their effects rather than deny that they exist. It directs attention to how biographies, training, values, language, and interactions influence coding and theorizing.[35][51][7]

This reframes the earlier dispute over pre-existing theoretical frameworks. Glaser treated advance theoretical frameworks, including constructionism itself, as a potential form of *forcing*: fitting data into preconceptions rather than allowing a theory to emerge.[50] Charmaz accepts the force of the warning against unexamined imposition but rejects the premise that a researcher can begin as a *tabula rasa*, or blank slate.

Constructionist researchers should acknowledge prior knowledge and theoretical preconceptions and submit them to rigorous scrutiny.[35] Prior literature and concepts can therefore inform inquiry, but should be treated as provisional and fallible rather than final truth.[8]

- **Against blank-slate coding:** Charmaz holds that no researcher is neutral because the empirical world is known through language and human action, which give observed realities form and meaning. [18]
- **Against forced coding:** She requires reflexive attention to how prior assumptions influence category construction, rather than treating familiar concepts as self-validating templates. [18][35]
- **For theoretically informed openness:** Existing literature may be read before research begins, but it remains revisable in light of data, comparison, and emerging analysis. [8]

Charmaz thus converts the question from “Can the researcher avoid prior theory?” to “How has prior theory entered the inquiry, and how has the researcher tested, revised, or resisted it?” This does not abandon grounded theory’s commitment to close engagement with empirical material; it makes the researcher’s conceptual participation part of the analytic object.

Her response to the emergence-versus-forcing controversy similarly changes the terms of the disagreement. In classic language, *emergence* means allowing conceptual categories and their relations to arise from sustained comparison of data rather than from pre-set hypotheses. *Forcing* means fitting data into categories selected in advance. Charmaz does not accept either the idea of wholly objective emergence or the legitimacy of unexamined analyst-led forcing. For Charmaz, *abductive reasoning* means considering possible explanations for data, forming hypotheses for each, checking them empirically, and pursuing the most plausible explanation.[6] Abduction is reasoning from a surprising observation to a possible explanation, then comparing possible explanations against further data and pursuing the most plausible one.[6]

Charmaz’s third position is reflexive abduction: constructivist grounded theory addresses the tension between “objective emergence from the data,” associated with CGGT, and researcher intervention that forces data, associated with SGT, by focusing on researcher reflexivity and abductive reasoning. Categories are interpretive constructions that earn analytic value through systematic comparison, further inquiry, and transparent examination of the researcher’s part in making them.[23][6][35] This retains the discipline of grounded-theory analysis while denying that disciplined analysis can erase interpretation.

Charmaz also shifts the meaning of verification. *Verification* here means procedures intended to establish whether a theoretical claim is reliable, valid, confirmed, or true. Positivist theory, as Charmaz characterizes it, seeks causal explanation, prediction, generality, and universality, and verifies theoretical relations through hypothesis testing.[62] Her interpretive theory instead emphasizes understanding; it treats understanding as abstract and interpretive, permits indeterminacy, and seeks patterns and connections rather than linear causality.[62]

This does not mean that constructivist grounded theory dispenses with standards. Charmaz proposes four evaluative criteria: credibility, originality, resonance, and usefulness. *Credibility* includes sufficient relevant data, incisive questions, systematic comparison, thorough analysis, and reflexivity concerning the researcher’s assumptions and actions.[8] These standards judge whether an interpretation is persuasive, illuminating, meaningful to the studied setting, and useful—not whether it has captured a universal truth from a viewpoint outside the research process.

The shift also changes the status of interviews and participants’ accounts. In constructivist grounded theory, an interview is an emergent interaction in which interviewer and interviewee explore experience together. Questions, listening, and follow-up shape the data; the interview is not a device for mirroring reality without mediation.[8] Researchers and participants co-construct data, and the researcher must record contextual and interactional features of that production rather than write as a distant expert.[25] Charmaz seeks to retain participants’ presence through active coding, raw data in theoretical memos, and writing that keeps participants’ meanings visible as analysis becomes more conceptual.[57]

Charmaz’s constructivism is not radical subjectivism. It is rooted in pragmatism and a relativist epistemology, while taking a middle ground between realist and postmodernist positions.[63] Charmaz assumes an *obdurate reality*: a real world that resists being made into whatever one wishes, while remaining open to multiple interpretations.[35][63] Worlds are constructed under historical and social conditions that shape collective practices, views, and action.[35] Constructivist grounded theory therefore accepts multiple, locally situated perspectives while treating interpretations as constrained by empirical engagement, social conditions, and analytic comparison.[51][8]

This produces provisional theory rather than a timeless final account. Interpretive theory assumes multiple realities, indeterminacy, linked facts and values, provisional truth, and social life as processual—continually unfolding rather than fixed.[62] The result is a situated theoretical rendering: an account shaped by where the researcher begins, whom they encounter, what they see and hear, and how they learn and think.[9] It can be revised or elaborated as new data and comparisons arise.[8]

There is a genuine disagreement about how far this redefines the final product. Berterö characterizes constructivist grounded theory as a narrative containing categories rather than a theory, whereas another account says it retains coding, memo-writing, and theoretical sampling while enabling researchers and participants to co-construct theoretical explanations.[64][24] The stronger reading is that Charmaz retains theory construction but changes theory’s claim: it is an interpretive, context-bound explanation rather than a universal, detached representation.

Charmaz’s position also should not be confused with Strauss and Corbin’s epistemology. Their work contains constructionist strands: they rejected a pre-existing reality “out there” and stated that truth is enacted.[57] Yet commentators also identify mixed postpositivist and constructivist language, including commitments to recognizing bias and maintaining objectivity alongside acknowledgement that researchers cannot be completely free of bias.[25] Charmaz is distinctive because she makes constructivism explicit and turns reflexive co-construction into a central foundation rather than a tension within an otherwise procedural method.[23][25]

What follows is that researchers using Charmaz’s version cannot simply borrow a coding technique while retaining a neutral-observer account of knowledge. They must state how their standpoint, data-generation relationships, prior concepts, and interpretive decisions participate in producing the resulting theory.[50]

Charmaz’s three procedural responses in the four-work comparison are open initial coding, reflexive category construction, and interpretive evaluation. First, she agrees with Glaser that initial coding should remain open-ended, stay close to data, and avoid applying pre-existing categories, while acknowledging that researchers bring prior ideas and skills.[18] Second, she treats codes and categories as researchers’ constructions and requires scrutiny of the languages, meanings, and assumptions through which both participants and researchers produce them; reflexivity and abductive reasoning make the researcher’s intervention accountable rather than unexamined forcing.[18][23] Third, she replaces verification as confirmation of an objective theory with credibility, originality, resonance, and usefulness; credibility requires sufficient relevant data, incisive questions, systematic comparisons, thorough analysis, and reflexivity about researchers’ assumptions and actions.[17][8]

Charmaz also made the politics of analytic writing part of this epistemological shift. In her 2006 account, she acknowledged that the neutral, objective tone of some of her earlier analysis had obscured its constructivism, and argued that neutral analytic discourse can erase the interpretive acts and ambiguities through which analysis is produced. She therefore called for transparent theorizing that shows how grounded theorists construct their theories.[28] Charmaz (2000) argued that grounded-theory coding could explicitly acknowledge multiple actors’ perspectives on social reality; a constructivist account should describe how data were collected and analysed and how theory was derived, avoiding the privileging of one subject’s account and helping identify researcher bias.[59]

This reflexive account also protects against privileging a single account as the account of reality. Charmaz argued that grounded-theory coding can acknowledge multiple actors’ perspectives on social reality when researchers describe how they collected and analysed data and derived the theory; such description exposes researcher bias and prevents one participant’s account from being treated as definitive.[59]

Corbin’s subsequent account shows that the Strauss–Corbin position itself continued to develop after the 1990 text. Writing in 2009 about the 2008 third edition of *Basics*, Corbin endorsed the constructionist view that researchers construct concepts and theories from stories that research participants themselves construct while trying to explain and make sense of their experiences and lives, both to the researcher and themselves; she also required self-reflection on how researchers influence research and are influenced by it.[17][60] She described the 2008 revision as combining what she considered best in Strauss’s method with contemporary thought, dropping dogma and making procedures more flexible.[60]

A later methodological account identifies this as a distinctive constructivist grounded-theory position: data and theories are constructed through researchers’ interactions with participants and emerging analyses, and researchers’ socio-cultural settings, academic training, and personal worldviews inevitably influence the data.[63]

Charmaz’s reasoning also departs from a purely inductive model. Constructivist grounded theorists work through an ongoing interplay of induction and abduction and are never *tabula rasa*—never wholly without prior knowledge.[63] This makes prior knowledge a reflexively examined resource in interpretation, rather than either a prohibited contaminant or a category template that decides the analysis in advance.[63][18][35]

Axial coding is available but optional: researchers who prefer a preset structure may use it, whereas those who prefer flexible guidelines can follow leads in their empirical materials without axial coding.[37]

Provisional sensitizing concepts: Charmaz’s 2006 constructivist approach allowed pre-existing concepts to serve as *sensitizing concepts*—provisional starting points for inquiry—rather than as fixed categories imposed on data. Researchers should acknowledge prior knowledge and theoretical preconceptions, scrutinize them rigorously, and treat relevant literature as provisional and fallible rather than final truth.[35][8]

Interpretive checking rather than perspective-free proof: Charmaz (2006) treated examination of contrary cases and systematic comparison of possible explanations with data as ways to test the adequacy of an interpretation. These procedures support the most plausible interpretation; they do not prove a final theory independent of perspective.[6][8]

Charmaz’s 2006 approach begins with open initial coding that stays close to the data, then uses focused coding to select the most analytically useful earlier codes. Focused categories remain provisional and are revised through further constant comparisons with data.

Charmaz (2008) characterized the 1967 formulation as a limited social constructionism: Glaser and Strauss assumed a social constructionist approach to the empirical world, but did not examine how researchers affected the research process, produced data, represented participants, or positioned analyses. Their reports emphasized generality rather than relativity and objectivity rather than reflexivity.[35]

The role of prior concepts is also constructive rather than eliminable. One account describes constructivist theory construction as combining empirical analysis with the researcher’s reflection on existing theoretical concepts, and describes constant comparison as extending both across data and categories and between field data and existing literature.[15] Thus, existing theory enters analysis as material for reflexive comparison and revision, not as a fixed coding grid.[15]

A reflexivity-and-relationality critique held that principal grounded-theory texts did not address how researcher–participant interactions affect interview and participant-observation data.[65] (Hall and Callery, 2001) Reflexivity attends to how researcher–participant interactions shape data, while relationality attends to power and trust in those relationships; both were identified as necessary for rigor in grounded-theory findings.[65]

A constructed grounded theory is not a fixed endpoint or an exact portrayal of reality; it remains provisional and open to later modification.[63]

Charmaz’s constructivist epistemology draws on symbolic interactionism: people act on the basis of meanings, meanings arise through interaction, and meanings are modified through interpretation.[66]

Constructivist grounded-theory principles should be followed from initial conceptualisation through writing the theory.[67]

### How did the grounded theory debate fit within the broader

The *paradigm wars* were disputes over the worldviews that should guide inquiry, not merely a battle between qualitative and quantitative techniques. Guba and Lincoln define a *paradigm* as a basic belief system or worldview that guides methodological choices as well as ontological and epistemological commitments. They argued that both qualitative and quantitative methods could be used under any paradigm.[68] Their 1994 account identified positivism, postpositivism, critical theory and related ideological positions, and constructivism as paradigms competing, or having recently competed, to guide inquiry, especially qualitative inquiry.[68]

In Guba and Lincoln’s distinction, postpositivism retains an independent reality but treats knowledge of it as fallible and probabilistic; objectivity is an ideal pursued through criticism and attempted falsification, not conclusive verification.[102][68]

Denzin locates the first major paradigm wars principally in the 1980s, when disputes concerned whether qualitative and quantitative methods and their associated paradigms were incommensurable; their effects continued through the 1990s and 2000s.[103]

The intellectual stakes were therefore larger than the choice between interviews and surveys. Critics of conventional inquiry challenged the theory-ladenness of facts, the underdetermination of theory by evidence, the value-ladenness of facts, and the interaction between researcher and researched.[68] The theory-ladenness problem means that facts are apprehended through theoretical frameworks; the underdetermination problem means that a coherent body of facts does not mechanically generate one inevitable theory.[69] These critiques directly exposed the vulnerability of a literal interpretation of grounded theory’s promise to derive theory inductively from data free of preconceptions.

Denzin described broader oppositions between discovery and verification, contextual subjective meaning and universal truth, and interactive inquiry and objectivity.[103]

**The Glaser–Strauss–Corbin dispute became a concentrated case of the paradigm wars because it made the abstract questions operational: whether researchers discover or construct categories, whether procedure protects against or conceals forcing, and whether rigor means verification or reflexive interpretive adequacy.** It was consequently a controversy about the foundations of qualitative knowledge, not simply about whether axial coding was useful.[6][12][70]

In the 1990s, Glaser defended his version as “traditional” or “classic” grounded theory and claimed continuity with the original formulation, while distinguishing it from Strauss and Corbin’s approach and other variants.[6] The split became public in 1992 when Glaser published a rebuttal of the revised approach Strauss had developed with Judith Corbin.[71] Strauss and Corbin’s *Basics of Qualitative Research* became highly influential partly because it appeared to offer clear ingredients, procedures, outcomes, and instructions.[6] Strauss had responded to student pressure in the 1980s and 1990s for step-by-step procedures by offering heuristics—rules of thumb—which were often treated as fixed rules.[6]

That pedagogical accessibility strengthened uptake but sharpened the methodological conflict. Interest in grounded theory grew substantially from the late 1980s, after Glaser and Strauss had diverged, and many early-1990s users referred almost exclusively to Strauss and Corbin.[6] By the late 1990s, researchers who had not encountered Glaser’s 1992 critique could remain unaware that the two strands made distinct methodological claims.[6] The disagreement was therefore institutionalized through teaching, textbooks, and disciplinary practice, not confined to scholarly exchanges between the founders.

Commentators mapped the divide onto the period’s competing paradigms, although the labels were contested. One account reports a general consensus that Glaserian grounded theory was positivist and Strauss interpretivist; another describes Glaser’s version as positivist and Strauss and Corbin’s as postpositivist.[6][72] A further analysis finds Strauss and Corbin’s texts oscillating between postpositivist and constructivist assumptions.[25] These inconsistent labels reflect the hybrid intellectual inheritance of grounded theory itself: its origins combined positivism, pragmatism, and symbolic interactionism, even as it challenged the positivistic mainstream of 1960s social research.[51]

The 1990s also brought postmodern, poststructural, feminist, narrative, and constructionist challenges to modernist confidence in objectivity, scientific authority, universal progress, and the right of expert researchers to speak for participants. Charmaz notes that postmodernists challenged Enlightenment assumptions in social theory and qualitative research.[35] The narrative turn, for example, valued respondents’ full stories, in tension with grounded theory’s practice of using excerpts to build theoretical statements.[35] These currents widened the argument beyond induction and coding to ask how research represents people, whose voices are preserved, how power shapes inquiry, and whether generalization suppresses difference.

Denzin also identifies performance and queer approaches among the expanding forms of qualitative publishing, while scientifically based research regained force by the end of the 1990s.[103]

Later theorists in the 1990s–2000s were more attentive to locality, partiality, power, control, voice, and narrative than the generation formed between the 1960s and 1980s.[6] Critics of classic grounded theory, including Charmaz (2006) and Adele Clarke (2005), objected to the core category as unnecessary or a barrier to understanding, and to classic grounded theory’s limited reflexivity, insensitivity to difference and variation, and oversimplification in the pursuit of an integrative theory.[6] Formal theory itself became problematic for some scholars because generalization could fail to represent complexity fairly and because postmodern thought questioned the public authority of overarching theory.[6]

This criticism did not produce a single replacement for grounded theory. It generated multiple versions: Glaser’s classic approach; Strauss and Corbin’s structured, pragmatist and interactionist approach; Charmaz’s constructivist version; and Clarke’s situational analysis, among others.[29][7] The plurality was methodological and philosophical.

Grounded theory was classified variously as positivist, constructivist, interpretivist, pragmatist, realist, and neo-empiricist.[6] In this setting, “grounded theory” became a family name for approaches that shared some analytic practices but disagreed about the status of data, researcher, theory, and validity.[70][26]

A procedural dispute within broader paradigm debates: In the 1990s, Glaser’s 1992 criticism of Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 methods cast them as excessively procedural and verification-oriented. Strauss had supplied heuristics in response to demands for step-by-step guidance, but students often treated these rules of thumb as mandatory rules, and *Basics of Qualitative Research* gained popularity because it appeared to offer explicit ingredients, procedures, outcomes, and instructions.[6][24]

In the 2000s, grounded-theory variants were increasingly defined by explicit ontological and epistemological commitments. Mills, Bonner, and Francis describe these variants as points on a methodological spiral shaped by how a researcher defines the researcher–participant relationship and what can be known. Charmaz made co-construction central and positioned the researcher as author of a reconstruction of experience and meaning.[25]

The paradigm-war framing should not be overstated. Guba and Lincoln themselves called the metaphor “undoubtedly overdrawn,” and by 2005 they argued that paradigms were beginning to inform one another across presumed lines of conflict.[68][73][74] They held that compatible elements could be combined—for example, among interpretivist, constructivist, postmodern-critical, and participative inquiry—but warned against mixing axioms that contradict one another.[73] This helps explain why grounded-theory researchers often share coding, memoing, comparison, and theoretical sampling while grounding them in incompatible accounts of knowledge.

Yet the institutional stakes were real. Guba and Lincoln (1994) held that positivist and postpositivist positions controlled many publication outlets, funding mechanisms, promotion and tenure processes, and dissertation committees. They reported growing acceptance of critical theory and constructivism in more papers at journals and professional meetings, new journal outlets, greater acceptance of qualitative dissertations, and qualitative-research guidelines from some funding agencies and programmes; they nevertheless described critical theory and constructivism as secondary, although important and increasingly influential.[68] A 2000 survey of Canadian universities found qualitative research unevenly accepted: psychology departments were relatively resistant, while nursing, sociology, and educational psychology were more welcoming.[6] Differences in faculty members’ epistemological assumptions could require students to defend their use of a qualitative paradigm.[6]

Accounts by constructivist grounded-theory doctoral researchers show how this could affect everyday training. They report resistance from mentors, curricula, graduate-study offices, and ethics boards, and pressure to conform to postpositivist institutional norms.[24] These experiences cannot establish uniform conditions across all universities, but they demonstrate that the dispute affected supervision, curricular legitimacy, and the practical defence of doctoral research. The paradigm wars thus operated in classrooms and assessment systems as well as in methodological texts.

The same pattern appears in disputes over quality criteria. Grounded-theory quality terms—credibility, applicability, transferability, dependability, and confirmability—take the place of conventional positivist criteria: internal validity, external validity, reliability, generalisability, and objectivity.[52] Other qualitative methodologists defended reliability and validity as appropriate terms and urged researchers to use verification strategies throughout inquiry.[75] (Morse et al., 2002) These arguments parallel the grounded-theory controversy: they ask whether rigor rests in correspondence to an objective truth, systematic self-correction during inquiry, negotiated trustworthiness, or situated interpretive adequacy.[75]

The constructivist trustworthiness criteria in this paradigm debate also include fairness and authenticity, alongside credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.[104][105]

The broader conclusion is therefore two-sided. The 1990s–2000s did not settle grounded theory’s philosophical identity; they made disagreement over that identity unavoidable. Glaserian claims to classic continuity, Straussian procedural elaboration, and Charmaz’s explicit constructivism each supplied a different answer to what qualitative analysis produces and how it should be judged.[6][23][24] This plurality enabled methodological development, but it also means that a grounded-theory study must declare its epistemological commitments rather than treating shared techniques as proof that all versions are interchangeable.[26][50]

The 1990s made the Glaser–Strauss/Corbin split a visible methodological and pedagogical conflict; the 2000s made its ontological and epistemological alternatives explicit. The two periods were shaped by different intellectual climates: later theorists of the 1990s–2000s gave greater attention to locality, partiality, power, control, voice, and narrative than the generation formed from the 1960s through the 1980s.[6]

A historical account places this conflict in successive transformations of qualitative inquiry. The blurred-genres moment of 1970–86 involved qualitative researchers questioning their place in research texts, and constructivist thinking became influential as Charmaz began developing a grounded theory through that lens.[54] Charmaz began publishing on constructivist grounded theory during the 1986–95 crisis of representation, while the subsequent triple crisis extended representation debates by adding legitimation—how to judge qualitative outcomes—and praxis—whether textual analysis can effect change.[54] Postmodernist thought permeated this later debate and influenced Clarke’s 2005 situational analysis.[54]

The 2000 *Handbook of Qualitative Research* separated “Paradigms and Perspectives in Transition” from strategies of inquiry, methods, and questions of interpretation, evaluation, and representation.[106]

The conflict over verification was also a conflict over the canons by which qualitative inquiry should be judged. In 1990, Corbin and Strauss wrote that qualitative studies were often judged by quantitatively oriented readers using quantitative canons; they argued that grounded theorists should retain the usual canons of good science while redefining them for qualitative research and the complexity of social phenomena.[38] They named significance, theory–observation compatibility, generalizability, consistency, reproducibility, precision, and verification as canons to be redefined, while warning against their positivistic connotations.[38]

The founders’ different intellectual inheritances supplied one basis for the later split. A historical account identifies Glaser as a quantitative, positivist scholar and Strauss as a pioneer of symbolic interactionism, and links these divergent epistemological and ontological traditions to their later methodological paths and the end of their collaboration.[19]

The original formulation was also read as seeking to legitimize generating theories from qualitative data within the positivist standards of scientific proof current in 1967. Charmaz (2000) observed that early descriptions implied one theory fitted to the facts and that data could be collected objectively without judgments of relevance or fit with the researcher’s emerging theoretical framework.[59] Strauss and Corbin later acknowledged that *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* overplayed induction partly for the rhetorical purpose of establishing the need for grounded theories.[6]

Grounded theory’s prominence during this period was substantial: between 1991 and 1998, grounded-theory methodologies received 2,622 of 4,134 Social Science Citation Index citations, nearly 64% of the total.[21] In educational research, Strauss and Corbin’s symbolic-interactionist methodological frame was compatible with interpretivist paradigms that gained popularity in the 1990s, an account offered for its uptake.[21] By the end of the twentieth century, constructivism had become the preferred qualitative paradigm across multiple disciplines, including education; Charmaz’s 2006 and 2014 textbooks supplied material that educational researchers found compatible with their paradigmatic leanings.[21]

A central criticism of original and Glaserian grounded theory was its realist view that data “could speak for itself” and that researchers could obtain objective data by examining many cases and jointly collecting and coding data to correct bias and make data objective.[76] Glaser rejected the characterization of grounded theory as an interpretative, symbolic-interactionist, constructionist, qualitative, describing, memoing, interview, or field method; he called it a conceptual theory-generating methodology.[30]

The founders did not claim one correct epistemology for grounded theory. Grounded theory in use can be influenced by different underlying epistemologies, including positivist, interpretivist, and critical assumptions.[3] Methodological gaps in seminal grounded-theory texts led second-generation theorists to develop frameworks underpinned by differing philosophies.[54]

The wider qualitative-research sequence named postmodernism (1990–1995), post-experimental inquiry (1995–2000), and the methodologically contested present (2000–2010).[15] These phases locate the grounded-theory controversy within successive challenges to settled methodological authority and evaluation.[15]

The dispute over quality criteria included opposition to universal checklists: critics argued that strict checklist emphasis restrains the diversity and multiplicity of qualitative practices by reducing them to technical procedures.[67]

For the earlier 1991–1998 citation evidence, the reported 2,622 grounded-theory-methodology citations out of 4,134 Social Science Citation Index citations equal 63.4%.[21]

A key wider shift concerned representation. From the mid-1980s, the crisis of representation, research presentation, and writing became central concerns, and qualitative research and analysis were understood as a continuous process of constructing versions of reality.[63] This supplied an intellectual setting in which Charmaz’s claim that neither data nor theory are simply discovered became methodologically intelligible.[63][9]

A key wider shift concerned representation.

By the late 1990s, a survey found that two out of every three published papers reporting qualitative research claimed to use grounded theory.[6]

The move toward abduction was itself part of the 1990s methodological reorientation. A handbook account states that early grounded-theory circles helped establish induction as the fundamental operation for finding new theories, whereas since the 1990s qualitative research has placed its hopes in abduction.[63] Criticism of purely inductive inquiry helped form qualitative research’s later self-conception.[63] Charmaz’s reflexive-abductive formulation thus joined, rather than stood apart from, this wider reassessment of how qualitative researchers develop explanations.[63][23]

Strauss and Corbin acknowledged in 1994 that Glaser and Strauss had overplayed induction in the 1967 book.[6] Since the 1990s, qualitative research has pinned its hopes on abduction rather than treating induction as the fundamental logical operation for finding new theories; Charmaz’s abductive reasoning belongs to this wider methodological shift.[6][63]

Charmaz’s abductive reasoning therefore joined this wider methodological shift rather than standing apart from it.[63][23]

Glaser continued to avoid adopting a particular theoretical paradigm because he believed doing so would restrict grounded theory’s broad potential.[29]

The procedural disagreement also aligned with a broader tension between formalization and intuition. Strauss and Corbin’s 1990 paradigm model oriented coding toward chains in which causes lead to phenomena and phenomena lead to consequences.[63] By contrast, a later formulation of Glaserian grounded theory condensed the alternative into “just do it”: enter the data or field and find what is interesting.[63] Qualitative-methodology discussion placed productive analysis between these endpoints, combining transparent rules with intuition and abduction.[63]

A periodization cited by Ralph et al. (2015) names the relevant wider qualitative-research moments as postmodernism (1990–1995), post-experimental inquiry (1995–2000), and the methodologically contested present (2000–2010); it places these after traditionalism (1900–1950), modernism (1950–1970), blurred genres (1970–1986), and the crisis of representation (1986–1990).[15] On this reading, grounded theory’s internal divergence formed part of a succession of contests over paradigm, philosophy, genre, approach, and method, rather than a self-contained disagreement among its founders.[15]

It occurred during qualitative inquiry’s postmodernism phase (1990–1995) and preceded the post-experimental inquiry phase (1995–2000) and the methodologically contested present (2000–2010).[71][15]

The paradigm wars did not replace one analytic orientation with another. The historical account records that modernist coding and analysis remained alive in contemporary qualitative research, alongside later interpretive orientations.[63] It also describes qualitative data analysis as a field of continuing debate across disciplinary, conceptual, epistemological, and methodological standpoints rather than one definitive best approach.[63] This continuing plurality explains why Glaserian, Straussian, and constructivist grounded theory persisted as distinct, non-interchangeable methodologies.[21]

### Synthesis

Charmaz’s 2006 intervention preserved grounded-theory work—coding, memoing, comparison, theoretical sampling, and theory building—but rejected the claim that these procedures allow a detached observer to discover one self-evident reality. She made category construction, participant–researcher interaction, prior knowledge, and researcher standpoint visible through reflexivity and abductive reasoning.[23][24][9] This placed grounded theory directly inside the 1990s–2000s paradigm controversies over objectivity, representation, power, generalization, and quality.[35][68][6] The result was not a final reconciliation: Glaserian, Straussian, and constructivist approaches remain distinct in their accounts of emergence, forcing, and verification.

But Charmaz changed the decisive question from whether the researcher has influenced theory to how that influence has been made analytically accountable.[23][35][8]

### What can 2020–2024 journal citation patterns establish about which position

Citation and publication patterns are indirect indicators of intellectual uptake, whereas a training program is a curriculum: it specifies assigned texts, learning objectives, exercises, and assessment. The educational review measures published studies rather than teaching, so its Straussian-use finding cannot establish what programs teach.[89]

If Charmaz’s 2006 book had the highest consistently measured citation count in the selected journals, that would show especially high visibility for constructivist grounded theory in that sampled methodological literature. It would not, by itself, show that citing authors adopted Charmaz’s constructivist epistemology.

Direct training evidence concerns particular settings and measures different things: the Spring 2020 *Research III: Qualitative Research Methods* doctoral course at The University of Texas at Austin’s Steve Hicks School of Social Work assigned a grounded-theory text, whereas a 2024 UK psychology account describes Constructivist Grounded Theory as among approaches widely used and taught in UK academic settings.[86][50] A Spring 2020 doctoral qualitative-methods course at the University of Texas at Austin required students choosing grounded theory to read Charmaz’s 2014 *Constructing Grounded Theory*; the syllabus assigns the 2014 edition, not Charmaz’s 2006 constructivist reformulation that is the historical comparison text.[86] A 2024 qualitative-psychology article, written from UK university contexts, describes Constructivist Grounded Theory among the approaches most widely used and taught in those settings.[50]

The curricular evidence also supports methodological pluralism. The Texas course taught epistemological traditions and treated grounded theory as one of five major qualitative approaches; a 2022 University of British Columbia interviewing course similarly made epistemological foundations, reflexivity, data analysis, and judging quality explicit topics.[86][91] A Fall 2024 introductory course at Florida International University likewise listed grounded theory alongside phenomenology, ethnography, and case study.[92] **Thus, contemporary training visibly includes constructivist grounded theory, but no evidence here shows that one grounded-theory school dominates programs overall.**[86][50]

Stough and Lee (2021) state that grounded-theory approaches are commonly taught in qualitative-research courses in colleges of education and appear in graduate-education qualitative-research textbooks.[21] The training examples add explicit evidence of plural instruction.

A Università della Svizzera italiana five-day workshop scheduled for 17–21 August 2026 teaches participants to apply Glaserian, Straussian, and Constructivist grounded-theory approaches to real data and present their findings.[93] The Social Research Association Grounded Theory course states, 《We will be using the approach developed by Strauss and Corbin and, more recently, by Kathy Charmaz, known as Constructivist Grounded Theory》; it teaches initial open, focused, and theoretical coding, memo writing, and theme development through workshops and group work.

The course is suitable for participants with very little or no experience, while some knowledge of qualitative research or attendance on the *Foundations of Qualitative Research* course is advisable.[94]

Pedagogical writing also treats the differences among grounded-theory versions as an object of teaching. *Teaching Qualitative Research: Versions of Grounded Theory* treats published grounded-theory accounts as instructional materials, reports that reading such accounts disclosed misapprehensions about qualitative-method use, and presents critical reading as examination of analytic claims.[95] *Qualitative Mindset behind Grounded Theory: Implications to Qualitative Research Training* argues that theory building requires a qualitative mindset comprising awareness of how knowledge is created, self-reflection, sensitivity to social context, flexible methods, and analytical rigor; it proposes progression from basic observation in early education to advanced theorizing at graduate level.[58]

In accounts by four doctoral researchers at different Canadian academic institutions, adoption of Constructivist Grounded Theory sometimes met resistance and conflict from mentors, program curricula, graduate-study offices, and ethics boards. The authors describe pressure to conform to post-positivist institutional norms as “a form of epistemological oppression.”[24]

The same four authors reported varied qualitative-methods preparation: one had a required qualitative-methodology course alongside quantitative research; one could choose a qualitative course for dissertation work; one took a qualitative course outside the program because the program offered only quantitative courses; and one had an optional qualitative course in addition to three mandatory quantitative courses.[24]

The Spring 2020 University of Texas at Austin syllabus also assigned Charmaz’s 1999 article *Stories of suffering: Subjective tales and research narratives* as a Week 2 reading introducing qualitative approaches.[86]

The Spring 2020 syllabus’s assignment of Charmaz shows Constructivist Grounded Theory in one course. They also reported resistance from mentors, program curricula, faculties of graduate studies, and ethics boards, and pressure to conform to post-positivist institutional norms. Together, these findings show constructivist grounded theory’s presence in one course alongside uneven and contested curricular adoption.[24][86]

### Synthesis

The six journals are a selected, scope-justified cross-disciplinary sample for a citation comparison: *Qualitative Research* directly promotes and debates qualitative methods, while the other titles extend the sample to methodological innovation, pedagogy, health, education, and experimental inquiry. The required common unit is a distinct eligible citing article published in 2020–2024, with the 1978, 1990, and 2006 named editions counted separately.[77][83][84][87][86]

The historical adjacent pattern is Straussian use in the 2000–2017 education-journal corpus: 127 of 210 studies (60.5%) cited a Straussian approach as a primary methodological approach, and Charmazian approaches exceeded Glaserian approaches from 2009 onward except in 2015.[21] Particular teaching examples show constructivist grounded theory in training: a Spring 2020 University of Texas at Austin doctoral course assigned Charmaz’s 2014 *Constructing Grounded Theory* for students selecting grounded theory, and a 2024 UK psychology article includes Constructivist Grounded Theory among approaches widely used and taught in UK academic settings.[86][50] No program-wide training leader has been demonstrated.[86][50]

1. Treat claims of contemporary dominance as curriculum claims only when supported by multi-program syllabus or curriculum data.
2. Treat 2020–2024 journal citations as evidence of intellectual attention only when they are counted separately for each grounded-theory position.

The added evidence sharpens the adjacent education-research pattern: in the 2000–2017 sample, Straussian approaches led numerically, Charmazian approaches overtook Glaserian approaches in citations from 2009 except in 2015, and the founding 1967 text remained cited throughout.[21] It also identifies training instances that teach more than one grounded-theory version, including a 2026 workshop teaching Glaserian, Straussian, and Constructivist approaches.[93]

### Open questions

The prevalence of particular grounded-theory positions in contemporary qualitative-research training programmes remains open. Curriculum documents, required reading lists, syllabi, instructor practices, and student assessments across a defined set of programmes would settle what is taught; 2020–2024 citation patterns establish disciplinary visibility rather than curricular dominance.
