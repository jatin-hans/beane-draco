# Literature Review of WISC-V and NEPSY-II in Pediatric ADHD

## Executive summary

Neither WISC-V nor NEPSY-II diagnoses ADHD in 8–12-year-old children, and both should be interpreted only within a developmental, multi-informant behavioural assessment. WISC-V is the stronger standardised measure for identifying a broad, norm-referenced working-memory weakness because its Working Memory Index directly operationalises auditory-verbal and visual working-memory performance, whereas NEPSY-II samples working-memory demands across tasks principally designed to assess attention, interference control, set maintenance, learning, or flexibility.

This is a construct-coverage conclusion rather than proof of superior ADHD diagnostic sensitivity, because no adequate age-specific head-to-head evidence establishes that either battery more accurately detects ADHD or clinically meaningful working-memory impairment.

NEPSY-II adds greater process-level and executive-attention detail for formulation, including errors, inhibition, sustained attention, response-set shifting, and behavioural observations, while WISC-V better situates working memory within the child’s general intellectual profile.

Ecological validity should not be claimed from a task’s everyday resemblance alone, and neither battery’s structured maximal-performance scores can substitute for evidence that performance predicts independent real-world functioning or for parent, teacher, and functional-impairment measures.

## Clinical and theoretical context

**Review question, study selection, and age-aligned evidence**

This review asks whether WISC-V or NEPSY-II better identifies ADHD or working-memory deficits in children aged 8–12, and whether pediatric-neuropsychological reviews establish superior ecological validity for either instrument.
Reported database-selection results establish the scope of the available review literature. A 2024 rapid review searched PubMed and Elsevier ClinicalKey in August 2023, used PRISMA 2021 systematic methods, retrieved 286 free full-text journal articles published from 2018 to 2023, removed 262 records for irrelevance, adult population, duplication, or focus on other pediatric mental-health disorders, assessed 10 full texts, and included four studies.[1] A systematic WISC review searched Web of Science, Scopus, PsycInfo, and MedLine from database inception through September 2024 using `(ADHD OR “attention deficit” OR hyperactive*) AND (WISC*)`; it required formal ADHD diagnosis, ages 6–16, WISC-III and/or WISC-IV, reported VC, PR/PO, WM/FFD, and PS scores, English or Spanish publication, and a high-impact journal.[2] Its eligibility criteria therefore address earlier WISC editions rather than WISC-V or a WISC-V–NEPSY-II comparison.[2]

Their overlap in working memory, attention, and executive-function content can obscure a more consequential distinction: WISC-V primarily locates a child’s performance within a broad intellectual-ability profile, whereas NEPSY-II samples developmentally organised neuropsychological processes in greater functional detail. For children aged 8–12, that division matters because ADHD diagnosis is behavioural and developmental, while test findings are used to formulate mechanisms, comorbidity, and intervention needs.

### What are the distinct roles of WISC-V and NEPSY-II in

WISC-V is an individually administered, norm-referenced intelligence battery for children and adolescents aged 6:0–16:11. Its five primary indices are Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed; its primary index scores have a normative mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15. The core clinical role of WISC-V is to estimate general intellectual functioning and to characterise broad cognitive strengths and weaknesses, rather than to measure ADHD symptoms directly.[9][10][11]

WISC-V subtest scaled scores have a normative mean of 10 scaled-score points and a standard deviation of 3 scaled-score points.[116]

WISC-V operationalises working memory through Digit Span and Picture Span in the primary Working Memory Index (WMI); Letter–Number Sequencing contributes to the ancillary Auditory Working Memory Index. The WISC-V revision changed the balance of the primary WMI relative to WISC-IV: revised Digit Span increased memory-sequencing demands, Picture Span introduced a visual working-memory measure, and Letter–Number Sequencing was moved out of the primary WMI. Thus, the WISC-V WMI is not simply the WISC-IV WMI under a new name; it places relatively greater emphasis on visual working memory and relatively less on auditory working memory.[10][12]

The WISC-V’s intellectual-assessment purpose also shapes interpretation. In clinical practice, FSIQ and factor-index composites are usually treated as the primary analytic level because composites are regarded as more reliable and comprehensive representations of a child’s performance than isolated subtests. WISC-V results are interpreted both nomothetically, by comparison with same-age norms, and idiographically, by comparing the child’s own scores for meaningful strengths and weaknesses.[13]

This broad profile has practical relevance in ADHD referrals. The WISC-V may identify intellectual disability, giftedness, specific cognitive strengths and weaknesses, and cognitive patterns that affect eligibility, educational placement, treatment planning, or assessment of possible learning disorder. In an ADHD context, it can help distinguish a child whose apparent inattention reflects underachievement, learning difficulty, language weakness, globally low cognitive performance, or an uneven profile from a child whose behavioural symptoms require a different explanatory formulation.[14][15]

NEPSY-II has a different centre of gravity. It is a developmental neuropsychological battery intended for children from age 3 through 16, organised across Attention and Executive Functioning, Language, Memory and Learning, Sensorimotor Functioning, Social Perception, and Visuospatial Processing. Rather than yielding a single global-intelligence estimate analogous to FSIQ, it yields selected subtest, process, contrast, and behavioural-observation data intended to help identify the developmental processes contributing to a referral problem.[16][17]

The Attention and Executive Functioning domain samples a wider range of executive operations than WISC-V’s working-memory and processing-speed indices. Its stated targets include inhibition of learned and automatic responses, monitoring and self-regulation, vigilance, selective and sustained attention, establishing and shifting response sets, nonverbal problem solving, planning and organisation, and figural fluency. New NEPSY-II executive-function subtests added in the revision include Animal Sorting, Clocks, and Inhibition.[18]

Several NEPSY-II tasks are particularly pertinent to an ADHD referral, although no task should be read as a disorder marker. Auditory Attention assesses selective and sustained auditory attention; Response Set adds complex attention, set shifting, and inhibition of a previously learned response; Animal Sorting samples concept formation and cognitive flexibility; and Inhibition uses a non-reading, Stroop-derived naming paradigm to assess rapid suppression of automatic responses and switching between response rules.[4][18][19]

NEPSY-II Response Set requires responses to matching or contrasting auditory stimuli while the child maintains or shifts a complex rule and inhibits previously learned responses.[117][18]

NEPSY-II also includes memory tasks that engage working-memory processes but do not necessarily estimate working-memory capacity in the same way as a WISC-V index. For example, Word List Interference requires repetition and ordered recall of two interfering word lists and is described as assessing verbal working memory, repetition, and recall after interference. By contrast, Memory for Designs and Narrative Memory involve working memory during task completion but were not designed as direct measures of working-memory capacity.[18][19][20]

NEPSY-II Narrative Memory assesses free recall, cued recall, and recognition of organized verbal material; language comprehension, encoding, and episodic learning affect its scores, so it is not a specific working-memory measure.[18]

The battery is deliberately flexible. A clinician can use a Full Assessment, a General Referral Battery, a Diagnostic Referral Battery focused on a referral question, or selected subtests that supplement other testing. NEPSY-II’s co-normed subtests, process scores, contrast scores, and scored behavioural observations support analysis of errors, speed, strategy, persistence, and the conditions under which performance fails—not merely whether a score is low.[17][19]

NEPSY-II interpretation emphasizes subtest, process, and contrast scores partly because subtests within a nominal domain need not correlate strongly, while tasks in different domains may share methods.[18]

This flexibility gives NEPSY-II a different clinical function from WISC-V. A WISC-V profile may indicate that a child has relatively weak cognitive proficiency, working memory, or processing speed; selected NEPSY-II tasks can then test more specific hypotheses concerning sustained attention, inhibition, response-set maintenance, cognitive flexibility, language, visuospatial processing, learning, or social perception. The two tests therefore overlap in some domains but make different contributions to case formulation.[11][19]

A recent ADHD-oriented test protocol illustrates this division of labour. In that protocol, WISC-V was assigned to general cognitive skills, abstract reasoning, attention, executive functions, and academic skills, whereas NEPSY-II was assigned principally to nonverbal skills, language, memory, and executive functions. The overlap in attention and executive functioning is clinically useful: concordance can strengthen a hypothesis, while disagreement can identify a more specific problem of task demands, modality, motivation, language, or context.[21]

Neither instrument’s score profile constitutes ADHD by itself. ADHD diagnosis requires developmentally inappropriate symptoms and impairment across settings, evaluated through clinical history and information from more than one informant.[22][23][1] Cognitive and neuropsychological measures instead contribute evidence concerning strengths, weaknesses, alternative explanations, co-occurring disorders, and intervention targets.[22][24][25]

This distinction is explicit in current clinical pathways. The Australian ADHD guideline discussed in a 2024 rapid review states that IQ or cognitive assessment may assist identification of differential or co-occurring conditions but is not required for diagnosis. The hospital pathway reviewed nevertheless paired WISC-V with parent and teacher ADHD ratings, clinical interview, observations, and an objective attention measure, exemplifying its use as one component of a multi-method evaluation rather than a replacement for it.[1]

The same logic applies to NEPSY-II. Its manual states that subtest scores can assist differential diagnosis of ADHD and other developmental disorders, and that results inform diagnosis and intervention planning; this is an adjunctive role, not evidence that a NEPSY-II pattern independently confirms ADHD. The appropriate clinical inference is that NEPSY-II can sharpen hypotheses about a child’s neuropsychological functioning after the referral question has been established through developmental history, multi-informant data, and clinical examination.[16]

**The clinically defensible division of labour is therefore broad cognitive profiling with WISC-V and targeted developmental neuropsychological hypothesis-testing with NEPSY-II; neither should be used as a substitute for behavioural diagnosis.** This permits an evaluator to use each instrument for the question it is designed to answer and prevents an index discrepancy or isolated executive-function score from being mistaken for an ADHD biomarker.[26][9]

Newberry’s 2025 dissertation provides a direct multi-instrument pediatric comparison: archival data from 272 children aged 8–16 were analysed with binary and multinomial logistic regression for concurrent validity across WISC-V, NEPSY-II, CPT-3, and CATA. Lower WISC-V Cognitive Proficiency Index scores and lower executive-function scores, including NEPSY-II Animal Sorting and Response Set, predicted any ADHD diagnosis relative to no diagnosis. Newberry concluded that NEPSY-II, CATA, and CPT-3 support targeted, efficient ADHD assessment over traditional tools such as WISC-V.[27][28]

Diagnostic sensitivity for ADHD is the proportion of children with an independently established ADHD diagnosis who obtain a positive result under a prespecified test decision rule. A valid comparison of WISC-V and NEPSY-II diagnostic sensitivity therefore requires independent reference diagnosis, explicit thresholds or multivariable algorithms for each battery, and reporting of sensitivity, specificity, confidence intervals, and external replication.

Sensitivity to working-memory deficits is a different outcome: it may denote discrimination of ADHD from comparison groups on working-memory tasks, identification of age-normatively low performance, detection of an independently defined working-memory-impaired subgroup, or association with an external working-memory criterion. Because there is no single gold-standard behavioural or cognitive definition of a working-memory deficit, these outcomes cannot be treated as interchangeable.

NEPSY-II comprises 32 subtests across its six domains and permits General, Diagnostic, Selective, or Full assessment approaches.[29][30] NEPSY-II reports scaled scores with a mean of 10 and standard deviation of 3, percentile ranks, and process scores.[29]

### What theoretical frameworks are employed in the reviewed research?

The most influential historical framework is Barkley (1997, Psychological Bulletin, 'Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD.')’s behavioural-inhibition model.[31] Barkley’s 1997 review proposed behavioural inhibition as the central impairment in ADHD and linked it to four executive neuropsychological functions: working memory, self-regulation of affect–motivation–arousal, internalisation of speech, and reconstitution, meaning behavioural analysis and synthesis. The model predicts secondary impairment in those executive functions and in motor control.[31][32]

Barkley’s model made working memory clinically salient because it treats working-memory weakness not as an isolated finding but as a downstream consequence of impaired behavioural inhibition and self-regulation. Barkley concluded that the evidence then available was strongest for deficits in behavioural inhibition, working memory, regulation of motivation, and motor control, while also stating that substantially more research was required to evaluate the model’s predictions.[31][32]

A second tradition is the executive-function theory of ADHD. This theory proposes that ADHD symptoms arise from deficits in executive functions, defined as neurocognitive processes that maintain an appropriate problem-solving set in pursuit of a later goal. It directs attention toward working memory, inhibition, planning, flexibility, fluency, monitoring, and organisation, and it motivated the move from simple attention tasks toward multi-domain executive-function assessment.[33][4][34]

Executive function is not a unitary construct in contemporary cognitive theory. The unity-and-diversity model identifies working memory, inhibitory control, and set shifting as correlated but separable functions. In early and middle childhood, factor-analytic and theoretical work gives strongest support to working memory and inhibitory control as separable primary domains; shifting may become more clearly separable later in development. This developmental point is especially important when interpreting 8–12-year-old children, because a poor score on an ostensibly shifting task may still partly reflect working-memory or inhibition demands.[35][36]

The task-impurity problem follows directly from this framework. Individual executive tasks require multiple processes—attention, processing speed, language, motor output, comprehension, motivation, and strategy in addition to the nominal executive construct—so the majority of variance in a single task is not necessarily attributable to the intended executive function. A low NEPSY-II Inhibition, Animal Sorting, or WISC-V Digit Span score is thus evidence about performance on a complex task, not a pure assay of one latent process.[36][5]

Working-memory theory provides a more specific framework for interpreting WISC-V and NEPSY-II memory findings. Working memory is commonly defined as the capacity to retain information briefly while manipulating or using it to guide behaviour. Baddeley and Hitch’s (1974) multicomponent working-memory model distinguishes domain-specific phonological and visuospatial storage from a domain-general central executive.[37] The Wager–Smith account distinguishes updating, dual processing, and serial reordering.[20][38][39]

This matters because WISC-V and NEPSY-II operationalise different slices of that construct. WISC-V Digit Span and Picture Span focus on auditory-verbal and visual span/manipulation under structured conditions. NEPSY-II tasks may impose working-memory demands while primarily targeting interference control, verbal recall, attention, response-set maintenance, language retrieval, or flexibility. A study or clinical interpretation that calls all such tasks “working memory” risks treating related task demands as a common measurement construct.[10][40][18][20]

Alternative working-memory accounts emphasise maintenance rather than the three central-executive processes of updating, reordering, and dual processing. Consequently, conclusions about working-memory impairment vary with the theoretical definition and task design: a task emphasising maintenance, a simple span, a complex span, a serial-reordering task, and a task with interference may each identify overlapping but non-identical child characteristics.[37][38]

A multiple-deficit framework challenges any one-process account of ADHD. Sjöwall and colleagues’ study of 102 children with ADHD aged 7–13 assessed executive functioning, delay aversion, reaction-time variability, and emotional functioning; executive functioning, reaction-time variability, and emotional functioning each independently contributed to distinguishing ADHD from controls. The authors interpreted this pattern as support for ADHD as a heterogeneous condition associated with multiple neuropsychological deficits.[41]

The dual-pathway model similarly proposes separable cognitive and motivational pathways. In this account, working memory, response-time variability, inhibitory control, and delay discounting do not all measure one underlying ADHD liability; they can represent partially independent routes to similar behavioural symptoms. Neuroanatomical work in adolescents has extended this model by examining cognitive and motivational pathways using working-memory, variability, stop-signal, and delay-discounting measures, though it does not establish a test-specific model for WISC-V or NEPSY-II.[42]

The heterogeneity framework has practical consequences for assessment. Latent-profile work in 8–13-year-olds with ADHD, autism, comorbid autism plus ADHD, and typical development found executive-function classes that did not reproduce DSM diagnostic categories. This supports equifinality: different combinations of executive weaknesses may accompany similar ADHD symptoms, and a single low WMI or NEPSY-II executive score cannot be expected in every child with ADHD.[43][39]

A related debate concerns causality. Some ADHD accounts treat executive-function weaknesses as mechanisms that generate symptoms; others treat them as non-causal factors that may support recovery, epiphenomena, or consequences of third variables. The causal role cannot be inferred from cross-sectional group differences or a profile discrepancy alone.[35][39]

Recent experimental work makes the disagreement more specific. A 2024 study manipulated working-memory and inhibition demands in four matched child tasks and found strong evidence against the proposition that increased inhibition demands impaired working-memory performance, including for ADHD groups. The authors interpreted the results as inconsistent with models making working-memory problems secondary to inhibition deficits and as more compatible with working memory affecting inhibition-task performance, while acknowledging a subgroup whose inhibition difficulty might not be explained by working memory.[44]

The WISC-V itself is embedded in competing psychometric and intelligence frameworks. Its development considered Cattell–Horn–Carroll (CHC) theory, neurodevelopmental research, and clinical utility, and its five primary indices map broadly onto verbal comprehension, visual-spatial ability, fluid reasoning, working memory, and processing speed. Yet a major review argues that WISC-V did not adopt CHC or Lurian theory as a comprehensive organising theory and is better understood as a collection of measures of different aspects of intellectual functioning that can be interpreted within CHC, cross-battery, or integrated school-neuropsychology approaches.[9][10][11]

The psychometric structure of WISC-V remains contested. Studies have considered four- and five-factor models, higher-order models, and bifactor models; graph-analytic approaches depart from factor analysis by not assuming that a common latent cause generates covariation among subtest scores. In a German ADHD-control sample, only partial scalar invariance was obtained after freeing intercepts for Vocabulary, Digit Span, Coding, Symbol Search, Picture Span, Working Memory, and Processing Speed, leading the authors to recommend great caution in clinical interpretation of primary-index profiles in children and adolescents with ADHD.[15][45][46]

NEPSY-II rests more explicitly on Lurian developmental neuropsychology and Kaplan’s process approach. Luria viewed cognitive functions as complex, interactive systems supported by flexible neural networks; impairment in one component can affect several higher-order functions that depend on it. Kaplan’s process approach adds qualitative attention to how a child arrives at an answer, making behavioural observations, errors, strategy, effort, and timing clinically meaningful rather than residual noise around a scaled score.[19]

The maximal-versus-typical-performance distinction provides the central theoretical bridge to ecological validity. Performance-based tests such as WISC-V and NEPSY-II are administered under highly structured, optimal conditions and primarily index processing efficiency—the algorithmic level of cognition. Parent and teacher ratings instead sample typical performance in comparatively unconstrained environments and primarily index success in goal pursuit—the reflective level.[47][48][49]

This distinction explains why formal executive-function scores and ratings often correlate weakly. In an adult ADHD sample, only 14% of people impaired on performance-based executive measures also reported impairment on an executive-function rating scale; in child ADHD work, BRIEF indices showed little association with performance-based executive or psychoeducational measures but related strongly to parent interviews and ratings of ADHD-related behaviour. Such divergence indicates different assessment targets, not that either performance tasks or ratings are inherently invalid.[47][50][48]

Judgment — Ecological validity has two separable meanings. Verisimilitude concerns whether a task superficially resembles ordinary activity; veridicality concerns whether test performance predicts or correlates with independently measured everyday functioning. Only veridicality supports a claim that a test score forecasts real-world performance. A Stroop-derived task may have plausible real-world relevance, but its resemblance to interference control in everyday life does not itself demonstrate functional prediction.

DSM-5 and ICD-11 provide categorical frameworks for classifying mental disorder, including ADHD, from behavioural symptoms, impairment, developmental history, and cross-setting evidence.[51] Dimensional frameworks, including RDoC and HiTOP-oriented work, instead model variation in symptom severity and cognitive processes across diagnostic boundaries.

These approaches answer different questions, so an executive-function score cannot be conflated either with a DSM diagnosis or with the full dimensional construct of ADHD.[22][35][43][21]

This theoretical plurality explains why WISC-V and NEPSY-II scores are clinically informative without becoming diagnostic proxies. Barkley’s account predicts a relationship between inhibition and working memory; unity-and-diversity models separate working memory, inhibition, and shifting; multiple-deficit accounts predict heterogeneity; CHC frames broad cognitive abilities; and Lurian-process approaches emphasise interacting functional systems and qualitative performance. The same child may therefore show a low WISC-V WMI, weak NEPSY-II inhibition, both findings, or neither, without any one pattern being necessary or sufficient for ADHD.[31][35][41][10][19]

Recent NEPSY-II structural research makes the task-specific premise of process interpretation empirically concrete for the age range central to this review. In a 2024 principal-axis exploratory factor analysis with promax rotation of NEPSY-II standardisation-sample correlation matrices for ages 7:0–12:11, six factors were retained after Horn’s parallel analysis, minimum average partials, exploratory graph analysis, Bayesian Information Criterion, scree testing, and eigenvalue-greater-than-one criteria were considered. The six factors were Finger Tapping, Visuospatial Processing, Inhibition, Memory/Language, Design Memory, and Facial Memory; Animal Sorting loaded with Inhibition, whereas Affect Recognition did not load saliently on a factor.[52]

For ages 7–12, the six common factors accounted for 44.4% of total NEPSY-II score variance, and the average specificity component was 37.3%; most scores met Kaufman’s criterion for high uniqueness, namely unique variance of at least 25% of total test variance that exceeds error variance. These findings support interpreting NEPSY-II primarily through task-specific elements rather than as indicators of broad, higher-order latent constructs.[52][53]

Their collective implication is that ADHD is associated with mean neuropsychological weaknesses, including working-memory weakness, but not with a universal or diagnostically specific cognitive signature.

Cognitive-energetic and arousal-regulation models associated with Sergeant attribute part of ADHD task performance to state regulation, activation, effort, and timing. Sonuga-Barke’s dual-pathway account adds a motivational delay-aversion pathway to executive-control explanations, predicting that reward contingencies and delay can affect performance independently of working-memory capacity.

The ecological-validity framework can be specified more precisely as representativeness and generalization. Representativeness concerns correspondence between the assessment form and context and natural situations; generalization concerns the extent to which test performance predicts behaviour in natural environments. These dimensions correspond to verisimilitude, resemblance between test and daily-life cognitive demands, and veridicality, the empirical relation of test scores to daily functioning.[54]

This framework distinguishes construct-driven from function-led assessment. Construct-driven tests assess constructs such as working memory without necessarily targeting prediction of behavioural functioning; function-led tests assess directly observable everyday behaviours and their action sequences.

### What in the development and clinical use of WISC-V and

The history of childhood ADHD assessment has moved from efforts to identify a characteristic cognitive profile toward a more cautious, multimethod formulation model. Early Wechsler work examined patterns such as the WISC-III Freedom from Distractibility Index and SCAD and ACID subtest configurations as possible indicators of attention and executive-function difficulty. These historical profiles remain useful for understanding why clinicians attend to Digit Span, Arithmetic, Coding, and Symbol Search, but they were developed using earlier editions and do not transfer automatically to WISC-V.[34][2]

This edition issue is substantive rather than cosmetic. WISC-III, WISC-IV, and WISC-V were published in 1991, 2003, and 2014, respectively, and the WISC-V altered the factor structure and subtest composition relevant to working memory and processing speed. Findings derived from WISC-III or WISC-IV profiles can provide historical hypotheses, but they are not direct evidence about WISC-V indices.[2][10]

Historical WISC research also shows why profile discrepancies require restraint. Mayes and Calhoun’s 2006 comparison found larger index discrepancies on WISC-IV than WISC-III and suggested that WISC-IV might better delineate strengths and weaknesses in children with ADHD. However, a later retrospective study of WISC-IV plus attention-test discrepancy scores found no significant utility for those delta scores in distinguishing clinically referred children with and without ADHD.[55][56]

The appropriate interpretation is not that cognitive patterns are clinically meaningless. WISC-based profiles can clarify which components of a child’s school difficulty merit intervention, and WISC results may contribute to ADHD-related decision-making in the context of comprehensive psychological assessment. The narrower conclusion is that profile patterns support formulation rather than diagnostic classification.[57][25][2]

WISC-V studies reinforce that position. A recent WISC-V ADHD study reported lower group performance on WMI, Processing Speed Index, Cognitive Proficiency Index, and FSIQ relative to controls, with WMI, PSI, and CPI differences reported at p < .001 and FSIQ at p = .005. The authors nevertheless recommend WISC-V indices as support for clinical judgment within a multimethod, multi-informant ADHD evaluation rather than as an independent diagnostic procedure.[58][34]

Another recent clinical study of WISC-V structural models and discrepancy patterns reached the same practical conclusion from a different analytic route. In its ADHD sample, relevant index discrepancies occurred in approximately half of participants, versus 24.5%–31.0% in the cited standardisation sample; the authors emphasised that about half of ADHD participants did not show the pattern and that a non-negligible normative proportion did. They concluded that the discrepancies were neither sensitive nor specific enough to serve as diagnostic markers.[9]

The study also highlights a psychometric constraint that matters for clinical overinterpretation. In its preferred model, the general factor accounted for 51.6% of common variance and had omega hierarchical of .652, while the orthogonalised WMI group factor had omega hierarchical of .096 and H of .117. The authors therefore recommend interpreting index differences as within-person comparisons anchored in general ability rather than treating each index as an independent, precisely defined latent construct.[9]

Children aged 8–12 are squarely within the intended ranges of both instruments, but developmental stage remains consequential. Working memory and inhibitory control are relatively well-supported primary executive domains in middle childhood, whereas set shifting may not be fully separable from them until later development. An 8-year-old’s difficulty on an executive task and a 12-year-old’s difficulty on the same task can therefore reflect partly different mixtures of basic capacity, strategy, language, sustained effort, speed, and executive control.[36][59]

For NEPSY-II, development also affects subtest selection. Word List Interference is available for ages 7–16, Auditory Attention and Response Set span ages 5–16 and 7–16 respectively, and Inhibition is described for ages 5–16. Clinicians should choose subtests that match the child’s presenting difficulty and developmental level rather than assuming that a generic “executive-function battery” samples the same construct identically across the 8–12 range.[19]

The NEPSY-II attention/concentration referral configuration includes List Memory, List Memory Delayed, Inhibition, Auditory Attention and Response Set, Word List Interference, and Word Generation.[118]

The original NEPSY was published in the United States in 1998 for ages 3:0–12:11 and included five domains and 27 subtests. NEPSY-II, published in 2007, extended the upper age range to 16:11, added Social Perception as a sixth domain, changed subtest content and scoring, expanded floors and ceilings, removed total domain scores, and introduced more detailed process and contrast scores. These changes mean that findings from original NEPSY studies are historically informative but should not be represented as direct NEPSY-II validation.[17][19][60]

The norming structures also bear on interpretation. The WISC-V US standardisation sample included 2,200 children and adolescents in 11 age groups, stratified by age, sex, ethnicity, region, and parental education. NEPSY-II standardisation involved 1,200 children aged 3–16, with 100 children in each of 12 age groups and stratification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, parental education, and geographical region.[11][19][17]

Normative comparison does not eliminate the base-rate problem of low scores. In a study of 1,200 healthy NEPSY-II standardisation participants aged 3–16, low scores occurred commonly enough that the authors supplied look-up tables to contextualise the number and pattern of low scores. For a child aged 8–12, one weak subtest should therefore be interpreted in relation to the base rate of low scores, the child’s broader profile, referral question, behavioural observations, and corroborating functional evidence.[61]

Comorbidity is a central interpretive issue in ADHD assessment. One ADHD neuropsychological sample reported associated learning disorders in 58% of patients, while a German WISC-V ADHD sample reported comorbid learning disorder in 69.2%. Because learning disorders, language difficulties, anxiety, mood symptoms, autism traits, sleep disturbance, and medication status can each affect cognitive performance, a WISC-V or NEPSY-II weakness cannot be assigned uniquely to ADHD without the differential formulation supplied by the wider clinical assessment.[21][15][24]

The influence of comorbidity can be observed directly in clinical research. In a WISC-IV study, children with ADHD plus psychiatric comorbidity had lower Processing Speed Index scores than those with ADHD alone, with means of 81.69 and 89.88 respectively, p = .008 and Cohen’s d = .417. This does not establish that processing speed identifies comorbidity, but it demonstrates that sample composition can change the apparent cognitive profile attributed to ADHD.[25]

Anxiety is particularly relevant to interpreting executive tasks. In a 2025 clinical sample aged 8–15 assessed with WISC-IV and selected NEPSY-II subtests, children with anxiety disorder demonstrated more efficient inhibition than both ADHD and ADHD-plus-anxiety groups, while more severe anxiety symptoms within the sample were associated with better inhibition-task performance. These findings concern inhibition and response time, not WISC-V or working-memory sensitivity, but they show why diagnostic group comparisons and symptom-level associations can yield different patterns.[7]

The study also illustrates limits of direct transfer to the present comparison. It used WISC-IV, not WISC-V; enrolled ages 8–15 rather than reporting an 8–12 subgroup; selected Visual Attention, Auditory Attention, and Inhibition during analysis rather than a priori; and did not assess ADHD diagnostic accuracy or comparative working-memory sensitivity. Its contribution is therefore clinical context for joint use of Wechsler and NEPSY-II measures, not a head-to-head answer about the requested editions.[6]

A similar caution applies to evidence from original NEPSY subtype studies. An archival sample of 138 children aged 6–12 found that the ADHD-hyperactive/impulsive group scored lower on behavioural-inhibition tasks, whereas the inattentive group scored lower on Tower planning but not on expected attention measures. This supports the historical use of neuropsychological tasks to investigate presentation-related differences, while showing that hypothesised attention deficits do not necessarily emerge on formal attention tasks.[62]

The original-NEPSY ADHD-presentation study of 138 children aged 6–12 was conducted by Couvadelli.[62]

An earlier dissertation by Pottinger studied 118 children aged 8–12 years with DSM-IV ADHD, principally inattentive and combined presentations. It administered Tower, Auditory Attention and Response Set, Phonological Processing, and Speeded Naming from the original NEPSY, not NEPSY-II.[119]

Couvadelli’s 2006 archival clinic study of 138 children aged 6–12 found diagnostic-group differences on NEPSY Tower, Auditory Attention and Response Set, Visual Attention, Statue, and Knock and Tap, as well as both CPT-II error measures, but not on Design Fluency.[62]

Theoretical history helps explain this inconsistency. Barkley’s inhibition-centred model identifies working memory as a predicted consequence of impaired behavioural inhibition, whereas a study of boys with ADHD initially found deficits in interference control, inhibition of ongoing responses, planning, and letter fluency but found none remained after controlling for age, IQ, and non-executive measures. The two findings differ in level of inference: one is a broad theoretical synthesis, the other is a covariate-adjusted result from a particular study.[31][63]

The contemporary clinical implication is component-specific interpretation. A child may have ADHD with intact formal working-memory performance but serious everyday organisational dysfunction; another may have a low WMI because of language, anxiety, learning disorder, fatigue, limited engagement, or broader cognitive weakness; and a third may show selective difficulty in inhibition or set shifting on NEPSY-II with average WMI. Cognitive scores are useful precisely because they differentiate these formulations, not because they collapse them into one diagnostic index.[36][64][10][65]

Testing context is also clinically meaningful. WISC-V and NEPSY-II are administered one-to-one in quiet, structured settings, which reduce competing demands and may not represent the home or classroom conditions under which ADHD symptoms become most impairing. Formal test performance consequently provides evidence of what the child can do under optimised conditions; teacher and parent reports contribute evidence about whether the child consistently does it while managing distraction, time, competing goals, and environmental demands.[1][47][24]

This is why cognitive testing should be linked to functional formulation. Working memory and organisational skills have each predicted ADHD-related differences in academic achievement and classroom performance, and one study of children with mean ages of 10.13 years in the ADHD group and 10.69 years in the comparison group found that phonological, visuospatial, and letter-updating working-memory scores were all lower in ADHD. Those findings identify a plausible functional mechanism for academic difficulty, not a test-based definition of ADHD.[66]

The WISC tradition began with the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children in 1949. Its historical emphasis on a multidimensional profile of verbally and nonverbally mediated performance explains the continuing clinical use of both global intellectual estimates and relative cognitive strengths and weaknesses.

### Synthesis

WISC-V and NEPSY-II developed from different assessment traditions and should be interpreted accordingly. WISC-V supplies a norm-referenced estimate of general cognitive ability and broad index-level variation, while NEPSY-II offers flexible, process-oriented sampling of developmental neuropsychological functions, especially attention and executive processes. Their joint use can clarify a child’s cognitive and functional profile, but ADHD remains a behavioural diagnosis requiring cross-setting, multi-informant clinical evidence.[9][19][22][24]

The theoretical literature rejects a single, obligatory ADHD cognitive signature. Inhibition-centred, working-memory, unity-and-diversity, multiple-deficit, and dimensional frameworks each explain part of the observed heterogeneity; WISC-V psychometric work also cautions against treating index scores as fully independent constructs. The appropriate conclusion for children aged 8–12 is therefore not to search for a diagnostic WISC-V or NEPSY-II pattern, but to use their convergent and divergent findings to test clinically specific hypotheses about the child’s learning, attention, executive control, comorbidity, and everyday impairment.[31][35][41][46][9]

Instrument-specific classification findings reinforce the distinction between ADHD diagnosis and cognitive profiling.In NEPSY-II manual data summarized in a naturalistic clinic study, children with ADHD Combined Type scored lower than matched controls on Auditory Attention and Response Set and Inhibition, with the largest effects for Response Set Total Correct and Response Set Combined Scaled Score.[67] In that clinic study, however, adding NEPSY-II Response Set Combined to age, biological sex, and Conners 3 parent ratings did not significantly improve classification beyond the rating-scale block (likelihood-ratio χ² = 2.76, p = .09); the augmented model classified 70.83% of cases, compared with 74.17% for Conners 3 alone.[67]

In the NEPSY-II ADHD special-group material, the lowest mean scaled scores were 7.8 for Auditory Response Total Correct and 8.3 for both Inhibition Combined and Inhibition Errors.[120]

In an outpatient clinic dissertation, NEPSY-II Response Set Combined had specificity of .96 at a cutoff two standard deviations below the mean; the cited result did not provide corresponding sensitivity, predictive values, or AUC.[67]

## ADHD diagnostic assessment in children aged 8–12

### How do WISC-V and NEPSY-II compare for diagnosing ADHD in

ADHD diagnosis in an 8–12-year-old is a clinical classification of persistent impairing symptoms across settings, not an inference from a cognitive-test profile. The American Academy of Pediatrics approach requires information from parents, teachers, and other adults across home, school, and peer settings, while considering alternative and co-occurring conditions.[24] Neuropsychological assessment therefore contributes to differential formulation, characterization of impairment, and intervention planning; it does not replace the multi-informant diagnostic process.[6][1]

**Neither WISC-V nor NEPSY-II is an established stand-alone diagnostic test for ADHD in children aged 8–12.** The DSM-5 clinical model treats working-memory and response-inhibition weaknesses as possible neurocognitive features, but explicitly does not treat tests of those abilities as sufficiently sensitive or specific diagnostic indices.[44] The 2023 meta-analysis of WAIS-IV and WISC-V performance similarly concluded that Wechsler performance patterns are not sufficiently sensitive or specific for diagnostic purposes and that ADHD is less associated with a particular cognitive profile than autism.[68]

Judgment — The clinically appropriate comparison is consequently not which instrument “diagnoses ADHD,” but which adds valid information after ADHD has been evaluated through developmental history, clinical interview, impairment assessment, and cross-setting ratings. WISC-V and NEPSY-II occupy different positions in that assessment strategy: WISC-V samples broad intellectual and cognitive-proficiency functioning, whereas NEPSY-II permits targeted sampling of attention, executive control, memory, language, visuospatial, social-perceptual, and sensorimotor processes.

WISC-V is most useful in an ADHD assessment for mapping general cognitive ability and possible relative weaknesses in working memory and processing speed. The WISC-V includes Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, Fluid Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed indices; unlike earlier WISC editions, its Visual Spatial and Fluid Reasoning indices replace the former Perceptual Reasoning Index.[34] In the Spanish WISC-V study by Rodríguez et al.

(2026, *Anales de Pediatría*), 132 children with ADHD and 56 controls aged 6–16 were diagnosed through prior clinical assessment, subsequently checked against DSM-5-TR criteria using histories and interviews with children, relatives, and teachers.[34] The ADHD group had mean WMI and PSI scores of 90.03 and 89.64, compared with 101.63 and 99.36 in controls.[34] In Rodríguez et al.’s control-versus-ADHD logistic regressions, WMI (*B* = −0.067, *p* < .001), PSI (*B* = −0.043, *p* = .007), and CPI (*B* = −0.091, *p* < .001) were significant predictors.

The 188 children in the 2026 WISC-V ADHD and control study had a mean age of 10.04 years.[34]

The 2026 Spanish ADHD-presentation study’s full sample of 188 children aged 6–16 years had a mean age of 10.04 years.[34]

The primary-WISC-V-index model correctly classified 75.5% of cases, and the CPI composite-index model correctly classified 76.6% of cases; these figures are overall correct-classification percentages.[58][34]

The direction of this WISC-V finding has been replicated in samples that include, but are not restricted to, the target age range. In a study of 24 unmedicated children with ADHD aged 6–15 and 24 controls, the ADHD group’s WMI was 87, its PSI was 93, and its general intelligence score was 94, compared with 111 for the control group’s general intelligence score; all reported scores remained in the normal range despite lower ADHD-group means.[21] Becker et al.

(2024, *Journal of Intelligence*) also found lower WISC-V scores in 91 children and adolescents with ADHD than in 91 matched controls, although the WMI difference was no longer statistically significant after Bonferroni–Holm adjustment.[15] Thus, WISC-V supplies a recurrent group-level cognitive pattern, but the pattern is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis of an individual child.

The WISC-V profile is also psychometrically constrained as a diagnostic classifier. Becker et al. (2024, Journal of Intelligence, 'Measurement Invariance of the WISC-V across a Clinical Sample of Children and Adolescents with ADHD and a Matched Control Group') found configural and metric invariance of the German WISC-V five-factor model across ADHD and control groups, but not full scalar invariance; intercepts for Vocabulary, Digit Span, Picture Span, Coding, Symbol Search, Working Memory, and Processing Speed required relaxation to obtain partial scalar invariance.[46][15] Their model-based reliability estimates further indicated that WISC-V measures general intelligence more precisely than its cognitive-subdomain indices.[46] The 2026 Spanish clinical study of 241 recently diagnosed children aged 6–17 found that WISC-V WMI and PSI were lower than the other primary indices, but approximately half the ADHD sample did not show the characteristic WMI/PSI discrepancy and one fifth to one third of the normative sample did.[9] These results make WISC-V appropriate for describing a child’s cognitive functioning and possible educational needs, not for converting an index discrepancy into an ADHD diagnosis.

NEPSY-II has a different diagnostic contribution. Its 32 subtests and four delayed tasks are organized into six theoretically derived domains: Attention and Executive Functioning, Language, Memory and Learning, Social Perception, Sensorimotor, and Visuospatial Processing. The manual does not derive domain scores because of psychometric considerations and the intended clinical sensitivity of individual subtest scores.[18] The Attention and Executive Functioning domain samples inhibition of automatic responses, monitoring and self-regulation, vigilance, selective and sustained attention, response-set shifting, nonverbal problem solving, planning, organization, and figural fluency.[18] Accordingly, NEPSY-II can refine hypotheses about executive mechanisms that WISC-V does not sample directly, particularly inhibition, set shifting, and task-specific error patterns.

NEPSY-II Inhibition separately assesses automatic naming speed, inhibition of an automatic response, and switching between naming and inhibition rules, allowing baseline naming speed to be distinguished from inhibition or switching costs.[117]

NEPSY-II Auditory Attention assesses selective and sustained auditory attention, and Response Set assesses shifting to and maintaining a new, complex response set, including inhibition of previously learned responses; both are relevant to attentional-control evaluation in ADHD.[4][69]

NEPSY-II findings in ADHD are selective rather than uniform. In the Egyptian study by El-Sheikh et al. (2017), children with ADHD performed more poorly than typically developing children in Attention and Executive Functioning, Language, and Memory and Learning.[70] In contrast, Panvino, Zaccaria, Pica, and Amitrano (2025, *Children*) assessed 48 clinically diagnosed participants aged 8–15 years—mean age 10.12 years—with ADHD, anxiety disorder, or comorbid ADHD plus anxiety, using WISC-IV and selected NEPSY-II tasks.[6] The diagnostic groups did not differ significantly on WISC-IV indices, including WMI, or on NEPSY-II Visual Attention, Auditory Attention, and most Inhibition conditions.[6] They did differ on NEPSY-II Inhibition condition B: the anxiety group had a higher combined score than the ADHD group, 7.73 versus 7.32, with *p* = .013 and *d* = 0.995, and completed the condition faster.[6]

This pattern gives NEPSY-II a plausible differential-assessment role where ADHD must be distinguished from anxiety or where executive dysfunction is clinically suspected. Panvino et al. (2025, Children, 'ADHD Children Take More Time to Inhibit Automatic Responses: A Comparison with Anxiety Disorders Using NEPSY-II') interpreted their findings as indicating that anxiety may modulate executive functioning on tasks requiring attention and inhibition, and emphasized inhibition in differentiating ADHD from anxiety disorders.[7] That is a narrower clinical role than diagnosis of ADHD itself: NEPSY-II task performance informs the cognitive phenotype and competing explanations for symptoms.

The only identified direct multimeasure comparison involving WISC-V and NEPSY-II is Newberry’s archival dissertation study of 272 children aged 8–16 who received ADHD or no-ADHD diagnoses and completed WISC-V, NEPSY-II, CPT-3, and CATA measures.[4] In its regression models, lower WISC-V CPI and lower NEPSY-II Animal Sorting and Response Set scores predicted ADHD-related diagnostic group membership, while the author concluded that WISC-V was less predictive than NEPSY-II and continuous-performance measures in the fitted models.[4] The models used forward stepwise binary and multinomial logistic regression with gender and other instruments, rather than matched test-by-test diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.[4] Its results therefore support the proposition that selected NEPSY-II executive measures can add predictive information within a broader battery, not a claim that NEPSY-II has demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy over WISC-V for 8–12-year-olds.

The historical WISC literature partly explains why cognitive profiles continue to be used in ADHD referrals. Anastopoulos, Spisto, and Maher’s 1994 WISC-III study examined the Freedom from Distractibility factor as a possible identifier of ADHD. A later WISC-III study of 98 children with ADHD and 81 controls aged 6–12 reported that Freedom from Distractibility was the most impaired profile and had the highest diagnostic accuracy among the profiles evaluated; its optimized cut-offs exceeded the traditional full- or partial-profile approach.[34][72][57] Mayes and Calhoun (2006) subsequently found that all children in their WISC-IV ADHD sample scored lowest on WMI or PSI, compared with 88% of their WISC-III sample scoring lowest on Freedom from Distractibility or PSI.[55]

Later evidence has narrowed the clinical interpretation of those historical profile findings. The WAIS-IV/WISC-V meta-analysis found age-expected performance overall in children and adults with ADHD, with only slightly reduced working-memory scores.[68] The 2026 WISC-V study likewise concluded that index discrepancies may complement assessment but cannot replace other diagnostic information.[9] The difference between the older WISC-III profile literature and later WISC-V synthesis is substantive: earlier work evaluated the relative separation achieved by particular profile rules, whereas contemporary work emphasizes overlap between ADHD and comparison groups, changing index structures, and the instability of individual score discrepancies. WISC-V index differences showed long-term stability correlations of only 0.06 to 0.43 in one clinical retest study.[13]

Wilson’s WISC-V meta-analysis estimated a pooled ADHD-group Working Memory Index mean of 93.7 standard-score points, 6.3 points below the normative mean of 100 (a small effect), and a Processing Speed Index mean of 92.3 points, 7.7 points below the norm (a medium effect).[68]

The same meta-analysis found no significant within-ADHD difference between Fluid Reasoning and any other WISC-V index.[68]

Judgment — For an 8–12-year-old referred for possible ADHD, use WISC-V when the referral asks whether general ability, working memory, processing speed, or learning-related cognitive demands help explain functional difficulty. Use selected NEPSY-II subtests when the referral asks which attention, inhibition, switching, planning, language, or memory processes contribute to the observed presentation. The tests are complementary because they operationalize different levels of analysis: WISC-V summarizes broad cognitive performance, whereas NEPSY-II decomposes specific neuropsychological processes.

1. Use ADHD clinical criteria, multi-informant ratings, interview, impairment, and differential diagnosis to determine ADHD status; do not use either WISC-V or NEPSY-II as a diagnostic replacement. [1][24]
2. Use WISC-V results to formulate cognitive strengths, working-memory and processing-speed weaknesses, and educational support needs, while avoiding diagnostic interpretation of isolated indices or discrepancy scores. [34][15]
3. Add selected NEPSY-II tasks when the clinical question concerns executive-control mechanisms or differentiation from anxiety and other co-occurring conditions. [6][18]

ADHD diagnostic-accuracy findings from separate WISC-V and NEPSY-II studies are not directly comparable because their samples and reference standards differ. Rodríguez et al. (2026) studied 188 children aged 6–16, including 132 clinically diagnosed ADHD cases and 56 controls, and verified DSM-5-TR criteria from clinical histories and interviews with children, relatives, and teachers.[34] By contrast, the unmedicated ADHD sample in the 2024 *Anales de Pediatría* study comprised 24 children aged 6–15 diagnosed under DSM-5 criteria, excluded other psychiatric diagnoses apart from specific learning disorders, and excluded children currently receiving or previously having received ADHD medication.[21] Other pediatric ADHD studies have used ICD-10 clinical diagnoses recruited through pediatricians, or DSM-5 K-SADS diagnoses supplemented by parent and teacher ratings and current parent-reported impairment while permitting comorbidities.[73][39] Medication exposure also differs: in one working-memory study, 16 of 34 children with ADHD were prescribed psychostimulants, withheld for at least 24 hours before each research session.[38] Diagnostic accuracy varies across settings, patient groups, and prior testing pathways.[65]

Evidence from WISC-III and WISC-IV cannot be treated as evidence for WISC-V because the editions differ in subtest composition, normative samples, index structure, and score interpretation.

A comparison of mean test scores in children already classified as having ADHD is discriminant-group evidence, not diagnostic-accuracy evidence. Diagnostic sensitivity requires a prespecified test threshold, an independent clinical reference diagnosis, and reporting of the complete classification table, including sensitivity and specificity.

## Working-memory deficits in children aged 8–12

Judgment — Working-memory assessment in ADHD is clinically consequential because low performance may guide supports for instruction following, learning, and self-management, but it is not equivalent to an ADHD diagnosis. The relevant comparison is therefore not which battery produces lower ADHD-group scores, but which identifies children who truly have a prespecified working-memory impairment.

### How were working-memory deficits defined in the studies used to

Working memory has two competing operational meanings in this literature. The broad clinical definition is temporary storage plus manipulation of information; the component-process definition reserves working memory for active operations on retained material—continuous updating, dual-processing, and serial/temporal reordering—and treats verbal, visual, and spatial short-term storage as separable memory systems.[6][35][38] This distinction matters because an apparently demanding span task may predominantly reflect storage, rehearsal, fluid reasoning, or strategy rather than active executive manipulation.

In WISC-V studies, working memory was generally represented by the continuous Working Memory Index (WMI): registration, maintenance, and manipulation of visual and auditory information, primarily from Digit Span and Picture Span. Letter–Number Sequencing contributes to the ancillary Auditory Working Memory Index (AWMI).[10][74][12] These studies usually tested ADHD–control mean differences or within-person index discrepancies rather than classifying an individual child as having a working-memory deficit.

The WISC-V Working Memory Index is described as measuring the registration, maintenance, and manipulation of visual and auditory information in conscious awareness, which also engages attention and concentration.[116]

For example, the WISC-V digital special-group study found WMI means of 98.8 in 24 children with ADHD and 104.6 in controls, a non-significant difference (p=.16; standard difference=.39); AWMI was 96.8 versus 107.4 (p=.01; standard difference=.72), and Letter–Number Sequencing was 9.1 versus 11.5 scaled-score points (p<.01; standard difference=.85).[74] A separate ADHD–control study reported a WMI of 87 in its ADHD group and a significant group difference, but its participants ranged from 6 to 15 years rather than constituting an 8–12 subgroup.[21]

NEPSY-II does not supply an equivalent unitary working-memory composite. Its Word List Interference subtest, applicable from ages 7–16, assesses verbal working memory, repetition, and ordered recall after interference: the child repeats two word lists and subsequently recalls each in presentation order.[18][19] Other NEPSY-II tasks include working-memory demands but are multiply determined: Auditory Attention B combines set maintenance, set shifting, inhibition, and working memory, while Word Generation involves language acquisition and retrieval, speed, attention, verbal productivity, and working memory.[40] Thus, a low WMI and a low Word List Interference or Auditory Attention score do not automatically denote the same latent impairment.

The clearest categorical impairment rule comes from purpose-designed ADHD working-memory research, not from either named battery. Fosco, Kofler, Groves, and colleagues defined impairment as a reordering, updating, or dual-processing component score at or below the 10th percentile of the non-ADHD group, equivalent to z≤−1.28; these component scores were derived from multiple experimental indicators rather than a single clinical subtest.[37] In that framework, reordering means holding and rearranging information, updating means replacing no-longer-relevant contents, and dual-processing means retaining information while completing another cognitively demanding operation.[37]

Conventional Wechsler span scoring is an uncertain proxy for this active-working-memory construct. In Wells, Kofler, Soto, Schaefer, and Sarver’s study of 34 children with ADHD aged 8–13, standard Digit Span Backward showed correlations of .03 with reordering, .22 with dual-processing, and −.03 with updating, but correlated .49 with IQ. Removing the discontinue rule and awarding partial credit increased associations with reordering and dual-processing to .58 and .53, respectively; the improvement arose from longer trials ordinarily omitted by standard administration.[38] **Accordingly, WISC-V provides a designated working-memory score, whereas NEPSY-II samples heterogeneous processes that may involve working memory; neither operationalization alone supplies a common deficit definition for a sensitivity comparison.**[10][18][40]

Contemporary executive-function accounts separate working memory from updating. Working memory requires simultaneous storage and processing, whereas updating requires revising the contents of working memory in response to incoming information while other information is retained. Storage-capacity measures such as forward digit or spatial span and manipulation measures such as backward span therefore represent different processes; combining forward and backward scores conflates capacity and manipulation.[75][76][77]

A clinical study of 48 children aged 8–15 administered WISC-IV and NEPSY-II concurrently. It operationalized working memory as the WISC-IV WMI, based on Digit Span and Letter–Number Sequencing, while selecting NEPSY-II Visual Attention, Auditory Attention, and Inhibition as task-level attention/executive measures. The ADHD, anxiety-disorder, and comorbid groups did not differ on WMI or on the reported NEPSY-II attention measures; across all participants, NEPSY-II Auditory Attention omission errors correlated negatively with WMI (r=−0.322).[69][7]

Other pediatric studies demonstrate further variation in deficit thresholds and measures. In an extremely-low-gestational-age cohort assessed at age 10, verbal working memory was measured with DAS-II Recall of Digits Backward and Recall of Sequential Order, and low performance in the risk models was defined as a normative Z-score ≤−1. Children classified with ADHD symptoms had twice the frequency of DAS-II working-memory scores ≤−2, while the corresponding multipliers for NEPSY-II attention, inhibition, shifting, and sorting measures ranged from 1.4 to 1.6.[78]

Within WISC-V, Digit Span comprises forward, backward, and sequencing trials; Picture Span assesses temporary maintenance and selection of visual information. Thus, low WMI, low Digit Span, poor backward or sequencing performance, a low AWMI, and low visual working-memory performance are distinct operational definitions and should not be collapsed.

Additional NEPSY-II tasks with variable working-memory demands include Auditory Attention and Response Set, Inhibition, Design Fluency, Animal Sorting, Memory for Designs, and Narrative Memory. Their scores also reflect selective or sustained attention, response control, switching, generativity, strategy, learning, or recall rather than a common standardized working-memory construct.

NEPSY-II List Memory is an age-appropriate verbal-memory task for children aged 8–12; like Word List Interference, it should not automatically be equated with a WISC-V working-memory score.[118][18]

NEPSY-II working-memory attribution is similarly task dependent. One study designated Memory for Designs as its NEPSY-II working-memory measure, whereas a NEPSY profile study treated working-memory difficulty as one of several possible explanations for low Tower and Knock and Tap performance, alongside planning, strategy formation, monitoring, inhibition, mental control, and problem solving.[79][62]

Within Digit Span, Forward primarily indexes capacity, whereas Backward and Sequencing involve both capacity and mental manipulation.[80]

### How was sensitivity defined in the studies used to answer

Diagnostic-accuracy sensitivity is the proportion of people who have a target condition according to an independent clinical reference standard and receive a positive index-test result. It therefore requires three prespecified elements: a target condition, a reference-standard classification of its presence or absence, and a positive test-score threshold.[81][65][82] For the present question, the target condition would be working-memory deficit—not ADHD—and a WISC-V or NEPSY-II score would need a defined positive cut-off.

Several superficially similar statistics do not meet that definition. Kofler and colleagues reported 89.1% sensitivity and 80.3% specificity when children were classified as impaired on at least one of three executive-function components; a related 10th-percentile analysis classified 50 of 55 ADHD cases as impaired on at least one component, yielding sensitivity of 90.91% and specificity of 75.31%.[39] These figures concern classification of ADHD cases using a multi-domain experimental executive-function battery, not detection of independently established working-memory impairment and not WISC-V versus NEPSY-II.

Likewise, WISC-V logistic-regression models that correctly classified 75.5% or 76.6% of ADHD and control cases report overall classification accuracy, not true-positive sensitivity.[34] A WISC-V discrepancy pattern also appeared about twice as often in an ADHD sample as in standardization data, yet roughly half of ADHD participants lacked it and one fifth to one third of normative participants showed it; the authors therefore characterized its sensitivity as limited and could not compute formal specificity or predictive values.[9] Effect sizes, mean-score differences, correlations with symptoms, and percentages impaired are informative but answer different questions from sensitivity.[37][35]

Sensitivity and specificity are also conditional estimates, not immutable attributes of a test. They vary with the clinical spectrum, setting, score threshold, prior testing pathway, and definition of the reference standard.[81][65][82][83] **Therefore, reported ADHD classification, group separation, and impairment prevalence cannot be converted into a ranking of WISC-V and NEPSY-II sensitivity for working-memory deficits.**

A valid comparative design must avoid incorporation bias: WISC-V performance cannot define the reference working-memory deficit when WISC-V is the index test, and the same applies to a NEPSY-II score used as its own reference. It should report 2×2 results or sensitivity and confidence intervals, specificity, prospectively specified thresholds, medication status, ADHD presentation, intellectual and language level, comorbidity, and demographic characteristics.

### Which test has better sensitivity for working-memory deficits in children

Neither WISC-V nor NEPSY-II has demonstrated superior diagnostic sensitivity for working-memory deficits in children aged 8–12. The direct answer is no sensitivity ranking: the relevant studies did not administer both instruments to an 8–12 sample, set comparable positive thresholds, and evaluate each result against an independent working-memory-deficit reference standard.[65][84] The strongest available evidence instead supports complementary descriptive roles: WISC-V provides a primary Working Memory Index (WMI) from Digit Span and Picture Span, and an ancillary Auditory Working Memory Index (AWMI) from Digit Span and Letter–Number Sequencing; NEPSY-II offers process-relevant subtests such as verbal recall after interference.[12][18][74]

Indirect evidence does not rescue a ranking. WISC-V studies show lower group-level working-memory performance in some ADHD samples: one 2026 study of 188 children aged 6–16 reported mean WMI scores of 101.63 in controls and 90.03 in the ADHD group.[34] Another ADHD-only study of 241 participants aged 6–17 found WMI mean=90.9 (SD=11.9), lower than verbal comprehension, visual-spatial, and fluid-reasoning means.[9] Yet the special-group findings differed across WISC-V indicators, with non-significant WMI but significant AWMI and Letter–Number Sequencing differences.[74] These results support modality- and task-dependent ADHD-group differences, not individual-level sensitivity.

A 2023 meta-analysis of WISC-V performance estimated pooled ADHD-group means of 93.7 WMI points and 92.3 PSI points, compared with the normative mean of 100 points.[68]

The same 2023 WISC-V meta-analysis found no significant within-ADHD-group difference between Fluid Reasoning and any other WISC-V index.[68]

NEPSY-II’s manual describes subtest-level scoring as more clinically sensitive than domain scores, but this is a rationale for score reporting rather than a diagnostic-accuracy estimate.[18] One mixed-age regression study reported that WISC-V WMI predicted ADHD on its own less strongly than NEPSY-II and CPT measures considered together, but that analysis neither defined a working-memory deficit nor calculated sensitivity; its comparison also included computerized attention tests.[4] It cannot establish a NEPSY-II advantage for working-memory detection.

The underlying ADHD phenotype is itself heterogeneous. In Fosco et al.’s youth sample, ADHD was associated with large reordering impairment (d=1.34), medium updating impairment (d=.64), and no supported group deficit in dual-processing; 67% had impairment in at least one component, while 42% met the impairment criterion specifically for reordering and another 33% were below average in that domain.[37] Reported prevalence estimates range from approximately 30% to 81%–84%, with other estimates of 62% and approximately 85%, consistent with differing task demands and impairment rules.[37] **The clinically meaningful conclusion is that a global WISC-V index or a single NEPSY-II subtest can miss different working-memory phenotypes; neither should be treated as a validated stand-alone detector of working-memory deficit or ADHD.**[68][85][35][1][9]

In Fosco and colleagues’ study of 45 children with ADHD and 41 without ADHD aged 8–13 years, the dual-processing group difference was Cohen’s *d* = 0.37, with BF01 = 1.33.[37]

In the same study, 75% of children with ADHD were below average or impaired on reordering, and none performed above average or better relative to the non-ADHD group.[37]

In the WISC-V digital special-group comparison, the ADHD–control separation on the Auditory Working Memory Index was 1.83 times the separation on the primary Working Memory Index (10.6 versus 5.8 index-score points). This modality-dependent contrast supports interpreting WISC-V working-memory findings by indicator rather than assuming that one composite captures a uniform deficit.[74]

The available jointly administered WISC-IV–NEPSY-II study provides no contrary ranking for the requested age range: in 48 clinically referred children aged 8–15, the WISC-IV WMI and the selected NEPSY-II attention/executive scores showed no diagnostic-group differences, and the study reported correlations and group-mean tests rather than sensitivity estimates.[69][86]

Martinussen, Hayden, Hogg-Johnson, and Tannock’s 2005 meta-analysis is foundational evidence that children with ADHD show working-memory weakness on average, particularly on demanding verbal and visuospatial paradigms, but it is not a WISC-V-versus-NEPSY-II sensitivity comparison. Willcutt, Doyle, Nigg, Faraone, and Pennington’s 2005 meta-analysis similarly established executive-function heterogeneity in ADHD rather than a universal working-memory deficit.

### Synthesis

Working-memory deficits were defined inconsistently across the relevant literature: WISC-V studies usually used continuous WMI/AWMI performance, NEPSY-II work used heterogeneous subtests with overlapping demands, and component-process studies used explicit impairment thresholds on purpose-designed reordering, updating, and dual-processing composites.[74][18][37] Sensitivity has a stricter meaning than ADHD-group score differences or classification accuracy: it requires a reference-defined deficit and a prespecified positive threshold.[81][65][82] No head-to-head 8–12-year-old study has supplied those elements for WISC-V and NEPSY-II. The defensible conclusion is therefore no demonstrated winner, alongside a practical direction: assess working memory by modality and executive demand, and interpret WISC-V and NEPSY-II findings as complementary evidence rather than interchangeable indicators.[37][35][84]

## Ecological validity and pediatric neuropsychologist reviews

Ecological validity has two principal dimensions: representativeness, the correspondence between an assessment’s form and context and natural situations, and generalization, the extent to which test performance predicts behavior in natural environments.[54] It therefore matters clinically because a child can show adequate performance under one-to-one examiner structure while struggling to initiate, organize, sustain attention, or manage working-memory demands in ordinary environments.

These reviews support a contextual interpretation of both batteries, but do not provide a cross-test ecological-validity hierarchy. Olson, Jacobson, and Van Oot (2013) frame ecological validity as necessary because neuropsychologists are asked both to identify cognitive deficits and to determine their effects on everyday functioning for prescriptive diagnostic and rehabilitation recommendations.[91] Price, Joschko, and Kerns (2003), *The ecological validity of pediatric neuropsychological tests of attention*, is a further pediatric assessment review lead.[54] Ahmad and Warriner’s 2001 *Review of the NEPSY: A developmental neuropsychological assessment* is a NEPSY-specific review lead, while Anderson’s review of executive-function assessment includes a NEPSY-II battery section but no WISC battery section.[92]

The conceptual literature underlying these reviews includes Franzen and Wilhelm’s 1996 account of ecological validity, Wilson (2023, Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology, 'Cognitive Profile in Autism and ADHD: A Meta-Analysis of Performance on the WAIS-IV and WISC-V')’s 1993 question of whether neuropsychological indices predict everyday activities, Burgess et al.[68] (1998, Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society, 'The ecological validity of tests of executive function')’s 2006 case for ecologically valid executive-function measures, and Chaytor, Schmitter-Edgecombe (2003, Neuropsychology Review, 'The Ecological Validity of Neuropsychological Tests: A Review of the Literature on Everyday Cognitive Skills'), and Burr’s 2006 work on improving ecological validity of executive-function assessment.[93][90][54]

### How does the ecological validity of NEPSY-II compare with WISC

NEPSY-II and WISC are standardized, examiner-administered performance assessments. Their scores principally represent what a child can do when goals, pacing, and task rules are externally supplied, rather than typical self-directed functioning across home and school.[48][47][36] NEPSY-II’s manual explicitly directs clinicians to integrate developmental history, test results, behavioral observations, and information from home, school, and relevant professionals because these sources describe functioning across environments more fully than testing alone.[16]

The NEPSY-II manual reports concurrent-validity work involving WISC-IV and ABAS-II, but the cited material supplies neither ecological-outcome coefficients nor a WISC-V comparison.[18]

WISC-V administration likewise occurs in a quiet, structured, one-to-one setting. An ADHD-focused rapid review notes that this setting differs from everyday environments containing competing attentional demands.[1] That difference has clinical meaning: better performance in structured testing may indicate that external structure and support ameliorate difficulties, rather than that the child has no difficulty outside the testing room.[48][47]

The broader executive-function literature does not justify treating informant ratings as a simple ecological-validity gold standard. Toplak, West, and Stanovich’s 2013 practitioner review characterizes performance tests as measures of processing efficiency and ratings as measures of success in goal pursuit.[48] Soto and colleagues found that executive-function performance-test and rating composites correlated at r = .30 and shared 9% of variance, whereas academic performance tests and ratings correlated at r = .63 and shared 40% of variance.[36] They further found that both methods uniquely predicted academic outcomes, while performance tests showed stronger cross-method prediction and ratings did not outperform tests for academic ratings.[36]

**Neither NEPSY-II nor WISC can presently be credited with superior ecological validity.** NEPSY-II’s explicit integration with contextual data and the WISC-V concern about structured administration are clinically useful interpretive points, but neither is a comparative demonstration that scores from one battery better predict everyday pediatric outcomes.[16][1]

Ecological validity is a predictive claim, not a claim that a task merely looks realistic. The field’s general conclusion is moderate real-world prediction for neuropsychological measures, especially when the functional outcome matches the tested cognitive domain.[87] Both NEPSY-II and WISC should consequently be interpreted as structured-performance data and combined with parent and teacher reports, interviews, observations, and domain-matched functional outcomes.[16][59] Ratings may better sample typical behaviour over weeks or months, whereas test performance identifies efficiency under standardized support; their modest convergence shows that they are complementary rather than interchangeable.[36]

### Which seminal papers established the field relevant to this comparison?

The foundational work did not begin with WISC-V or NEPSY-II. It began with competing accounts of ADHD as a disorder involving behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, executive functions, motivation, timing, and heterogenous developmental pathways. Barkley (1997, Psychological Bulletin, 'Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD.')’s 1997 *Psychological Bulletin* review, 《Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD》, provided an influential inhibition-centered theoretical account linking impaired behavioral inhibition to broader executive control.[31][32] Willcutt, Doyle, Nigg, Faraone, and Pennington’s 2005 *Biological Psychiatry* meta-analysis, 《Validity of the executive function theory of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a meta-analytic review》, synthesized 83 studies comprising 3,734 ADHD participants and 2,969 non-ADHD participants.[33] These works established executive dysfunction as a serious explanatory framework, while subsequent accounts emphasized that executive dysfunction is neither universal nor necessarily singular in ADHD.[35]

The working-memory lineage is especially important for interpretation of WISC-V WMI and NEPSY-II memory/executive scores. Martinussen, Hayden, Hogg-Johnson, and Tannock’s 2005 *Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry* paper, 《A Meta-Analysis of Working Memory Impairments in Children With Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder》, used exploratory meta-analytic procedures and included 26 empirical studies published from 1997 through December 2003.[101] Martinussen and Tannock (2006, Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology, 'Working Memory Impairments in Children with Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder With and Without Comorbid Language Learning Disorders')’s 2006 study, 《Working Memory Impairments in Children with Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder With and Without Comorbid Language Learning Disorders》, placed ADHD working-memory difficulty in the context of language-learning comorbidity.[102] Together, these papers made working memory a central but conditional target for ADHD neuropsychological research.

The WISC lineage began with profile and discrepancy approaches. Prifitera and Dersh’s 1992 article, 《Base rates of WISC–III diagnostic subtest patterns among normal, learning-disabled, and ADHD samples》, documented WISC-III pattern base rates across normal, learning-disabled, and ADHD groups.[103] Anastopoulos, Spisto, and Maher’s 1994 *Psychological Assessment* study, 《The WISC-III Freedom from Distractibility factor: Its utility in identifying children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder》, tested a precursor of later working-memory profile reasoning.[34] Snow and Sapp’s 2000 WISC-III study examined SCAD, Bannatyne, and ACID patterns and reported congruence between its ADHD sample and the WISC-III manual ADHD sample.[104] These papers established the historical rationale for inspecting low digit-span, arithmetic, coding, or related patterns in ADHD evaluations.[104]

The earlier-edition NEPSY literature followed a different route. The U.S. NEPSY was published in 1998 with five domains, 27 subtests, and an age range from 3 years, 0 months to 12 years, 11 months; NEPSY-II was published in the U.S. in 2007, adding and deleting subtests, removing total domain index scores, adding Social Perception, expanding floor and ceiling ranges, and extending the age range to 16 years, 11 months.[17] Schmitt and Wodrich’s 2004 *Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology* study, 《Validation of a Developmental Neuropsychological Assessment (NEPSY) through comparison of neurological, scholastic concerns, and control groups》, reported group differences on four of five NEPSY domains and described its findings as preliminary validity support for several NEPSY indices.[60] The NEPSY-II development program subsequently conducted ten special-group studies, including children with ADHD, as initial clinical-validity work.[60][18] It also collected normative data from 1,200 children aged 3–16 years during 2005–2006, stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, parent education, and geographic region.[19] The NEPSY-II deliberately removed total domain scores in favor of subtest, process, contrast, and behavioral-observation scores.[19]

The NEPSY-II manual identifies concurrent-validity studies involving WISC-IV, adaptive-behaviour measures, attention ratings, and other neuropsychological instruments, but the cited manual chapter provides neither ADHD-group sensitivity and specificity estimates nor coefficients relating NEPSY-II scores to adaptive outcomes.[18]

The inhibition lineage runs through the Stroop conflict paradigm. Panvino et al. identify Stroop’s 1935 paradigm as the basis for the NEPSY-II Inhibition task: an automatic response must be suppressed to produce a conflicting response.[6] That lineage supports use of specific NEPSY-II subtests to characterize attention, inhibition, switching, and error patterns; it does not make the NEPSY-II a single ADHD diagnostic marker.

**The seminal literature establishes ADHD-related executive-function and working-memory constructs, but it does not establish diagnostic superiority of WISC-V or NEPSY-II.** The practical consequence is that modern battery interpretation should retain the distinction between construct-level theory, observed cognitive profile, and categorical diagnosis.[32][33][105][34][60]

Chaytor and Schmitter-Edgecombe (2006, 'Improving the ecological validity of executive functioning assessment')’s 2003 *Neuropsychology Review* article, 《The Ecological Validity of Neuropsychological Tests: A Review of the Literature on Everyday Cognitive Skills》, established a distinct review contribution focused on ecological validity and everyday cognitive skills.[54][90] Toplak, West, and Stanovich’s 2013 practitioner review, 《Do performance-based measures and ratings of executive function assess the same construct?》, established a separate interpretive framework: performance-based measures use standardized examiner administration and generally assess accuracy or response time, whereas ratings ask informants about difficulties carrying out everyday tasks.[48] The review characterized performance measures as information about processing efficiency and ratings as information about success in rational goal pursuit.[48] Across its 20 studies, 68 of 286 relevant correlations were statistically significant and the overall median correlation was .19.[48]

Nigg’s 2005 review, 《Neuropsychologic theory and findings in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: the state of the field and salient challenges for the coming decade》, articulated a multiprocess account in which response inhibition, delay aversion/reward processing, timing, working memory, arousal, and executive control are separable candidate mechanisms. This framework precludes treating one low cognitive-test score as the disorder itself.

Sbordone’s 1996 work and Chaytor and Schmitter-Edgecombe’s 2003 review distinguish verisimilitude, the surface resemblance of a task to ordinary activity, from veridicality, its empirical prediction of real-world functioning. A task that appears more naturalistic therefore does not thereby have greater ecological validity.

### Which recent publications show the current state of the art?

Current WISC-V research has moved from profile description toward psychometric structure, measurement invariance, and clinically usable predictive models. Becker et al. tested the German WISC-V second-order five-factor model in 91 ADHD participants and 91 matched controls.[15] The model showed configural and metric invariance, but full scalar invariance failed; partial scalar invariance required freeing the Vocabulary, Digit Span, Picture Span, Coding, Symbol Search, Working Memory, and Processing Speed intercepts.[15] The authors therefore advise substantial caution in primary-index interpretation for children and adolescents with ADHD.[15]

Becker et al. also found that WISC-V general intelligence was more precisely measured than the primary cognitive domains. The second-order factor had omega hierarchical .816 and construct replicability H=.860, whereas first-order factor omega-hierarchical-subscale coefficients ranged from .155 to .181 and no first-order factor met the stated construct-replicability threshold.[15] This is a material current-state result: a WISC-V index may be clinically descriptive, but its unique variance should not automatically be treated as a precise estimate of a separable cognitive mechanism.

Lavigne-Cerván and colleagues’ 2026 clinical ADHD study similarly found WMI mean 90.9, SD 11.9, and PSI mean 90.8, SD 12.8, both lower than VCI 101.4, VSI 99.3, and FRI 100.0.[9] Its preferred structural model was a constrained four-factor higher-order model, whereas bifactor solutions were improper.[9] After general ability was accounted for, the orthogonalized Working Memory factor showed omega hierarchical subscale .096 and H=.117.[9] The study reported that WISC-V discrepancy patterns were about twice as common in its ADHD sample as in WISC-V Spain standardization data, but approximately half of the ADHD sample lacked the pattern and 20%–33% of the normative sample showed it.[9] The observed discrepancy pattern is therefore associated with ADHD at group level rather than a usable individual diagnostic signature.[9]

In the 2026 Spanish ADHD sample, the within-person differences between WMI and VCI, VSI, and FRI had effect sizes of d = 0.85, 0.68, and 0.73, respectively.[9] After children with diagnosed specific learning disorder were excluded, mean WMI was 90.7 points and the relevant discrepancy effects ranged from d = 0.77 to 0.96.[9]

The 2026 *Actas Españolas de Psiquiatría* study represents a second current direction: multivariable prediction from WISC-V scores. It reported WMI means of 101.63 in controls and 90.03 in ADHD participants, and PSI means of 99.36 and 89.64, respectively.[34] Binary logistic-regression coefficients were −0.067 for WMI, −0.043 for PSI, and −0.091 for CPI. Its reported correct-classification percentages were 75.5% from primary indices and 76.6% from composite indices.[58][34] Classification accuracy combines true positives and true negatives and cannot be read as sensitivity without the underlying diagnostic cross-tabulation.

Mohammadi Rafat Panah et al.’s 2025 psychometric study compared 250 ADHD students in elementary grades one through three during the 2022 academic year with 250 non-ADHD normative participants.[95] Its abstract states that processing speed was the only WISC-V subscale demonstrating diagnostic validity and that working memory, visual-spatial ability, fluid reasoning, and verbal comprehension did not adequately distinguish ADHD from non-ADHD students.[95] The displayed table nevertheless labels Digit Span Forward, Digit Span Backward, Digit Span Consecutive, Picture Span, and Letter-Number Sequence as having diagnostic validity, but gives no p-values for those rows; it labels Coding, Symbol Search, Random Cancellation, and Structural Cancellation as having diagnostic validity with p=0.001.[95]

Current NEPSY-II research is more often subtest-specific than battery-wide. Panvino et al.’s 2025 study selected NEPSY-II Visual Attention, Auditory Attention, and Inhibition tasks during analysis rather than prospectively.[6] It found clinically relevant inhibition-related differences among ADHD, anxiety, and comorbid groups, but the small final sample of 48 participants aged 8–15 and retrospective design shaped its inference.[6] In another recent clinical study, Romero-Ayuso et al.[6] (2024, Children, 'Enhancing Ecological Validity: Virtual Reality Assessment of Executive Functioning in Children and Adolescents with ADHD') used NEPSY-II Auditory Attention and Cognitive Flexibility alongside Digit Span, Stroop, Trail Making, Zoo Map, and SmartAction-VR. ADHD participants showed more NEPSY-II flexibility errors, while neurotypical participants scored higher on Digit Span forward and backward.[106][107] Those findings show group differences across distinct measures rather than a comparative test-battery result.

Recent ecological-validity work increasingly uses virtual reality rather than conventional psychometric batteries. Romero-Ayuso et al.’s 2024 sample included 76 participants aged 9–17 years, 40 with ADHD and 36 neurotypical participants.[106] The ADHD group had lower SmartAction-VR accuracy and more total errors, commissions, new actions, and forgotten actions; more forgotten actions correlated with lower daily-life independence, r=−.281, p=.024.[106] Seesjärvi et al.’s 2023 EPELI work is explicitly framed as virtual-reality assessment of goal-directed behavior in real-life contexts.[106][107] This frontier evaluates whether task performance predicts meaningful everyday functioning, rather than merely whether a task appears realistic.

The most recent broad WISC synthesis is Lavigne-Cerván et al.’s review, 《Thirty years of ADHD and WISC in scientific research: A systematic review of cognitive profile and index discrepancies》. The journal issue is dated January 2026, although another record identifies it as 2025.[2] It analyzed 27 studies and 6,486 participants through September 2024, but its inclusion criteria restricted the instrument evidence to WISC-III and WISC-IV. The current state of the art is therefore psychometrically sophisticated for WISC-V and clinically active for NEPSY-II subtests, yet still not a comparative WISC-V–NEPSY-II diagnostic-accuracy literature.[26][2]

In the 2026 WISC-V regression study, the composite-index model’s reported ADHD-control classification accuracy exceeded that of the primary-index model by 1.1 percentage points (76.6% versus 75.5%).[34]

WISC-V was published in 2014; its Working Memory Index includes Digit Span and Picture Span. Findings from WISC-IV and earlier editions remain contextual evidence rather than quantitative evidence about WISC-V.

Contemporary ADHD assessment is multimethod and criterion-based: diagnosis requires developmental symptom history, impairment across settings, and multi-informant evidence. WISC-V and NEPSY-II are adjuncts to clinical formulation and differential assessment rather than stand-alone ADHD diagnostic tests.

### What is the methodological rigor of the key studies?

Diagnostic accuracy requires a prespecified target condition, a reference standard for determining whether that condition is present, a positive-test threshold, and a comparison of index-test classifications against the reference standard. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the target condition who test positive.[65] STARD further requires cross-tabulation or distributions of index-test results by reference-standard status, accuracy estimates with precision such as 95% confidence intervals, methods for missing and indeterminate data, and participant-flow reporting.[81] These requirements distinguish diagnostic accuracy from a significant group mean difference or a regression coefficient.[81]

The strongest WISC-V studies use appropriate designs for their stated psychometric questions. Becker et al. used matched ADHD and control groups and multigroup confirmatory factor analysis to test measurement invariance.[15] Its limitation for diagnosis is conceptual rather than procedural: measurement invariance evaluates whether the score model operates comparably across groups, not whether a cut score identifies ADHD. The high learning-disorder comorbidity in the ADHD sample, 63 of 91 participants or 69.2%, also makes the findings clinically representative but not specific to ADHD without learning disorder.[15]

The 2026 WISC-V clinical-profile study used 241 recently diagnosed ADHD participants without a comparison group.[9] It compared observed discrepancy frequencies with WISC-V Spain standardization-base rates, explicitly characterizing the comparison as descriptive and not computing formal diagnostic-accuracy indices.[9] Its use of confirmatory factor analysis, predefined model-fit criteria, AIC, residual inspection, and rejection of improper bifactor solutions strengthens its structural conclusions.[9] Its absence of a neurotypical or alternative clinical comparison group means that the observed WMI and PSI profile cannot be attributed specifically to ADHD.[9]

The 241-participant WISC-V ADHD sample was recruited through public child mental-health clinics; it retained participants with specific learning disorder (21.2%), excluded other comorbidities, and assessed 94.6% while unmedicated.[9]

The 2026 WISC-V regression study adjusted group comparisons for age and sex, tested normality, linearity, and variance homogeneity, and used MANCOVA/ANCOVA plus forward stepwise Wald logistic regression.[34] It reported classification percentages and significant WMI, PSI, and CPI regression coefficients.[58] It did not report threshold-specific sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, confidence intervals, or external validation; its classification percentages therefore provide a model-level summary rather than an interpretable diagnostic test property.[34]

The Spanish clinical study reviewed approximately 502 reports from a private-clinic convenience sample collected between September 2023 and December 2024; it excluded 276 cases for comorbidity and 38 for age, leaving 188 participants for analysis.[34]

The 188-participant Spanish WISC-V study recruited convenience samples from one private clinic and excluded 276 of approximately 502 reviewed cases because of potentially compromising comorbidity; the reported account does not specify whether diagnostic decisions were blinded to WISC-V findings.[34]

The 2025 WISC-V diagnostic-validity study has a 500-person ADHD and normative-comparison sample drawn from first-, second-, and third-grade students.[108] It also reports test–retest procedures with a 2–4-week interval.[108][85] However, it identifies no ROC-derived threshold and acknowledges lack of software to define an optimal cut point; its internal inconsistency on working-memory results prevents a stable inference about working-memory diagnostic validity.[85]

The Tehran WISC-V study’s reported finding that processing speed distinguished the 250 students with ADHD aged 7–9 years from 250 non-ADHD participants, while working memory and the other named cognitive domains did not, came from a confidence-interval-based validity procedure rather than reported sensitivity, specificity, ROC curves, or area-under-the-curve estimates.[85][95]

The NEPSY-II ADHD literature supplies clinically informative group contrasts but less direct classification evidence. In Elsheikh et al. (2017, Psychology, 'Neuropsychological Performance of Egyptian Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder')’s 2017 study of Egyptian children aged 6–12, the ADHD group comprised 37 children and the typical-development group 29 children.[70] Children with ADHD scored below typically developing children on Attention and Executive Function, Language, and Memory and Learning.[70] Children with ASD and ASD-plus-ADHD scored below the ADHD group on all other NEPSY domains except visuospatial processing, while ASD and ASD-plus-ADHD groups did not differ significantly on NEPSY. NEPSY differences therefore identify broad neuropsychological burden, but the observed profile does not uniquely separate ADHD from autism-spectrum presentations.[70]

Panvino et al.’s NEPSY-II study uses clinically relevant ADHD, anxiety, and comorbid comparison groups and excluded current psychotropic medication at assessment.[6] It recruited 71 referrals but analyzed 48 participants after applying IQ, language, symptom, and comorbidity criteria.[6] Its retrospective design, sample size, and post hoc subtest selection mean that its inhibition findings are best understood as a focused clinical-profile result.[6]

The Newberry dissertation has the advantage of administering WISC-V and NEPSY-II to the same 272 clinically evaluated children, thereby avoiding the cross-study instrument differences that undermine indirect comparison.[4] Its diagnostic groups were based on prior clinical evaluations, nearly all participants had a previous medical or psychological diagnosis, and 94.9% had comorbidity.[4] Planned adjustment for comorbidity was not performed because comorbidity had too little variability.[4] It also found floor concentration on NEPSY-II Auditory Attention and Response Set Total Correct, where many children received the minimum score of 1.[4] These properties make it a useful real-world clinical-record analysis, but not a clean estimate of ADHD-specific comparative sensitivity.

Newberry’s 2025 archival sample comprised 129 children with inattentive ADHD, 110 with combined ADHD, one with hyperactive ADHD, and 32 without ADHD; their mean age was 10.7 years.[4]

Newberry described the records as coming from a single clinician: 88.2% of participants received an ADHD diagnosis from that clinician, and 94.9% had a prior medical or psychological diagnosis.[4]

A separate outpatient dissertation examined 597 consecutive assessments of children aged 6–18 years conducted from 2010 to 2019; 297 children (49.8%) received an ADHD diagnosis.[67] Among those diagnosed with ADHD, 69.0% had at least one comorbidity, most commonly specific learning disorder, oppositional defiant disorder, or major depressive disorder.[67]

The field’s strongest working-memory methodology increasingly avoids assigning a single conventional score to a pure working-memory construct. Fosco, Kofler, Groves, Chan, and Raiker’s 2020 study enrolled 45 ADHD and 41 non-ADHD children aged 8–13 and defined impairment as a score at or below the 10th percentile of the non-ADHD distribution, z≤−1.28.[37] ADHD diagnosis required a DSM-5 diagnosis based on K-SADS, elevated parent and teacher ratings, and current impairment.[37] The study found group deficits in working-memory reordering and updating, not dual processing; 67% of ADHD participants were impaired on at least one component.[37] Its component analyses used a prespecified three-component principal-components solution and reported preregistration, data availability, and an explicit limitation that impairment relative to a comparison group does not demonstrate daily-life impairment.[37]

Kofler and colleagues’ 2019 heterogeneity study strengthens this approach further. It assessed 136 children aged 8–13, including 55 with ADHD, used multiple tasks per executive-function construct, and reduced 36 task variables to three component estimates.[39] Its Reliable Change Index impairment rule classified a child as impaired only when performance was reliably below the non-ADHD distribution at p<.05, accounting for test–retest reliability and comparison-group variability.[39] Eighty-nine percent of ADHD participants were impaired on at least one executive-function component, and sensitivity analyses controlling age, gender, and SES produced sensitivity 81.82% and specificity 80.25%.[39] These are sensitivity estimates for a multi-task executive-function impairment classification relative to ADHD case status, not estimates for WISC-V or NEPSY-II working-memory scores.[39]

Wells, Kofler, Soto, Schaefer, and Sarver’s 2018 study illustrates why conventional span scores require careful interpretation. In 34 children with ADHD aged 8–13, traditional WISC-IV Digit Span Backward did not predict working memory or achievement and corresponded more strongly with fluid reasoning.[38] Removing the discontinue rule and using modified scoring reduced the fluid-reasoning association from r=.49 to r=.15 and increased associations with latent reordering/dual-processing working-memory estimates to r=.53–.58 and academic achievement to r=.49.[35] The authors nevertheless stated that the lack of normative data limited immediate clinical use of the modified method.[38]

**The key methodological conclusion is that WISC-V and NEPSY-II studies predominantly demonstrate profile differences, score structure, or prediction of pre-existing diagnosis, whereas working-memory component studies use multiple cognitively informed tasks and independent clinical case definitions.** This rules out converting low WMI, low NEPSY-II scores, or WISC–NEPSY profile scatter directly into a diagnosis or into a comparative sensitivity claim.[58][53][39][35]

A direct comparison would require the same consecutively or representatively referred 8–12-year-old children to complete both instruments under standardized conditions, with ADHD diagnosis determined independently of the test results. It would define working-memory impairment against an independent latent-variable or functional criterion and compare paired ROC curves or other paired accuracy metrics while accounting for score dependence and multiplicity across subtests.

Diagnostic studies should report structured or semi-structured ADHD ascertainment, multi-informant ratings and impairment, medication status, and comorbid learning, language, autism-spectrum, anxiety, mood, sleep, and intellectual conditions. They should also avoid criterion contamination, in which neuropsychological findings contribute to the clinical diagnosis against which those same findings are evaluated, and avoid overgeneralizing from clean ADHD-versus-typically-developing comparisons to heterogeneous clinical referrals.

### Synthesis

The foundational literature makes working memory, inhibition, and executive-function heterogeneity central to ADHD assessment, and the contemporary literature provides increasingly sophisticated methods for evaluating WISC-V score structure and multi-task working-memory constructs.[32] WISC-V studies consistently report lower average WMI and PSI in ADHD groups, but profile discrepancies overlap extensively with normative patterns and primary-index interpretation is constrained by incomplete scalar invariance and limited reliable unique variance.[33][15][39][9] NEPSY-II studies identify ADHD-related weaknesses in attention, inhibition, flexibility, language, and memory, but their group effects are not ADHD-specific classification evidence.[9][15][70] The direct comparative signal comes only from the 8–16 archival dissertation, in which selected NEPSY-II measures outperformed WMI in joint predictive models without reporting sensitivity.[70][4] The appropriate conclusion is complementarity in clinical formulation, not demonstrated superiority for ADHD diagnosis or working-memory-deficit detection in children aged 8–12.[4]

## Conflicting evidence, influence, and research gaps

### Which findings conflict?

Judgment — The central disagreement concerns what counts as a working-memory deficit and what a neuropsychological score is expected to do: characterize a cognitive weakness, identify ADHD, or predict impairment in daily life. These are different validation targets, so apparently contradictory findings often answer different questions.

Working-memory impairment estimates in pediatric ADHD range from approximately 30% in earlier studies to 62%–85% in later multi-task, latent-variable, or component-based studies; a meta-regression estimated 81%–84% clinically significant impairment and 98% below the mean of typically developing children on measures with high executive working-memory demands. The higher estimates emerged when studies separated executive working-memory processes from short-term storage and used multiple indicators rather than observed scores from single tasks.[37][39][35]

WISC findings are also non-uniform. A systematic review found the familiar relatively lower working-memory/processing-speed pattern in 21 of 28 study-data blocks (75%; n = 4,680), but seven blocks (25%; n = 551) had discordant profiles. The WISC-V meta-analysis by Wilson (2023, Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology, 'Cognitive Profile in Autism and ADHD: A Meta-Analysis of Performance on the WAIS-IV and WISC-V') and colleagues concluded that ADHD was less associated with a particular cognitive profile, despite slightly lower working-memory scores.[68][2] **The recurring WISC pattern is therefore a group-level association, not an ADHD-specific cognitive signature.** The pattern can overlap with specific learning disorder profiles.[9]

NEPSY results likewise vary by task and clinical comparison. Children with ADHD performed below typically developing children in NEPSY Attention/Executive Function, Language, and Memory/Learning domains in one study. In a subtype study, the ADHD-hyperactive/impulsive group showed lower inhibition-related performance on Knock and Tap, Statue, and CPT commission errors, whereas the ADHD-inattentive group showed weaker Tower planning but not the expected attention-test weakness.[70][62] These results support task- and presentation-specific weaknesses rather than a stable NEPSY diagnostic profile.

A particularly important internal conflict appears in the recent WISC-V diagnostic-validity article. Its abstract states that WISC-V working memory did not adequately distinguish students with and without ADHD, yet its table labels Digit Span Forward, Digit Span Backward, Digit Span Sequencing, Picture Span, and Letter-Number Sequencing as having diagnostic validity. The surrounding narrative then states that these working-memory tests were nonsignificant at alpha = .05.[85] This inconsistency prevents that paper’s working-memory conclusion from serving as a stable diagnostic claim.

Case-control WISC-V results also disagree on whether the Working Memory Index differentiates ADHD. One study reported an ADHD-group WMI of 87, significantly lower than controls but still within the normative range. In a German clinical sample, the WMI difference no longer remained significant after Bonferroni-Holm correction, although Digit Span retained a medium-to-large group effect.[21][15] The clinical utility of WISC index discrepancies remains contested: recent authors reported meaningful WMI/PSI discrepancies, while earlier WISC-IV work judged analogous discrepancies too small to be clinically useful.[9]

In the German matched-control WISC-V study, Digit Span remained lower in the ADHD group after Bonferroni–Holm correction, with Cohen’s d = −0.76, although the WMI difference was no longer significant.[15]

The inhibition literature illustrates a related theoretical conflict. Reviews report that apparent inhibition-task deficits in ADHD may largely reflect working-memory demands, and longitudinal data found stable working-memory-only impairment in 44% of children with ADHD compared with 13% with stable joint working-memory and inhibition impairment and 5% with stable inhibition-only impairment.[35][44] Yet an earlier experiment found that depleted inhibitory resources reduced n-back accuracy, whereas a newer Stroop/complex-span experiment found that elevated inhibition demands did not impair working-memory performance.[44] The newer study attributes the discrepancy partly to the poor correspondence between n-back and complex-span tasks, reported as meta-analytic r = .20.[44]

Ecological and predictive validity findings conflict in a different way. Toplak, West, and Stanovich’s review found only 68 significant correlations among 286 performance-test/rating-scale correlations (24%), with a median correlation of .19.[109][48] Several studies found ratings better predicted functional outcomes, whereas Soto and colleagues reported stronger predictive validity for performance tests and identified mono-method, mono-informant questionnaire outcomes as a key difference in prior comparisons.[36] **The performance-test/rating discrepancy is principally a difference in measurement target, not a simple contest between valid and invalid measures.**[109][49]

Working-memory results conflict across task content and demand. In a purpose-designed pediatric battery, children with ADHD showed large impairment in serial/temporal reordering (d = 1.34), medium impairment in continuous updating (d = 0.64), and generally intact dual-processing working memory; these executive processes are not equivalent to phonological or visual-spatial short-term stores.[35][39]

In Wells et al. (2018), 34 children with ADHD aged 8–13 years (mean age 10.41; 11 girls) had WISC-IV Digit Span Backward scores that failed to predict working-memory deficits, which the review attributed particularly to early application of the discontinue rule.[1]

A study using coloured digit series found that children with ADHD performed significantly worse than controls on simple short-term recall but not on the working-memory condition, demonstrating that a result from a verbal storage task cannot be generalized to executive manipulation.[77]

### What plausibly explains the conflicting evidence?

The strongest explanation is non-equivalent construct operationalisation. Many measures labelled working memory emphasize short-term storage rather than the executive processes of updating, dual processing, or temporal reordering. Kasper and colleagues’ meta-regression increased the estimated ADHD working-memory effect from d = 0.69–0.74 to d = 2.0–2.2 when analyses emphasized measures with executive working-memory demands.[39] Digit Span Backward illustrates the problem: it may show minimal association with working-memory measures while correlating substantially with fluid IQ at r = .49.[38]

Age changes the apparent prevalence of impairment. Karalunas and colleagues classified approximately 85% of children as working-memory impaired at age 7, but 30% of that cohort followed an improving trajectory and no longer met impairment criteria by age 13. A study of children aged 8–13 similarly stated that its older age distribution may have lowered its estimate relative to younger samples.[37] This developmental variation matters directly for the requested 8–12 age range.

ADHD itself is heterogeneous. Executive functioning, reaction-time variability, and emotional functioning each independently distinguished ADHD from controls in one multiple-deficit study.[41] Executive-function classes did not reproduce ADHD, ASD, or comorbid diagnostic categories; instead, classes primarily differed in severity of executive dysfunction.[43] Subtype composition, sex composition, anxiety, learning disorder comorbidity, medication status, IQ exclusions, and whether symptoms are treated categorically or dimensionally can therefore change the profile observed in a given sample.[35][2][15]

Task impurity is the second major explanation. No single task measures only one process: working-memory, inhibition, motor speed, perceptual processing, language, motivation, and test-taking behavior can all influence performance. Multiple tests per construct are needed because most variance in a single test reflects processes other than the nominal executive function.[39][35][44] Consistent with this, WISC-IV WMI correlated only modestly with NEPSY-II Auditory Attention omission errors, r(48) = −.322.[6] **A WISC working-memory index and a NEPSY attention/executive subtest should therefore not be treated as interchangeable indicators of one unitary deficit.**

Analytic decisions also alter sensitivity. In Kofler and colleagues’ pediatric ADHD study, a one-factor executive-function model had 98.77% specificity but only 34.55% sensitivity, whereas separate executive-function components identified much more heterogeneity.[39] Impairment prevalence also depends on cut points: children can fall just above a threshold despite below-average performance, and classification against a non-ADHD group does not itself describe daily functional impact.[39][37]

The ecological-validity conflict follows from the distinction between maximal and typical performance. Performance tests are administered under examiner-structured, optimal conditions and index efficiency of cognitive abilities. Ratings describe goal pursuit in less constrained everyday settings and may reflect both executive abilities and behavioral skills.[47][36] Shared informant and shared method can inflate the apparent relation between ratings and questionnaire-based functional outcomes.[36] **Conflicting functional-validity results can thus arise even when both measures capture clinically useful but distinct aspects of functioning.**

Findings based on earlier WISC editions or the original NEPSY cannot be pooled with WISC-V and NEPSY-II findings: editions differ in subtests, factor structures, norms, and scoring. Willcutt et al. (2005) and Nigg (2005) established that executive-function and working-memory weaknesses occur in important subsets of children with ADHD but are neither necessary nor sufficient features in every child.

### Which work is influential in this literature?

Citation influence was assessed here by the citation counts displayed on source records, while retaining the originating platform because database counts are not interchangeable. OpenAlex constructs citation links by matching extracted reference lists to indexed works, and its field-weighted citation impact normalizes citations by publication year, work type, and subfield over the publication year plus three subsequent years.[110] OpenAlex coverage differs from Web of Science and Scopus because it omits references to works it does not index; a cited comparison reported 586 million references in OpenAlex, 725 million in Web of Science, and 727 million in Scopus.[111]

Barkley (1997, Psychological Bulletin, 'Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD.')’s 1997 theory article, *Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD*, is the citation-dominant foundational work in the supplied literature, with 4,794 APA PsycNet citations.[31][112] Its influence is theoretical: it frames ADHD through behavioral inhibition and self-regulation rather than demonstrating superiority of WISC-V or NEPSY-II.

Korkman, Kirk, and Kemp’s 2010 article, *NEPSY-II: A Developmental Neuropsychological Assessment, Second Edition*, is the most highly cited named-test source identified here, with 246 CrossRef citations and 264 Scilit all-time citations, the latter at the 97.77th percentile.[89][113] The difference between 246 and 264 is a database-metric difference rather than competing evidence about the test.

For pediatric ecological validity, Wallisch, Little, Dean, and Dunn’s 2018 scoping review had 49 Web of Science citations and 50 Crossref citations on the journal page.[88] Schmitter-Edgecombe and colleagues’ broader review displayed 250 citations on Academia.edu.[114] **These reviews are influential for ecological-validity concepts, but neither establishes a direct NEPSY-II-versus-WISC ecological-validity ranking.**

Recent WISC-V ADHD diagnostic-validity papers have limited displayed citation signals. The cited WISC-V diagnostic-validity article showed two Scopus citations by DOI and zero CrossRef, OpenAlex, and OpenCitations citations, while a 2023 WISC-V working-memory article displayed no SciELO citations.[85][3] These metrics identify foundational theory and instrument-development sources as the influential works, rather than recent evidence resolving the named-test comparison.

### What are the research gaps

The principal diagnostic-accuracy gap is a prospective, paired comparison of WISC-V and NEPSY-II in clinically representative 8–12-year-olds. It should use an independently established ADHD reference standard, preregistered score thresholds, and report sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals for each battery and their incremental value when combined. STARD requires clear eligibility criteria, recruitment method, test and reference-standard detail, prespecified cutoffs, participant flow, result distributions, and accuracy estimates with precision.[81][82]

The working-memory question requires a different reference target from ADHD diagnosis. Working-memory sensitivity should be defined as the proportion of children with independently established working-memory impairment who test positive, using a prespecified criterion based on multiple construct-valid measures rather than a single span task. Traditional neuropsychological-test syntheses estimate 30%–37% working-memory impairment in ADHD, whereas cognitively informed approaches estimate 75%–85%; these divergent rates demonstrate that the criterion definition materially determines the apparent sensitivity of any clinical battery.[35] **Neither WISC-V nor NEPSY-II can presently be designated the more sensitive test for working-memory deficits in 8–12-year-olds because the decisive paired, independently anchored comparison has not been performed.** Direct comparative work is explicitly identified as a pediatric ADHD research gap.[4][11]

The ecological-validity gap is equally specific. A direct comparison should test whether WISC-V and NEPSY-II scores predict preregistered outcomes in school functioning, organization, peer and family functioning, and activities of daily living, using teacher, parent, clinician, and performance-based outcomes. Ecological validity requires veridicality—prediction of real-world outcomes—rather than verisimilitude, or superficial resemblance between a task and an everyday activity. Outcome respondent, population, illness severity, and outcome method can moderate ecological-validity estimates, and standardized outcomes are needed for cross-study comparison.[114][87][107][36]

Future studies should model ADHD presentation, medication exposure, learning disorder and anxiety comorbidity, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic conditions, and age within the 8–12 range. They should avoid routine IQ exclusions that can inadvertently exclude children with weak working memory, administer multiple tasks per construct across separate sessions, and model shared and unique executive-function variance.[35] This design directly addresses fatigue, time-of-day effects, task impurity, and the heterogeneity that makes a single test score clinically misleading.[39][35]

1. Use WISC-V and NEPSY-II as complementary descriptive tools, not diagnostic proxies for ADHD. [33][43]
2. Define ADHD diagnostic accuracy and working-memory detection as separate outcomes with separate reference standards. [82]
3. Evaluate ecological validity through prospective prediction of standardized everyday outcomes, not task realism alone. [114][87][36]

The diagnostic-accuracy sample should comprise consecutively referred, clinically characterized 8–12-year-olds across multiple sites and include non-ADHD clinical comparators rather than only typically developing controls. ADHD reference classification should combine DSM-based structured assessment, multi-informant symptoms, developmental history, and documented cross-setting impairment.

Index-test interpretation should be blinded to the ADHD reference-standard result, because readers who have access to clinical information or reference-standard results can produce artificially high agreement between an index test and the reference standard.[115][65]

### Synthesis —

ADHD-related working-memory weakness is robust at the group level, but its estimated prevalence and apparent test sensitivity change sharply with task demands, construct definition, developmental stage, and analytic model. WISC studies recurrently identify relatively lower working memory and processing speed, while NEPSY studies identify task- and presentation-specific executive weaknesses; neither pattern is an ADHD-specific diagnostic signature.[2][68][70][62] The most consequential conflict is therefore not between WISC-V and NEPSY-II, but between treating structured performance scores as diagnostic markers and treating them as partial indicators of heterogeneous cognitive mechanisms.

Ratings add information about everyday goal pursuit, whereas performance tests assess cognitive efficiency under controlled conditions.[109][49][47] The field’s next decisive study is a paired, preregistered comparison in clinically representative children aged 8–12 using independent ADHD and working-memory reference standards alongside longitudinal real-world outcomes.[35][81]

### Open questions

A direct paediatric-neuropsychologist review establishing that NEPSY-II has greater ecological validity than WISC/WISC-V was not identified, and no direct 8–12-year ADHD study establishes a sensitivity or specificity ranking of WISC-V versus NEPSY-II for working-memory deficits. These questions require preregistered, adequately powered head-to-head studies using a prespecified working-memory criterion and independent functional outcomes, with age-stratified ADHD and clinical-comparison samples, medication status, comorbidity, and incremental validity over ratings and diagnostic interview explicitly modelled.
