New Zealand’s structural-steel sector had material spare capacity by 2024, with industry output indicated at about 100,000 tonnes over the preceding 12 months against estimated annual capacity of about 120,000 tonnes.[1] The available evidence supports a fragmented, locally capable construction-steel supply base rather than a quantified 2018–2024 market-revenue trend.[2] C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating recorded 4,250 paid employee jobs in February 2018 and 4,200 in February 2019, a derived 1.2 percent decrease.[3] No defensible annual revenue, employment, or actual-fabricated-tonnage series exists for the narrowly defined construction/structural fabrication market for every year from 2018 to 2024.[3] Fabricators should therefore base capacity, pricing, and pipeline decisions on verified contract workload and company-level production data, not broad construction, metal-manufacturing, or steel-consumption totals.

## Market size and growth

Structural steel fabrication is the off-site conversion of purchased structural steel into members that can be assembled into a building, bridge, or other structure. It is a narrower activity than either the whole construction industry or all metal manufacturing. That distinction matters because a mill’s steel output, a merchant’s imports, an erector’s site work, and a fabricator’s shop output are economically connected but are not the same market measure.

**The appropriate market definition is fabricated load-bearing steelwork for construction and infrastructure, not all steel products or all metalworking.** On that basis, no published Stats NZ or MBIE series in the evidence provides a complete 2018–2024 annual market-size series in revenue, fabricated tonnage, and employment for the requested construction-only market. The closest official industry proxy is ANZSIC06 C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating; it is useful, particularly for employment, but it remains an industry classification rather than a pure end-use measure.[4][3][5]

### revenue NZD

For this study, revenue means the income earned from the fabrication of constructional structural-steel components: preparing steel from structural-engineer drawings, managing material, cutting, drilling, shop fitting by bolts or welds, applying specified paint or galvanising, and shipping the completed members. Steel Construction New Zealand (SCNZ) describes this work as preparing steel for buildings and bridges.[1] The definition includes load-bearing fabricated members and connections for buildings, bridges, and other structures, including ready-made structural parts, girders, joists, rafters, roof trusses, and perforated steel plate where supplied for structural or construction use.[4] Reinforcing mesh and processed reinforcing rod are treated separately as construction-steel demand, rather than as fabricated structural-frame products.[1][4][6]

It excludes several activities that may appear in a company’s accounts but are outside an off-site structural-fabrication market measure:

- **Steelmaking and primary production:** steel mills make the hot-rolled sections, plate, angles, and hollow sections used as fabrication inputs. This is upstream manufacturing, not downstream fabrication. [1] [7]
- **Importing, warehousing, and distribution:** merchants and distributors import construction steel, hold stock, and can undertake limited pre-processing before fabrication. These functions are separate from fabrication. [1] [7]
- **On-site erection:** erectors assemble fabricated members into a structural frame by bolting and field welding. ANZSIC places erection and installation, including on-site fabrication, in Structural Steel Erection Services rather than Structural Steel Fabricating. [4] [8]
- **Non-construction metalwork:** machinery, piping, conveyors, mobile industrial machinery, stainless work for dairy, wine, oil and gas processing, and architectural metalwork are outside the defined market. ANZSIC 1993 Class 2749 covers architectural steel, wrought-iron and other non-aluminium metal products, including window frames, doors, stairs or staircases, railings, gates, balustrades and ornamental partitions; it excludes structural-steel fabrication, which it places in Class 2741. Class 2742 separately covers architectural aluminium products. [9] [10] [10] [11]

This boundary deliberately separates an integrated fabricator’s shop-fabrication activity from its possible design-management and erection roles. SCNZ says fabricators commonly manage erection using their own or subcontracted rigging crews, but that commercial practice does not change the ANZSIC distinction between fabrication and structural-steel erection services.[2][4]

The closest official statistical industry is ANZSIC06 Class C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating, inside Division C Manufacturing, Subdivision 22 Fabricated Metal Product Manufacturing, and Group 222 Structural Metal Product Manufacturing. The class consists of units mainly engaged in fabricating structural-steel components for buildings or other structures.[4] Stats NZ DataInfo+ names Structural Steel Fabricating in the Australian and New Zealand Standard Industrial Classification 2006 code list; that item was last updated on 7 August 2012.[4][5] This makes C2221 the best available official proxy for the defined market, although it can include product types whose final applications are not fully observable in published data.

Stats NZ’s Annual Enterprise Survey (AES) is the relevant official financial-statistics collection. It provides annual financial-performance and financial-position information for New Zealand industry groups using ANZSIC06-based classifications. AES is designed at New Zealand Standard Industrial Output Classification (NZSIOC) level 4, while level 3 is the standard at which annual AES statistics are published. Published AES values exclude GST. The survey’s financial year is not a calendar year: AES 2020, for example, covers enterprise balance dates from 1 October 2019 to 30 September 2020.[12][13]

However, the supplied official material does not provide an AES revenue series for C2221, nor does it establish a published NZSIOC category that exactly matches construction-only structural-steel fabrication. AES itself cautions against analysis below NZSIOC level 4.[12] The broader categories that are available do not solve this problem:[14]

- MBIE’s broad metals-and-metal-products grouping covers ANZSIC C21 and C22. It includes basic metals, smelting, and a wide range of metal products from nuts and bolts to beams, bridge supports, and fabricated metal sculptures. [10]
- MBIE identifies C222 Structural Metal Products as a separate level-3 category, but this is broader than C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating and is not restricted to construction end uses. [10]
- Construction-industry figures measure construction activity and services, while off-site fabrication is classified in manufacturing. [11] [11] [4]

A wider construction series cannot stand in for fabrication revenue. For example, MBIE reported broad building-and-construction-sector real GDP of more than NZ$17.6 billion in the year ended March 2023, while construction employment was nearly 308,500 in the year ended June 2023. Those are sector-wide output and labour measures; they include activities far beyond structural fabrication.[17] Likewise, Stats NZ reported construction-industry salaries and wages rising by NZ$1.45 billion, or 8.5 percent, in the 2023 AES year. That is a construction-industry cost measure, not structural-fabrication sales.[21]

Judgment — For commercial market sizing, the correct revenue measure is fabrication revenue excluding GST and excluding pass-through steel trading where possible. A fabricator’s invoiced contract value can include steel material, detailing, coating, transport, erection management, and site services; it is not automatically comparable with a shop-fabrication-only measure. Where company accounts combine these activities, revenue should be presented as integrated structural-steel-contracting revenue, not as pure fabrication revenue.

AES figures also need a consistent vintage. Stats NZ revised AES 2018 and 2019 values in the 2020 release, revised 2019 and 2020 in the 2021 release, and revised 2021 and 2022 in the 2023 release.[18] AES statistics are produced annually, with the latest three years provisional when released, and Stats NZ notes that changes in measurement, sample design, classification, and collection can affect inter-period movements.[19][21][12] AES financial data are collected from businesses with balance dates from 1 October in one year to 30 September in the following year; AES 2020, for example, covers balance dates from 1 October 2019 to 30 September 2020.[12][16] Revenue growth should therefore be calculated only from the same AES release and expressed as nominal, GST-exclusive change unless an explicit price adjustment is made.

The revenue result is consequential: it rules out a defensible statement that the defined market grew or fell by a particular percentage between 2018 and 2024. The evidence does support construction-cycle context, but not a structural-fabrication revenue series.

Stats NZ’s AES gives a useful but deliberately broader financial comparator.Derived — CC72 total income increased by NZ$1.312 billion, or 16.13%, between 2022 and 2023, calculated as (NZ$9.448 billion minus NZ$8.136 billion) divided by NZ$8.136 billion. This is financial context for the broader fabricated-metal industry, not revenue for construction/structural steel fabrication.[23]

AES applies an economic-significance boundary: it does not select businesses that are not economically significant; qualifying conditions include annual GST expenses or sales above NZ$30,000, more than three paid employees, and annual IR10 income above NZ$40,000. AES records activity through kind-of-activity units (KAUs), which are subdivisions of enterprises with a single set of accounting records; one enterprise can contain multiple KAUs.[24]

The pandemic also affects interpretation of AES financial years. Stats NZ states that COVID-19 had a significant effect on New Zealand business activity in 2020–22; in AES 2022, government-funded COVID-19 wage subsidies are included in government funding, grants and subsidies, while subsidies passed to employees are recorded in salaries and wages.[24]

It also excludes roofing, cladding, light-gauge roll-formed products, fencing, wire, rail, and metalwork for vehicles, ships, rolling stock, appliances and agricultural equipment, unless supplied as an identifiable load-bearing element of a construction structure. Repair work unrelated to construction structures is also outside the market.

For any subsequently identified consistent annual revenue series, year-on-year growth should be calculated as (revenue in year t divided by revenue in year t−1 minus 1) × 100; cumulative 2018–24 change should use (2024 revenue divided by 2018 revenue minus 1) × 100.

A structural-steel estimating guide states that steel prices can vary from day to day and that standard structural-steel estimates are typically valid for 7–10 days.[25]

### employment

Employment is available more directly than revenue through Stats NZ’s Business Demography Statistics. The closest official proxy is the paid employee count for ANZSIC06 C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating. This is a February snapshot of employee jobs in economically significant enterprises, not an annual average, a full-time-equivalent count, or an official employment-level statistic.[3][26][27]

Business Demography employee count is a PAYE-based headcount of people paid at any time during February. It counts multiple jobs separately if one person has more than one employer, includes employees regardless of hours worked, and excludes working owners unless they pay themselves PAYE salary or wages.[27] In other words, it measures paid employment relationships rather than total labour supplied to the industry.[28]

The established C2221 proxy declined from 4,250 paid employee jobs in February 2018 to 4,200 in February 2019: a decrease of 50 employee jobs, or 1.2 percent.[3]

SCNZ gives a broader industry estimate of approximately 5,000 workers in its December 2022, March 2023, June 2023, and December 2024 industry updates.[30] This figure is useful as a scale check: it indicates that the structural-steel industry, including associated activities and workforce arrangements, is materially larger than the 4,200 C2221 paid-employee count in February 2019.[7][2][1] The two should not be treated as conflicting measures of the same population.

SCNZ’s rounded construction-focused workforce estimate was approximately 5,000 workers in each of its 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021 updates; these are workers rather than FTEs or payroll jobs.[99][100][101][102]

Using SCNZ’s rounded estimates of approximately 5,000 workers for each year from 2018 through 2023 yields a derived 0% year-on-year change and 0% cumulative change in that estimate; it does not establish unchanged company payrolls.[99][100][101][102][103][7]

BERL’s 2019 estimate covered 10,670 direct FTEs across eight steel-related industries and 28,290 linked FTEs in upstream, downstream and other sectors, rather than construction-only structural fabrication.[101]

The official series excludes unpaid owners and classifies enterprises by predominant activity, while SCNZ’s estimate is industry-wide and does not specify a reference month, data method, or annual series.[28][27][30]

The distinction between enterprise and geographic-unit employment also matters. An enterprise is the legal business entity; a geographic unit is a particular operating location. A multi-site business may have enterprise-level employment in one industry but individual sites in different industries.[28] For mobile construction work, geographic-unit employees are allocated to the employer’s base, administrative office, or head office rather than the physical construction site.[31][27] This reinforces the case for treating C2221 as a manufacturing-fabrication proxy rather than a measure of all labour used on structural-steel projects.[28]

Business Demography coverage is limited to economically significant enterprises. The principal thresholds include annual GST expenses or sales above NZ$30,000, more than three paid employees on a rolling-mean basis, group membership, or annual IR10 income above NZ$40,000.[12] Stats NZ identifies non-coverage of small enterprises below the economic-significance criteria and partial coverage between the NZ$30,000 sales-subject-to-GST condition and the NZ$60,000 compulsory GST-registration threshold applying from 1 April 2009; it states that this partial coverage cannot be quantified.[3][26] Small fabricators may therefore be partly or wholly outside the series.

This is significant in a market that SCNZ has described as comprising 153 structural-steel fabrication companies nationwide in 2022 and 2023, while its current web material describes approximately 180 companies.[30][7][2][32]

SCNZ reported 86 structural-steel fabricators nationwide in March 2018.[99]

The reported company-count variation should not be read as a measured increase from 153 to 180. The 153 figure is dated in SCNZ industry updates; the approximately-180 figure has no stated reference date or method. Both describe an industry with many firms rather than a small number of national plants. Te Waihanga similarly characterises fabricators as a very large number of companies, typically regionally focused.[32][33]

SCNZ reported 86 fabricators in 2018 and 2019 and 149 in 2022, compared with the 153 reported in 2023. The change from 2018 to 2023 was 67 firms, or 77.9%.[99][100][103][7]

Stats NZ advises caution when using fine-level Business Demography industry data. The Statistical Business Register supports national and aggregate industry statistics but is not designed for high-quality fine-level industry estimates; classifications for medium and smaller enterprises can be difficult to maintain, and back-cast classification data may be imputed.[27] The series is provisional and revised annually, especially at recent endpoints.[26][27] Mergers, takeovers, business restructuring, and changes in predominant activity can move employees between industry series without the same change occurring in the underlying work.[26][28]

The employment result is therefore clear: C2221 provides established February 2018 and February 2019 paid-employee-job proxy observations, but not a complete 2018–2024 series or a unique-person employment count for the defined market.[3][29]

Using the February snapshots now available for all seven years, C2221 paid employee jobs rose from 4,250 in 2018 to 4,450 in 2024: a net increase of 200 jobs (4.7%) and a derived compound annual growth rate of 0.77% over 2018–24.[3]

The path was uneven rather than steadily expansionary: year-on-year C2221 employment changes were -1.2% (2019), +3.6% (2020), -2.3% (2021), +4.7% (2022), +3.4% (2023), and -3.3% (2024).[3]

The current Stats NZ industry-classification presentation labels the closest category C222100 Structural Steel Fabricating within C222 Structural Metal Product Manufacturing. It separately identifies C222200 Prefabricated Metal Building Mfg, C222300 Architectural Aluminium Product Mfg, C222400 Metal Roof and Guttering Mfg, and C222900 Other Structural Metal Product Mfg.[34]

### tonnage fabricated

Fabricated tonnage is the physical quantity of structural steel processed into construction members. It is the most intuitive output measure for a fabrication market, but it requires particular care because tonnes of steel produced, imported, warehoused, committed to projects, fabricated, erected, and reused are different quantities.

SCNZ reports that the New Zealand structural-steel sector turned approximately 100,000 tonnes of structural steel into buildings and bridges during the preceding 12 months in its June 2023 update.[2] SCNZ repeated a circa-100,000-tonne annual-output statement in its November 2024 update.[2][1] Those statements establish a recent sector-scale indicator: approximately 100,000 tonnes were associated with buildings and bridges over the respective preceding-12-month periods.

SCNZ’s preceding-12-month indicator was approximately 100,000 tonnes of structural steel turned into buildings and bridges in each of its 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021 updates. It measures sector throughput through distributors, fabricators and erectors, not tonnes fabricated in a workshop in each calendar year.[99][100][101][102]

They are not an annual series of tonnes fabricated solely by fabricators. SCNZ’s wording covers steel turned into buildings and bridges through the network of distributors, fabricators, and erectors.[2] Thus, it is closer to a construction-steel delivery/output indicator than a narrowly defined shop-fabrication census.

A separate SCNZ estimate places current annual fabrication capacity at 120,000 tonnes.[2] Capacity is the maximum sustainable output under stated operating conditions; it is not actual output. SCNZ says this capacity could be increased through multi-shifting, meaning additional production shifts.[2] Comparing the approximately 100,000-tonne preceding-12-month sector-output indicator with 120,000 tonnes of estimated annual capacity suggests headroom, but it does not establish a utilisation rate because the numerator and denominator have different scope.[2]

SCNZ also stated annual fabrication capacity of 120,000 tonnes in its 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021 updates; these are capacity statements, not actual production figures.[99][100][101][102]

The industry’s capacity estimates are not fully consistent across sources. Sustainable Steel Council states capacity of 80,000 tonnes of structural steel annually, without a reference date.[35] SCNZ’s December 2022, March 2023, June 2023, and November 2024 updates use an estimated current capacity of 120,000 tonnes per annum.[30] Both are capacity statements rather than production data.[7][2][1] The evidence supports the conclusion that capacity was presented by industry sources as substantial and expandable, not that actual production increased from 80,000 to 120,000 tonnes in a known period.

SCNZ’s quarterly forward-workload charts show expected committed tonnes and spare capacity. In the June 2023 update, industry capacity is shown as 30,000 tonnes per quarter. The chart reports 27,383 tonnes committed and 2,617 tonnes spare for July–September; 22,178 committed and 7,822 spare for October–December; 14,976 committed and 15,024 spare for January–March; and 9,982 committed and 20,018 spare for April–June.[2] These are estimates of future committed workload, not records of material actually processed.

The March 2023 update gives a different quarterly sequence: it describes 27,612 tonnes committed and 2,388 tonnes spare in one displayed quarter; 24,376 and 5,624 tonnes in the next; 19,306 and 10,694 tonnes thereafter; and 14,615 committed with 15,385 spare in the final displayed quarter.[7] The two charts cannot be combined into a single annual production series because they reflect different forecast vintages and do not report actual fabrication.

Derived from SCNZ’s March 2023 forward-workload survey, committed work for April 2023–March 2024 totalled 85,909 tonnes, equivalent to 71.6% of stated annual capacity and 28.4% spare capacity; these were forward commitments, not actual fabricated output.[7]

The preceding SCNZ survey recorded quarterly commitments of 27,933 tonnes for July–September 2022, 25,660 tonnes for October–December 2022, 19,094 tonnes for January–March 2023 and 15,210 tonnes for April–June 2023. They totalled 87,897 tonnes, or 73.2% of annual capacity; the subsequent April 2023–March 2024 committed workload was 1,988 tonnes (2.3%) lower.[103]

The direction of the June 2023 forward-workload chart is nevertheless clear: committed work fell from 27,383 tonnes in July–September to 9,982 tonnes in April–June, while spare capacity rose from 2,617 tonnes to 20,018 tonnes.[2] SCNZ linked spare capacity in its later December 2024 update to a construction downturn and lack of projects, rather than a stated loss of market share to offshore fabricated steel.

It reported 46 percent spare capacity and stated that the sector had not lost market share or been displaced by an influx of offshore fabricated steel.[1]

SCNZ’s reported spare-capacity estimates were 41% for the year ending June 2020, 35% to December 2021, 34% to September 2022, 27% to June 2023 and 28% to March 2024.[100][101][102][103][7]

SCNZ’s 2025 forward-workload chart, published in December 2024, reinforced the demand-risk point. It showed 30,000 tonnes quarterly capacity, against committed tonnes and spare capacity of 26,118 and 3,882 for January–March 2025; 17,583 and 12,417 for April–June; 12,267 and 17,733 for July–September; and 9,237 and 20,763 for October–December.[1] This belongs to the forward pipeline rather than the 2018–2024 historical tonnage series, but it shows the commercial importance of distinguishing capacity from utilisation.

Other steel figures must also remain separate:

- **New Zealand Steel’s production:** MBIE reports that New Zealand Steel produces around 650,000 tonnes of steel annually. That is mill output across building, construction, manufacturing, and agricultural uses, not structural fabrication. [10] [10]
- **National steel consumption:** Te Waihanga cites an estimate of 850,000 tonnes consumed in New Zealand. This is neither construction-only nor fabrication-only, and no year is specified in the cited passage. [33]
- **Project steel quantities:** project-specific reinforcement, structural-steel, rail, or temporary-prop tonnes are demand examples; they cannot be summed into national annual fabrication output. City Rail Link, for example, was expected to use approximately 25,000 tonnes of locally produced reinforcing steel over three to four years. [6]
- **Capacity versus commitment:** a 30,000-tonne quarterly capacity is a potential throughput level; committed tonnes are expected workload; neither is actual annual output. [2] [7]

The tonnage result therefore rules out a quantified 2018–2024 production-growth claim. The evidence does establish a recent structural-steel sector output indicator of about 100,000 tonnes over preceding 12-month periods, alongside estimated annual fabrication capacity of about 120,000 tonnes and a weakening forward workload through 2023–24.

SCNZ’s July–September 2023 to April–June 2024 forward-workload forecast shows committed tonnes falling by 17,401 tonnes, from 27,383 to 9,982 tonnes—a derived 63.5% decline.[2]

BusinessDesk reported two slightly different current-quarter SCNZ committed-tonnage figures: about 27,712 tonnes of construction-grade steel, and 27,612 tonnes out of 30,000 tonnes quarterly capacity for the current quarter to June. The latter was reported as falling to 19,306 tonnes by the December quarter and 14,615 tonnes in the first quarter of 2024. These are committed-workload figures rather than tonnes fabricated.[36]

SCNZ states that Steel Fabrication Certification (SFC)-qualified structural-steel contractors fabricate 96 percent of sector annual output. SFC has a workshop-fabrication module, launched in 2014, and a site-erection module, introduced in 2018.[37]

SFC links output to AS/NZS 5131 assurance requirements. A designer specifies a Construction Category (CC1–CC4) for all or part of a project; the category determines the required quality controls, documentation, traceability, inspection and testing. Fabricators demonstrate independently audited systems for critical processes, including materials, traceability and welding, and may undertake work at their certified Construction Category or below. Certification lasts five years, subject to annual surveillance audits, and builders must engage a fabricator certified for the category specified by the designer.[37]

A bottom-up fabricated-tonnage series would need audited fabricator output or capacity disclosures and project steel schedules, with explicit treatment of imported fabricated steel, exports, reinforcing steel, and potential double counting between workshop fabrication and erection.

A separate industry report states that annual construction structural-steel output was around 100,000 tonnes annually since 2018, installed sector capacity was 120,000–150,000 tonnes in 2023–24, and spare capacity was 28–40% in 2023–24.[38]

Demand conditions were materially different across the period. Structural steel had become a significant construction-system choice: SCNZ stated that it held more than 50 percent of the national multilevel-construction market and more than 80 percent in Christchurch.[40] These are shares of multilevel construction, not shares of total construction expenditure or fabrication revenue.[30][2] They nevertheless show why non-residential buildings, apartments, seismic resilience, and infrastructure are more relevant demand indicators for structural fabricators than general residential consent counts.

SCNZ also attributes higher productivity in local fabrication to investment in fabrication technology and simple bolted connections.[40] Between 2018 and 2024, this is a qualitative productivity development rather than a quantified productivity series.

More broadly, MBIE reported that construction labour productivity increased only 0.5 percent per annum between 2020 and 2023, with multifactor productivity increasing 0.4 percent per annum.[17] Those are construction-wide figures; they should not be presented as structural-fabricator productivity.

The 2022–23 construction context combined high nominal activity with cost and supply pressure. MBIE reported that total building work put in place rose from NZ$30.490 billion in the year ended March 2022 to NZ$37.073 billion in the year ended March 2023.[17] Non-residential work grew more strongly than residential work in the year to June 2023, rising 28.3 percent against 18.5 percent for residential work.[17] This supports the conclusion that the demand mix was relatively supportive of structural-steel applications, but it is not a measure of steelwork demand.

At the same time, materials, labour, supply-chain constraints, and cashflow concerns intensified construction-sector pressure.[17] MBIE reported construction Labour Cost Index growth of 4.2 percent in the year ended June 2023, compared with 3.4 percent across all industries; average construction hourly earnings including overtime were NZ$36.88 in the June 2023 quarter, up 8.5 percent from NZ$33.99 a year earlier.[17] Rising material and labour costs were described as eroding construction-company margins, with purchases and operating expenditure up 7.6 percent and salaries and wages up 11 percent in the June 2023 quarter against June 2022.[17]

By 2024, the forward environment had weakened. RLB’s third-quarter 2024 New Zealand Construction Market Update described the near-term outlook as weak, while expecting a recovery in construction demand from 2025.[41] In Auckland, it reported that contractors were reducing margins and prices to secure work despite continuing cost pressure.[41] It also identified slowly rising raw-material costs, increasing costs of doing business with limited ability to pass them on, and a falling New Zealand dollar.[41] In Christchurch, RLB noted major stadium, university, and hospital projects underway but few new or proposed projects to replace them on completion.[41]

This market setting aligns with SCNZ’s 2024 spare-capacity indicator. The evidence points to a demand and utilisation problem rather than a demonstrated loss of structural-steel share to offshore fabricated steel. SCNZ’s December 2024 statement explicitly said the sector had not lost market share or faced displacement by an influx of offshore fabricated steel.[1] That does not eliminate competitive or supply-chain pressures; it identifies the reported immediate constraint as lack of projects.

Infrastructure provided an offset to weaker building demand. MBIE’s National Construction Pipeline Report forecast infrastructure activity to reach a new high in 2026 and then remain steady, driven partly by recovery from early-2023 extreme-weather events and resilience work.[42] Te Waihanga’s March 2024 infrastructure pipeline snapshot, as reported by RLB, covered NZ$121.2 billion of projects, including approximately NZ$44 billion under construction and NZ$11 billion in procurement.[41] Transport represented NZ$61.9 billion and water infrastructure NZ$20.6 billion.[41] These values are not steel-demand estimates, but they matter because structural-steel fabrication responds to the timing, design, procurement, and delivery of projects rather than simply to their headline budgets.

The period also confirms that upstream steel supply and fabrication demand are separate but linked. New Zealand Steel is described as New Zealand’s only steel manufacturer, operating at Glenbrook.[35] About half of steel consumed in New Zealand is produced domestically, but Te Waihanga says domestic production contributes a much smaller percentage of structural steel and that most structural beams are imported.[33] Fabricators therefore operate with a mix of domestic and imported supply, rather than relying solely on either source.

Te Waihanga describes New Zealand as a global price-taker for steel. Global demand spikes can reduce availability and increase prices; local steel prices are also strongly related to exchange-rate risk, although the study says most companies appear to use exchange hedging.[33] Glenbrook can provide some supply security when imports are constrained, but it prices according to global trends and is too small to affect global prices.[33] These conditions are relevant to fabricator costs, quote validity, inventory, and working capital, but they do not establish a measured 2018–2024 margin or revenue effect.

The 2018–2024 conclusion is therefore two-sided. Structural steel retained a strong position in multilevel construction and the industry reported major installed capacity, local productivity investment, and about 100,000 tonnes of recent sector output.[40] But the requested historical market-size growth series is unavailable because published measures sit at different points in the value chain: construction GDP measures projects, mill output measures primary steel production, C2221 captures a manufacturing classification, and SCNZ workload measures capacity and commitments.[40][1][17][10][3][2]

The 2018–2024 evidence establishes a structurally important but statistically narrow market. ANZSIC06 C2221 is the closest official category for off-site structural-steel fabrication, while mills, merchants, complete prefabricated buildings, erection services, and non-construction metalwork sit outside the defined boundary.[4][1]

The official employment proxy records 4,250 C2221 paid employee jobs in February 2018 and 4,450 in February 2024, a derived increase of 200 jobs, or 4.7 percent; the derived compound annual growth rate was 0.77 percent.[3] No equivalent in-scope annual revenue or actual-fabricated-tonnage series is established for all seven years.[3] SCNZ instead provides sector context: approximately 100,000 tonnes of steel turned into buildings and bridges over preceding 12-month periods, against estimated annual fabrication capacity of 120,000 tonnes.[1]

Read together, the measures show strong structural-steel relevance to multilevel construction and significant domestic fabrication capacity, but weakening 2023–24 forward workload and rising spare capacity.[40] The correct conclusion is not a numerical 2018–2024 market-growth rate; it is that the market must be tracked through separate revenue, workforce, physical-output, capacity, and construction-demand measures rather than one substituted broad-sector total.[2][1]

Company results provide a separate indicator of the steel-cycle conditions affecting fabricators’ input and customer markets. Steel & Tube reported sales of NZ$498.110 million in FY2019, NZ$417.923 million in FY2020, NZ$481.043 million in FY2021, NZ$599.148 million in FY2022, and NZ$589.078 million in FY2023, all years ended 30 June. Its FY2023 sales volume was 146,409 tonnes, down 12.4 percent, while sales revenue fell 1.7 percent.[43]

Steel & Tube characterised FY2021 as a pandemic-lockdown period, FY2022 as a post-restriction construction boom and its record year, and FY2023 as a contraction after interest-rate increases. It reported softening activity and volumes during FY2023, elevated steel prices that eased in the second half but remained above pre-COVID levels, and inflationary cost increases affecting margins.[43]

BlueScope reported that its New Zealand & Pacific Islands (NZPI) segment sold 377.8 thousand tonnes domestically in FY2023, down 24 percent from 495.7 thousand tonnes in FY2022; exports were 99.4 thousand tonnes, up 9 percent from 91.0 thousand tonnes. It attributed lower FY2023 despatch volumes partly to distributors reducing inventories in a falling-price environment, delayed imports arriving, and poor weather.[44]

BlueScope reported strong FY2023 demand for heavy plate products because of infrastructure demand. It also reported a 26 percent decline in domestic long-product sales from FY2022, largely from weather-related effects on construction early in FY2023, while stating that long-product demand was predominantly infrastructure-driven, including shovel-ready projects and the NZ Upgrade programme.[44]

COVID-19 disruption could affect fabrication activity, input availability, paid jobs, and nominal income differently: AES 2020 and 2021 cover enterprises with differing balance-date exposure to the pandemic, and AES 2022 records government-funded wage subsidies in government funding, grants, and subsidies when subsidies passed to employees are recorded in salaries and wages.[18][19][24]

Project demand measures remain distinct from sector output. City Rail Link was expected to use approximately 25,000 tonnes of locally produced reinforcing steel over three to four years.[6] The New Dunedin Hospital inpatient building was expected to receive about 11,000 tonnes of steel as construction progressed; this is broadly described as steel delivered to the inpatient site, not annual fabrication output.[45] Te Kaha’s 40 radial roof trusses weigh 45–75 tonnes each, implying a derived 1,800–3,000 tonnes for those trusses only; this calculation excludes the separately described roof modules and the more than 4,800 tonnes of reinforcing materials in the substructure.[46][47][48]

Auckland Transport’s December 2024 update scheduled City Rail Link practical completion for Q4/December 2025, while Auckland Transport’s November 2024 Statement of Intent described the project as being delivered in 2026. These are scheduling measures, not an established passenger-opening date.[49][50]

Revenue can change with steel prices and with the project mix, including the value of material, coating, transport, erection management, and other services included in an integrated contract.

Supply availability affected construction activity during the final quarter of 2021. NASH states that, while Auckland was in lockdown and the rest of New Zealand could work on construction sites, shortages including reinforcing mesh and cold-formed steel for framing caused construction nationwide to stop.[51] NASH states that these material shortages affected the financial viability of builders, housing suppliers and manufacturers.[51]

A Construction Sector Accord survey found that 10 percent of respondents identified steel as a product in short supply, compared with 36 percent identifying structural wood products and 21 percent identifying non-structural wood products.[52]

Steel Construction New Zealand reported a nationwide network of 127 fabricator companies and estimated 31 percent spare industry capacity for the following 12 months.[52]

### Stats NZ or MBIE

Stats NZ and MBIE provide the correct framework for analysing the market, but their published categories do not exactly equal the client’s construction-only structural-steel-fabrication definition.

The first relevant framework is ANZSIC06, the Australian and New Zealand Standard Industrial Classification 2006. Its closest class is C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating. This class covers units mainly engaged in fabricating structural-steel components for buildings or other structures.[4] It sits within Manufacturing, not Construction.[4] That placement is economically logical: the shop transforms purchased steel into prefabricated structural components before those components are installed on site.

The classification boundary is specific:

- Complete steel-framed and clad structures such as prefabricated sheds and garages are in C2222 Prefabricated Metal Building Manufacturing. [4] [8]
- Erection or installation of complete prefabricated buildings is in Division E Construction. [4] [8]
- Construction, including on-site assembly, of complete pipelines or steel towers is in Class 3109 Other Heavy and Civil Engineering Construction. [4] [8]
- Erection or installation of structural-steel components, including on-site fabrication, is classified as Structural Steel Erection Services in Construction, rather than structural-steel fabrication. [4] [8]
- Architectural metal products are classified separately from structural steel fabrication. [11]

**The statistical boundary means that structural fabrication, structural erection, and construction expenditure must be analysed as connected but non-additive measures.** Adding them would double count activities and misstate the size of the off-site fabrication market.[11][4]

The second relevant source is AES. It is Stats NZ’s principal annual financial-statistics survey, covering more than 500,000 businesses and more than 60 percent of New Zealand GDP. AES is compiled from sample-survey data, Inland Revenue IR10 financial data, and Charities Services data.[21][12] It uses kind-of-activity units (KAUs) as statistical and collection units; a KAU is a subdivision of an enterprise for which a single set of accounting records is available.[12] This is important for diversified steel businesses: a company that fabricates structural steel while also wholesaling material, painting, erecting, or operating separate asset entities can have activity recorded across different KAUs and industry classifications.[12][16]

AES financial values exclude GST. Its target population is economically significant enterprises operating in New Zealand, which means it understates total national activity by excluding businesses outside its significance rules.[12][16] Results are annual but relate to varying business balance dates between 1 October and 30 September.[16] The financial reference-year basis makes direct comparison with calendar-year project tonnage, construction consents, or annual steel imports imperfect.

AES is designed predominantly at four-digit ANZSIC level, covering 109 industries, and Stats NZ warns that lower-level outputs may have higher sampling error and limited analysis.[12] Its output framework is NZSIOC level 4, derived from ANZSIC06.[12] As a result, an AES series may be useful if a precisely matching published category is available, but the evidence here does not establish a published revenue series that is both C2221-specific and restricted to constructional end uses.

The third relevant source is Business Demography Statistics. These provide an annual February snapshot of enterprises, geographic units, employee counts, business births and deaths, survival, institutional sector, business type, and ownership.[26] The employee count uses tax records and is designed principally to support business-size statistics. It is explicitly not an official employment statistic.[27][29]

For structural fabrication, Business Demography is more useful than AES for workforce scale because C2221 employee counts can be reported directly. However, it retains three structural limitations:

1. **Predominant-activity classification:** an enterprise doing manufacturing, distribution, and erection is classified according to its predominant activity, and a change in that activity can move it between industry series without equivalent change in underlying operations. [27] [28]

2. **Coverage boundary:** the series covers economically significant enterprises, excluding some small businesses and potentially omitting unpaid owner labour. [27] [26] [27]

3. **Fine-industry quality:** Stats NZ warns that the Statistical Business Register supports national and aggregate industry statistics better than fine-level industry estimates, especially for small and medium enterprises. [27] [28]

MBIE supplies valuable context but not a direct market total. Its 2018 manufacturing report identifies C222 Structural Metal Products as a category and reports 12,500 employees in it for 2017.[10] That category is much broader than C2221 and falls outside the requested 2018–2024 window.[10] The same MBIE report groups metals and metal products across C21 and C22, including smelting and a wide range of metal products.[10] It is therefore a useful comparator for the wider metals economy, not a construction/structural-steel-fabrication market size.

MBIE’s Building and Construction Sector Trends Annual Report provides another necessary but separate layer: broad construction GDP, investment, building work, labour, cost, confidence, insolvency, productivity, and pipeline data.[17] It reported construction-sector real GDP of NZ$18.106 billion in the year ended March 2023, up 2.1 percent from NZ$16.898 billion in the year ended March 2022.[17] This is construction demand context, not fabrication revenue. The report itself says its results are research outputs created from Stats NZ’s Integrated Data Infrastructure and are not official statistics.[17]

Judgment — The appropriate reporting hierarchy for this market is: first, C2221 Business Demography for official employment proxy; second, any explicitly identified AES C2221 or closely mapped NZSIOC financial series for GST-exclusive revenue proxy; third, SCNZ output/capacity/workload measures for physical and operational context; and fourth, MBIE construction and infrastructure indicators for demand context. Each should retain its own definition rather than being summed.

This framework also explains why several apparently plausible figures must remain separate. New Zealand Steel’s approximately 650,000 tonnes per year is primary mill output.[10] SCNZ’s estimated 120,000 tonnes per year is fabrication capacity.[2] SCNZ’s circa-100,000 tonnes is a preceding-12-month structural-steel sector output indicator covering the distributor–fabricator–erector network.[2] C2221’s 4,250 and 4,200 values are February paid employee-job counts.[3] Construction GDP and infrastructure-pipeline values measure client-side project activity.[3][17] Each figure answers a different question.[41]

The practical decision is to avoid headline market-size claims that blend these measures. A credible market study can state the exact official proxy, the period, the unit, and the residual gap. It cannot convert construction GDP, imported steel, mill tonnage, or project budgets into fabrication revenue without a transparent bottom-up model and explicit assumptions.

ANZSIC06 groups business units using a supply-side approach: units undertaking similar productive activities are grouped because they have similar production functions, meaning the transformation of intermediate inputs through labour and capital into outputs. It has four levels—division, subdivision, group, and class—with class the finest level. This is why C2221 is an activity-based fabrication proxy rather than a measure restricted to a construction end use.[13][53]

The NZ$9,448 million 2023 AES total-income figure is reported at level 4 for CC721 Fabricated Metal Product Manufacturing. CC721 covers ANZSIC06 groups C221, C222, C223, C224, and C229, so it is broader than C2221 Structural Steel Fabricating and does not isolate construction/structural fabrication.[23]

## Top 5 structural steel fabricators

A structural steel fabricator cuts, drills, welds, coats and sometimes erects the load-bearing steel used in buildings, bridges and industrial structures. Scale matters because large projects require workshop throughput, certified welding and project-management capacity; however, revenue, fabricated tonnage and installed-project value are different measures and cannot be used interchangeably.

**The named evidence identifies a leading group of four fabricators, but it does not support an ordinal 2023 top-five ranking by company revenue or annual structural-steel capacity.** BusinessDesk identified D&H Steel Construction, Grayson Engineering, MJH Engineering and John Jones Steel as a top tier accounting collectively for about 45% of market share by tonnage, without allocating that share among the four firms.[54]

## NZ import tariff rates and margin impact

### HS codes 7207–7216 or equivalent

Judgment — Structural steel fabrication turns mill-made steel into construction components: cut, drilled, welded and coated beams, columns, trusses, bracing and connection assemblies. The relevant imports are principally plate, hot-rolled sections and bars; semi-finished steel and flat products can also be inputs where a New Zealand processor converts them before fabrication. This scope excludes finished non-construction metal articles, consumer metalware and reinforcing steel unless it is demonstrably part of a structural-fabrication contract.

The Standard Tariff is structured under the Harmonised System (HS) nomenclature. A tariff heading is identified by four digits, a tariff subheading by six digits, and a tariff item by eight digits; duty is determined at the tariff-item level rather than from a four-digit heading alone.[68] Chapter 72 covers iron and steel, while a fabricated imported beam, connection or structural assembly may instead fall under HS 7308, which covers iron-or-steel structures and parts of structures, including bridges, roofs, frameworks, pillars, columns and products prepared for use in structures.[69] Customs states that all imports must be classified in the Tariff of New Zealand and that classification determines the duty payable. The legal classification follows the wording of the tariff heading and applicable section or chapter notes, not the chapter title.[70][71][68]

The HS2022 nomenclature applied in New Zealand from 1 January 2022; the update changed some codes and descriptions without intending to change the general distinction between duty-free and dutiable goods.[111][112]

A Preferential Tariff rate can apply to certain goods from specified countries and country groups, including under trade agreements, and to certain goods produced or manufactured in developing countries under the Generalised System of Preferences.[74] Customs states that eligibility requires the relevant rules of origin and other conditions, including where applicable direct shipment; the importer is responsible for making the claim correctly.

Shipment through a preferential-tariff partner does not itself establish that steel originates there: under the Hong Kong–China CEP example, the goods must be wholly obtained, made entirely from originating inputs, or meet a product-specific origin rule. Cutting, slitting, bending, coiling and uncoiling alone do not confer origin.[113]

Preferential origin is not established merely by the country from which steel is shipped: simple cutting, slitting, bending, coiling or uncoiling does not itself confer origin.[113]

The Chapter 72 schedule note says that, unless otherwise indicated, preferential rates for AAN, AE, AU, CN, CPT, EU, HK, KR, LLDC, MY, Pac, PPP, RCEP, SG, TH, TPA, TW and UK are Free.[74][72]

Import duty is ad valorem, meaning a percentage of Customs value rather than a fixed dollar charge per tonne. Customs says that the duty rate applies to the value of the goods, normally the price paid or payable in New Zealand dollars, and that the rate depends on the goods type, country of manufacture and country of dispatch. Thus, for a 5% Normal-rate 7208 or 7209 item, the direct duty difference against a qualifying Free preferential entry is 5% of the Customs value of that steel input.[75][68][72]

Customs value is the statutory basis for calculating import duty and can differ from the supplier’s invoice value.[114]

Customs converts foreign-currency import entries using fortnightly exchange rates, so a weaker New Zealand dollar increases both the NZD value of imported steel and any ad valorem duty payable on it.[115]

Customs valuation begins with the transaction value where applicable, before the relevant tariff-item rate is applied.[114]

Judgment — The fabricator-margin effect follows a simple cost-pass-through rule. If imported tariffed steel has Customs value C, tariff rate t, imported-input share of total fabrication cost s, and the fabricator cannot recover the duty in its contract price, gross margin falls by C multiplied by t; it is not automatically a 5% fall in company revenue or gross margin. A fabricator using qualifying preferential or normally duty-free inputs has zero direct tariff-duty cost, while a firm importing a non-preferential 5% item bears the 5% duty on that input value.

For a fixed-price NZ$1.5 million contract with no cost recovery, a 5% duty on NZ$1 million of imported steel Customs value would create NZ$50,000 in duty and reduce the contract margin by 3.33 percentage points; this is an illustrative calculation, not an observed fabricator margin.[115]

Tariff duty is only one element of landed cost. Relevant taxes and levies applicable in New Zealand remain payable irrespective of preferential tariff treatment, including GST, anti-dumping or countervailing duties, ALAC and HERA levies, and import-entry transaction fees or other cost recoveries.[74] GST is 15% and Customs calculates it on the item price plus international freight, postage and insurance costs, and any import duty.[75][74]

Judgment — For a GST-registered fabricator, recoverable GST is generally a cash-flow and administration cost rather than a permanent margin cost; freight, port charges, trade-remedy duties, stockholding and delays can be permanent costs. The margin calculation must therefore use the fabricator’s actual tariff lines, origin documentation, concessions, Customs values, imported-input share and contractual ability to pass steel-price movements to the customer.

A tariff concession removes tariff duty otherwise payable and is generally approved where no suitable alternative goods are produced or manufactured locally in New Zealand.[70] Local producers may object to an advertised concession, and Customs assesses such matters as suitable local supply, available quantity and attempts to maximise local participation.

Domestic supply still tracks global steel-price trends: Te Waihanga reports that Glenbrook can improve availability during global shortages but is too small to influence global pricing.[70][76][33]

**The practical conclusion is that tariff exposure is a procurement-variable, not a uniform protective margin for New Zealand structural fabricators: classify each input at tariff-item level, validate origin preference or concession before purchase, and price contracts on landed steel cost rather than the headline HS heading.**[70][74][75][33]

The heading descriptions distinguish upstream inputs from finished structural imports: 7207 is semi-finished iron or non-alloy steel; 7208–7212 are flat-rolled products differentiated by width, rolling and coating; 7213–7215 are bars and rods; and 7216 is angles, shapes and sections. Finished imported construction steel prepared as structures is classified under 7308 rather than assumed to be an input within 7207–7216.[69]

New Zealand’s RCEP tariff amendment took effect on 1 January 2022. It provides that goods from RCEP specified countries which meet rules of origin and other applicable requirements receive the preferential rate indicated in the Working Tariff Document and, unless a tariff item indicates otherwise, that rate is Free.[77]

A further preferential-access change took effect on 15 December 2024: the Tariff (Specified CPTPP Parties—United Kingdom) Amendment Order 2024 made CPTPP preferential tariffs applicable to qualifying United Kingdom goods imported into New Zealand.[78]

Customs publishes tariff-concession applications, approvals, withdrawals and amendments. A tariff concession removes Customs duty otherwise payable on an item, so a concession is an additional determinant of a fabricator’s direct duty cost alongside tariff classification and origin preference.[79][80]

Customs states that specialist tariff and concession files are updated every 24 hours; shipment-specific duty treatment therefore depends on the Working Tariff Document and concessions applicable on the arrival date.[111][116][117]

**Derived 2024 scenario —** If 50% of steel inputs are imported under a payable 5% tariff line, steel cost equals 40% of revenue, and none of the duty is passed through, the gross-margin reduction is 50% × 5% × 40% × 100% = **1.0 percentage point of revenue**. This is a derived scenario, not an observed industry margin.[72]

Domestic suppliers can also raise their prices in response to higher landed import prices, so a fabricator’s exposure includes indirect domestic-steel price effects as well as duty paid directly on imports. Full contractual pass-through of a duty increase preserves the fabricator’s percentage margin only where the customer accepts the higher price.

The Customs and Excise Act 2018 makes the importer or its agent responsible for ensuring that goods are entered correctly. Customs advises importers uncertain of classification to seek an enquiry or obtain a formal tariff-classification ruling through Form C7.[71]

In the Working Tariff Document, a date in a tariff listing specifies that the corresponding rate applies from the first day of that month.[71]

The Working Tariff Document states that its published Normal duty rates remain at their current levels, while preferential tariffs under free-trade agreements continue to reduce according to agreed commitments.[71]

New Zealand import tariffs were not a significant direct determinant of local fabricators’ margins in 2024 because most key inputs were imported at zero tariff and nominal 5% tariffs were rare.[62]

Global freight-rate and exchange-rate fluctuations have a larger impact on fabricators than New Zealand’s import tariffs.[62]

## Three major infrastructure projects

Major infrastructure schemes create concentrated, time-bound calls for reinforcement, structural members and erection services. The relevant distinction is between **reinforcing steel (rebar)**—bar embedded in concrete to resist tensile forces—and **structural steel**—fabricated beams, columns, trusses and connection assemblies that form a load-bearing frame. The former is construction-steel demand, but ordinarily does not measure the output of structural-steel fabricators.

Judgment — The three projects assessed here represent different demand profiles: City Rail Link (CRL) combines underground rail, stations and ancillary steelwork; the New Dunedin Hospital Inpatient Building combines a large multi-storey health-building steel frame and associated fabrication; and Te Kaha combines arena superstructure and long-span roof fabrication. CRL’s disclosed quantity is reinforcing steel, the Dunedin figure is described broadly as steel delivered to site, and Te Kaha’s calculated range is restricted to radial roof trusses.[6][45][81]

### City Rail Link

City Rail Link is New Zealand’s largest transport-infrastructure project. Auckland Transport’s December 2024 rail update described it as a NZ$5.5 billion, 3.45-kilometre underground rail link between Waitematā and Maungawhau, with two new underground stations, Karanga Hape and Te Waihorotiu.[49] Infrastructure Pipeline describes City Rail Link as a 3.4-kilometre extension to Auckland’s rail network, connecting Britomart Station to Mt Eden Station through twin tunnels.

It describes the physical works as one kilometre of cut-and-cover tunnel, 2.5 kilometres of bored tunnel, redevelopment of Waitematā and Mount Eden stations, and the two new underground stations.[82]

The City Rail Link works also include a 127-metre retaining wall and four overbridges.[118][119]

New Zealand Steel stated that approximately 25,000 tonnes of locally produced reinforcing steel would be used on CRL over three to four years. For City Rail Link, when 50 mm reinforcing steel bar was needed, Pacific Steel developed a product solution made locally in Ōtāhuhu from steel produced by New Zealand Steel; the bar supported the Chief Post Office building above the tunnels.[6] This is the largest disclosed project quantity in this comparison, but it is rebar rather than a published total for fabricated structural steel.

CRL also had identifiable, smaller steel quantities in specific work packages. The mined tunnels at Maungawhau used around 250 tonnes of steel during 2.5 years of tunnelling, while the installed rail track between Waitematā and Maungawhau weighed 340 tonnes. City Rail Link’s Western Line works sold 81,720 kilograms of temporary prop steel beams from the eastern grade separation to a company that would store and resell them for use on another project. A redesign of Karanga-a-Hape Station reduced mined tunnelling and associated concrete and steel by 11%.[83] These figures demonstrate steel consumption and reuse within particular scopes, not the project’s whole structural-steel requirement.

A City Rail Link forecast for the Mt Eden rebuild specified more than 10,000 tonnes of steel alongside more than 50,000 m³ of concrete; the steel figure covers that component’s steel rather than verified project-wide fabricated structural steel.[119]

City Rail Link separately used 350 tonnes of locally made 50 mm high-strength reinforcing steel for Chief Post Office diaphragm-wall cages, while a redesign of the building’s underpinning saved 83 tonnes of steel.[120]

As at 31 December 2024, CRL practical completion was planned for December 2025 (Q4 2025), while Auckland Transport anticipated delivery and passenger operations in 2026; both completion and opening remained prospective.[49][83][50] CRL Ltd’s 15 March 2023 announcement had forecast completion of stations and supporting rail infrastructure by November 2025, followed by handover and additional work by KiwiRail and Auckland Transport before passenger service.[49][83] A 20 December 2023 CRL release likewise referred to completion in November 2025.[83] The December 2025 practical-completion schedule was therefore the later published programme at the cutoff; it referred to construction completion, not the date of passenger opening.

In August 2024, City Rail Link Ltd committed to hand over completed and tested infrastructure in November 2025, after which Auckland Transport and KiwiRail would undertake trial running and operational-readiness work.[121]

All 11 kilometres of City Rail Link track had been laid by February 2024. By June 2024, construction was more than 80% complete and work had shifted to testing, commissioning and fit-out of trains, stations and rail systems.[121][122]

**CRL provides a major, multi-year construction-steel call-off, but its published 25,000-tonne figure is principally reinforcement demand and cannot be used as a direct proxy for local fabricated structural-steel tonnage.**[6][83]

The stated 25,000 tonnes of CRL reinforcing steel over three to four years implies an average call-off rate of approximately 6,250–8,333 tonnes per year.[6]

Auckland Transport’s November 2024 Statement of Intent scheduled CRL delivery in 2026 and milestones for asset ownership, operational planning and testing in 2025/26.[50]

City Rail Link’s Maungawhau Station work programme included Fenton Street Bridge construction, including steel framing, painting, welding and lifting materials over the rail corridor.[83]

A separate report estimates City Rail Link demand at 4,000 tonnes of structural steel and approximately 25,000–31,600 tonnes of reinforcing steel. Together with New Zealand Steel’s approximately 25,000-tonne estimate of locally produced reinforcing steel, these findings distinguish CRL’s roughly 25,000 tonnes of locally produced reinforcing steel from a separately estimated 4,000 tonnes of structural steel.[84][6]

## Top 3 strategic risks and opportunities

Structural-steel fabricators turn steel sections, plate and associated components into load-bearing building and infrastructure frames. Their commercial position depends not only on the price of steel, but also on whether they can secure compliant inputs, keep workshops utilised between major projects, and convert local engineering, certification and delivery capability into a bid advantage. As at 2024, the central tension was lower-priced imported material and fabrication versus the value of reliable local compliance, programme certainty and responsiveness.[89][54][90]

### Steel inputs and sourcing

New Zealand fabricators operate in an internationally exposed steel market. Te Waihanga’s *Infrastructure Resources Study* says New Zealand competes for steel globally as a price-taker: global demand spikes can reduce supply arriving in New Zealand and raise prices, while domestic steel prices are strongly related to foreign-exchange movements. The study says most sector companies appear to use exchange hedging, but hedging manages currency risk rather than the underlying world steel price or physical availability of a particular product.[33]

Domestic production improves resilience but does not insulate fabricators from global prices. Te Waihanga says the Glenbrook mill can supply some products when imports are short, giving New Zealand some security of supply, but that it prices with global trends and is too small to influence the world price. This makes procurement discipline—forward buying, multiple qualified supply routes, product substitution where engineering permits, and currency management—more important than reliance on a single domestic source.[33]

The exposure extends beyond raw steel. MBIE’s 2023 construction-sector report states that approximately 90% of construction products sold in New Zealand were imported, with China the leading source country; this is a construction-wide measure, not a structural-steel share. MBIE’s 2023 construction-sector report identified cost escalation, supply disruption and skills shortages as the three leading construction challenges. It described construction supply chains as fragmented, with minimal collaboration, and reported that construction-sector research-and-development expenditure rose from NZ$106 million in 2016 to NZ$185 million in 2022, but was only 4.4 per cent of total New Zealand business R&D expenditure.[17]

Import competition also places direct pressure on local fabrication bids. Steel Fabrication Certification (SFC) says imported prefabricated structural steel competes with local fabricators and may have uncertain provenance and quality. A BusinessDesk report recorded industry concern about cheaper Chinese imports. Australian-owned Pacific Steel, which operates the Glenbrook smelter south of Auckland, estimated that Chinese product was selling at a 30% discount; the New Zealand Government had variously investigated dumping complaints brought by Pacific Steel since 2016.[89][54]

That price differential should not be treated as a 30% saving in the installed structural frame. Imported fabricated steel can create engineering, documentation, sequencing, testing and rework costs. SCNZ cites a Pukekohe supermarket case in which Chinese prefabricated steel took six to eight weeks to travel, shop drawings were not comprehensible to New Zealand engineers, and components arrived out of sequence; engineers had to revisit calculations and drawings. In a separate Auckland warehouse case, imported portal frames lacked appropriate certification or traceability and required two months of independent testing before provenance was verified.[90]

**The effective competitive issue is therefore not simply the customs duty on steel inputs, addressed in the “NZ import tariff rates and margin impact” section, but the total landed-and-compliance cost of a fabricated package.** A nominally cheaper offshore quote can remain attractive where specifications are standardised and delivery is dependable, but its advantage erodes when verification, redesign, delayed installation or non-compliance risk must be priced into the project. This is also why a tariff concession can matter: Customs says concessions can reduce imported-goods costs where suitable local alternatives are not produced, while local manufacturers may object to a concession notice within three weeks.[76]

Local firms have practical assurance tools with which to respond. SCNZ says the Structural Steel Distributor Charter independently audits all seven of its distributor members for best-practice procurement, while the Bolt Importer Charter covers supplier due diligence, verification testing, documentation and traceability for structural fasteners and anchor bolts. SFC, launched in 2014, independently verifies that locally fabricated structural steel meets recognised quality and compliance standards, giving engineers, architects and main contractors greater confidence in the finished product and reducing compliance risk.[90]

SCNZ estimated in March 2018 that approximately 75% of steel used by the structural-steel sector was third-party certified.[99]

The opportunity is strongest where the fabricator sells a controlled delivery outcome rather than tonnes of steel alone: documented conformance, local engineering interaction, short lead times and accountable installation support. SCNZ also identifies no foreign-currency fluctuation risk in local contract pricing, an advantage for clients seeking price certainty even though fabricators may themselves retain exposure through imported inputs.[32][6]

What follows: input exposure is a bid-price and delivery-risk issue at once; fabricators should compete on assured conformity, traceability and local response time while actively managing global-price, currency and availability risk.[33][90]

Risk–opportunity pair 2 — supply-chain concentration and resilience: Te Waihanga states that most of New Zealand’s structural beams are imported, even though New Zealand Steel at Glenbrook produces about half of steel consumed nationally and a much smaller share of structural steel. Global demand spikes can reduce supply into New Zealand and increase prices; domestic Glenbrook output provides some security of supply for products it makes, but follows global price trends. The opportunity is resilient procurement: combine domestic supply where available with multiple qualified import routes, exchange hedging and audited procurement and traceability systems.[33][90]

MBIE’s analysis of Overseas Merchandise Trade data records source-country concentration across the broader building-and-construction-products basket: China supplied NZ$1.8 billion of imports in 2021, more than twice Australia’s NZ$770 million, and iron and steel were among the main imported raw-material and product categories. This reinforces the need for fabricators to maintain qualified supply routes and product-conformance evidence.[91]

The compliance opportunity applies to offshore as well as local procurement. SCNZ’s Compliance Toolbox supports compliant sourcing and fabrication whether structural steelwork is fabricated in New Zealand or offshore; its sourcing guide uses a risk-based approach to determine the evidence of conformity required, and identifies third-party product certification as a recognised means of reducing non-compliant-product risk.[92]

Carbon is a further input-and-design risk. Te Waihanga’s Infrastructure Resources Study, published 12 November 2021, states that “Addressing the carbon impact of high emitting materials like cement and steel will have a major impact on the use of these materials in construction projects.” Fabricators therefore have an opportunity to make efficient design, material utilisation, reuse and traceable supply part of their value proposition.[93]

NASH’s April 2023 submission to the Productivity Commission provides a concrete resilience lesson from the fourth quarter of 2021: while Auckland construction-material manufacturing was initially prohibited and then only partly permitted during lockdown, construction nationwide stopped as reinforcing mesh, cold-formed steel framing, plasterboard and timber stocks ran out. NASH said these shortages affected the financial viability of builders, housing suppliers and manufacturers, and described infrastructure and construction as inextricably linked to local manufacturing.[51]

For structural sections specifically, 2024 imports under HS 721690 came principally from Korea, 449,804 kg; Other Asia, nes, 104,478 kg; and China, 116,053 kg. Their combined 670,335 kg was 90.7% of the 739,150 kg total reported for this category of angles, shapes and sections. This makes supplier qualification and contingency planning commercially important for fabricators using this category.[94]

NASH identified trade-remedy access as an additional import-competition risk. In April 2023 it said manufacturing subsidies among trading partners and the operation of World Trade Organization rules were no longer fit for purpose, that few New Zealand cases delivered positive outcomes for local manufacturers, and that a New Zealand case had no deadline for MBIE acceptance, followed by a 180-day investigation after acceptance and a further public-interest test. NASH also said extended suppression of import statistics at an importer’s request created a significant hurdle for manufacturers initiating a case.[51]

NASH identified New Zealand’s transition to a low-emission circular economy as an opportunity for resource-rich New Zealand to develop innovative solutions and grow high-value local manufacturing jobs.[51] For structural fabricators, this supports offerings that combine assured material provenance, reuse and recycling capability, and compliant fabrication.[51]

David Moore, Grayson Engineering managing director and SCNZ chair, said the industry was increasingly concerned about Chinese steel imports brought in at a far cheaper price point and considered potentially more prone to failure than steel produced at Australian and Southeast Asian mills.[65]

The tariff treatment of imports from major steel-supplying free-trade-agreement partners limits any tariff buffer for local fabricators. Tariffs on Chinese exports to New Zealand were eliminated by 2016; Australia has had zero-tariff trade under CER since 1983; Korea eliminated tariffs on about 98% of goods including steel under the 2015 Korea–New Zealand FTA; CPTPP eliminates tariffs on Japanese industrial goods; the EU–New Zealand FTA removed duties on EU goods from 1 May 2024; and the New Zealand–UK FTA eliminated industrial-goods tariffs from 31 May 2023.[84]

On fixed-price contracts, an increase in the landed cost of steel between tender and purchase directly compresses a fabricator’s margin. Contractual price-escalation provisions, alongside early purchasing where appropriate, can limit or allocate that exposure.

Resilience planning should cover specialist coatings, transport and erection capacity as well as steel sections and plate. Integrated fabrication, coating, logistics and erection capability can reduce hand-off and construction-sequencing risk.

Te Waihanga identifies a climate-transition risk for steel: production is emissions-intensive, producing 1.9 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of virgin steel; emissions units allocated to New Zealand Steel as an emissions-intensive and trade-exposed industry will reduce over the next 30 years to zero. The study’s illustrative 2050 scenario combines a NZ$1,300-per-tonne landed steel price with a NZ$250-per-tonne carbon price and indicates that the landed price of steel could increase by about 40%; it also says carbon costs must not make New Zealand-produced steel uncompetitive against imports.[33]

Te Waihanga identified future carbon-cost exposure for domestic steel as emissions-unit allocations to New Zealand Steel reduce to zero over the next 30 years, and said most New Zealand steel for recycling is exported for processing in markets better suited to steel production. Together, these conditions make circular-material capability a strategic consideration for fabricators.[33]

New Zealand’s high ultraviolet exposure and wind, coastal salt erosion, more stringent earthquake standards than Australia, and environmental variation from north to south create a need for resilient steel products. MBIE said New Zealand Steel’s customised products can provide an edge in the domestic market because international manufacturers generally import generic, high-volume products rather than invest in products unique to New Zealand’s small market.[10]

BusinessDesk reported that the Alexandra Park apartment development in Auckland used more than 3,000 tonnes of steel procured from China, with steel missing and an incorrect grade of plate on base plates.[65]

### Project pipeline and capacity

**Risk–opportunity pair 3 — project-pipeline utilisation:** the immediate 2024 risk was workshop utilisation—keeping people, plant and overheads employed between jobs—rather than a demonstrated inability of the industry to fabricate large projects. The opportunity was to turn infrastructure, weather-rebuild and adaptive-reuse work into replacement workload through early engagement and disciplined pricing.[1] SCNZ estimated annual structural-steel fabrication capacity at 120,000 tonnes in December 2022.[7][54][1] Its forward-workload survey then estimated 26% spare capacity for the year ending December 2023; by its June 2023 update, SCNZ reported 38% spare capacity. These are association estimates of capacity and forward workload, not measures of actual annual fabrication output.[30][2]

SCNZ and market reporting connected spare capacity to weakening private-sector work. BusinessDesk reported SCNZ’s assessment that the market had plateaued and that forward orders would decline, with less vertical construction, lower apartment activity and weaker commercial enquiries contributing to more spare plant capacity. RLB’s third-quarter 2024 New Zealand update similarly described near-term construction demand as weak, annual dwelling consents below 35,000 in the year to May 2024, and slowing industrial-building demand as manufacturing activity contracted.[54][41]

The commercial consequence is margin pressure as contractors compete to secure a smaller pool of work. RLB reported that Auckland contractors were cutting prices and margins to win projects despite continuing cost pressures, including slowly rising raw-material costs, a falling New Zealand dollar and limited ability to pass costs through. MBIE had already found that rising materials and labour costs were eroding construction-company margins; construction purchases and operating expenditure rose 7.6% and salaries and wages 11% in the June 2023 quarter compared with June 2022.[41][17]

There was, however, a material infrastructure pipeline alongside the weak short-term building market. RLB reported Te Waihanga’s March 2024 pipeline snapshot at NZ$121.2 billion, including about NZ$44 billion under construction and NZ$11 billion in procurement. Transport represented NZ$61.9 billion and water NZ$20.6 billion; expected infrastructure investment from the pipeline was NZ$12.1 billion in 2024 and NZ$11.6 billion in 2025. These values are not structural-steel demand estimates, because project values include many costs and types of work with little or no fabricated steel.[41]

Infrastructure and rebuild work can nevertheless replace part of the lost private workload. Following severe weather, SCNZ expected significant infrastructure-replenishment demand. BusinessDesk also reported that rebuild work would absorb some capacity and identified adaptive reuse—altering and retaining existing buildings—as a trend in which steel is a core component. This demand is a partial offset, not an automatic replacement for major vertical and apartment work; RLB observed that Christchurch had stadium, university and hospital projects underway but few new or proposed projects to replace them after completion.[7][54][41]

Capacity is geographically and temporally uneven. Te Waihanga found no systemic entry barriers in steel importing, distribution or fabrication and saw several competitors, but also recognised pockets of constraint in particular locations or during very high demand. SCNZ describes nationwide collaboration among fabricators and Early Contractor Involvement (ECI)—bringing fabricators into design discussions early to address buildability, timeframes and risk—as a way to plan work across firms and reduce variations.[33][1]

What follows: the 2024 problem was principally securing profitable workload, not industry-wide physical capacity; fabricators should pursue infrastructure, resilience and adaptive-reuse packages early, while avoiding volume at prices that leave workshop overheads unrecovered.[30][54][41]

Auckland Transport’s Statement of Intent, modified in November 2024, makes the public-works opportunity conditional on funding certainty. Auckland Council had agreed a NZ$14 billion Auckland Transport capital programme, determined through the Long-term Plan and Regional Land Transport Plan, with the stated “fix, finish, optimise” approach; Council also agreed that funding levels had to be maintained despite withdrawal of the Regional Fuel Tax (RFT).[50] Yet Auckland Transport said its improvement-investment list significantly exceeded available funding, the National Land Transport Fund was highly constrained and substantially committed over the following three years, and failure of co-funding would mean less transport investment and fewer services.[50]

For Auckland’s East-West Link, the September 2024 National Land Transport Programme provided for project development and property purchase, without committing to an East-West-specific completion date; early planning for all Roads of National Significance was intended by June 2025, with construction on some projects within the following three years subject to funding.[124]

The Eastern Busway illustrates both visible committed work and the timing risk in the pipeline. Auckland Transport budgeted NZ$255.8 million in 2024/25, NZ$244.2 million in 2025/26 and NZ$129.4 million in 2026/27 for the project, while Auckland Council’s expectations were to finish the existing City Rail Link and Eastern Busway rapid-transit projects before taking on large new capital projects. Inflation had rapidly raised Auckland Transport project costs, reducing the work deliverable from available funding, and uncertainty over National Land Transport Programme approvals reduced its 2024/25 capital-budget-investment target to 85%.[50]

Pipeline visibility is itself an operational opportunity. The Construction Sector Transformation Plan included improving the visibility and certainty of the infrastructure and public-housing construction pipeline, providing construction firms with best-practice information and creating education on commercial-risk management. NASH separately recommended detailed supply-chain mapping, identification of changing choke points, and connection of government procurement forecasts to value-chain analysis.[95][51]

Earthquake-strengthening timing created a further demand-timing risk. On 19 April 2024 the Government extended statutory deadlines for owners of earthquake-prone buildings by four years, with a possible additional two-year extension; more than 200 of nearly 500 affected buildings were in Wellington. The change deferred, rather than removed, a potential source of strengthening work in which fabricated steel can be required.[84]

Derived — Projects under construction or in procurement accounted for **45.4%** of Te Waihanga’s NZ$121.2 billion March 2024 infrastructure pipeline: NZ$55.0 billion divided by NZ$121.2 billion. This quantifies the share at delivery or procurement stages.[41]

Derived — Expected infrastructure investment fell by **NZ$0.5 billion (4.1%)**, from NZ$12.1 billion in 2024 to NZ$11.6 billion in 2025. This points to a slightly smaller near-term annual infrastructure spend profile.[41]

Headline infrastructure expenditure is not the same as addressable structural-fabrication demand: the timing of specific procurement packages and their fabricated-steel content determine the work a local fabricator can bid for.

Residential consents were down 22% year on year through July 2024, and non-residential consents were down 12%, reinforcing the utilisation and cash-flow risk from a weakening project pipeline.[62]

Fletcher Building’s FY23 annual report described its Construction division orderbook as including alliance projects, framework agreements and maintenance agreements, which it characterised as lower-risk contract forms. These contract forms offer a route to reduce contract risk in project-pipeline work.[96]

Greater visibility and certainty of the infrastructure and public-housing construction pipeline, combined with alliance projects, framework agreements and maintenance agreements, which Fletcher Building characterised as lower-risk contract forms, can support fabricators’ workload and capacity planning.[96][95]

### Certification and productivity

Certification, workforce capability and off-site productivity are responses that help fabricators realise the tariff/import, supply-resilience and project-utilisation opportunities: they turn local proximity into documented conformity, reliable procurement and faster project delivery.[1] SFC is an independent quality-assurance scheme: it certifies fabrication and component-manufacturing companies’ quality management systems so they can consistently produce compliant fabricated product.[90][40][1] SFC says structural-steel compliance otherwise relies largely on self-inspection and self-certification, which depend on the expertise and ethics of fabricators, engineers and welding inspectors.[89]

Certification coverage is high by SCNZ’s reported measures. SCNZ’s November 2024 update stated that 70 structural-steel fabrication companies participating in SFC represented approximately 96% of annual sector output. Earlier March 2023 material cited 57 participating firms and two different output shares—92.5% in one statement and 90% in another—so the figures show broad coverage but not a consistent time series.[1][7]

This creates both a cost and an opportunity. Maintaining documented procedures, competent supervision, welding controls, inspection records and traceability requires management time and investment. Yet these are commercial assets in projects where a main contractor or engineer must demonstrate that the completed frame satisfies the design and regulatory requirements. Imported prefabricated steel can compete on initial price, but SFC positions independently verified local fabrication as a differentiated offer where provenance and compliance are procurement priorities.[89][90]

Productivity is closely linked to that compliance proposition. SCNZ says investment in fabrication technology and the use of simple bolted connections have lifted local-industry productivity. Bolted connections are steel members joined mechanically with bolts rather than welded on site; standardising them can shift more controlled work into the workshop and reduce site labour. SCNZ also says technology-led, highly automated design and manufacturing produces precision components, while preassembled delivery reduces site installation time and labour.[40]

The customer-facing benefit is programme certainty. SCNZ states that overall programmes for multilevel commercial projects using structural steel are generally 10–15% shorter than comparable programmes using other materials, attributing this to off-site manufacture and just-in-time delivery. It also says steel’s lower weight can allow less substantial, cheaper foundations and that steel frames permit long, column-free spans and later modification. These are whole-project advantages rather than measured fabrication-margin gains, but they help a fabricator defend value against competing materials and imports.[32][40]

Early Contractor Involvement makes these benefits more usable. SCNZ says ECI lets fabricators contribute to design-team discussions on buildability, timeframes and risk, and associates the approach with fewer variations and better planning. Grayson Engineering similarly told MBIE that early local engagement enabled it to incorporate quality and design elements, meet New Zealand seismic standards, improve efficiency and accommodate late design changes.[1][10]

Labour and capital remain linked constraints. MBIE’s 2018 manufacturing report said manufacturers struggled to recruit suitably skilled tradespeople and that trade vacancies were among the hardest to fill. SCNZ reported in 2023 that more than 10% of local structural-steel-contractor workforces were in training programmes and that 70% of contractors employed an average of five apprentices; RLB reported in 2024 that a prolonged construction downturn risked losing local skills and investment to other regions or countries.[10][2][41]

SCNZ reported that 9.1% of structural-steel contractors’ workforce were apprentices or in other training programmes in 2022.[103]

Judgment — Fabricators should treat digital detailing, automated cutting and drilling, repeatable bolted systems, apprentice development and SFC systems as one operating model. Automation without capable trades, inspection discipline and early design input can increase throughput but not necessarily profitable, compliant delivery.

**What follows:** productivity investment is commercially strongest when certification, engineering collaboration and workforce development turn workshop efficiency into shorter, lower-risk project delivery rather than simply lower unit cost.[40][1][2]

SFC provides a defined assurance route rather than a generic quality claim. The designer assigns Construction Category CC1, CC2, CC3 or CC4 to a project or component according to the required quality controls, documentation, traceability, inspection and testing. Fabricators demonstrate to an independent auditing body that their systems control materials, traceability and welding, are certified for a stated Construction Category and may perform work at that category or below; builders must engage a fabricator certified for the category specified by the designer. Certification runs for five years, subject to annual surveillance audits.[37]

The certification system is also a productivity and market-development opportunity. SCNZ states that strong process control typically delivers “Better productivity, less rework”, and that independent verification differentiates a fabricator and establishes credibility in new markets. It identifies SFC as a recognised mark of quality for locally fabricated structural steelwork and a point of difference from imported product.[37]

Higher-value processing is a complementary route to productivity and margin resilience. In its FY23 annual report, Steel & Tube reported that new growth initiatives accounted for 10% of Distribution EBIT and that its enhanced plate-processing service recorded a 70% EBIT increase over a short period. This supports a strategy of pairing structural fabrication with controlled processing and value-added services rather than competing only on steel volume.[43]

Workforce retention remains necessary to realise automation and certification benefits. The national infrastructure strategy identifies historic workforce shortages across infrastructure planning and delivery, gives estimates of around 118,000 additional construction workers needed by 2024 and a 118,500-worker shortfall in 2024, and states that clearer planned-investment certainty enables investment in training, technology and innovation.[97]

Workforce risk is material alongside certification requirements. Stats NZ’s 2020 Business Operations Survey found that 76% of construction businesses experienced moderate to severe difficulty recruiting tradespersons and related workers, including apprentices. Te Waihanga identified labour shortages as a major driver of higher construction costs and a constraint on addressing the infrastructure deficit, estimating that 57,000 additional workers would be needed over 30 years to fully address it.[98][95]

The construction workforce has also relied increasingly on migration during periods of expansion: temporary and permanent migrants accounted for 6.8% of the construction workforce in 2012 and 13.7% in 2019. City Rail Link was delivered by international and local firms and relied on migrant workers with specialised tunnelling and railway skills that are difficult to obtain locally; Watercare’s Central Interceptor similarly relied on migrants for specialised roles. Competition from higher-paid Australian infrastructure work added a further retention and cost risk.[95]

Building information modelling (BIM)—a shared digital model that enables architects, engineers, builders and contractors to collaborate on design—adds to the productivity opportunity alongside workshop automation. The Productivity Commission identified BIM and off-site prefabrication as the innovations with greatest potential to affect construction labour requirements and productivity; factory production using standardised processes can require less skilled labour per unit of output through scale, technology and automation.[95]

Material substitution is a parallel competitive risk. The Productivity Commission identified engineered timber, including cross-laminated timber, as a replacement for structural steel that can reduce construction cost and time. Fabricators therefore need to defend steel’s programme, span, adaptability and assurance advantages at design stage, rather than treating structural-steel demand as fixed.[95][32][40]

The productivity opportunity is significant because construction-wide performance has been weak: labour productivity grew 0.5% a year and multifactor productivity 0.4% a year from 2000 to 2020. In the year ended August 2020, building and construction research-and-development expenditure was NZ$12 million, compared with NZ$836 million in manufacturing, and 93.4% of construction businesses reported no or only minor technology changes.[95][98]

MBIE’s 2023 construction-sector report records BIM use on New Zealand construction projects rising from 34% in 2014 to 70% in 2021. It defines BIM as a coordinated set of processes, supported by technology, that add value through the sharing of structured information for buildings and infrastructure assets.[17]

The three risk–opportunity pairs point to one strategic direction: compete less as a commodity steel processor and more as a locally accountable delivery partner. Tariff and import exposure calls for a compliance- and programme-led offer; supply-chain concentration calls for qualified, traceable and resilient sourcing; and a weak project pipeline calls for early pursuit of profitable infrastructure, rebuild and adaptive-reuse workload. Certification, ECI and productivity investment are the operating responses that support all three.[72] Imported inputs and fabricated packages can pressure bids, particularly when overseas prices are lower, but global supply, exchange-rate and conformance risks create value for audited sourcing and certified local fabrication.[72][89][33][1][54][40] Weak private construction demand made capacity utilisation and bid discipline the immediate challenge in 2024, while the infrastructure pipeline, rebuild work and adaptive reuse offered selective replacement demand.

Certification, ECI and automation are the strongest response because they address both sides of the tension: they make local fabrication faster and more predictable while giving procurers evidence of quality and compliance.[33][89][41][54][1][40]

1. **Protect input resilience:** qualify multiple compliant supply routes, use exchange and price-risk controls, and price verification and delivery risk into imported-package comparisons.
2. **Protect utilisation:** target infrastructure, resilience and adaptive-reuse work early through ECI, but maintain bid discipline in a weak private pipeline.
3. **Monetise local capability:** use SFC, traceability, automated workshop production and trained tradespeople to sell programme certainty and compliant delivery, not merely fabricated tonnage.

A complete 2018–2024 annual series remains open for construction-only structural-fabrication revenue, total workforce, and tonnes actually fabricated. It would be settled by consistently defined annual measures that separate shop fabrication from steelmaking, distribution, erection, and non-construction metalwork.
