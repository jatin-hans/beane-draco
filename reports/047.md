# Lithium Extraction Methods and Environmental Footprints, 2015–2024

## Executive summary

The defensible 2015–2024 verdict is that lower-impact transition was incremental, not wholesale: Atacama remained dependent on solar brine evaporation, Australian supply on hard-rock spodumene mining, and Chinese salt-lake operators diversified among brine-separation methods.

Atacama’s pond footprint was demonstrably large, with SQM reporting 3,227 hectares and Albemarle’s 1,043-hectare permitted configuration for 94,000 t/y LCE implying 111 m² of ponds per annual tonne of permitted capacity; this is the land-use burden that DLE can in principle reduce, not proof of equivalent commercial DLE displacement.[1][2][3] Water intensity cannot be ranked responsibly across the three settings from gross reported flows because Atacama disclosures distinguish brine extraction from freshwater use, while hard-rock and salt-lake routes use different water categories and process boundaries.

Measured Atacama brine drawdown and linked aquifer and wetland risks make hydrological management central there, whereas Australia’s principal mine-site liabilities are disturbed land, waste rock, tailings, water management and energy-intensive conversion.[2][4][5] Regulation made pumping, monitoring, closure and rehabilitation financially material, but 2024 suspension of Albemarle’s Kemerton expansion shows that technically feasible lower-impact or more local processing still required viable lithium prices and costs.[6]

## Extraction methods and environmental footprints, 2015–2024

Lithium extraction did not converge on a single “best” method between 2015 and 2024. Chile’s Salar de Atacama retained solar evaporation of pumped brine; Australia expanded open-pit spodumene mining and concentration; and Chinese salt-lake operators pursued a wider set of brine-separation routes, including adsorption and membrane-based systems. Their environmental burdens therefore sit in different places: alteration of salar brine and wetland-linked groundwater in Chile, land disturbance, tailings and energy-intensive mineral processing in Australia, and chemical-, freshwater- and materials-intensive separation in China.

**The central conclusion is that 2015–2024 brought process optimisation and capacity expansion much more than wholesale replacement of incumbent extraction methods.** Conventional ponds remained dominant in Atacama; hard-rock mining remained the Australian production base; and Chinese DLE-type methods were developing unevenly across salt lakes rather than replacing all evaporation and precipitation routes.

### How do the environmental footprints of Chile’s Atacama Desert

Atacama’s two operating lithium producers during the period were SQM and Albemarle. Both used conventional salar-brine extraction: wells pump lithium-bearing brine from the salt flat’s subsurface aquifer to surface ponds, solar evaporation concentrates the brine, sequential salts precipitate, and the concentrated lithium solution then undergoes chemical purification and conversion. This is not direct lithium extraction (DLE): it is an evaporation-based concentration route whose production cycle is measured in many months rather than hours or days. SQM’s route precipitated sodium chloride and potassium chloride before concentrating the lithium-bearing solution to approximately 6% lithium; Albemarle similarly concentrated brine to approximately 6% lithium before transfer to La Negra for chemical purification and lithium-carbonate conversion.[1][2]

SQM describes pumping Salar de Atacama brine from depths of 1.5–150 metres and concentrating it to as much as 6% lithium, or 30–35% lithium chloride.[8][56][123]

SQM reported pumping brine from depths of 1.5–150 m and concentrating it in staged ponds for 13–16 months, reaching up to 6% lithium or 30–35% lithium chloride before chemical conversion.[56][123]

SQM described pumping Salar de Atacama brine from depths of 1.5–150 metres; its concentrated brine reached up to 6% lithium, equivalent to 30–35% lithium chloride, before chemical conversion.[8][56][123]

Albemarle’s brine route was mature before 2015 and remained the operating system through 2024. At Salar de Atacama, it pumped raw brine from the aquifer through wells and trenches, transferred it to a series of solar evaporation ponds, and moved the concentrated solution to the La Negra plant near Antofagasta. Albemarle states that this extraction method had operated since 1983 or 1984, depending on the report version, and that pond concentration took approximately 18–24 months.[4][7] At La Negra, boron, calcium and magnesium were removed before conversion to lithium carbonate and lithium chloride, including technical- and battery-grade carbonate.[3][4]

SQM likewise retained a pond-based route. Its Salar de Atacama operation extracted brine through vertical pumping wells, directed it to gathering ponds, evaporation ponds and metallurgical plants, and used sequential salt precipitation to concentrate lithium-bearing brine.[8][1] The operating method therefore shared Atacama’s defining environmental mechanism with Albemarle: removal of brine from a connected aquifer system and concentration of that brine over large surface areas.

SQM’s solar-concentration circuit precipitated halite, sylvinite, potassium carnallite, bischofite and lithium carnallite, with raw materials and products exchanged between its lithium and potassium circuits.[8][123]

The physical scale of that legacy system was substantial. SQM reported approximately 3,227 hectares of evaporation ponds in 2021 within an OMA extraction area of 81,920 hectares.[1] Albemarle reported two different pond configurations because its disclosure described both an expansion-stage configuration and a broader environmentally approved configuration: four square kilometres for approximately 45,000 tonnes per year of LCE output, expanding to 8.4 square kilometres, or 836 hectares, for a target above 80,000 tonnes per year with its Salar Yield Improvement Program; a separate permit description reported approximately 1,043 hectares of solar evaporation ponds supporting 94,000 tonnes per year of permitted LCE capacity.[4][2] These figures should not be collapsed into a single universal pond-area intensity because they refer to different capacity and operating configurations.

Atacama producers did improve recovery within this conventional route. Such improvements matter because every percentage point of lithium recovered from the same brine volume can raise output without proportionately increasing brine pumping or pond expansion. SQM reported that historic lithium yield had been around 43%, rising to 56% through process improvements by production and research teams. Its report distinguished “global yield,” reported as 42.98%, 42.89% and 42.80% in 2019, 2020 and 2021, from “IGS yield,” a lithium-production-system measure reported as 43.70%, 54.50% and 50.70% in those years.[1] These are different denominators and should not be treated as a single comparable recovery series.

SQM modelled 211 million m³ of brine pumping over a seven-year reserve simulation, with average lithium-process efficiency of approximately 52% after pond-recovery factors.[8]

SQM’s recovery programme included well differentiation, salt handling and reagent changes. It differentiated lower-chloride wells with lithium concentration around 0.6%, enabling direct entry into the evaporation system after halite precipitation and improving well availability.[1] It tested bischofite-salt squeezing platforms from 2018; the company said the method could increase lithium yield by 3%, with 320,000 m² of platforms, five installed by the end of 2021 and two further platforms implemented in 2022.[1] SQM estimated that these platforms could recover 8,942 tonnes of LCE and add 3.0% to lithium yield; improved salt harvesting was estimated to recover a further 1,091 tonnes LCE and add 0.4%.[1] A further planned calcium-chloride treatment sought to prevent lithium loss as lithium sulfate by precipitating calcium sulfate instead; SQM estimated 9,100 tonnes LCE per year of recovery and a 3.1% lithium-system yield benefit.[1]

SQM also investigated lithium-sulfate recovery and sulfate-removal methods intended to reduce lithium losses within its pond-based process.[124]

Albemarle’s corresponding programme was the Salar Yield Improvement Program (SYIP), a modification to the evaporation route rather than a conversion to DLE. The programme used mechanical grinding, washing and vat leaching of harvested bischofite and lithium-carnallite salts to recover entrained lithium while limiting magnesium dissolution.[2][3] Historical pond recovery was around 50%, with a range from below 40% to the mid-50% range.[2][3] K-UTEC’s 2017 testwork supported Albemarle’s assumption that recovery could reach around 65%, while SRK considered 65% more realistic than an idealised 80% case but noted gaps in the mass-balance and representativeness evidence.[2][3]

The projected efficiency gains were economically important to Albemarle’s contracted resource base. Its technical report stated that the ability to extract the full production quota before expiry depended on increasing evaporation-pond recovery from around 40% to about 65%.[2][4] Yet the reported result remained a forecast during much of the period: an earlier plan targeted operation in 2021, was delayed in depressed lithium-market conditions, and reserve modelling assumed two salt-treatment plants would operate from 2024.[2] The 2024 reserve model later used a 40% to 60% recovery ramp through 2027, rather than a confirmed 65% achievement in 2024.[6]

The Atacama footprint is not solely pond area. It is a hydrological footprint imposed on an arid closed basin whose marginal wetlands and lagoons depend on groundwater discharge. The relevant distinction is between **brine extraction**, which removes saline mineral-bearing water from the salar core, and **freshwater extraction**, which supplies industrial processing and can affect alluvial and marginal aquifer systems. They are separate water categories and cannot be added or compared as if they were the same kind of water use.

Albemarle’s approved brine-extraction rate increased from 142 L/s to 442 L/s after quarterly increases began in October 2016.[3] Its 2016 environmental approval included an increase of 300 L/s to 442 L/s, 16.9 L/s freshwater pumping from Tucucaro and Tilopozo wells, and an increase in solar-evaporation surface from 326 to 836 hectares; the project began operating on September 28, 2016.[2] For resource modelling, 442 L/s equated to approximately 14 million m³ of extracted brine in a 365-day year, which the report expressed as 171,000 tonnes LCE at an average lithium concentration of 0.20%.[4]Derived — **that is approximately 82 m³ of extracted brine per tonne LCE in the stated theoretical contained-lithium basis, but it is not a freshwater-consumption figure and it excludes recovery losses.**

Hydrological effects were documented most clearly in the Atacama setting. Brine levels fell near extraction wells, with drawdown reaching several metres in some locations and declining with distance from wells.[2][4] The water-balance model calibrated for 2015–2020 reported average total brine extraction of 2,059 L/s from the salt-flat nucleus, against 400 L/s evaporation, 742 L/s storage outflow, 707 L/s direct recharge, 466 L/s brine underflow and 2,024 L/s storage inflow.[1] These are basin-model terms, not measurements of freshwater consumed per tonne of product.

A later baseline for SQM’s Extraction Reduction Plan found that water extraction between 1986 and 2020 had affected water levels in the salar core and alluvial aquifer system. The largest core declines were on the western edge, while drawdown cones occurred around extraction wells in the alluvial aquifer; the marginal-zone decrease was described as insignificant.[5] The EIA also identified vegetation-cover loss from lower aquifer levels as an impact requiring explanation and mitigation.[5] In the Aguas de Quelana system, a Phase II indicator was activated in September 2022; research reports attributed the decrease to SQM brine extraction, to a lesser extent Albemarle extraction, and low basin rainfall during 2020–2022.[8]

Atacama’s monitoring architecture grew more intensive during the period. SQM’s RCA No. 226/2006 monitoring network covered 225 points: 196 groundwater wells, five industrial-water pumping wells, 18 surface-water rulers, four gauging stations and two meteorological stations. It measured brine and water-table levels, lagoon levels and surfaces, pumping volumes, water chemistry and recharge.[8] Monitoring of contingency-plan and Peine indicators increased from monthly to daily from December 1, 2018.[5] Albemarle’s model calibration used 48,265 measurements from 176 wells between November 1997 and December 2021 and reported a 4.77% scaled root-mean-square error for the 2021 average comparison.[4]

SQM’s 2021 update to the Early Warning and Environmental Monitoring Plan under RCA 226/2006 addressed Soncor, Aguas de Quelana, Peine and eastern-margin vegetation, expanded monitoring coverage, and recognized declining algarrobo-tree vitality near Camar-2, with monitoring and reforestation measures.[126][123][125]

Albemarle reported 150 real-time monitoring wells and periodic participation by an Indigenous-community representative.[126][123][125]

Atacama’s environmental footprint is consequently best understood as a connected-system problem. Ponds add land occupation and evaporative water loss; extraction changes brine levels; freshwater withdrawals can alter alluvial-aquifer conditions; and sensitive lagoons and vegetation depend on groundwater conditions. Neither gross pumping nor a single water-intensity number captures that full interaction.

**The Atacama result is a mature solar-evaporation industry that raised recovery and constrained pumping while retaining its defining footprint: extensive ponds and intervention in a hydrologically connected salar system.**

### Australia’s hard-rock mines

Australian lithium production differs fundamentally from Atacama production. The resource is generally spodumene, a lithium-bearing hard-rock mineral. Ore is mined from open pits or underground workings, crushed, ground and physically separated to make spodumene concentrate. This mine-and-concentrator route has no brine-evaporation ponds. Its environmental footprint is instead dominated by mine disturbance, waste rock, tailings, groundwater or surface-water management, dust, ore processing and—where chemical conversion occurs—high-temperature and acid-intensive refining.

Greenbushes used heavy-media separation, wet high-intensity magnetic separation, flotation and filtration to produce chemical- and technical-grade spodumene concentrates.[127]

At Greenbushes, Western Australia, the operating footprint consisted of an open-pit mine and three mineral-processing plants: two chemical-grade plants and one technical-grade plant. Albemarle described site water as sourced from rainfall and stored in process dams.[6] The operating open-pit mining and processing area covered approximately 3,500 hectares, including mining, tailings storage, processing plants, open pits and waste-rock dumps.[6][17] Greenbushes was already a mature producer at the start of the period; lithium production had been continuous for more than 20 years.[18]

Greenbushes’ mineral-concentration flowsheet includes heavy-media separation, magnetic separation, flotation and filtration after crushing and grinding.[127]

Greenbushes’ concentrate-making circuit included crushing, classification, heavy-media separation, magnetic separation, flotation and filtration, followed by tailings storage.[127]

At Wodgina, the method was also open-pit hard-rock mining and spodumene concentration. Mineral Resources upgraded the site to a 750,000-tonnes-per-year spodumene plant producing 6% spodumene concentrate, completed in 2019.[18] Albemarle acquired a 60% stake in 2019, production was idled in the weaker market, and concentrate production resumed in the second quarter of 2022.[19][6] The restart shows that Australian hard-rock capacity did not simply expand in a straight line: supply responded to market conditions as well as geology and plant capability.

Pilgangoora became another major Western Australian hard-rock operation during the expansion wave after 2016. The broader market record identifies Pilbara Minerals’ Pilgangoora among the Australian start-ups of 2017–18 and notes subsequent expansion by Pilbara Minerals.[3] Pilbara Minerals also pursued a Mid-stream Demonstration Plant with Calix in FY24, focused on “sustainable lithium extraction and processing.” That was a technology-development initiative, not a demonstrated replacement of open-pit mining.[20]

Pilbara Minerals produced 620.1 kt of spodumene concentrate at Pilgangoora in FY2023 and 725,329 dry metric tonnes in FY2024 as its P680 expansion took effect.[128][20]

Hard-rock mining’s principal intermediate product is chemical-grade spodumene concentrate. Albemarle described hard-rock ore feeding mines at 0.5–2.5% lithium oxide and leaving them as 6% Li2O concentrate.[21] Wodgina’s concentrate is likewise specified as 6% spodumene concentrate.[18] This intermediate product occupies a different supply-chain position from Atacama’s concentrated lithium chloride brine. It must still be chemically converted into carbonate or hydroxide before battery use.

Judgment — **Hard-rock conversion normally begins with thermal conversion of alpha-spodumene to the more reactive beta-spodumene, followed by acid roasting, leaching, impurity removal and precipitation or crystallisation of lithium chemicals. The high-temperature roasting and acid circuit create a more energy- and reagent-intensive downstream step than solar brine concentration, although modern plants can recover heat, water and reagents.**

Pilbara Minerals reported 149,817 tonnes CO₂-equivalent of Scope 1 and 2 emissions at Pilgangoora in FY2023, up from 106,000 tonnes CO₂-equivalent in FY2022; the remote operation relied primarily on diesel because it lacked grid power.[129]

Pilgangoora’s 6 MW solar plant was commissioned in December 2022 and was expected to displace approximately 3.8 million litres of diesel per year and avoid 9,900 tonnes CO₂-equivalent of emissions annually.[129]

Talison reported Greenbushes Scope 1 and 2 emissions of 203,182 tonnes CO₂-equivalent in calendar year 2024, equivalent to 0.144 tonnes CO₂-equivalent per tonne of lithium mineral concentrate.[128]

Albemarle’s Australian supply chain illustrates both integration and its economic difficulty. Greenbushes concentrate was fully consumed by the mine’s joint-venture owners—Albemarle and Tianqi/IGO—for downstream conversion.[3] Talison shipped chemical-grade concentrate to Albemarle facilities in China and Australia for processing into battery-grade lithium hydroxide, while tolling entities in China could convert it into carbonate or hydroxide.[6] Albemarle therefore participated not only in mining but in the downstream chemical network.

Kemerton in Western Australia was designed to bring part of that conversion chain onshore. Operations began in 2022 using acid baking to make lithium hydroxide monohydrate, with stated capacity of 50,000 tonnes per year; it received spodumene concentrate from Greenbushes.[22] Yet the period also showed the challenge of Australian conversion economics.

In 2024, Albemarle stopped construction of Kemerton Trains 3 and 4, placed Train 2 in care and maintenance, and kept Train 1 operating.[6] Albemarle explicitly linked the decision to the lithium-price environment, saying expanded Australian conversion was less economical at prevailing prices.[6]

The financial scale of this retrenchment was large. Albemarle reported US$296.2 million of capital expenditure for Kemerton Trains 3 and 4 in 2024, US$535.7 million in 2023 and US$44.0 million in 2022.[6] It also recorded US$62.3 million in contract-cancellation and take-or-pay charges, alongside employee-related charges, for stopping construction and placing capacity into care and maintenance.[6] Its 2024 restructuring charges and asset write-offs totalled US$1.134 billion, primarily linked to stopping Kemerton Trains 3 and 4; the filing separately described US$1.0 billion of charges from assets with no future economic value after the decision.[6] The outcome is clear: vertical integration was technologically feasible, but not always commercially viable under 2024 lithium pricing and Australian cost conditions.

Rehabilitation is more visible in the Australian hard-rock model because mining creates open pits, waste-rock landforms and tailings facilities that must be managed and closed. Talison reviewed rehabilitation-liability costs quarterly and contributed to Western Australia’s Mining Rehabilitation Fund, which can finance rehabilitation of abandoned mines if an operator fails to fulfil its obligation and recovery efforts fail.[24] Under the Mining Act 1978, Talison was required to maintain permits, contribute annually to the statutory fund and pay a royalty on lithium-concentrate sales.[19] At Wodgina, Albemarle and Mineral Resources agreed to share rehabilitation obligations according to ownership interests, apart from specified pre-completion and reserved-mineral liabilities.[25]

The applicable Western Australian framework combined the Mining Act 1978 and Environmental Protection Act 1986, supplemented by project-specific approvals and licences. Pilbara Minerals reported that Pilgangoora was assessed and approved under Part V of the Environmental Protection Act and the Mining Act, with annual independent compliance audits and no material licence breaches in FY23.[26] Greenbushes also operated under state and Commonwealth approvals, including the EPBC Act, Environmental Protection Act, Mining Act and an approved Mine Closure Plan.[3] These frameworks required closure planning, environmental management and compliance monitoring; the producer records do not attribute recovery improvements directly to those rules.

**The Australian result is an expanded hard-rock industry whose environmental burden is principally land, tailings, water management and energy-intensive conversion, with state closure and rehabilitation obligations making those costs explicit rather than optional.**

### China’s salt lakes

China’s salt-lake industry cannot be treated as a simple replica of Chilean pond evaporation. Its operations are spread across lakes with materially different lithium concentrations, magnesium-to-lithium ratios, co-products and climatic conditions. Those chemical differences determine whether evaporation, precipitation, adsorption, solvent extraction, membranes or electrochemical separation is commercially attractive.

Chinese salt-lake processing routes during 2015–2024 also included solvent extraction, electrodialysis and reverse osmosis alongside solar ponds, adsorption and nanofiltration.[132][133]

Qinghai salt-lake brines are predominantly sulfate-type and commonly magnesium-rich, while Tibet has both sulfate- and carbonate-type salt lakes.[132]

Judgment — **The magnesium-to-lithium ratio is a central process variable in salt-lake lithium extraction. A high ratio means abundant magnesium must be separated from relatively scarce lithium, making simple precipitation more difficult and often increasing reagents, water use, equipment complexity or recovery losses.**

The policy setting favoured more intensive use and upgrading of salt-lake resources. Qinghai’s provincial spatial plan for 2021–2035 prioritised integrated, orderly and efficient salt-lake-resource development and salt-lake-industry upgrading to build a world-class salt-lake industrial base.[31] Qinghai’s government and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology jointly issued the Action Plan for Building a World-Class Salt Lake Industrial Base in Qinghai, 2021–2035, which the Qinghai Salt Lake Industry annual report described as providing industrial, tax, fiscal and financial support for resource integration and industrial upgrading.[32] Earlier provincial policies named in a 2018 National Energy Administration report included the Qinghai Thirteenth Five-Year New Materials Industry Development Plan, policies supporting new materials, and Interim Measures for Development and Utilisation of Qinghai Salt Lake Lithium Resources.[33]

Those policies encouraged technological upgrading but did not impose one single extraction technology. Conventional solar concentration and precipitation continued where brine chemistry and climate permitted them. Other Qinghai operations developed adsorption and membrane separation systems to isolate lithium from high-magnesium brines. The resulting environmental footprint changes by route rather than disappearing.

Chinese salt-lake routes during 2015–2024 also included reverse osmosis, electrodialysis and membrane–crystallisation alongside evaporation, adsorption, nanofiltration and solvent extraction.[133][134]

- **Evaporation and precipitation:** Solar concentration can rely heavily on climate energy and avoid some thermal energy demand, but it occupies land, retains brine for long periods, exposes operations to weather and leakage risks, and may generate salt residues or chemical treatment streams. Atacama-style ponds are therefore only one kind of salt-lake footprint, not a universal feature of all Chinese operations.

- **Adsorption:** An adsorbent selectively captures lithium ions from brine, after which an eluent strips lithium from the material for downstream purification. Adsorption can reduce dependence on long solar-pond cycles and may suit low-lithium or high-magnesium brines, but it requires repeated adsorption/desorption cycles, elution water, consumable adsorbent and management of fouling or degradation.Judgment — **Adsorption’s environmental advantage is conditional: it can reduce pond area and some reagent demand, but it may shift burden toward freshwater, energy and solid consumables.**

- **Membrane separation:** Nanofiltration and related membranes use selective transport to separate ions, particularly to manage magnesium and other impurities. They can reduce some chemical inputs, but membrane fouling, replacement, cleaning chemicals, pressure energy and concentrate disposal are material operating constraints.Judgment — **A membrane plant should be assessed as a water-and-materials system, not simply as a “pond-free” system.**

- **Electrochemical extraction:** Electrochemical methods use an electric potential to selectively insert, transport or release lithium. They promise targeted separation and potentially lower chemical consumption, but their commercial scale depends on electrode lifetime, energy use, impurity tolerance and sustained selectivity.Judgment — **By 2024, electrochemical lithium extraction remained less mature at industrial scale than conventional evaporation or adsorption in Chinese salt lakes.**

The source material supports that Chinese operations were pursuing industrial upgrading but does not provide a common set of site-level water, land or aquifer metrics for salt lakes comparable with the Atacama records. The appropriate comparative conclusion is therefore methodological: China had the broadest extraction-technology portfolio among the three settings, but this diversity prevents a single national environmental-footprint number from being meaningful.

China’s salt-lake development also sat within a broader national and provincial industrial-policy objective: supplying lithium chemicals and battery materials, not merely extracting raw brine. Qinghai’s 2018 guideline set a target of 170,000 tonnes per year of lithium-carbonate production and 60 GWh per year of battery-cell capacity by 2025.[33] These were policy targets, not confirmed end-2024 production outcomes.

Environmental governance for Chinese salt lakes included the Qinghai Province Salt Lake Resources Development and Protection Regulations, effective from August 1, 2001, and related frameworks identified as the River and Lake Chief System, Water Resources Management Regulations, Water Withdrawal and Utilization Management Measures, and Ecological Management Regulations.[34] This framework combines resource-development policy with ecological and water-management instruments. It differs from the Chilean model of individual RCA pumping approvals and from Western Australia’s mine-closure and rehabilitation-fund model.

The direct social-conflict record is also different. The source material identifies a broader dispute over ownership of Tibetan lithium between the Indigenous population and the Chinese government.[35] It does not identify a named Indigenous water-rights dispute tied to a particular Qinghai salt-lake operation during 2015–2024. That distinction matters: a general contest over resource ownership should not be represented as a documented local water-rights case at every Chinese salt lake.

The central environmental contrast is therefore not “brine is clean and rock is dirty,” or the reverse. Solar evaporation reduces the need for high-temperature concentration energy but requires large pond systems and interacts directly with a salar’s water balance. Hard-rock mining avoids salar-brine depletion and pond acreage, but creates mine disturbance, tailings and a more energy-intensive physical and chemical processing chain. Chinese salt-lake systems can reduce dependence on conventional pond concentration where adsorption or membranes work, but often move environmental pressure into freshwater supply, chemical management, electricity and consumable materials.

Atacama has the clearest documented hydrological impact record. The region’s monitoring and technical reports record drawdown near wells, extraction-related effects on water levels, an activated early-warning indicator in Aguas de Quelana, and ongoing proposals to lower pumping.[2][5][8] That does not mean every local water-level change is attributable solely to mining: low rainfall was an identified aggravating factor in Aguas de Quelana, and the basin contains multiple extractors and interacting brine and freshwater systems.[8][4]

Australia’s records instead make the closure and land-management burden more visible. Greenbushes’ expansion approvals include water management, waste and tailings disposal, site monitoring and an approved Mine Closure Plan.[3] Talison’s statutory rehabilitation-fund obligation and quarterly review of rehabilitation liabilities show how Western Australian governance converts future closure risk into an operating financial responsibility.[24][19] The hydrological record is less developed in the cited material: water sources are identified, but no comparable aquifer-level trend is reported.

China’s environmental comparison must remain route-specific. Qinghai policy explicitly seeks orderly, efficient and integrated salt-lake use, and its resource-management framework includes water-withdrawal and ecological-management measures.[31][34] But an adsorption plant, an evaporation-and-precipitation plant and a membrane system cannot be ranked from policy labels alone.

Their environmental performance depends on brine chemistry, lithium recovery, freshwater sourcing, brine disposal or reinjection, reagent consumption, energy source, membrane or adsorbent life, and the quality of downstream waste management.

**The comparative result is that Chile carries the strongest documented salar-hydrology constraint, Australia the clearest mine-land and rehabilitation obligation, and China the broadest process-technology variation; no defensible overall “lowest-impact” ranking exists without harmonised site-level measures.**

Atacama’s high lithium concentrations and high evaporation rates support low-cost solar concentration, whereas Qinghai’s high magnesium-to-lithium brines make magnesium–lithium separation difficult and favour adsorption, membrane, extraction or calcination routes suited to high-magnesium brine.[50][41]

Zabuye’s high-lithium carbonate brine has very low magnesium content, making solar-pool concentration and crystallisation more favourable there than in Qinghai’s high-magnesium-to-lithium brines.[132][133]

On the criterion of land committed specifically to solar-evaporation ponds, Australian hard-rock mining ranks first because it has no brine ponds, Chinese salt-lake operations rank second because their separation route is site-specific, and conventional Atacama brine evaporation ranks third because SQM reported 3,227 ha of ponds and Albemarle reported configurations of 4–8.4 km².[1][3]

That pond-land ranking would change if measured freshwater consumption, attributed aquifer effects, full-route energy demand, or verified commercial DLE performance showed a different burden distribution among the routes.[14][13]

Albemarle reported average Salar de Atacama brine lithium concentration of 1,400 mg/L, with a range from 900 to nearly 7,000 mg/L, whereas Chinese salt-lake brines are mainly magnesium-sulfate subtype brines with high magnesium-to-lithium ratios.[3][51] (Technology; Song et al., 2017)[51]

The higher lithium concentration and exceptionally arid Atacama setting support prolonged solar concentration, while high magnesium relative to lithium in many Chinese brines makes selective magnesium removal and lithium recovery central to process choice.[3][51]

### How did the differing starting points and commitments of Chile’s

The starting resource explains much of the strategic difference. Atacama producers inherited highly developed brine infrastructure and a favourable evaporation environment. Their commercial problem was therefore how to recover more lithium from existing brine and pond inventories while operating within pumping limits, quotas and environmental monitoring. Albemarle’s SYIP and SQM’s salt-recovery projects fit that logic: both sought higher lithium recovery from an established evaporation system rather than an immediate replacement of that system.[2][1]

Chile’s contract and permit structure also shaped timing and scale. Albemarle’s 2016 agreement increased its pumping allocation from 142 L/s to 442 L/s and increased cumulative lithium-metal quota from 200,000 to 540,240 tonnes.[2] The agreement required investment in a third Chilean battery-grade lithium-carbonate plant with at least 20,000–24,000 tonnes per year of battery-grade LCE capacity by December 31, 2022, or the additional quota would be sharply reduced.[2] This linked access to resource value with downstream conversion capacity, strengthening Albemarle’s position as an integrated producer rather than a brine-only supplier.

SQM’s position was also that of an integrated lithium-chemical producer. It sold approximately 156,800 tonnes of lithium carbonate and hydroxide in 2022, which it described as about 20% of the lithium-chemicals market.[10] Its Atacama operational strategy combined pond-based extraction, lithium-recovery programmes, downstream chemical capacity and a planned reduction in brine pumping.[1][8] That combination made SQM’s competitive position less dependent on simply expanding wellfield extraction.

Albemarle’s position was geographically broader. In Chile it owned both Salar de Atacama and La Negra, producing brine and battery-grade carbonate.[3] In Australia it held stakes in Greenbushes and Wodgina hard-rock operations, while in China it owned conversion capacity at Qinzhou, Meishan, Chengdu and Xinyu.[6] Its supply chain therefore linked brine carbonate, Australian spodumene concentrate and Chinese and Australian hydroxide conversion. The company itself described the strategy as remaining vertically integrated from resources to advanced materials.[21]

That integration did not remove commercial exposure. Albemarle’s decision to scale back Kemerton in 2024 shows that a technically integrated chain can still be uneconomic when lithium prices fall and local conversion costs rise.[6] The more durable advantage was diversity: it could source lithium from Atacama brine and Australian hard rock, then convert materials across Chile, Australia and China.[6]

Ganfeng Lithium’s role differed again. The available producer comparison places Ganfeng’s estimated 2023 mine-site output at 30,000 tonnes LCE, with production origins in China, Australia and Argentina.[52] That geography is consistent with a diversified resource-and-conversion strategy rather than dependence on one salar or one hard-rock mine.

Its relative position in this comparison is therefore diversification across resource types and jurisdictions, though the supplied evidence does not establish a specific Chinese salt-lake operating footprint for Ganfeng.

Pilbara Minerals’ position was more concentrated in Australian hard rock. The same 2023 comparison estimates Pilbara mine-site output at 60,000 tonnes LCE and lists Australia as its production origin.[52] Its Pilgangoora operation was governed by the Western Australian Mining Act and Environmental Protection Act, with annual independent compliance audits reported in FY23.[26] Its FY24 Calix partnership signals an attempt to develop more sustainable mid-stream processing, but not a demonstrated commercial shift away from spodumene mining.[20]

Community and Indigenous relationships are most documented in Atacama. The operational area lies within the Atacama La Grande Indigenous Development Area and includes Atacameño/Lickanantay communities.[1][10] Albemarle had formal agreements from 2016 with the Council of Atacameño Peoples and 18 Atacameño communities, including 3.5% of Salar Plant lithium carbonate and potassium chloride sales and joint environmental monitoring and surveillance.[2] SQM maintained working groups and participatory monitoring arrangements with Camar, Toconao, Talabre and Socaire communities.[53][5]

These arrangements coexisted with conflict and regulatory scrutiny. Communities submitted observations and claims against SQM’s compliance programme, and the Council of Atacameño People rejected the National Lithium Strategy announced in April 2023, citing lack of dialogue and concern over CODELCO’s conduct.[8][5] Albemarle was sanctioned by the SMA with a fine of 4,713 Annual Tax Units for extracting brine above the authorised amount and failing to implement all early-warning-plan measures in the Alerta Acuífero sector; Albemarle challenged the resolution.[54] These are concrete disputes over extraction limits, ecosystem safeguards and consultation. They should not be reduced to a generic claim that every Indigenous community had the same water-rights conflict.

Australia also involves Indigenous land interests, but the supplied material does not identify a named Indigenous water-rights dispute tied to Greenbushes, Wodgina or Pilgangoora during 2015–2024. Albemarle acknowledges that its Chilean and Western Australian sites are on Indigenous Peoples’ lands and describes due diligence, formal agreements and grievance mechanisms.[6] That is a rights-management commitment, not evidence of a documented water-rights case.

China’s supplied record identifies a Tibet-wide contest over lithium ownership between Indigenous people and the Chinese government, but not a site-specific Indigenous water-rights dispute at a named salt-lake operation.[35] The appropriate conclusion is not that no social conflict exists, but that the documented disputes in this comparison are structurally different: Atacama’s record centres on pumping, wetlands, monitoring, consultation and community participation; Australia’s on land management and mine rehabilitation; and China’s on state-led resource governance and industrial development.

The producer-level positioning that follows is clear:

- **SQM:** A large, integrated Chilean brine-to-chemical producer whose competitive strength rests on Atacama pond infrastructure, recovery improvement, downstream lithium-carbonate and hydroxide sales, and scale. Its binding environmental issue is reducing extraction and safeguarding connected hydrological systems while maintaining chemical output. [10][1][8]

- **Albemarle:** The most geographically diversified producer in this comparison, combining Atacama brine carbonate, Australian spodumene interests and conversion plants in Chile, Australia and China. Its advantage is portfolio flexibility; its 2024 Kemerton retrenchment demonstrates that downstream localisation is constrained by processing economics. [6]

- **Ganfeng Lithium:** A diversified Chinese producer with mine-site production exposure across China, Australia and Argentina. Its strategic relevance is multi-region resource access rather than a documented single-site salt-lake advantage in the supplied record. [52]

- **Pilbara Minerals:** An Australian hard-rock specialist whose position rests on Pilgangoora spodumene output, Western Australian regulatory compliance and prospective mid-stream technology development. It remains primarily upstream relative to integrated brine-to-battery-chemical producers. [52][26][20]

Ganfeng Lithium acquired a 49% interest in Minmetals Salt Lake through its acquisition of Yili Hongda; Minmetals Salt Lake used nanofiltration membrane separation and had 10,000 tonnes per year of lithium-carbonate capacity.[55]

### Synthesis

Atacama, Australian hard rock and Chinese salt lakes followed different technological paths because their starting resources were different. Chile’s mature brine producers retained solar evaporation and improved recovery, while environmental constraints increasingly pushed lower pumping and tighter monitoring. Australia expanded open-pit spodumene production and sought downstream hydroxide conversion, but 2024 economics curtailed part of that conversion build-out. China’s salt-lake policy encouraged a broader family of extraction and separation technologies, making route-specific assessment essential. Across all three settings, the 2015–2024 transition was not from “high-impact old technology” to “low-impact new technology”; it was a shift toward higher recovery, more monitoring, more downstream integration and selectively deployed new separation methods.[2][8][3][6][31][32]

1. Treat brine withdrawal, freshwater withdrawal and water consumption as separate metrics in subsequent comparisons.
2. Compare pond land only among brine operations; compare mine disturbance, tailings and rehabilitation separately for hard-rock mines.
3. Treat DLE pilots, demonstrations and commercial production as distinct stages.
4. Assess producer positioning through both extraction resource and downstream conversion capacity, because battery-material value is created after extraction as well as at the mine or salar.

Albemarle’s La Negra chemical-processing site listed water consumption of 14.3 tonnes of water per tonne of LCE sold; this is a plant water-consumption intensity, distinct from Salar de Atacama brine extraction.[3]

**Across 2015–24, conventional ponds dominated Atacama, while Chinese salt-lake operations combined continuing evaporation with earlier commercial adsorption-based DLE deployment.** Atacama’s documented pond systems occupied hundreds to thousands of hectares and required more than a year of concentration time.[2][1][56] Chinese DLE projects had identifiable commercial capacity by 2017–19, but neither country has a sector-wide DLE-adoption percentage because project capacities, pilot counts, pond areas and production denominators measure different things.[11][62] The practical comparison is not “pond water use versus DLE water use”: ponds consume large land areas and evaporate brine, whereas DLE can shrink land demand but may require substantial freshwater for elution and more electricity.[12][59][13]

## Aquifer levels and Indigenous water rights, 2015–2024

Aquifer impacts and Indigenous water rights are linked but not interchangeable. An aquifer is a water-bearing geological formation; its water level responds to pumping, recharge, evaporation, seepage and seasonal conditions. Indigenous water-rights disputes concern who may decide over, use, protect or be consulted about water and water-connected territory. The 2015–2024 evidence shows the strongest quantified drawdown record in Chile’s Salar de Atacama brine system; Australia’s documented effects are more localised around tailings facilities; and China’s reported salt-lake impacts centre on depletion, wetland withdrawals and lake shrinkage.

### What Indigenous water rights disputes were connected to Chile’s Atacama

**The Salar de Atacama’s brine aquifer experienced extraction-related drawdown, concentrated around production wells.** The technical review states that SQM extracted brine at 1,700 L/s and Albemarle at 442 L/s; brine levels fell, with drawdown greatest near extraction wells, reaching several metres at some monitoring points and declining with distance.[2] Albemarle’s 2022 reserve assessment separately attributed drawdown of up to approximately 14 m in the salar’s southwest region to SQM pumping.[4]

The hydrogeological baseline for SQM’s Extraction Reduction Plan found that water extraction from 1986 to 2020 affected both the salar core and the alluvial aquifer system. The greatest core declines occurred on the western edge; alluvial-aquifer wells showed cones of depression—localised lowered water-level zones around pumping wells—while the marginal-zone decline was described as insignificant.[8] This longer baseline overlaps 2015–2020 but does not isolate those years.

Freshwater monitoring presents a more differentiated picture. Albemarle’s technical review reported that freshwater aquifer levels generally remained within historical values after allowing for seasonal evaporation-driven variation, while larger freshwater withdrawals had produced local reductions near Soncor, Aguas de Quelana and upstream of La Punta–La Brava. The review reported no significant lagoon-system or protected-ecosystem effects at that point.[4] At Camar-2, a 2017 monitoring account reported water-table decline from industrial-water extraction of no more than 2 m relative to natural conditions, except immediately around wells where several metres of decline could occur within roughly 100 m.[67]

Management thresholds were crossed in several sensitive systems. Soncor entered Phase II in 2019 and again in the first half of 2021; Soncor and East Edge Vegetation also recorded Phase II activations in 2022. The East Edge activation was attributed to a seasonal minimum followed by recovery, rather than collectively attributed to lithium pumping.[1] In Aguas de Quelana, the L5-10 indicator entered Phase II in September 2022; SQM’s research reports attributed the decline to SQM brine extraction, to a lesser extent Albemarle extraction, and low rainfall during 2020–2022.[8]

This makes the Chilean result spatially clear: core brine pumping produced substantial, well-proximate drawdown, while freshwater effects were localised around industrial-water abstraction and environmentally sensitive lagoon systems.

The PMB monitoring table recorded mixed freshwater-level movement rather than a uniform decline between 2015 and 2019.[68]

At La Punta–La Brava, BA-05 declined by 0.028 m, from 2,300.151 metres above sea level on 6 January 2015 to 2,300.123 metres above sea level on 19 March 2019.[68]

At La Punta–La Brava, BA-07 increased by 0.020 m, from 2,300.003 metres above sea level on 6 January 2015 to 2,300.023 metres above sea level on 19 March 2019.[68]

At La Punta–La Brava, BA-16 declined by 0.048 m, from 2,300.850 metres above sea level on 6 January 2015 to 2,300.802 metres above sea level on 19 March 2019.[68]

At Peine, BA-21 increased by 0.085 m, from 2,300.072 metres above sea level on 7 March 2015 to 2,300.157 metres above sea level on 24 March 2019.[68]

At Peine, BA-22 declined by 0.003 m, from 2,300.281 metres above sea level on 9 March 2015 to 2,300.278 metres above sea level on 24 March 2019.[68]

### What Indigenous water rights disputes were connected to Australian hard-rock

Australian hard-rock lithium mines do not pump brine or use evaporation ponds. Their principal groundwater pathways are pit dewatering, borefield supply, tailings-storage-facility seepage and recycling of mine water.

At the former Altura Lithium Pilgangoora operation in Western Australia, groundwater near the tailings storage facility (TSF) stood 15–18 metres below ground level before operations. At bore MB-01-02, it rose from 14 m below ground level in August 2018 to 8 m in July 2019, then declined to 9 m by November 2019. This is a 6 m shallowing followed by a 1 m deepening relative to the August 2018 reading.[69] The 2019 audit linked the local rise to initial tailings deposition and described the later reduction alongside beach development and increased solids deposition.[69]

At Greenbushes in Western Australia, the documented groundwater impact is principally a water-quality and seepage issue rather than a measured regional water-level decline. Tailings seepage was identified in six bores in the shallow paleochannel aquifer, while the deep aquifer was not affected in the cited assessment; later monitoring confirmed one seepage-affected well. Seepage from the TSF2 western embankment had been reported since 2015, and buried collector drains installed from 2017 returned seepage to the mine-water circuit.[3] The paleochannels are hydraulically connected with local wetlands and surface-water features, making seepage management significant beyond the TSF footprint.[3]

Greenbushes reported no groundwater withdrawal in calendar years 2022 or 2023, while water consumed was 2,484 ML and 2,749 ML respectively; its process-water system includes tailings-water recovery, recycling dams and a water-treatment plant commissioned in September 2021.[57][24] Pilbara Minerals likewise upgraded decant-water recovery and reuse in FY2022 to reduce abstraction from subsurface aquifers.[70]

The Australian pattern is therefore local groundwater mounding or seepage near tailings infrastructure, not the broad brine-aquifer drawdown characteristic of Salar de Atacama.

Talison states that Greenbushes process water comes from rainfall stored in on-site process-water dams and mining voids, and that process water is recovered and recycled.[23]

Talison identifies its surface-water storage dams, recharged by rainfall, as its sole raw-water source and identifies raw-water supply as a critical business risk.[57]

Talison monitors groundwater through bores around the Greenbushes process plants and tailings dams to track water quality.[23]

In the broader Pilgangoora project area, mining had locally altered groundwater levels through drawdown around pits and supply bores and groundwater mounding around existing tailings facilities; typical groundwater depth was 23–53 m below ground level.[71]

At Greenbushes, mine water came from captured precipitation runoff, mine sumps and recycled water from tailings storage facilities rather than groundwater aquifers through production or dewatering wells.[3]

### What documented impacts on local aquifer levels occurred in China’s

China’s salt-lake evidence is concentrated in Qinghai’s Qaidam Basin, where lithium, potash and other brine-mineral operations share highly water-constrained salt-lake systems. The reported impacts combine groundwater and brine depletion, lake contraction, wetland withdrawals and disrupted recharge.

At West Taijinar Salt Lake, continued extraction of shallow, high-grade inter-crystal brine—brine held between salt-crystal layers—is described as lowering brine levels. The same analysis warns that low salt-rock permeability, viscous brine and weak hydraulic gradients can slow recovery and form a persistent underground brine funnel, meaning a long-lived depleted zone around extraction.[34] That is a depletion mechanism relevant to aquifer response, although it is not expressed as a numerical water-level time series.

Surface-water loss is more concretely reported. A Qinghai inspection-rectification checklist reported that West and East Taijinar Lakes had nearly dried up and that Xidabuxun Lake’s water volume fell from 10.1 million m³ in 2003 to 460,000 m³ in 2018.[47] Another account reported that West Taijinar had dried by 2013, while East Taijinar nearly dried after river–lake connectivity was blocked during 2011–2017; it linked disrupted recharge and mineral-mining development to these outcomes.[34]

Water-withdrawal enforcement demonstrates continuing pressure during the requested period. In December 2023, China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment publicly criticised three major Qaidam brine-mining factories for breaching water-use quotas through illegal groundwater pumping and withdrawals from protected wetlands and lakes, and ordered the pumping stopped.[46] Qinghai Salt Lake Industry’s resource branch was reported to operate 16 surface-water pumping stations withdrawing 21.36 million m³ annually for equipment cleaning, while Mahai mining-area companies were reported to withdraw more than 7 million m³ annually from Mahai Wetland.[47]

China’s documented pattern is salt-lake shrinkage, brine depletion risk and high-volume surface-water and groundwater withdrawal. It differs from Chile because reported observations quantify lake condition and withdrawals more often than aquifer drawdown at a named lithium operation.

In 2022, Qinghai Salt Lake Industry’s Lanke Lithium subsidiary exceeded its groundwater-withdrawal allocation by 1.34 million m³, while the company’s potash-fertilizer subsidiary exceeded its allocation by 4.02 million m³.[47]

From September 2019 to September 2023, Golmud Zangge Potash Fertilizer Co. illegally withdrew 126 million m³ of brine from South Huo Buxun Lake and did not take freshwater from the Quanji River source specified by its water permit.[47]

### Indigenous water rights disputes connected to Chile’s Atacama Desert

Chile contains the clearest Indigenous disputes in this comparison. The principal parties were Atacameño/Lickanantay communities, particularly Peine and Camar, the Consejo de Pueblos Atacameños (CPA), SQM, environmental regulators and CORFO.

In 2019, the Indigenous communities of Peine and Camar and the CPA challenged the Superintendence of the Environment’s approval of SQM’s Salar de Atacama Compliance Program before the Antofagasta Environmental Court. The court annulled the approval.[1][72] The dispute arose in an enforcement setting that included excess brine extraction, shortcomings in monitoring and contingency arrangements, and alleged failure to respond adequately to declining vitality of Algarrobo trees around the Camar-2 freshwater well.[8]

SQM’s compliance actions included first stopping Camar-2 pumping, second dismantling its extraction infrastructure, third monitoring and irrigating Algarrobo trees, fourth implementing a Camar conservation plan, and fifth providing fodder to the Camar community. The company reported that the well had not operated since February 2018 and that dismantling and community dialogue were completed in August 2021.[1][60] These measures connect water extraction with ecological and community effects, even though they were not a legal adjudication allocating Indigenous water rights.

The dispute also concerned how brine should be defined. The CPA argued that brine should be understood as water within an interconnected salt-flat ecosystem rather than merely as mineral input, and challenged the assumption that the salar core was hydraulically independent of marginal lagoons.[73] This is central because Chile regulates freshwater through water-use rights while brine quotas are set through environmental assessment.[73]

Consultation rights remained contested during the period. On 12 December 2023, Chile initiated an ILO Convention No. 169 Indigenous Consultation Process for SQM’s Extraction Reduction Plan with Camar, Talabre, Socaire, Peine and Toconao.[8] The consultation process suspends SEIA assessment deadlines until it concludes.[5] In January 2024, 18 communities of the Consejo de Pueblos Atacameños, led by the Comunidad Atacamena de Socaire, occupied access to the SQM plant, citing lack of free, prior and informed consent and accumulated impacts of water extraction and air pollution.[74]

Albemarle’s documented relationship with Indigenous organisations took a different form. It reported agreements since 2016 with the CPA and 18 Atacameño communities, including a 3.5% share of Salar Plant lithium-carbonate and potassium-chloride sales and joint environmental monitoring and surveillance.[2] These are negotiated benefit-sharing and monitoring arrangements, rather than named water-rights litigation.

Chile’s record therefore establishes Indigenous disputes over consultation, environmental compliance, brine’s hydrological meaning and pumping-linked ecological effects; it does not show a court allocation of Indigenous water rights against SQM or Albemarle during 2015–2024.

The Camar community challenged the 2018 CORFO–SQM settlement on the ground that the ILO Convention No. 169 Indigenous consultation had not occurred, and the Supreme Court ordered CORFO to conduct consultation before proceeding.[74]

In 2019, Atacameño communities mobilized against Wealth Minerals’ proposed project in the northern Salar de Atacama, and lawsuits stopped the initiative in court.[75]

The Consejo de Pueblos Atacameños opposed the Wealth Minerals proposal in part because of overexploitation and its effects on water.[75]

In 2022, Chile’s State Defense Council sued Albemarle Limitada and two copper-mining companies for alleged environmental damage from Monturaqui–Negrillar–Tilopozo aquifer extraction that it said lowered water levels beyond environmentally permitted limits and affected the Tilopozo meadows.[73]

Albemarle’s RCA 21/2016 authorized 23.5 L/s of freshwater extraction, mainly from the Monturaqui–Negrillar–Tilopozo aquifer that sustains the Tilopozo meadows, alongside 442 L/s of brine extraction.[73]

Albemarle offered to cease use of 18.5 L/s of its 23.5 L/s Monturaqui–Negrillar–Tilopozo water rights in the 2022 case.[73]

In 2020, SQM signed an extrajudicial agreement with the Camar Atacameño community for CL$1.700 million, including protection of the Salar’s water source.[75]

In 2023, Indigenous communities represented through the Council of Atacameño Peoples rejected the SQM–Codelco memorandum because they said they had not been consulted beforehand.[74]

### Indigenous water rights disputes connected to Australian hard-rock lithium

No documented Indigenous water-rights dispute tied to an Australian hard-rock lithium operation is identified for 2015–2024. The documented matters concern native title, cultural heritage, land-and-water interests and consultation.

At Greenbushes, the mining tenure predates the 1 January 1994 Native Title Act future-act threshold. The technical report consequently states that the project was not required to obtain agreements with local native-title claimant groups.[3] Nonetheless, cultural, archaeological and ethnographic surveys involved Gnaala Karla Booja, South West Boojarah and Wagyl Kaip Native Title Groups and nominated Noongar representatives in 2015, 2016 and 2018.[3] The Blackwood River is a registered significant Aboriginal site, placing cultural-water protection alongside mine-water management.[3]

At Pilgangoora, Njamal Traditional Owners were notified of a 2020 works-approval process, and the regulator recorded no comments.[30] At Wodgina, the Karriyarra People were party to a land-use agreement dated March 2001, but the reported agreement is not a water-rights dispute.[18]

The Australian evidence therefore documents Indigenous interests and consultation around hard-rock lithium mining, while the disputes seen in Chile over pumping, basin hydrology and consultation did not appear as a named water-rights case in these operations.

### What Indigenous water rights disputes were connected to Chinese salt

China’s documented dispute pattern is broader and less legally specified than Chile’s. A reported account identifies conflicts and tensions between local people and mining companies on the Tibetan Plateau over reduced water resources and contaminated groundwater and streamflow.[46] These conflicts connect lithium extraction to water insecurity, but the cited account does not name a particular salt-lake operation, Indigenous claimant or legal outcome.

A separate Tibet-wide account states that ownership of Tibetan lithium is disputed between the Indigenous population and the Chinese government.[35] That identifies parties in a resource-ownership conflict, rather than a documented water-rights proceeding tied to a named salt-lake lithium operation.

The China evidence therefore supports a general relationship between mining, contested water conditions and Indigenous or local resource claims, but not a case roster comparable with Chile’s CPA, Peine and Camar proceedings.

### Synthesis —

**Chile had the most clearly documented extraction-linked aquifer drawdown and the most developed Indigenous water-governance disputes during 2015–2024.** Salar de Atacama evidence records several-metre well-proximate brine drawdown, up to approximately 14 m in the southwest attributed to SQM pumping, local freshwater reductions, threshold activations and community challenges to compliance and consultation.[2][4][1][8] Australia’s evidence instead centres on TSF-local groundwater mounding and seepage, alongside water recycling and consultation on native title and heritage.[69][3][57] China’s evidence shows salt-lake shrinkage, brine-depletion risk and unlawful groundwater and wetland withdrawals, while Indigenous water-rights cases are not identified at operation level.[47][34][46]

## Regulatory frameworks and lower-impact transition, 2015–2024

Environmental regulation shaped lithium extraction in three different ways during 2015–2024. Chile attached operating limits, monitoring and consultation to project-specific environmental approvals; Australian states centred mine approval, progressive rehabilitation and financial assurance; China combined mining-right, water, restoration and industrial-upgrading policies. These systems constrained expansion and created incentives to obtain more lithium from each permitted unit of brine or ore, but they generally did not prescribe a single lower-impact extraction technology.

### What Chilean environmental impact assessment requirements applied to Atacama lithium

Chile’s applicable framework was Law No. 19,300 on General Environmental Bases, amended by Law No. 20,417, and the Environmental Impact Assessment System regulation, Supreme Decree No. 40/2012 (RSEIA). Mining projects had to enter the Sistema de Evaluación de Impacto Ambiental (SEIA)—the national environmental-permitting system—before development; projects and modifications in or near officially protected areas also required assessment under Article 10.p.[8]

SEIA used three relevant instruments. First, an Estudio de Impacto Ambiental (EIA), or Environmental Impact Study, was required where significant environmental effects were expected and avoidance, mitigation or compensation measures had to be agreed. Second, a Declaración de Impacto Ambiental (DIA), or Environmental Impact Declaration, applied where environmental review was required but legally significant effects were not expected. Third, a Consulta de Pertinencia let a proponent seek a ruling on whether SEIA entry was required. Approval produces a Resolución de Calificación Ambiental (RCA), the environmental qualification resolution that binds the project’s operating conditions.[2][4]

Indigenous consultation was integrated into this process where an EIA project could directly affect Indigenous peoples under Article 85 of the RSEIA and Supreme Decree No. 66. The process requires good-faith consultation through representative institutions and allows affected peoples to influence environmental assessment; omission when legally required can expose an environmental approval to cancellation or revocation.[62][76]

At Salar de Atacama, RCAs translated those general rules into operating controls. Albemarle’s RCA No. 021/2016 authorized a brine increase to 442 litres per second (L/s), freshwater pumping of 16.9 L/s from Tucucaro and Tilopozo wells, and expansion of solar-evaporation surface from 326 hectares to 836 hectares. The approval included monitoring and an early-monitoring plan.[2] SQM’s RCA No.

226/2006 governed brine extraction, industrial-water pumping, monitoring and early-warning arrangements, including monitoring groundwater quantity and quality and environmentally sensitive lagoons, wetlands, flora and fauna.[8]

Freshwater and brine followed different legal routes. Industrial freshwater withdrawals required Dirección General de Aguas (DGA) rights, while brine wells were treated as extraction of a mineral resource rather than groundwater requiring water rights. Albemarle held freshwater rights totalling 23.5 L/s at Tilopozo, Tucucaro and Peine, with 16.9 L/s then in use; its brine was separately authorized through its RCA. **The practical result was that a change in extraction, production or approved operating conditions was permit-dependent, not merely an internal operational decision.**[2][4]

Chile announced its National Lithium Strategy in 2023; SQM’s 2022 Salar Futuro proposal, presented in that policy context, envisaged more than US$1.5 billion of investment in Advanced Evaporation Technologies, direct lithium extraction (DLE), yield improvements and seawater use.[10][11]

### What Australian state-level mining codes applied to hard-rock lithium operations

Western Australia was the principal Australian hard-rock jurisdiction in the period. The Mining Act 1978 (WA) and Mining Regulations 1981 governed tenure, mining leases, exploration licences, mining proposals and mine operations. The Environmental Protection Act 1986 (EP Act) provided the state environmental regime: potentially significant proposals could be referred for Part IV assessment, while processing plants, tailings facilities and other prescribed premises required Part V construction and operating approvals.[62]

Pilbara Minerals reported that Pilgangoora was regulated under the Mining Act and the EP Act, administered respectively by the Department of Mines, Petroleum and Exploration/DMIRS and the Department of Water and Environmental Regulation. It reported independent annual compliance audits and no material licence breaches in FY2023.[26] Greenbushes operated under the Mining Act, EP Act, federal EPBC Act, a Part V operating licence, an approved Mine Closure Plan and mining-proposal conditions; its expansion was approved in 2019 subject to Ministerial Statement No. 1111.[3][23]

The state system did not apply uniformly across Australia. In the Northern Territory, Core Lithium’s Finniss project used the Mining Management Act 2001, which required a Mine Management Plan (MMP), and the Environment Protection Act 2019 for environmental approval. Core reported that the BP33 underground mine’s environmental approval completed its environmental-impact assessment, after which MMP approval was the remaining approvals step.[77] The Water Act 1992 also applied to licensed discharge of mine-affected water.[78]

Other state systems were similarly plan-and-security based: Queensland used the Mineral Resources Act 1989 and Environmental Protection Act 1994; Victoria used the Mineral Resources (Sustainable Development) Act 1990; South Australia used the Mining Act 1971; and New South Wales used the Mining Act 1992. Each paired mining tenure with environmental approval, rehabilitation planning, financial assurance or bonds, and periodic reporting.[79] **Australia’s regulatory distinction is jurisdictional: there is no single national hard-rock mining code, even though federal EPBC approvals can add biodiversity and rehabilitation conditions.**[79]

For Western Australian proposals, the application-validation checklist identified the Rights in Water and Irrigation Act 1914 (RIWI Act), alongside the EP Act and Country Areas Water Supply Act (CAWS Act), as an applicable framework for water matters.[30]

For applicable Western Australian mine-water withdrawals, the Rights in Water and Irrigation Act 1914 was a relevant water-licensing framework alongside the Mining Act 1978 and the Environmental Protection Act 1986.[71]

### What Chinese resource management policies applied to salt lake lithium

China’s national and Qinghai provincial framework combined mineral-resource planning, water management, environmental assessment, pollution control, mine restoration and industrial policy. The National Mineral Resources Plan (2016–2020) treated lithium as a strategic mineral and supported salt-lake-brine technology development, including resource evaluation and extraction advances in major salt-lake areas. Qinghai’s policies then pursued integrated salt-lake development rather than lithium extraction in isolation.[35][80]

The Qinghai Provincial People’s Government and Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued the 《青海建设世界级盐湖产业基地行动方案（2021—2035年）》, the Action Plan for Building a World-Class Salt Lake Industrial Base in Qinghai. It set an industrial-upgrading direction and supplied industrial, tax, fiscal and financial support. Its stated long-term target was salt-lake industry output of RMB120 billion by 2035.[32][81][82]

Qinghai’s 2021–2025 mineral-resources plan and 2021–2035 spatial plan required green-mine construction for new mines and upgrading for operating mines. They required compliance with national “three-rate” standards: mining recovery, beneficiation recovery and comprehensive-utilization rates. New mines could not use nationally restricted or obsolete mining and processing methods, while operations with poor recovery or serious waste had to rectify performance.[83][31]

Water governance was also material. The “Three Red Lines” framework sets controls on total water extraction, water-use efficiency and wastewater discharge, requiring provincial and county limits on withdrawal. A 2024 assessment of West Taijinel stated that water-use efficiency had significantly improved under these policies, although it did not quantify the change.[34] Conversely, enforcement in Qaidam showed that policy targets did not guarantee compliance: in December 2023, China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment criticized three major brine-mining factories for exceeding water quotas through illegal groundwater pumping and taking water from protected wetlands and lakes.[46]

China’s framework thus promoted both expansion and resource efficiency. **Its direct regulatory levers were recovery, utilization, water and restoration requirements; its industrial-policy levers were fiscal and financial support for upgrading high-magnesium/lithium-ratio brines, high-purity products and integrated salt-lake processing.**[32][31][34]

China’s Environmental Impact Assessment Law (《中华人民共和国环境影响评价法》) covers environmental impact assessment for plans and construction projects and was listed in the applicable environmental-law framework.[84]

China’s National Mineral Resources Plan covered 2016–2020, and Qinghai’s Mineral Resources Overall Plan covered 2021–2025; the latter required green-mine construction, stricter mineral-development efficiency entry standards, and compliance with mining-recovery, beneficiation-recovery and comprehensive-utilization indicators.[35][83][85]

Qinghai’s 2024–2026 Salt Lake Resources High-Quality Development and Utilization Three-Year Action Plan proposed stronger reserve management, scientifically controlled development intensity, green mining technologies and a salt-lake-mining technical-standard system.[80]

At December 31, 2024, Salt Lake Shares reported RMB75,984,906.56 of restricted cash as an environmental-governance and restoration guarantee deposit.[39]

### How did Chile’s environmental impact assessment requirements influence extraction efficiency

In Chile, regulatory effects were clearest in pumping control, monitoring and environmental information, not in mandated metallurgical recovery technology. SQM’s RCA No. 226/2006 required an Environmental Monitoring Plan and action when new impacts appeared; its compliance programme provided for reduced brine extraction under Phase II triggers.[1] In November 2021, the Superintendence of the Environment (SMA) imposed provisional 30-day controls: continued online monitoring, activation thresholds for the Peine system, brine pumping capped at 1,280 L/s, and industrial-water pumping capped at 120 L/s.[1]

SQM’s compliance response also created measurable operating changes. Monitoring frequency rose from monthly to daily from December 1, 2018; online monitoring and reporting began June 3, 2019; the company made extraction and monitoring information publicly accessible; and its reported net brine extraction for June 2018–May 2020 was 12,178,604 cubic metres below the authorized volume, exceeding the committed reduction of 9,800,922 cubic metres.[8][5] Its maximum annual-average brine drawdown fell to 1,187.96 L/s from November 2020, while industrial-water flow was limited to 120 L/s from October 2021.[8][5]

SQM’s environmental-monitoring-plan update was a US$450,000 project with an estimated execution start of September 1, 2021; it did not change the operation’s authorised life through 2030.[126]

Albemarle’s permit ceiling and quota horizon created a commercial reason to improve recovery. Its production model used 414 L/s average brine pumping against a 442 L/s regulatory maximum because of environmental restrictions.[3] Its approved quota expired in 2043, and the reserve model stated that underperformance could not be made up by pumping above the 442 L/s limit.[4] Albemarle’s Salar Yield Improvement Program (SYIP) sought to raise pond recovery from about 40% to about 65% through grinding, washing and leaching harvested bischofite and lithium-carnallite salts.[4]

SQM likewise reported operational efficiency gains within a constrained permit environment: historical lithium recovery of around 43% had increased to 56% through process improvements by production and research teams. But its separate table reported global yields of 42.98%, 42.89% and 42.80% for 2019–2021, while the narrower IGS lithium-system yield was 43.70%, 54.50% and 50.70%; these are different denominators and should not be combined.[1] **Chile’s framework demonstrably changed permitted pumping and monitoring behaviour, while recovery improvements were implemented by operators within that framework rather than shown to be caused by the EIA rules themselves.**[1][2]

Australian evidence points in the same direction. At Greenbushes, recovery improvement was operationally documented: chemical-grade plant 2 increased from roughly 50% recovery in 2021 to a 68% average in Q4 2022 after process improvements. The available record identifies the improvements as plant-performance work, not as a mining-code requirement.[18] Western Australian rules nevertheless shaped how the facility was approved, monitored, licensed and closed.[3]

China’s operating figures similarly show technical variation by process rather than a quantified policy effect. At Qarhan, LanKe Lithium’s single-tower adsorption was reported at about 60% adsorption-section yield, while Zangge Lithium’s simulated-continuous adsorption process was reported at about 80%. LanKe trial-operated a 6,000-tonne-per-year mother-liquor-recovery demonstration unit in June 2023 and reported improved overall recovery without supplying a percentage.[44][86] The policy framework favoured such higher-recovery development, particularly for difficult high-magnesium/lithium brines, but it does not isolate policy as the cause of either result.[86][32]

A separate process comparison reported approximately 80–90% lithium recovery in Zangge’s simulated-continuous adsorption stage, versus approximately 60% in Lanke’s adsorption stage; neither is a complete brine-to-battery-grade-carbonate recovery rate.[133]

A separate account put Zangge’s simulated continuous-adsorption recovery at 80%–90%, compared with about 60% for Lanke’s adsorption stage.[133]

A separate account put Zangge’s simulated-continuous adsorption-stage recovery at 80–90%, compared with approximately 60% for Lanke’s adsorption stage; neither figure measures full-plant recovery.[133]

The SMA approved SQM’s Salar de Atacama compliance programme on August 29, 2022; its commitments included reducing the authorized maximum brine-extraction limit to 822 L/s by 2027 and reducing industrial-water extraction.[10]

SQM reported that its lithium reserve estimate used average life-of-mine lithium process efficiency of approximately 52%, incorporating brine type at each production well and process-recovery factors.[10]

SQM’s December 2023 reserve simulation applied evaporation-pond recovery factors to 211 million m³ of pumped brine and reported average lithium-process efficiency of approximately 52%.[62][8]

### How did Australian state-level mining codes influence hard-rock extraction efficiency

Chile’s Law No. 20,551 regulated closure of mining sites and facilities from November 2012. It required closure plans, decommissioning information, estimated implementation costs and financial assurance.[10] SQM’s Salar de Atacama closure-plan update was approved by SERNAGEOMIN on August 9, 2022 under Resolution No. 1381. Measures included removing structures and equipment, de-energizing facilities, closing access and installing signage.[1][5]

Albemarle’s closure plan, approved under Res. Ex. No. 287/2019, was more specific about evaporation ponds: it required pond backfilling and profiling; removal of pond liners from slopes and covering pond bottoms; dismantling infrastructure; demolition and disposal of concrete and steel; and ground profiling. The stated execution period was 17 months.[4][2] Its plan also required quarterly post-closure monitoring of 221 wells for three years, covering water quality, evaporation and groundwater and surface-water flux.[2] Albemarle’s reported financial-assurance closure estimate for La Negra and Salar de Atacama was US$40.89 million, including US$19.06 million for Salar de Atacama; this was an assurance estimate, not necessarily the operator’s eventual closure cost.[2]

Western Australia required all mine sites to be decommissioned, rehabilitated, made safe and stable, and prepared for future land use under the Mining Act 1978. Mine Closure Plans had to be periodically revised and resubmitted; statutory guidelines effective March 3, 2020 required an MCP for every mine operation.[87][88] MCPs require closure risks, completion criteria, monitoring, maintenance and financial provisioning to be considered during operations rather than deferred to the end of mine life.[88]

The Western Australian Mining Rehabilitation Fund (MRF) added a quantified financing mechanism. Mining Act tenure holders had to report disturbance annually, and the levy was 1% of the Rehabilitation Liability Estimate (RLE). The RLE was calculated by multiplying disturbed hectares in specified categories by category unit rates; it was a levy input, not an estimate of actual rehabilitation cost.[89][90] For example, Category A land—including Class 1 residue facilities, waste dumps, heap or vat leach facilities and saline-water/process-liquor dams—carried a rate of A$50,000 per hectare; land under rehabilitation was Category E at A$2,000 per hectare.[90][89]

The Northern Territory used a different approach. Mining activities authorized under the Mining Management Act had to lodge security based on likely disturbance under the approved MMP. It could fund government action to prevent, minimize or rectify environmental harm or complete rehabilitation.[91] **Australia’s state regimes therefore made rehabilitation a continuing operational and financial obligation: Western Australia uses closure planning plus a pooled disturbance levy, while the Northern Territory uses project-specific remediation security.**[79][91]

China’s Mine Geological Environment Protection Provisions required mining-right applicants to obtain approved protection-and-restoration plans containing impact assessment, restoration measures, monitoring, cost estimates and restoration-deposit arrangements. Operators had to update plans when mine area, scale or extraction method changed, carry out restoration concurrently with extraction and treat restoration expenditure as a production cost.[92]

Before closure, operators had to complete restoration and receive acceptance. Authorities could use restoration deposits where work was late or inadequate, while the operator remained responsible for any funding shortfall; specified breaches could produce fines up to RMB30,000 and, in some cases, a five-year restriction on new mining-right applications.[93] Qinghai’s planning approach did not require restoration to a pre-development salt-lake state; it contemplated ecological restoration balancing safety, landscape, habitat diversity and ecological equilibrium.[34]

At Pilgangoora, Pilbara Minerals reported average lithia recovery of 72% through March 23, 2020, including plant start-up, and reported daily recoveries of 74–76% in the same campaign against design criteria of 72–78%, depending on ore feed.[94]

At Greenbushes, CGP2 recovery was 50.5% in May–December 2021, 64.0% in 2022 and 68.2% in the fourth quarter of 2022 after plant-optimization changes; the documented attribution is to process work, not to a state mining-code requirement.[3]

Greenbushes Chemical Grade Plant 1 recovered 72.1% of the lithium contained in ore processed in 2022 into concentrates averaging 6.06% Li₂O; that recovery ends before chemical conversion.[127]

CGP2 replaced CGP1’s ball-mill circuit with high-pressure grinding rolls, but the anticipated incremental recovery had not been demonstrated and actual operation generated more unrecoverable lithium slimes than expected.[3]

At Pilgangoora, Pilbara Minerals’ P1000 design included a 15-megalitre raw-water-storage dam and an expanded borefield pumping-and-piping network, for which applicable water permissions and environmental approvals were part of project development.[95][71]

The Mining Rehabilitation Fund’s Rehabilitation Liability Estimate is determined from the type and area of disturbance and rehabilitation conducted, so land qualifying as Land Under Rehabilitation can reduce the levy and improve the economics of progressive rehabilitation.[96]

Pilbara Minerals’ P1000 planned high-pressure grinding rolls, duplicated ball mills and flotation circuits, and pre-flotation magnetic separation; these were processing choices, not technology mandates of Western Australian mining codes.[95]

At Pilgangoora, the proposed mine-water-discharge limit was less than 256,000 kL per year at a maximum expected flow of 50 L/s, and the proponent proposed first assessing use of the water in processing ponds or transfer to the Iron Stirrup Pit before environmental discharge.[30]

### How did China’s resource management policies influence salt-lake extraction efficiency

Chile’s controls made increased pumping uncertain and costly, strengthening the economic value of higher recovery from the permitted brine stream. SQM’s compliance programme contained 52 actions, cost more than US$50 million, and required regular mandatory reporting.[8] Its extraction-reduction EIA, submitted in January 2022, remained under evaluation in December 2024.[62] The programme and EIA proposed a progressive maximum annual-average brine rate of 822 L/s by 2028, replacing the earlier path to 1,700 L/s, alongside industrial-water limits of 120 L/s.[8][5]

For Albemarle, a finite quota and pumping cap created a clear economic incentive for SYIP. The company’s model stated that reaching its production quota depended on lifting pond recovery from roughly 40% to 65%; forecast average life-of-mine operating cost was about US$3,000 per metric tonne CIF Asia, with post-SYIP sustaining capital around US$54 million per year.[18][3] The 65% recovery remained a forecast with test-data limitations rather than an achieved 2015–2024 result.[3]

Chile had DLE activity but not established commercial conversion by the cutoff. Albemarle commissioned a DLE pilot at La Negra in 2024. It described DLE as recovering lithium directly from brine and reinjecting lithium-depleted brine, with potential recovery improvement up to 85%; the pilot was intended to validate scale-up and feasibility.[9] SQM’s Atacama DLE project was listed as a pilot with a 2024 start date.[11] **The Chilean framework therefore made DLE and recovery improvements strategically attractive, but permitting, pilot status and compliance costs mean it had not demonstrated commercial, regulation-driven DLE replacement of ponds by December 2024.**[62][9][11]

Western Australia’s clearest financial incentive concerned disturbed land. The MRF’s 1% annual levy rises with the RLE, while qualifying land as under rehabilitation is assigned a much lower category rate.[89] That creates an incentive for progressive rehabilitation, but does not demonstrate that it made a cleaner concentrator, electric calciner or other lower-impact hard-rock technology economical.

Pilbara Minerals’ Calix Mid-stream Demonstration Plant illustrates the distinction. The plant was a joint venture intended to develop more sustainable lithium extraction and processing, but the company described it as a demonstration facility rather than established commercial conversion.[20] Greenbushes economics were instead explicitly sensitive to ore grade, concentrate price and plant performance: its model assumed US$1,500 per tonne CIF China chemical-grade concentrate and produced an after-tax NPV at 8% of US$13.2 billion.[3] In 2024, Albemarle stopped Kemerton Train 3 construction and placed Train 2 into care and maintenance amid falling lithium prices and cost-reduction measures, showing that market conditions could override approved conversion capacity.[6]

China paired operator-funded restoration with industrial support. Qinghai’s 2021–2035 action plan supplied industrial, tax, fiscal and financial policy support; provincial measures sought better recovery from high-magnesium/lithium brines and technologically mature, economically viable development.[32][86] Salt Lake Shares reported a government grant balance of RMB1,333,332.70 at year-end 2024 for a 10,000-tonne-per-year high-purity lithium-carbonate project.[39]

Chinese salt-lake economics nevertheless remained project-specific. Resource-management enforcement could impose major cost shocks: Salt Lake Shares reported RMB1.8968328 billion in mining-right transfer revenue payable under the 2023 collection rules and charged the amount to current-period costs.[97] A separate assessment reported excess production at Bieletan and RMB1.897 billion payable in resource-transfer proceeds.[98] These charges show that resource-right compliance can materially affect investment capacity, but they were not reported as DLE-transition costs.

LanKe Lithium reported overcoming the technical difficulty of extracting lithium from high-magnesium/lithium-ratio salt-lake brine in 2017 and mastering key adsorption-extraction technology and equipment.[40]

LanKe Lithium completed its 20,000-tonne-per-year battery-grade lithium-carbonate expansion in July 2021, bringing installed capacity to 30,000 tonnes per year.[40]

Lanke Lithium reached 40,000 tonnes per year of lithium-carbonate capacity in 2024.[135][133][132]

A separate account states that Lanke had reached 10,000 tonnes per year of capacity in 2018, subsequently expanded and upgraded to 40,000 tonnes per year, and reached that nameplate output in 2024.[135]

Salt Lake Shares reported an adsorption–membrane-separation coupled industrial demonstration that extracts lithium salts from ultra-high-magnesium/lithium-ratio, low-lithium brine.[39]

Salt Lake Shares’ 40,000-tonne-per-year basic-lithium-salts integrated project received environmental-impact-assessment and soil-and-water-conservation approvals; changing the process route, adding old-brine pretreatment, simplifying equipment and planning additional products optimized total investment by approximately 14.58%.[97][80]

Salt Lake Shares recorded a RMB76,646,059.22 mine-environment governance-and-restoration deposit as restricted cash at June 30, 2024.[80]

Salt Lake Shares prepared the 《盐湖资源高质量开发利用三年行动方案（2024—2026年）》, which calls for unified resource planning, development and management, stronger mine-geology and reserve management, mine and processing standards, green-mining technology and solid-to-liquid solution-mining technology.[39]

Reported investment intensity was RMB157,000 per tonne of annual capacity for LanKe Lithium’s 20,000-tonne project started in 2017, RMB162,000 per tonne for Salt Lake BYD’s 30,000-tonne project planned in 2016, and RMB177,000 per tonne for Salt Lake Shares’ 40,000-tonne project planned in 2022.[40]

### What rehabilitation obligations did Chile’s requirements impose on Atacama lithium

Across 2015–2024, Chile imposed the strongest project-specific extraction constraints: RCAs, early-warning thresholds, Indigenous consultation and enforcement directly controlled pumping and required new permits for material operating changes. Australia’s state systems most clearly embedded rehabilitation in mine economics through closure plans, disturbance reporting, levies or remediation security. China combined lifecycle restoration duties and water controls with an industrial-policy push for higher recovery and integrated salt-lake development. The common effect was to raise the value of extracting more lithium from an authorized resource base; the differing effect was institutional—Chile controlled brine and water operations, Australia controlled land disturbance and closure, and China combined resource efficiency with industrial expansion. Commercial DLE and other lower-impact transitions remained driven by site chemistry, process performance, commodity prices and capital availability as well as regulation.[8][89][92][32][6]

The approved Salar de Atacama closure plan reported a total closure and post-closure cost of 485,807 UF, comprising 319,504 UF for closure and 166,303 UF for post-closure.[5]

The Salar de Atacama closure plan requires maintenance of access closures and signage every five years in perpetuity and monthly inspections in perpetuity.[5]

## Battery-grade standards, processing economics and producer positioning, 2015–2024

Battery grade is a finished chemical qualification, not a property of a salar brine or a spodumene concentrate. Across the three extraction settings, the decisive commercial step is therefore downstream purification: removing ions, metals, magnetic particles and moisture to produce lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃) or lithium hydroxide monohydrate (LiOH·H₂O) within customer limits. Costs must likewise distinguish mining or brine concentration from chemical conversion into saleable battery material.

Lithium carbonate is used in lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP), lithium-manganese-oxide (LMO) and many nickel-manganese-cobalt (NCM) cathodes; lithium hydroxide monohydrate is commonly used for high-nickel NCM and nickel-cobalt-aluminium (NCA) cathodes.[52][139][131]

### What battery-grade lithium purity standards are relevant to comparing extraction

China’s product standards offer route-neutral benchmarks. YS/T 582-2023, 《电池级碳酸锂》, was published on 20 December 2023, implemented on 1 July 2024, and replaced YS/T 582-2013; it applies to battery-grade lithium carbonate made by any method. The earlier standard specified chloride at or below 0.003% and sulfate at or below 0.08%.[100][101]

GB/T 26008-2020, 《电池级单水氢氧化锂》, is the Chinese national standard for battery-grade lithium hydroxide monohydrate, applicable to material produced by various methods and effective on 1 May 2021 according to the national standards portal. Its D1 grade specifies 56.5–57.5% LiOH·H₂O, with Na no higher than 0.005%, K 0.003%, Fe 0.0007%, Ca 0.002%, Cu 0.0001%, and carbonate 0.40%.[102][103]

Producer specifications demonstrate why assay alone is inadequate. SQM reported customer requirements of 99.5% lithium-carbonate purity, magnetic particles below 500 ppb, and sodium, magnesium and calcium each no higher than 0.05%. Albemarle’s 2022 battery-grade carbonate specification required at least 99.30% Li₂CO₃ plus limits for chloride, potassium, sodium, magnesium, sulfate, iron oxide, calcium and water. Ganfeng’s unground battery-grade LiOH·H₂O specification requires LiOH of at least 56.5% and magnetic substances of no more than 50 ppb.[8][4][104]

**Battery-grade comparison must use the final chemical and its impurity profile, not the extraction route or lithium concentration of an intermediate.** The Chinese standards can compare final carbonate or hydroxide from Atacama evaporation, Chinese salt-lake DLE, and Australian spodumene conversion, but do not imply equal cost or recovery.[105][103][101]

### How does brine evaporation attain battery-grade lithium purity?

Conventional evaporation first concentrates brine and rejects salts. At Salar de Atacama, pond evaporation raises lithium from roughly 0.15–0.20% to 6% over about 12–18 months; magnesium and boron remain material contaminants at that point.[106] Albemarle reports that its sequential pond system precipitates almost all sodium and potassium and approximately 95% of magnesium before the brine reaches 6% lithium.[4]

Second, chemical refining turns concentrated lithium chloride into battery material. At La Negra, Albemarle acidifies the brine and uses solvent extraction to reduce boron from a nominal 0.8% by weight to below 10 ppm, then uses chemical precipitation to remove other impurities before carbonation, washing, drying and packaging.[4][3] SQM similarly identifies boron, magnesium and calcium removal followed by conversion into technical- and battery-grade carbonate and hydroxide.[62]

SQM reported that 0.2%-lithium brine became 99.91%-pure lithium carbonate, while its reported customer requirement was 99.5%; these are an achieved result and a minimum requirement, respectively.[60][8] Albemarle’s cited product specifications differ by date: the 2017-attributed battery-grade specification was at least 99.80% Li₂CO₃, while the 2022 specification was at least 99.30%.[2][4]

The conclusion is that ponds provide low-energy concentration and partial impurity rejection, but battery grade comes from the integrated pond-plus-refinery chain. This also distinguishes Atacama’s conventional route from Zabuye’s solar-pond crystallization, which was reported to yield roughly 50–75% lithium-carbonate concentrate rather than battery-grade carbonate in one step.[55][107]

Zabuye’s original salt-gradient solar-pond process produced a concentrate containing 65% lithium carbonate before refining it into industrial-grade carbonate and hydroxide.[135][133]

### What were the processing costs of brine evaporation from 2015–2024?

Albemarle provides the clearest Atacama time series. Its Salar de Atacama/La Negra cash operating cost per metric tonne of lithium carbonate was US$3,198 in 2016, US$3,770 in 2017, US$4,014 in 2018, US$4,339 in 2019 and US$3,552 in 2020. These were site cash costs excluding depreciation and depletion, not pond-only processing costs.[2]

SRK’s life-of-operation models show why boundaries matter. One model reported US$1,927/t Li₂CO₃ processing cost and US$3,310/t life-of-mine C1 cost; the latter comprised US$718/t salar costs, US$1,927/t processing, US$665/t shipping and general and administrative costs, with royalties separately stated at US$1,372/t.[2] A later model reported US$2,223/t processing cost and US$4,155/t C1 cost.[4]

For broad 2024 method comparisons, the International Lithium Association estimated solar-evaporation CAPEX at US$23,000–34,000 per annual tonne of LCE capacity and OPEX at US$4,800–8,000/t LCE. Goldman Sachs modelled conventional ponds at US$3,300–4,900/t LCE, FOB and pre-royalty, at 40–60% recovery.[12][11]

These results show a cost advantage from sunlight rather than zero-cost processing: ponds avoid energy-intensive concentration, but require long residence time, major land infrastructure, downstream reagents and finishing. Different cost scopes explain the apparent range better than a single universal evaporation cost.

### How does direct lithium extraction from brine attain battery-grade lithium

Direct lithium extraction (DLE) is a selective separation front end: it captures lithium from brine without relying on months or years of solar concentration. In adsorption DLE, lithium chloride is captured in an adsorbent and stripped, commonly with warm water; some sorbents are reported to recover more than 90% of lithium.[11] Ion-exchange DLE uses a selective material and can recover around 90%, but may require acidic stripping and associated reagent consumption.[11]

The recovered stream still requires refining. Remaining divalent ions must be removed; lithium chloride must be concentrated; then lithium carbonate is precipitated and washed, or lithium hydroxide is produced through downstream chemical conversion. **DLE reduces the upstream impurity burden and pond footprint; it does not eliminate battery-grade refining.**[11][52]

China supplied evidence of both commercial claims and pilot-stage technology. A 2024 industry report described an EVE Energy plant using Sunresin technology to produce up to 10,000 tpa battery-grade LCE from 100–120 mg/L brine.[12] Salt Lake Shares reported completion of pilot validation for membrane-capacitive selective adsorption, with targets of at least 90% recovery, 5 mol/L LiCl, and limits on sodium, magnesium, potassium and boron; these are targets rather than commercial results.[39]

The practical implication is that DLE should be judged separately at three stages: lithium capture, depleted-brine management or reinjection, and final chemical purification. High recovery at the first stage does not itself prove battery-grade output.

Aluminium-based adsorption DLE had been used commercially in China by Lanke Lithium, Zangge Mining and Jintai Lithium to produce lithium chemicals from continental brines.[12]

Chinese commercial DLE projects were commissioned between 2017 and 2020.[108]

### What were the processing costs of direct lithium extraction from

Quantified DLE costs during 2015–2024 were mainly estimates. The International Lithium Association’s 2024 comparison put DLE CAPEX at US$45,000–80,000 per annual tonne of LCE capacity and OPEX at US$4,500–7,500/t LCE. Its CAPEX range exceeds that of solar evaporation, principally because DLE equipment must be customized to brine composition.[12]

Goldman Sachs’ 27 April 2023 project model produced lower figures: DLE OPEX of approximately US$2,800–3,600/t LCE, FOB and pre-royalty, at 70–90% recovery; CAPEX intensity was approximately US$26,000–34,000/tpa LCE. The model explicitly makes costs sensitive to brine grade and reagent availability and cost.[11] Its mid-case DLE projects had estimated NPVs of US$0.6–1.1 billion and IRRs of about 20–30%, compared with US$0.3–0.7 billion and 20–25% for conventional ponds.[11]

A separate 25,000-tpa-LCE pilot-scale geothermal-brine estimate put DLE OPEX at US$6,200/t LCE and CAPEX at US$225 million.[109] Thus, the relevant economic conclusion is not that DLE is uniformly cheaper: it has higher initial technical capital requirements, while its recovery advantage can offset those costs where brine chemistry and reagent use are favourable.

DLE operating costs include sorbent or membrane replacement, regeneration chemicals, energy, freshwater and downstream conversion.[11][12][109]

A Chinese project comparison reported lithium-extraction costs of RMB20,000/t for Zabuye solar ponds, RMB30,000/t for Yiliping nanofiltration, RMB60,000/t for Xitaijinair nanofiltration plus reverse osmosis and for Dongtaijinair ion-selective membranes, and RMB30,000–40,000/t for Chaerhan adsorption.[55]

Salt Lake Shares reported 2021 lithium-carbonate production cost of RMB28,200/t and estimated first-quarter 2022 cost of approximately RMB30,600/t.[40]

Lanke’s reported adsorption route used freshwater to wash the adsorbent before membrane concentration; its total lithium-carbonate cost was reported at about RMB40,000 per tonne in 2018.[132]

DLE operating-cost boundaries include energy, freshwater and chemicals used where brine pH is changed or heated to as much as 80°C to improve lithium recovery.[110]

Salt Lake Shares stated that the annual loss rate of the adsorbent used in its new 40,000-tpa lithium-carbonate project was below 10%.[97]

### How does hard-rock extraction and refining attain battery-grade lithium purity?

Hard-rock lithium begins with mining and beneficiation rather than direct chemical production. Greenbushes upgrades ore containing about 2.5% Li₂O into chemical-grade spodumene concentrate containing about 6% Li₂O. Its SC6.0 specification is at least 6% Li₂O and no more than 1% Fe₂O₃.[3] This is a chemical feedstock, not battery-grade carbonate or hydroxide.

First, the concentrate is calcined above 1,000°C to convert refractory alpha-spodumene into acid-reactive beta-spodumene.[22] Second, it is ground, sulphuric-acid roasted, water-leached and purified through precipitation, filtration and ion exchange to remove iron, magnesium, calcium and other contaminants. Third, soda ash precipitates lithium carbonate; alternatively, sodium hydroxide conversion produces lithium hydroxide.[52]

An efficiently operating acid-bake route is estimated to recover about 77% of lithium from alpha-spodumene to lithium hydroxide. The multiple impurity-removal stages create modest lithium losses at each stage.[22] This means hard-rock conversion carries an intrinsic energy, acid, alkali, waste-handling and recovery penalty relative to selling concentrate.

The resulting supply-chain structure during the period was clear: Australian mines supplied SC6-type concentrate, while conversion occurred largely in China or in emerging Australian refineries. Talison shipped Greenbushes concentrate to Albemarle plants in China for battery-grade hydroxide conversion.[18]

### What were the processing costs of hard-rock extraction and refining

The cost record separates mine/concentrator costs from refined-chemical costs. Pilbara’s 2016 Pilgangoora study forecast life-of-mine operating cash cost of US$205/t of spodumene concentrate, FOB, including production, corporate administration and royalties, with tantalum by-product credits.[111] In June 2020, Pilbara reported approximately US$350/dmt SC6 CIF China at around 65% utilisation.[112] These are concentrate costs, not LCE refining costs.

Pilbara Minerals reported FY2024 FOB unit operating costs of A$654 per dry metric tonne of concentrate, covering mining, processing, transport, port charges and site-based administration net of tantalite credits; its CIF China cost was A$818 per dry metric tonne after freight and royalties. These are concentrate costs, not battery-grade chemical-conversion costs.[20]

For integrated refining, SQM’s Mt Holland reserve evaluation assumed 75% recovery to concentrate, 85% refinery recovery and total operating cost of US$4,979/t lithium hydroxide.[10] A cross-route study estimated an Australia-plus-China chain—Australian SC6 mining and Chinese conversion into LCE—at US$4,830/t LCE. The same study put Australian mining and concentration alone at around US$570/t SC6, and Greenbushes at US$255.71/t SC6.[113]

Method-level estimates diverge: the International Lithium Association gave hard-rock OPEX of US$6,000–18,000/t LCE, whereas Goldman Sachs gave US$3,300–4,900/t LCE. The direction is consistent despite the differing ranges: high-temperature calcination, acid roasting, reagent use and waste management make final chemical conversion economically significant.[12][11] Spodumene-derived hydroxide has an estimated 80–85% lithium yield and can generate 10–12 tonnes of solid waste per tonne of lithium hydroxide.[52]

Greenbushes reported chemical-grade-plant ore-processing costs of A$16.76/t in 2021 and A$22.53/t in 2022 for CGP1, and A$18.64/t in 2021 and A$24.47/t in 2022 for CGP2; these are ore-processing costs rather than costs per tonne of LCE.[3]

### How did purity standards and processing costs affect SQM’s supply-chain

SQM’s Atacama position combined solar evaporation with large-scale Chilean chemical conversion. It reported customer carbonate requirements of 99.5% purity, magnetic particles below 500 ppb, and Na, Mg and Ca each at or below 0.05%; it separately reported 99.91% carbonate purity from its process.[8][60] This enabled SQM to sell battery-grade carbonate and hydroxide, not merely brine.

SQM reported approximately 156,800 tonnes of lithium carbonate and hydroxide sales in 2022, roughly 20% of the lithium-chemicals market; 93% of lithium sales were in Asia.[10][1] Its 2024 lithium chemical plant capacity reached 210,000 tonnes LCE and it marketed almost 205,000 tonnes LCE.[62]

SQM completed lithium-carbonate and lithium-hydroxide expansions in 2022, reaching stated effective capacities of 180,000 tonnes and 30,000 tonnes, respectively.[62][8]

Its cost advantage was rooted in high lithium concentration, high evaporation and solar energy, but CORFO rights materially shaped economics: these rights and agreements represented approximately 79% of reported 2022 operating costs.[8] SQM’s route therefore created a strong export-oriented battery-material position, while its value capture remained sensitive to royalty and contract payments rather than extraction cost alone.

### How did purity standards and processing costs affect Albemarle’s supply-chain

Albemarle’s Atacama route concentrated brine to about 6% lithium, then moved it to La Negra for boron removal, chemical precipitation and conversion into technical- and battery-grade carbonate.[4] Its battery-grade carbonate output rose from 13,323 tonnes in 2015 to an estimated 35,895 tonnes in 2021, while technical-grade output declined from 10,945 to 6,829 tonnes.[4]

This shift demonstrates increasing positioning in battery chemicals rather than intermediates. The 2016 CORFO amendment reinforced that strategy: Albemarle had to invest in a Chilean battery-grade carbonate plant of at least 20,000–24,000 t/y LCE by 31 December 2022 or lose most of the additional quota.[4]

Albemarle’s Chile route also linked to a wider conversion network. It could sell Salar/La Negra product to third parties or use it internally in downstream facilities; Greenbushes concentrate was shipped to China and Australia for battery-grade hydroxide conversion.[2][6] Its reported Atacama cash costs and SRK models show a competitive but not directly battery-grade-specific cost base.[2]

Albemarle began construction of its Kemerton, Western Australia, lithium-hydroxide processing plant in March 2019, with a stated A$1 billion (US$709.4 million) investment; the refinery was designed to convert Greenbushes spodumene concentrate into lithium hydroxide.[114][25]

In July 2024, Albemarle stopped construction of Kemerton Train 3 and placed Train 2 into care and maintenance.[6]

Albemarle proposed a lithium-hydroxide manufacturing plant at the Kemerton Strategic Industrial Area in Western Australia using spodumene-containing ore concentrate as feed.[115]

In 2022, Albemarle recorded an US$8.4 million expense for revised estimates of its obligation to construct Kemerton lithium-hydroxide conversion assets, attributing the anticipated cost overruns to supply-chain, labour and COVID-19-pandemic-related issues.[116]

### How did purity standards and processing costs affect Ganfeng Lithium’s

Ganfeng’s published specifications show a strategy centred on refined battery chemicals. Its battery-grade LiOH·H₂O specification requires LiOH of at least 56.5%, Na and K each no higher than 0.003%, Fe no higher than 0.0008%, and magnetic material no higher than 50 ppb; its micronized product also specifies a D50 particle-size range of 4–22 μm.[104]

**Ganfeng’s supply-chain position was therefore defined by conversion and qualification across multiple feedstocks, not by a single extraction route.** Its published product standard supports cathode-grade markets where particle form and trace contaminants matter as much as principal lithium content. Ganfeng’s position differs from a pure brine producer because it can use hard-rock, brine-derived and recycled material as inputs to its conversion system.

The economic consequence is that Ganfeng faces exposure to feedstock and conversion costs. A reported 2023 comparison placed its lithium-salt unit cost at RMB210,000/t and gross margin at 12.53%, versus RMB47,000/t and 72.45% for Salt Lake Co.; these are company business figures rather than direct process-cost comparisons.[98] The comparison nevertheless shows that downstream capability does not insulate a converter from weak price conditions or costly input material.

Ganfeng Lithium stated that its lithium-hydroxide production feedstock came from spodumene concentrate produced at Australia’s Mount Marion mine, which commenced production in February 2017 and was described as producing about 400,000 tonnes of lithium concentrate annually.[104]

In 2015, Ganfeng invested in Reed Industrial Minerals Australia and acquired rights and interests in its Mt Marion spodumene project, linking Australian hard-rock feedstock to Ganfeng’s lithium-chemical supply chain.[29]

Ganfeng stated that it had industrialised technologies for extracting lithium from brine, mineral ore and recycling.[117]

In 2020, Phase 3 of Ganfeng’s Mahong Plant commenced operation, adding 50,000 tonnes per year of battery-grade lithium-hydroxide production capacity.[29]

Ganfeng reported supplying battery-grade lithium hydroxide and lithium carbonate to international automobile and power-battery companies including LG Chem, BMW and Tesla.[117]

### How did purity standards and processing costs affect Pilbara Minerals’

Pilbara Minerals’ core business remained Australian spodumene concentrate. Its SC6.0 product is a roughly 6%-Li₂O intermediate, while its FY2024 5.2% target grade was chosen to balance yield, unit cost and customer processability.[118][119] Neither is a battery-grade chemical specification.

Pilgangoora produced 725,329 dry tonnes of spodumene concentrate in FY2024.[20]

Pilbara pursued downstream participation through partnerships. Its POSCO joint venture was designed for a 43,000-tpa lithium-hydroxide plant in Gwangyang, South Korea, supplied by 315,000 tpa of Pilgangoora concentrate.[26] In FY2024, the venture reported initial technical- and battery-grade hydroxide volumes and Train 1 reaching 45% of nameplate capacity.[119]

Pilbara’s disclosed costs—US$350/dmt SC6 CIF China in June 2020 and US$440/t or A$675/t in the March 2024 quarter—remain concentrate-operation costs, not battery-chemical conversion costs.[112][120] Its strategic limitation and opportunity were the same: selling concentrate avoids the capital and technical risk of refining, while conversion partnerships offer access to battery-grade margin and customer qualification.

### Synthesis

**The 2015–2024 pattern is a hierarchy of value addition: brine or ore extraction creates feedstock; selective separation or concentration creates an intermediate; and impurity-controlled chemical conversion creates battery-grade material.** Atacama evaporation producers SQM and Albemarle held advantages in solar concentration and integrated carbonate production, but still depended on chemical purification. Australian hard-rock producers supplied high-grade concentrate but faced energy- and reagent-intensive conversion before reaching battery markets. Chinese salt-lake operations demonstrated both conventional brine treatment and commercially claimed DLE, although pilot targets remain distinct from finished-product qualification.[4][52][12][39]

1. Compare producers by final certified carbonate or hydroxide quality, not by brine grade or SC6 concentrate grade.
2. Treat evaporation, DLE and hard-rock costs as boundary-specific: state whether they include extraction, conversion, royalties, freight and capital.
3. View DLE primarily as a recovery, land-use and brine-management alternative whose commercial value depends on final purification and reagent economics.

A concentrate cost per tonne cannot be ranked directly against a finished-carbonate cost per tonne of LCE: route-cost comparisons require matching product grade, currency year and the operating boundary, including whether mining, conversion, royalties, freight and capital are included.[108][27][3]

SQM and Albemarle sold refined lithium chemicals, Ganfeng combined hard-rock and other feedstocks with lithium-chemical conversion, and Pilbara Minerals principally sold Pilgangoora spodumene concentrate while pursuing downstream partnerships.[53][14][117][121][122]

### Open questions

Comparable annual freshwater-consumption rates per tonne of LCE for each of the three settings remain open, as do like-for-like commercial DLE adoption rates with a stated capacity or production denominator for Chilean and Chinese operations. The unresolved hydrological questions are operation-specific measured aquifer changes in Australian hard-rock and Chinese salt-lake areas, and the extent to which each observed change is attributable to lithium operations rather than climate, other water users or natural variability. Indigenous water-rights disputes also require operation-specific identification of the affected peoples, asserted rights, parties and outcomes. These questions would be settled by dated operating records that pair LCE output with water category and volume, DLE capacity and actual production, monitored water-level series with attribution analysis, and formal dispute or rights determinations.
