# Analysis of Women’s Labor Force Participation in Four Countries, 1970–2025

## Executive summary

Women’s labour-force participation rose most dramatically in Saudi Arabia, reached and sustained the highest level in Iceland, increased more gradually in South Korea, and followed a long decline then recent recovery in India.

On the common ILO-modelled female age-15-and-over measure, 2025 rates were 70.01% in Iceland, 56.78% in South Korea, 33.57% in Saudi Arabia, and 32.42% in India.[1] Saudi Arabia’s 2018–2025 increase of 11.75 percentage points coincided with a package of mobility, employment and labour-market reforms and with widening entry across education groups, but timing alone does not identify the causal contribution of each reform.[2][3] Korea’s education gains and family-policy expansion have reduced, but not removed, the childbearing-related career interruption and concentration of women in non-regular and part-time work.

Iceland’s combination of high female employment, public childcare, individual non-transferable parental-leave rights and comparatively egalitarian norms shows what supportive institutions can sustain, although its participation rate is not directly comparable to every national age-based measure.

India’s recent measured recovery should not be equated with a completed transition to good jobs because agriculture, informal and unpaid family work, measurement changes, care constraints and weak non-farm job creation remain decisive.

Population ageing will make retaining and increasing women’s work especially important in South Korea and Iceland, while Saudi Arabia and India retain larger potential gains from bringing younger and increasingly educated women into secure paid employment.

## Definition and historical participation

Women’s labour-force participation rates show how many women are attached to the labour market, whether through a job or an active search for one. They do not show whether those women have found work, how many hours they work, or whether their work is formal, secure, or well paid. Those distinctions matter particularly in this comparison: Saudi Arabia’s recent entry surge, South Korea’s gradual increase amid care-related career interruptions, Iceland’s high-participation economy, and India’s decline-and-recovery path can look very different depending on the measure used.

### What do women's labor force participation rates measure in Saudi

The female labour-force participation rate (FLFPR) is the female labour force divided by the relevant female population, expressed as a percentage. The labour force includes women who are employed and women who are unemployed but actively seeking employment. It therefore measures labour-market attachment, not employment alone. Saudi-focused research states the formula directly: women employed or actively seeking work divided by the working-age female population, multiplied by 100.[4] The general international definition similarly describes participation as the share of the working-age population that is working or actively looking for work.[5]

The international unemployment definition also requires a person without work to be available for work, as well as seeking it.[99]

For the common World Bank indicator based on International Labour Organization (ILO) modelled estimates, the denominator is women aged 15 and over. The indicator is explicitly labelled “Labor force participation rate, female (% of female population ages 15+) (modeled ILO estimate).” The same 15+ definition is stated for Saudi Arabia: economically active women include both employed women and unemployed women.[6][2] It is also stated for the Republic of Korea.[7][8] This 15+ ILO-modelled series is the most useful common numerical benchmark for Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Iceland and India.

The numerator includes jobseekers. A woman with no job but who is actively seeking one counts in the labour force; a woman who wants work but is not searching or is unavailable under the survey rules is normally classified as outside it.[9][10] This distinction is consequential in Saudi Arabia, where the recent expansion of participation included women entering job search as well as employment, and where female unemployment initially remained high even as participation rose.[11][12] It is equally consequential in India, where changes in measured participation can reflect changing job search as well as changing employment.

Saudi Arabia’s Labour Force Survey requires unemployed people to have no job or business, to be currently available and to have searched actively; it also counts future job starters as unemployed.[99]

Employment-to-population ratios answer a different question: how many women actually have jobs as a share of all women in the relevant age group. The World Bank-oriented explanation defines female employment rates as employed women divided by the female working-age population and explicitly separates this from participation, which additionally includes searching unemployed women.[13][4] Saudi Arabia’s reported 21.78% female employment-to-population ratio, for example, cannot be read as its female participation rate.[14] Similarly, the OECD’s Korean statistic that 66% of women aged 25–64 were employed in 2023 is an employment rate, not an unemployment-inclusive participation rate.[15]

For Saudi female nationals in Q3 2024, GASTAT reported a labour-force participation rate of 36.2% and a separate employment-to-population ratio of 31.3%.[100]

Part-time work also counts as employment, and hence counts toward participation. Under ILO-oriented guidance, employment can include someone who worked even part-time or for at least one hour during the survey reference period, usually one week; it also includes self-employment and work for pay, profit or family gain.[13] A high participation rate can therefore coexist with relatively short average hours, a high share of part-time jobs, or a large number of insecure and informal jobs.

Saudi Arabia’s Labour Force Survey counts at least one hour of unpaid work in a family enterprise during the reference week as employment; it also counts temporary absence from a job to which the worker will return.[99]

Conversely, a lower participation rate can conceal substantial unpaid household production and care work that national accounts and labour-force surveys do not classify as market employment.[13][16]

Unpaid domestic and care work is generally excluded from labour-force participation. Meal preparation, cleaning, upkeep of the dwelling, and care for children or sick, older, or disabled household members are economically essential activities, but they are not normally counted as employment if produced and consumed within the household.[13][17] This exclusion is central to interpreting women’s rates because women undertake disproportionately large shares of unpaid care work in most settings. The rate is therefore an indicator of market labour supply, not a measure of all productive work performed by women.[13][18]

The Saudi national discussion sometimes uses a less precisely bounded phrase, “working-age population,” rather than publishing the 15+ denominator alongside the rate. The Saudi National Labour Observatory defines participation as the share of the working-age population that is employed or actively seeking work, but elsewhere in its report uses inconsistent age ranges: a glossary gives ages 15–65, while the methodology separates youth aged 15–24 from working-age people aged 25–64.[19] A Saudi-citizen analysis defines participation as the share of adult Saudi women who had a job or were actively looking for one and explicitly excludes the expatriate population.[11] These national measures illuminate the participation of Saudi citizens, but they should not be mechanically merged with the all-resident 15+ ILO-modelled measure.

Saudi Arabia’s official Labour Force Survey covers civilians aged 15 and over living in private dwellings, including Saudi nationals and eligible non-Saudi residents who have lived in the Kingdom for at least six months.[99][101]

GASTAT defines its national female labour-force participation rate as the female labour force divided by the female population aged 15 and over.[99][101]

Saudi Arabia’s official household labour-force survey defines participation for residents aged 15 and over, including eligible non-Saudis; a Saudi-national rate requires a nationality-specific tabulation.[99]

Population scope is particularly important in Saudi Arabia. Women migrants often arrive specifically for employment or to join an employed spouse, and foreign women can raise an overall Gulf participation rate without indicating equivalent labour-market integration of national women.[20] Saudi-focused analyses therefore distinguish Saudi women from expatriate women, while the harmonised international series is designed for the country’s female population as defined in the underlying data.[11][2] The report should keep these populations separate rather than treating “women in Saudi Arabia” as one homogeneous labour-market group.

GASTAT’s Labour Force Survey covers civilian, non-institutional residents aged 15 and over in private dwellings, including Saudi and non-Saudi residents; non-Saudis generally must have lived in the Kingdom for at least six months.[99][101]

Age scope is also decisive. The World Bank/ILO indicator used here is 15+, but the OECD defines its general labour-force participation indicator for ages 15–64.[6][21] Other international comparisons use women aged 15–64.[22][23] Still others use “prime-age” women aged 25–54, an age band intended to reduce differences caused by education entry and retirement timing.[24][25] Iceland’s 89.6% figure in one OECD-oriented table, for example, is an average for people aged 25–54 in 2010–15 rather than all women aged 15+.[25] India’s historical values of 52.5% in 1980 and 46.3% in 1990 are likewise for women aged 25–54.[26]

The age composition of a country can alter an all-adult participation rate even if participation at every age is unchanged. People aged 25–54 are generally most likely to be in the labour force, while longer education delays entry and retirement systems influence exits at older ages.[22][23] South Korea’s exceptionally rapid ageing, Iceland’s comparatively different age structure, Saudi Arabia’s large youth population, and India’s younger population make a 15+ rate partly a demographic composition statistic as well as a behavioural one.

That is why like-for-like age-specific rates are preferable when the question is whether women at a given life stage have become more likely to work.

A further distinction is between women’s participation rate and women’s share of the labour force. The latter is women’s share among all people in the labour force; it can rise because women participate more, because men participate less, or because the population composition changes. The World Bank Gender Data Portal lists “Labor force, female (% of total labor force)” as a separate indicator from female participation.[27] Saudi Arabia’s reported 18.75% female share of the total labour force is therefore not a statement that 18.75% of Saudi women participate.[14] The same caution applies to the claim that women comprised 28% of Saudi Arabia’s labour force in 2022.[28]

A related but distinct indicator is the female-to-male participation ratio. It divides the female participation rate by the male participation rate and multiplies by 100.[29] Saudi Arabia’s ratio of 38.60 in 2021 thus means that the female rate was 38.60% of the male rate, not that 38.60% of women participated.[30] This ratio is useful for tracking relative gender gaps, but it cannot replace the women’s own participation rate.

The international statistical boundary has also changed. Under the older 13th International Conference of Labour Statisticians (ICLS) standard, employment included paid or profit-seeking work, self-employment, and own-use production of goods such as subsistence farming.[29][31] The 19th ICLS resolution in 2013 broadened the general concept of work to include own-use services, but narrowed the labour-force concept by excluding subsistence farmers whose output is mainly intended for their household’s own use.[31]

The boundary break is especially relevant for India, where agriculture, unpaid family labour, home-based work, and informal activity are important components of women’s economic activity.[26][5] It is less likely to drive Saudi Arabia’s recent movement, which occurred largely through new employment and job search in a more formalising labour market, although measurement design still matters.[32] Iceland and South Korea have more complete administrative and labour-force-survey systems, but their reported rates still vary with age scope, employment classification and survey timing.[29][30]

Labour-force surveys are the preferred source for internationally comparable participation data because they can include people with jobs, active jobseekers, workers in all sectors, multiple-job holders, self-employed people and family workers.[29][30] Population censuses tend to yield lower rates because they use fewer labour-market questions and allow less probing.

Establishment surveys omit unemployed people, workers in small establishments and many informal workers.[29][30] These methodological differences are not technical footnotes: they change who is counted, especially among women whose work is home-based, intermittent, unpaid within a family enterprise, or combined with care responsibilities.

Women’s work is frequently undercounted when surveys do not probe adequately for farming, family-enterprise work, home-based production or irregular informal work.[33][16] The problem is compounded where a male household member answers on behalf of women in the household.[33][5] Time-use surveys can better capture subsistence production, informal activity and unpaid work, but they are costly and therefore much less frequently fielded than labour-force surveys.[33][16] India’s low measured participation must consequently be read alongside its large informal and home-based work economy; a low rate is not evidence that women contribute little economic labour.

**The practical comparison rule is simple: use the female 15+ ILO-modelled rate for common cross-country levels, identify the country-specific population and age group whenever using national rates, and never substitute employment, hours, women’s share of the workforce, or a gender ratio for female participation.**[6][13][29]

This rule makes the later policy and demographic analysis interpretable. It separates a woman’s decision or ability to enter the labour force from whether she obtains a job, the type of job she obtains, and how much care work remains outside the measured economy.

Iceland’s principal national labour-force-participation series covers women aged 16–74.

#### Saudi Arabia: from exceptionally low participation to a rapid recent rise

Saudi Arabia began the period with extremely low measured female labour-market participation among Saudi nationals. A historical development-plan table reports that women represented 5.1% of the Saudi female working-age population in the labour force in 1985 and 5.3% in 1990; its 1995 figure of 5.5% was a plan estimate rather than an observed outcome.[35] The same source reported 27,000 Saudi women in the labour force in 1975, rising to 103,000 in 1980 and 168,900 in 1990.[35] Those are labour-force headcounts rather than participation rates, but they show that the number of Saudi women supplying labour expanded with population growth and educational expansion.

Saudi Arabia’s official Labour Force Survey began in 1999, so it provides no observed survey participation rate for 1970; earlier Saudi-national labour-force headcounts come from a different historical source.[101]

The scale of the gender divide in the late twentieth century was large. In 1990, the historical source reports 5.3% participation among Saudi women of working age and 54.4% among Saudi men.[35] Saudi women accounted for only 168,900 of a civilian labour force of 5.77 million; foreign workers accounted for 66.7% of that total.[35] The labour market was therefore shaped simultaneously by low national female participation and heavy reliance on expatriate labour.

Saudi women’s work was concentrated in education and the public sector during the earlier development period. One historical table reports 62,558 Saudi women teachers, equal to 73% of Saudi female civil-service employment in the table.[35] Educational expansion was substantial: the number of female schools rose from 453 in 1970 to 6,883 in 1987, and the number of female teachers rose from 5,000 to 67,800.[35] Yet women’s training options were narrowly channelled.

In 1987, only five of 27 diploma- and certificate-level educational types were available to women, and 10,521 of 13,187 female students in those programmes were in teachers’ institutes.[35]

The education-to-work mismatch was also visible in higher education. In 1987, 28,846 of 44,130 female university students in the institutions discussed were in education or social and human sciences, and 36,620—83%—were in fields leading to teaching positions.[35] The number of female high-school graduates rose from 1,674 in 1975 to 18,211 in 1988, while higher-education opportunities for women did not expand at the same pace according to the source.[35] This helps explain why education growth alone did not yield a proportionate expansion of occupational choice or market participation.

The Fourth Five-Year Development Plan, covering 1985–1990, was the first official Saudi plan to recognise women’s labour-force participation as a serious policy problem and to identify jobs for educated and skilled Saudi women as a challenge.[35] The historical analysis judged that the development plans then under discussion did not supply policies likely to produce much greater female participation.[35] The following decades retained a marked mismatch between women’s educational attainment and employment opportunities.

The harmonised annual 15+ series places Saudi Arabia’s female participation at a minimum of 14.90% in 1991, with an average of 20.24% across 1990–2025.[2] The annual values supplied for the recent acceleration are 21.82% in 2018, 24.36% in 2019, 29.31% in 2020, 34.22% in 2021, 34.10% in 2022, 34.28% in 2023, 33.31% in 2024, and 33.57% in 2025.[2] The series therefore indicates a sharp rise from 2018 through 2021, followed by a plateau around one-third of women aged 15+.

Saudi national reporting produces somewhat different levels because it uses a Saudi-citizen focus and national definitions. The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development reports female participation rising from 17% in 2017 to 36.3% in the first quarter of 2025.[3] The Saudi Press Agency reports a rise from 21.2% in 2017 to 35.4% at the end of the third quarter of 2024.[36] Brookings reports participation of Saudi women rising from 20% in late 2018 to 33% at the end of 2020.[11] These rates are not contradictory in substance: all show a large increase in Saudi women’s labour-market attachment, though their populations, reference periods and seasonal adjustments differ.

GASTAT reported a 36.2% labour-force participation rate for Saudi female nationals in Q3 2024, compared with 36.3% in Q1 2025.[100][102]

The magnitude of the recent Saudi increase was broad across ages. Brookings reports that women aged 40–54 experienced participation increases of more than 20 percentage points between the end of 2018 and the end of 2020. Most other age groups rose by at least 10 percentage points, while the youngest and oldest age groups rose by approximately 3–5 points.[11] The World Bank’s MENA comparison identifies Saudi Arabia as the only country in its regional group with participation rising across all age groups between 2017 and 2022.[20]

GASTAT reported that Saudi female nationals aged 15–24 had labour-force participation rates of 18.0% in Q3 2024 and 18.4% in Q1 2025.[100][102]

The change also reached women outside the university-educated group. Participation among Saudi women with basic and intermediate education more than doubled between 2017 and 2021 in the World Bank’s regional comparison.[20] A Saudi employment diagnostic reports that women with less than high-school education had an eightfold increase in participation between 2017 and 2021 and high-school-educated women more than tripled their participation, while participation among university-educated women was relatively stable.[32] The composition of the female labour force shifted accordingly: women with university degrees were 70% of the female labour force in 2017 but 52% of the female workforce by 2022, while women with high-school education or less rose from 16% to 35%.[32]

The recent rise was not simply an expansion of job search without employment. Saudi women’s employment rate within the labour force rose from 68% at the end of 2018 to 76% in 2020, while female unemployment fell from 32% to 24%.[11] A later diagnostic reports that female participation began to rise sharply in 2018 without a corresponding increase in unemployment, indicating that many new entrants found work.[32] The same diagnostic’s shift-share analysis attributes 70% of female employment gains from 2017 to 2021 to the participation effect and finds a minimal demographic effect.[32]

GASTAT reported that 31.3% of Saudi-national women were employed as a share of the relevant female population in Q3 2024, compared with a 36.2% labour-force participation rate that also counted unemployed jobseekers.[100]

In Q3 2024, GASTAT reported a 31.3% employment-to-population ratio for Saudi women, compared with its 36.2% Saudi-female labour-force participation rate for the same quarter.[100]

GASTAT reported unemployment among Saudi women of 12.1% in Q3 2025, 1.5 percentage points below Q3 2024.[103]

GASTAT reported an “employed Saudi females rate” of 29.7% in Q3 2025, three percentage points above its level five years earlier; this employment measure is not a labour-force participation rate.[103][104]

Saudi Arabia’s path is therefore an unmistakable transformation from low female participation to a much larger female labour supply, especially after 2018. Its 2025 15+ rate of 33.57% remained below the 2025 world average of 51.41%, but the historical direction and recent magnitude are among the most pronounced in the four-country comparison.[2][1]

#### South Korea: gradual long-run growth, interrupted by motherhood and segmented employment

South Korea’s female participation increased during rapid economic development, urbanisation, educational expansion and fertility decline. A World Bank paper published in 1987 reports that participation of women from non-farm households was 38% and had been rising steadily.[37] The paper attributes the increase to strong economic growth, rural-to-urban migration, rising education and reduced fertility.[37] These were mutually reinforcing changes: industrialisation created wage work, urbanisation altered household production and access to jobs, education raised women’s potential earnings, and reduced fertility changed the amount of time required for childbearing.

Statistics Korea’s Economically Active Population Survey is the national source for South Korea’s labour-market series.[105]

Korea had already achieved universal primary education by 1970, though high-school enrolment remained unequal in 1980: 74.4% for boys and 62.2% for girls.[37] Women later overtook men in tertiary attainment.

Among people aged 25–34, 27% of women and 35% of men had tertiary education in 1997; both reached 51% in 2005; by 2023, the shares were 77% for women and 63% for men.[15] This education reversal did not automatically produce gender parity in employment or participation.

The Korean female labour market historically had an “M-shaped curve”: employment was high in women’s early twenties, fell in the late twenties, rose again through the thirties and forties, then declined at older ages.[38] The pattern is consistent with women leaving paid work around marriage and childbearing and returning later.

The source reports that the curve became less steep as women’s employment rose faster than men’s, indicating that more women remained attached to work through the years when career interruption had previously been common.[38]

A Korean paid-leave case study reports female participation rising from 48.8% in 2000 to 60.0% in 2019.[38] Among women in their thirties, the proportion remaining in the labour market rose from 54.1% in 2000 to 64.1% in 2019.[38] The study presents paid leave as one contributor to this development, while the broader economic and institutional context also changed.

The relevant policy mechanisms belong in “Policy interventions and cultural shifts”; the historical point here is that Korea’s most visible long-run advance came partly through reducing, rather than eliminating, the mid-career withdrawal associated with family formation.

The annual ILO-modelled 15+ series gives South Korea a 1990–2025 average of 50.83%, a minimum of 47.12% in 1998, and a maximum of 56.83% in 2024.[7] Its recent values were 53.22% in 2018, 53.77% in 2019, 53.14% in 2020, 53.65% in 2021, 55.03% in 2022, 56.04% in 2023, 56.83% in 2024 and 56.78% in 2025.[7] The small fall in 2020 is consistent with a visible COVID-19-era dip in South Korean female participation identified in an OECD-oriented discussion.[10]

South Korea’s official female employment rate—not its female labour-force participation rate—was 47.0% in 2000, 51.6% in 2019, 50.7% in 2020 and 54.7% in 2024.[106][105]

The International Labour Organization’s G20 comparison reports that women’s participation increased by more than four percentage points in South Korea over the six-year comparison ending in the first quarter of 2025.[39] It also reports that Korea was among the G20 countries whose narrowing gender participation gap since 2012 was close to or greater than the reduction required under the Brisbane Goal.[39] The national participation rate nevertheless remained substantially below Iceland’s high-participation level and was only moderately above the 2025 world average.[1]

The main structural restraint in South Korea is not educational scarcity but the interaction of family care, workplace norms and a segmented labour market. The OECD reports that women aged 25–64 had an employment rate of 66% in 2023, up from 55% in 1996, but still below the OECD average of 73%; the gender employment gap fell from 35 to 19 percentage points.[15] Highly educated women have not escaped this gap.

South Korea’s Ministry of Employment and Labor reported a female employment-to-population rate of 54.7% in 2024 for women aged 15 and over; this measures employment, not unemployment-inclusive labour-force participation.[105]

South Korea’s female employment rate rose from 50.7% in 2020 to 54.7% in 2024; the share of married women experiencing career interruption was 17.6% in 2020.[105][106]

Tertiary-educated women aged 25–34 were 7 percentage points less likely to be employed than their male peers, and the gap widened to 18.3 points for tertiary-educated adults aged 25–64.[15]

Childbirth has large long-run labour-market consequences in Korea. The OECD estimates mothers’ long-term labour-income loss following childbirth at approximately 66%, largely due to prolonged career interruptions; fathers’ earnings are described as largely unaffected.[15] Women often exit regular jobs upon motherhood and return to lower-paid non-regular positions, and mothers are three times more likely than fathers to be in non-regular employment.[15] That pattern explains why rising aggregate participation does not by itself signal equal access to stable, high-quality careers.

In 2020, South Korea classified 1.506 million married women aged 15–54, or 17.6% of that population, as career-interrupted; the share was 4.4 percentage points lower than in 2015.[106]

Time use shows the household counterpart to the Korean participation pattern. In the 2014 Time Use Survey, women spent more than 3.5 hours per day on unpaid work such as housework and childcare, compared with 49 minutes for men.[15] Men spent 2.5 more hours per day in paid work than women.[15] The persistence of this division of labour makes it difficult for women to sustain continuous full-time careers, particularly in workplaces where long hours and regular employment remain the norm.

Cultural attitudes reinforce institutional constraints. In World Values Survey data for 2017–22, 59% of Korean men and 47% of Korean women agreed or strongly agreed that men should have more right to a job than women when jobs are scarce.[15] Korea also recorded very high agreement with propositions that men make better business executives or political leaders.[15] These attitudes are not proof of the cause of any particular annual rate change, but they describe the environment in which women’s education, work aspirations and family choices operate.

South Korea’s trajectory is thus a gradual upward shift in female participation, not a rapid discontinuity. Economic development and education brought more women into the labour force; family-related exits became less severe; but care burdens, workplace segmentation and gender norms have prevented women’s human-capital gains from translating fully into equal labour-market attachment.

#### Iceland: persistently high participation, with measurement-sensitive levels

Iceland is the high-participation case in the available comparison. An OECD-oriented source reports that Iceland’s female participation rate was 85.4% in 2008, the highest among the OECD countries shown in that figure.[40] Another source identifies Iceland as one of six OECD countries with female participation of at least 85% in mid-2014.[42] A 2019 comparison reports Icelandic female participation at 84.4% and parental leave at 17.7 full-rate-equivalent weeks, where full-rate equivalent converts the leave duration to the number of weeks that would be available at 100% wage replacement.[43]

These figures place Iceland among the world’s highest female-participation economies during the 2000s and 2010s. A separate OECD-oriented table gives a participation rate of 89.6% for Iceland for people aged 25–54, averaged across 2010–15.[25] That prime-age measure is expected to be higher than an all-adult measure because it excludes most students, younger workers and older people who have retired. It should not be compared directly with the 15+ values used for the common World Bank/ILO series.

The 2008 observation reports 85.4% without stating an age scope; the 2010–15 observation reports 89.6% for people aged 25–54; and the 2019 snapshot reports 84.4% without an age range in the supplied passage.[40][25][43] These observations establish Iceland as a high-participation case under their stated source measures.[40][25][43]

Iceland’s participation path belongs to the broader Nordic pattern of high female employment and substantial female representation in health, education and public-sector work.[31] Its high rate reflects more than any single family-policy instrument.

A durable combination of women’s education, accessible labour demand in public and private services, social infrastructure, and a social norm of women’s paid work supports labour-market attachment across the life course.Judgment — The central comparative inference is that Iceland demonstrates the difference between a high female participation equilibrium and a one-off policy effect: institutions, labour demand and household arrangements reinforce one another over decades.

The evidence also identifies transport access as a work-enabling condition in Iceland. Iceland introduced programmes intended to improve public-transport safety, frequency and accessibility, with the stated aim of enabling women, particularly women with low incomes, to participate more actively in the workforce.[44] This matters because participation is partly a practical question of whether jobs can be reached safely and affordably, not merely whether legal equality exists.

The Icelandic evidence supplies few historical observations before 2008. Its contribution to the four-country comparison is therefore level and institutional contrast rather than a continuous 1970–2025 numerical trajectory. Iceland was already a high-participation country in the available 2008, 2010–15, 2014 and 2019 comparisons, and it remained high in the 2025 15+ series.[40][25][42][43][1]

#### India: decline through the 2000s and early 2010s, then a recent recovery

India has three separate measured comparisons. First, a United Nations prime-age series reports 52.5% for women aged 25–54 in 1980, 46.3% in 1990 and 47.7% in the latest year in the 1990s.Derived — the rate declined by 6.2 percentage points from 1980 to 1990, calculated as 46.3 minus 52.5, then was 1.4 points higher in the latest 1990s observation, calculated as 47.7 minus 46.3.[26] The same table reports India’s total fertility rate at 4.5 births per woman in 1980–85 and 3.3 in 1995–2000.[26]

A different India series reports a decline from 34.1% in 1999–2000 to 27.2% in 2011–12.[33][5] This was a fall of 6.9 percentage points.Derived — The decline is calculated as 27.2 minus 34.1, using the published India observations for 1999–2000 and 2011–12.[5] The source describes the decline as particularly puzzling because it coincided with economic growth and rising female education rather than with a straightforward deterioration in women’s human capital.[5][31]

Several explanations have been proposed for India’s decline. They include greater school enrolment among girls and young women, weak job opportunities for women, a household-income effect in which families can afford for women not to seek work, and mismeasurement of women’s economic activity.[33][5] These explanations are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

More education can reduce measured participation in the short run by keeping young women in school; rising household income can reduce economic necessity; weak growth in jobs considered acceptable or suitable for women can suppress demand; and survey design can miss informal or home-based work.

India’s education-participation relationship is notably non-linear. Research summarised in an IZA conference paper describes a U-shaped pattern: participation is high among women with little or no schooling and among women with completed tertiary education, but lower at intermediate schooling levels.[31] The explanation is that women with very little education often work out of necessity in subsistence or informal activity, while women at intermediate education may withdraw from low-status work without gaining access to suitable salaried work. Participation rises again when tertiary credentials command higher potential earnings and when suitable jobs exist.[33][31]

The Indian U-shape became shallower over time.[31] A separate 1990–2023 analysis reports an inverted-U relationship between women’s tertiary enrolment and India’s female participation rate.[45] These results use different education measures and methods, but they point to the same broad conclusion: expanding education does not automatically increase aggregate female participation. The outcome depends on whether labour demand expands in occupations and locations compatible with women’s education, mobility and family responsibilities.

Household income and gender norms also shape India’s participation. A cross-country research synthesis reports a substantial negative association between other household income and women’s participation in India, though the relationship moderated over time.[31] It also reports that husbands’ education had a strong negative association with women’s participation above and beyond household income, and that decomposition studies identified this factor as important in the stagnation of urban Indian women’s participation and across India.[31] The interpretation is not that education itself reduces women’s work; rather, education, status, social expectations and household income can jointly produce a male-provider/female-housewife pattern.

Norms differ sharply within India. A qualitative comparative study describes restrictive norms in rural Haryana and more acceptance of women’s participation in urban Chennai, where work is often associated with financial distress.[45] An Odisha study finds that Hindu women in villages with stronger Adivasi presence reported less adherence to caste-purity norms of female seclusion and were more likely to report paid work outside the home.[46] In Madhya Pradesh, men and women overestimated local disapproval of women working outside the home, and experimental correction of those misperceptions increased women’s labour-force participation.[46] These findings show that labour-market behaviour is shaped by locally transmitted beliefs, not simply national income or schooling.

Work location is another constraint. Field experiments in India find that women are often willing to work from home but reluctant to work outside the home even when outside work would substantially raise household income and is offered in safe locations close to home with childcare access.[47] A West Bengal experiment found that flexible, home-based data-entry work increased take-up and made women subsequently more willing to accept less flexible work outside the home; participants also reported more liberal gender-role attitudes in short-run follow-up.[46] Work from home can therefore be a bridge into market activity, though it is not a substitute for safe mobility and equal access to outside employment.

India’s sectoral development helps explain why education expansion did not necessarily bring a large participation rise. The country’s supply of educated women grew more rapidly than its working-age population, while growth in white-collar services and public-service-linked jobs regarded as suitable for educated women did not keep pace.[31] India also lacked the export-oriented manufacturing expansion that generated large-scale female employment in parts of East Asia and Bangladesh, while women’s employment opportunities in agriculture declined.[31] The result was a shortage of employment opportunities that matched women’s education, aspirations and social constraints.

There were nevertheless important service-sector openings. Studies cited in the IZA World of Labour review identify the expansion of Indian IT call centres as increasing women’s participation in a way similar to the earlier expansion of clerical employment.[23][22] A United Nations paper also noted growing employment of Indian women in offshore back offices and call centres, often from their own homes.[26] These activities did not become large enough to offset all losses and constraints nationally during the 2000s, but they illustrate how the sectoral character of growth matters.

India’s labour-force measurement is especially consequential because women’s non-agricultural work is highly informal. A United Nations account states that the informal economy accounted for nine out of every ten women working outside agriculture in India in the figures cited.[26] Home-based production, contributing family work and work embedded in household enterprises are harder to identify with standard survey questions.

The India series should thus be interpreted as a measure of reported labour-market attachment under the applicable survey definitions, not a complete measure of all productive labour women perform.

A recent ILO G20 comparison for people aged 15–64 in urban India reports that women’s participation increased by more than eight percentage points between 2012 and 2024 and by more than four percentage points from Q4 2019 to Q4 2024.[39] A research abstract separately describes a decline between 1994 and 2004 and a sudden rise from 2018 onward.[45] No definition-consistent Indian PLFS endpoint for 1970 is established.[26][5]

A separate UNDP brief gives a circa-2023 Indian female participation rate of 35.1%, compared with 76.4% for men.[18] The difference from the 2025 32.42% modelled estimate should not be interpreted as a measured two-year national decline because the cited passages do not specify matching survey dates, age coverage or estimation methods.

The substantive conclusion is more stable: India’s female participation remains far below men’s and below the global average, despite the recovery since 2018.[18][1]

India’s history demonstrates why a single “development brings women into work” narrative fails. Education expanded, fertility fell, and modern service industries emerged, but labour demand, social norms, household income, safety, informality and survey measurement all shaped whether these changes appeared as rising female participation. The future policy discussion must therefore distinguish measures that expand women’s capabilities from measures that change the availability, safety, acceptability and quality of paid work.

#### Cross-country historical comparison

The available national and age-specific observations identify sharply different historical paths: Saudi Arabia experienced a rapid recent rise, South Korea increased from 2000 to 2019 and in the recent 15–64 comparison, Iceland recorded high participation under several distinct measures, and India declined in the 1990s–2010s before a recent urban recovery.[3][36][38][39][40][25][43][26][5]

Saudi Arabia and India had similar 2025 levels in the harmonised series, but their recent trajectories differ. Saudi Arabia’s increase was a rapid policy-era surge from a very low base, with participation rising sharply after 2018.[32][2] India’s recent rise followed a longer decline through the 2000s and early 2010s, with recovery from 2018 onward.[5][45] South Korea entered the period at a considerably higher level than Saudi Arabia and India and increased more gradually through a transition away from the M-shaped employment pattern.[38][7]

Iceland’s position is qualitatively different again. Its available evidence is consistent with a mature high-participation economy in which women’s employment is broadly normalised across the life course.[40][42][43] Its high level should not be treated as a benchmark that can be mechanically reproduced by importing a single childcare, leave or legal policy into countries with different labour demand, family structures, social norms and demographic profiles.[48][22]

The common 2025 comparison also must not be projected backwards as if all four countries followed a common path. Saudi Arabia’s female participation was historically very low among nationals and rose rapidly only in the late 2010s and early 2020s.[35][11] South Korea’s female participation rose during industrialisation and later increased as women’s mid-career attachment improved.[37][38] India’s participation fell in several historical series despite fertility decline and rising education.[26][5][31] Iceland was already at high levels in the available 2008–19 comparative observations.[40][43]

The historical comparison therefore decides the correct analytical approach for the remainder of the report: policy changes should be read against each country’s starting structure, not against a presumed common development sequence. Saudi Arabia’s central question is how recently lowered barriers interact with new labour demand; South Korea’s is how to reduce the motherhood and job-quality penalty; Iceland’s is how to sustain high attachment across a changing population; India’s is how to turn women’s education and aspirations into safe, acceptable and adequately paid work.

India’s Ministry of Labour and Employment defines LFPR as the proportion of the working-age population that is working or looking for work, and reports the PLFS female 15+ rate rising from 23.3% in 2017–18 to 24.5% in 2018–19, 30.0% in 2019–20 and 32.5% in 2020–21. GASTAT derives its principal labour-force indicators from labour-force-survey estimates, while its publication also uses administrative records from the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development, the General Organization for Social Insurance and the National Information Center.[99]

The cost is that a country-year value can be partly modelled rather than a directly published national survey estimate, particularly where reporting is sparse.[29] It is therefore appropriate for broad levels and direction, but national policy analysis should also use the relevant domestic labour-force survey where population scope and timing matter.

Saudi Arabia revised its Labour Force Survey questionnaires in 2011 and 2018, changed interview modes during and after the COVID-19 period, began continuous weekly sampling in Q1 2023, and expanded work and job-search questions in Q1 2025.[101][99]

The series’ intended harmonisation does not eliminate uncertainty. The ILO modelled estimates can be revised when new national data or methodological updates become available.[31] The World Bank advises against treating such values as a basis for country ranking where uncertainty is high, and the ILO advises careful use.[29] This does not negate the observed Saudi surge or the broad cross-country gaps; it means that small annual movements, especially differences of a few tenths of a point, should not be overinterpreted as substantive behavioural change.

GASTAT expanded its employment and unemployment questions in 2011, changed to computer-assisted personal interviewing in 2013, began quarterly collection in Q2 2016 and redesigned the survey around 19th International Conference of Labour Statisticians standards in 2018.[101]

Saudi survey collection moved to telephone interviewing in Q2 2020, combined personal and telephone interviews from Q2 2022, and adopted weekly sampling, continuous quarterly collection and sample rotation in Q1 2023.[101]

A Saudi questionnaire revision beginning in Q1 2025 added questions on formal and informal paid work, digital-platform and home-based work, expanded checks of job search, and measurement of informal-sector employment; it also replaced the ICSE-93 employment-status classification with ICSE-18.[99]

GASTAT used the 1992 Census to weight its 1999–2003 labour-force results, the 2004 Census for 2006–09, the 2010 Census for 2011–20 and the 2022 Census for 2021–25; changes to population weights can affect subgroup rate estimates.[101]

Age bases create the largest visible incompatibility. The harmonised female series is 15+, but OECD presentations may use 15–64 and comparative research may use 25–54.[6][21][22][25] A 25–54 rate excludes adolescents remaining in education and older women who have retired.

A 15+ rate includes both. South Korea’s 64% rate in one OECD-oriented discussion is for women aged 25–64, while its 2025 World Bank/ILO rate of 56.78% covers women aged 15+.[10][1] Iceland’s 89.6% 2010–15 prime-age figure and 70.01% 2025 15+ figure are likewise not interchangeable.[25][1]

India’s historical values reveal the same problem. The 52.5% 1980 and 46.3% 1990 figures refer to women aged 25–54.[26] The 34.1% figure for 1999–2000 and 27.2% for 2011–12 come from another series whose supplied passage does not state the age denominator.[33][5] India’s 2025 32.42% figure is the 15+ ILO-modelled estimate.[1] The differences may reflect real historical change, different age ranges, different collection methods or combinations of all three. The correct presentation is to state the measures beside their age bases rather than to splice them into one apparently seamless series.

India also has an urban-coverage issue in the ILO G20 comparison. The report’s recent comparison uses people aged 15–64, but India is urban areas only and its actual dates are Q4 2019 to Q4 2024 rather than the report’s otherwise general Q1 2019 to Q1 2025 comparison window.[39] That measure is valuable for identifying a recent urban participation increase, but it is not a national all-India series and should not be placed alongside Saudi or Korean all-population rates without that label.

Saudi Arabia has both an age-base and a population-scope issue. The international indicator covers women aged 15+.[2] National accounts of the recent increase commonly focus on Saudi women rather than all women resident in the country, because expatriate women have a distinct migration-driven employment pattern.[11][20] Saudi national reports also present rates at quarterly dates, while the ILO-modelled series is annual.[3][2] These series tell related but not identical stories: national quarterly indicators are best for Saudi-citizen policy outcomes, while the modelled 15+ series is best for the four-country comparison.

Saudi Arabia’s labour-force survey changed from annual to quarterly reporting and underwent changes in questionnaires, interview modes, sample design, census weights and measurement of informal work.[99][101]

Saudi Arabia’s national labour-market series begins in 1999. Its releases shifted from annual to semi-annual and then quarterly from 2016, questionnaires and collection methods changed repeatedly, and additional work and job-search questions were introduced in Q1 2025; unadjusted national rates therefore require caution in exact pre/post comparisons.[101][99]

Sectoral and employment-status differences also shape measurement. Establishment surveys cannot measure participation correctly because they exclude unemployed jobseekers and often miss informal or small-establishment employment.[29] This limitation is especially acute for India, where informal work has been widespread among women outside agriculture.[26] Censuses can also undercount women’s participation because limited questions leave little opportunity to distinguish productive work from domestic activity.[29][13]

The 2013 labour-force boundary change is a second cross-period break. Earlier statistical practice could count production of goods for own use, including subsistence farming, as employment; the newer labour-force definition excludes subsistence farmers producing mainly for own use.[31] The change is most likely to influence countries and subpopulations with substantial subsistence agriculture.

In India, it can alter both participation and the sectoral classification of women who combine small amounts of off-farm work with larger amounts of own-use farm production.[31] The change matters less for interpreting Iceland’s and South Korea’s high-income, predominantly non-subsistence labour markets, though all international series still require attention to the standard used.

Measurement misses women’s work in all four countries in different ways. In Saudi Arabia, women’s work may be constrained by formal employment access and family permission, making nonparticipation statistically visible but socially heterogeneous.[12][55] In South Korea, women’s reduced-hours, non-regular and interrupted work can remain visible in surveys but may be obscured if analysis focuses only on standard full-time employment.[15] In Iceland, a high participation rate does not reveal the distribution between full-time and part-time work.[24] In India, unpaid family work, agricultural activity, home-based production and informal work pose the greatest risk of undercounting.[33][26][5]

The distinction between participation and work intensity must be retained throughout. Participation answers whether women are in the labour force. Hours answer how much paid work they do. Part-time employment shares answer the composition of employment among women who are already employed. The OECD-oriented historical definition of part-time work commonly uses less than 30 usual hours per week in the main job, but national thresholds can differ.[24] Saudi Arabia’s reported female part-time share of 2.81% in 2015 is a share of employed women, not a participation rate.[56] The separate “Employment patterns by education, sector and hours” section addresses these distinctions in detail.

The same logic applies to unemployment. The unemployment rate has the labour force—not the female population—as its denominator.[9] A fall in female unemployment can occur because women find jobs, because they cease searching and leave the labour force, or because both processes occur.

Saudi Arabia’s 2018–20 evidence is informative precisely because it reports participation, employment within the labour force and unemployment separately.[11] Interpreting only one of those indicators would give an incomplete account.

The report should also avoid conflating participation with representation in management, professional employment or specific sectors. The ILO notes that women account for less than 20% of managerial jobs in Saudi Arabia, South Korea and India.[39] This demonstrates vertical occupational inequality, not the proportion of women in the labour force. Similarly, Saudi women’s changing share in education and health describes sectoral allocation among employed women, not their overall labour-market participation.[32]

**The valid comparison is therefore not a single uninterrupted 1970–2025 line. It is a layered comparison: a harmonised 15+ annual ILO-modelled series for 1990–2025; national and age-specific historical observations for earlier decades; and separate measures for employment, unemployment, hours, sector and population group.**[6][1][35][26][29]

This layered method rules out false precision. It permits strong conclusions about broad trajectories—Saudi Arabia’s rapid recent increase, South Korea’s gradual rise, Iceland’s high participation and India’s earlier decline followed by recovery—while retaining the different populations and definitions that generated the observations.

India’s earlier employment–unemployment observations came from National Sample Survey rounds. The Periodic Labour Force Survey began in 2017, creating a source and design break between earlier survey observations and the 2017–2025 series.

India’s headline Periodic Labour Force Survey female labour-force participation rate was 41.7% in 2023–24 under the usual-status measure, for women aged 15 and over.

Iceland’s national rate commonly covers ages 16–74, whereas the commonly cited national and international measures for Saudi Arabia, South Korea and India cover women aged 15 and over. This age-base difference limits direct level comparisons.

Female participation is shaped by both labour supply and labour demand. On the supply side, education, fertility, household income, care responsibilities, safety, mobility and gender norms affect women’s willingness and ability to seek work. On the demand side, the number, location, pay, hours and social acceptability of jobs affect whether women can obtain work worth taking. The field often frames the decision through a woman’s market wage and reservation wage: the market wage is what she can earn, while the reservation wage is the value she places on time at home or the wage at which entering paid work becomes worthwhile.[22][23]

Economic development does not determine one universal female-participation path. A cross-country relationship between GDP per capita and female participation is often described as U-shaped: participation can be high in poor economies with widespread subsistence work, lower in middle-income economies during structural change, and higher in rich economies with education and services.[13][5] Yet the relationship is weak, varies strongly by region and is not robust across all datasets and methods.[33][16] Saudi Arabia and India had lower female participation than most countries at similar income levels in the 2025 cross-section, despite recent gains.[16]

The U-shaped framework is most useful as a warning against simplistic inference. It explains why women’s labour-market participation can fall when household incomes rise and women leave subsistence or low-productivity work, and why it can rise later with education and service-sector employment.[57][5] It does not predict individual country histories well enough to explain Saudi Arabia’s rapid post-2018 increase, Korea’s M-shaped life-course pattern, Iceland’s high participation or India’s extended downturn. Those outcomes depend on national industrial structures, care institutions, gender norms and the distribution of power within households.

#### Saudi Arabia: oil-based development, education expansion and a late opening of labour demand

Saudi Arabia’s development model combined oil wealth, government spending, public employment, a large expatriate workforce and a conservative gender order. Oil and gas were estimated to account for about 50% of GDP and roughly 85% of export earnings in the cited account.[55] The oil boom from 2003 to 2014 produced substantial employment growth through government spending, yet Saudi women continued to face limited labour-market opportunities.[32] This contrast shows that aggregate job creation does not automatically generate jobs accessible to women.

Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 began in April 2016; its National Transformation Program included Qurrah childcare support and transport support for Saudi women employed in the private sector.[107]

The earlier labour market offered a narrow occupational channel to educated Saudi women. Teaching and health were especially important, while technical and commercial pathways were heavily restricted for women in the 1980s.[35] As a result, Saudi women could acquire education while confronting occupational segregation and a shortage of jobs matching their qualifications.

In 2015, 68% of unemployed Saudi women had a bachelor’s degree or higher, compared with 21% of unemployed Saudi men.[55] The problem was not simply an insufficient supply of educated women; it was the fit between qualifications, acceptable occupations and available jobs.

Women’s education expanded markedly. Female schools and teachers grew rapidly between 1970 and 1987, and women later formed the majority of university graduates.[35][55] By 2020, Saudi Arabia’s female-to-male literacy ratio was 98, female-to-male enrolment was 1.0 at primary and secondary levels and 1.1 at tertiary level, and women accounted for 70.6% of tertiary graduates in natural sciences, mathematics and statistics in 2021.[58] This educational convergence created a large potential female labour supply before employment opportunities and legal access expanded to the same degree.

The post-2016 transformation differed from the oil-boom period because women’s labour-market entry rose amid diversification, stronger private-sector demand and legal and institutional change. Saudi women’s private-sector employment grew by 78% between 2016 and 2022, compared with 21% for Saudi men; women added approximately 425,000 jobs and men approximately 278,000.[32] More than 70% of women’s added jobs occurred in 2021 and 2022.[32] The timing suggests that changing demand and entry barriers mattered more than simple demographic growth.

Vision 2030 was launched in April 2016, before the late-2010s acceleration in Saudi women’s participation.[107][104]

Women’s jobs also became less concentrated in education and health. Over 65% of employed Saudi women worked in education and health in 2016, compared with around 37% in 2022.[32] Employment gains occurred in business services, IT and communications, energy and health; food services, retail and manufacturing also added Saudi women while expatriate employment in those industries fell.[32] Sectoral diversification broadened the kinds of jobs available to women, though many women’s jobs remained exposed to oil-linked public spending through education, health and retail.[32]

Social expectations were a material constraint before and during the recent transition. A Saudi analysis identifies guardianship and sex segregation as barriers rooted partly in legislation and sustained by social norms.[55] A 2022 report describes large-scale female participation as an emerging Saudi norm, while also stating that traditional gender norms continue to limit women’s participation relative to men’s.[12] Family influence remains strong: 62% of surveyed women said they would be unlikely to take a job their parents did not approve of, and 67% said family had a major influence on their choice of industry.[12]

Role models can influence aspirations. Among young Saudi women exposed to working female role models, 36% expected to reach senior executive levels, compared with 28% among women with less such exposure.[12] A study of Saudi undergraduates cited in the same report found that women with working mothers were more likely to plan to work full-time.[12] These associations are relevant to the sustainability of participation growth because the rapid increase itself may alter perceived normality of women’s paid work.

Saudi Arabia’s labour-force growth has also been supported by a young population. The National Labour Observatory reports that the number of people under age 30 exceeded 18 million and identifies this as a source of labour-force growth.[19] Yet historical decomposition finds that demographic change had only a minimal effect on Saudi women’s employment gains from 2017 to 2021, while increased participation accounted for 70% of the gain.[32] The near-term demographic question is therefore not whether more women of working age exist, but whether the economy can absorb a large cohort of educated women into jobs with viable pay, transport, safety and career progression.

#### South Korea: export-led development, educational achievement and the work–care conflict

South Korea’s earlier development path created a different foundation. Rapid industrialisation in East and Southeast Asia moved workers, including women, from agriculture into manufacturing, while improved education and export-oriented production created new jobs.[5][33] Korea’s early social-protection system was closely tied to large manufacturing firms and public organisations central to export-oriented growth.[59] Women’s participation rose within this industrialising economy, but occupational access was restricted: a 1987 World Bank review found gains in professional and technical jobs but a narrow range of opportunities and an absence of women in management.[37]

South Korea later transitioned toward high-technology production and an information-and-communications-technology-driven economy.[59] This raised the education required for desirable jobs and increased competition for secure regular employment. Korea’s labour market is described as dual: high-quality jobs are limited, while women who re-enter after career interruptions often return through lower-paid non-regular work.[15] Thus, a highly educated female population faces a labour market in which a career break can have unusually large and lasting costs.

Korean women’s tertiary attainment rose exceptionally fast. By 2019, 76.5% of women aged 25–34 had attained tertiary education, compared with 63.8% of men.[38] By 2023, the corresponding shares were 77% and 63%.[15] Yet education did not remove the motherhood penalty because seniority-based compensation, long-hours workplace norms and non-regular employment make continuous tenure especially valuable.[15]

The work–care conflict is central. Korean women often report having to choose between paid work and childcare; some work less than desired, while others remain employed but have fewer children than they want.[38] Women performed over 3.5 hours of unpaid work per day in the 2014 Time Use Survey, against 49 minutes for men.[15] This unequal allocation of care interacts with career structures that penalise reduced hours and interruption. Full-time work remains the norm, while part-time roles often have lower pay, fewer protections and atypical contracts.[15]

In South Korea, use of reduced working hours during childcare rose from 1,891 women in 2015 to 13,059 women in 2020.[106]

South Korea reduced its statutory maximum working week from 68 to 52 hours in 2018.[108]

Korean gender norms support a male-provider/female-primary-caregiver model even as young women’s educational attainment and economic autonomy rise.[15] The result is a tension distinctive to South Korea: women have high potential market wages, but the household and workplace arrangements make combining work and family costly. That tension helps explain both rising female participation and persistently large employment and earnings gaps.

Fertility decline sharpens the demographic stakes. Korea’s total fertility rate was 1.3 children per woman in 2001, 1.08 in 2005 and below 1 from 2018.[38][60] The country’s old-age dependency ratio rose from 7.3 people aged 65+ per 100 people aged 20–64 in 1964 to 29.3 in 2024, and is projected to reach 122 by 2084.[15] A shrinking working-age population increases the economic value of mobilising women’s skills and sustaining women’s labour-force attachment through motherhood and later life.

In South Korea, women’s average age at first marriage rose from 26.5 years in 2000 to 30.8 years in 2020, while the total fertility rate fell from 1.48 to 0.84 children per woman over the same period.[106]

#### Iceland: high participation as an institutional and social equilibrium

Iceland’s high participation is consistent with the Nordic pattern in which women are strongly represented in public-sector-linked health, education and care work.[31] Its female participation is not only a consequence of high income.

It reflects a long-standing institutional environment in which women’s paid work is socially normal, family policy supports attachment to work, and labour demand exists across sectors that employ women.[40][42][43]

The relevant family and work arrangements include paid leave, public care infrastructure and accessible work. Iceland’s parental leave is classified in one ILO comparison as a family entitlement usable by one family member, rather than an individual non-transferable entitlement for each parent.[61] Its transport programmes have aimed to improve safety, frequency and accessibility so women, including low-income women, can participate more actively in the workforce.[44] These features affect the practical compatibility of paid work and household responsibilities.

The Iceland comparison cautions against treating high participation as equivalent to full gender equality in job quality or unpaid work. Participation records entry into the labour force; it does not show occupational segregation, wage gaps, leadership representation or who performs care.Judgment — For Iceland, the relevant analytical question is not whether women are attached to the labour market—they overwhelmingly are—but whether demographic ageing, care demand and occupational sorting alter the sustainability and equality of that attachment.

#### India: structural change without a sufficiently broad female employment engine

India’s experience shows how structural transformation can reduce women’s reported participation if employment opportunities contract in the sectors and locations accessible to women. In many developing economies, structural change moves workers from agriculture toward manufacturing and services, alongside urbanisation, education and fertility change.[5][33] In India, women’s agricultural opportunities declined while export-oriented manufacturing did not expand as a major female-employment engine in the way it did in Bangladesh and parts of East Asia.[31]

The labour-demand side is therefore crucial. Educated Indian women increasingly sought work, but employment in white-collar services and public-service-linked work did not grow as rapidly as the supply of educated women.[31] At the same time, the informal economy remained a dominant source of non-agricultural work for women.[26] The labour market offered a combination of limited formal jobs, insecure informal work and social constraints on mobility that discouraged participation for many women able to rely on household income.

Agriculture and unpaid family work complicate both economic reality and measurement. In South Asia, women have historically had high agricultural employment shares, and one regional comparison reports that 55% of employed women in South Asia worked in agriculture.[62] Many women also perform unpaid family work.

Across South Asia and Southeast Asia, at least one-third of working women were estimated to be contributing family workers in 2012.[5] India’s participation rate may therefore fall both because women leave low-productivity agricultural work and because their replacement activities, if home-based or unpaid, are not consistently captured.

Caste, seclusion norms, safety concerns and household bargaining shape whether women can convert education into paid employment. The local-norms evidence from Odisha and Madhya Pradesh shows that attitudes about women working outside the home vary substantially even across nearby communities.[46] The experimental evidence from West Bengal shows that flexible work can change later willingness to accept outside employment.[46] These results point to a dynamic process: work opportunities can alter norms, but existing norms determine who can take the first opportunity.

Childcare alone does not necessarily overcome these constraints. A randomised daycare-centre intervention in Rajasthan left women’s overall labour-force participation essentially unchanged, although the probability of being paid in cash increased by 2.3 percentage points.[47] The result fits the broader finding that childcare can change care arrangements without drawing large numbers of women into market work where jobs, mobility or household permission remain binding constraints.[63][48] The policy implication is developed later, but the historical lesson is clear: India’s recovery cannot be explained by schooling or childcare alone.

#### Common mechanisms and limits of common explanations

Across the four countries, education raises potential market earnings but does not guarantee participation. Education expansion was dramatic in Saudi Arabia and South Korea, high and persistent in Iceland, and substantial in India.[58][15][31] Saudi women’s earlier labour-market participation remained low despite being the majority of university graduates, while Korea’s employment gap persisted after women overtook men in tertiary education.[55][15] In India, education interacted with the availability of suitable jobs and social norms, producing non-linear participation patterns.[31]

Fertility also has different meanings across the four cases. Fewer children can free time for paid work, and research using exogenous changes in family size generally finds higher fertility reduces women’s labour-market participation.[13] But participation can also reduce fertility by raising the opportunity cost of childbearing.[23] South Korea’s very low fertility is linked to work–care conflict and does not by itself imply high female participation; India’s fertility fell while participation fell in important historical periods; and Saudi Arabia’s recent participation increase was driven mainly by behavioural and labour-demand changes rather than demographics.[60][26][32]

Childcare and supportive services can raise women’s labour supply, but their effects depend on the starting environment. A broad cross-country comparison finds higher female employment where public spending on family benefits is higher and cites evidence that childcare availability can increase women’s labour supply.[13] The empirical literature on childcare-price effects is heterogeneous: some studies find substantial participation responses, while others find no statistically significant effect.[48] The effectiveness of childcare therefore depends on job availability, informal-care alternatives, local norms, work hours and the quality and accessibility of services.[63][48]

Labour demand is the common missing variable in simple accounts that focus only on women’s preferences. Saudi Arabia’s post-2018 increase coincided with diversification, private-sector expansion and programmes that encouraged firms to hire women.[20][11] South Korea’s export-oriented industrialisation created jobs but later labour-market segmentation limited women’s progression.[5][15] Iceland’s high participation rests on a labour market and public-service structure in which women’s work is widely integrated.[31] India’s decline was associated with shrinking agricultural opportunities without sufficiently broad expansion of female-intensive manufacturing or suitable formal services.[31]

The historical evidence therefore gives a country-specific baseline for later interventions and demographic projections. Saudi Arabia has the largest recent increase from a low base and must convert entry into durable employment. South Korea has a high-education, ageing workforce but must reduce care-driven interruption and non-regular employment. Iceland has high attachment to work but must sustain it through changing care and age structures. India’s challenge is to build demand for women’s labour that is safe, formalising where possible, geographically accessible and compatible with changing household norms.

### Synthesis — historical participation and measurement

The four countries do not share one female-participation history. Saudi Arabia moved from very low participation among Saudi women to a rapid post-2018 increase; South Korea rose more gradually but retains a large motherhood and job-quality penalty; Iceland remained the high-participation case in the available comparative observations; and India declined through important parts of the 1990s–2010s before recovering after 2018.[35][11][38][1][5][45] The common 15+ ILO-modelled series permits a defensible 2025 level comparison—70.01% in Iceland, 56.78% in South Korea, 33.57% in Saudi Arabia and 32.42% in India—but not a seamless 1970–2025 history.[1] Education, fertility and growth matter in every case, yet their effects depend on labour demand, care arrangements, informality, social norms and the measurement boundary itself.[16][5][23]

### How do changes in women's labor force participation correlate with

Childcare assistance can raise the return to paid work by lowering the cost of formal care, but its effect depends on whether formal care substitutes for unpaid care, relatives, domestic workers, or care provided by mothers themselves. Across countries, women’s participation tends to rise when unpaid care is reduced, shared more equally, or made compatible with market work.[13][23]

Saudi Arabia’s sharp post-2018 participation rise coincided with Qurrah, but Qurrah alone is not the leading explanation. An impact evaluation cited by the Saudi employment diagnostic found take-up of about 15% of eligible mothers; many preferred domestic workers or nannies, while others preferred to care for children themselves.[32] In a survey of 2,000 women aged 18–40 in Dammam, Jeddah and Riyadh, 56% of working mothers used domestic workers or nannies and spent about one-fifth of income on them; among non-working mothers, wanting to focus on children was the most common reason for not job-searching, while fewer than one-fifth named affordable or high-quality childcare as a reason.[65] The Saudi diagnostic therefore concludes that childcare subsidies may not substantially increase participation when programme design conflicts with families’ revealed care preferences.[32]

South Korea’s 2012–13 childcare expansion was followed by continued increases in women’s participation, but education, cohort replacement and employment conditions also changed over the same period.[60] Women’s overall labour-force participation was 50% in both 2005 and 2012, while participation among women in their thirties moved only from 55% to 56%, despite rapidly expanding subsidised childcare.[60] From 2008 to 2017, childcare enrolment rose from 61% to 84%, but participation among women in their thirties increased only from 55% to 59%.[60] Care constraints still mattered: nearly half of mothers reporting a career break in the 2012 National Childcare Survey identified lack of reliable childcare as the primary reason.[60]

India’s limited-duration or limited-coverage childcare services cannot alone explain the national decline and subsequent rise in women’s participation.[47] In Rajasthan, randomized daycare centres left women’s overall labour-force participation essentially unchanged but increased the probability of cash-paid work by 2.3 percentage points.[47] This result fits evidence that norms around maternal work, low baseline market work, weak labour demand, and low or discriminatory wages can remain binding after a time constraint is relieved.[47]

**Childcare policy is most likely to raise participation where it both creates usable care and connects to viable paid work; it may instead replace informal care or change the type and hours of existing work.** Broad reviews find that improved childcare availability usually raises women’s labour supply, including in 21 of 22 low-income-country studies, but estimates differ materially by setting.[73][48][63]

Korea provides the strongest temporal association. Female labour-force participation rose from 48.8% in 2000 to 60.0% in 2019 while paid leave was introduced and expanded; the Korean case study characterises the leave system as helping women remain attached to work and cites a 2020 study finding a 0.9% increase in the female employment rate.[38] Among women in their thirties, the share remaining in the labour market rose from 54.1% in 2000 to 64.1% in 2019, moderating the classic M-shaped profile in which women leave employment during childbearing years and return later.[38]

Actual leave use qualifies the Korean association. In 2017 there were 22 female and 23 male leave users per 100 live births, and a 2018 survey found seven in ten working parents had never used parental leave.[60] Leave access was also unequal across firms: large-enterprise workers had more scope to take leave, whereas small and medium enterprise workers faced difficulties.[38] Korea’s leave architecture therefore improved attachment for some workers while leaving important coverage and workplace-enforcement barriers.

Iceland’s parent-specific leave, introduced in 2000–03 and extended in 2021, can redistribute care and support mothers’ employment without establishing its effect from the national participation trend alone.[44][61] Men accounted for about 3% of all parental leave before mother and father quotas in the early 2000s and approximately 45% by the 2025 OECD account.[44] Iceland is also among countries where reserved father leave has maintained strong maternal leave take-up while increasing fathers’ involvement in care.[61] This establishes a major change in caregiving behaviour, although the provided evidence does not quantify an Icelandic female-participation response.

After Icelandic fathers’ independent entitlement reached three paid months in 2003, around 90% reportedly used paternal leave; in 2007, fathers took an average of 101 days, compared with 181 days for mothers.[110]

Saudi participation rose during the wider Vision 2030 reform period, while maternity leave later rose from 10 to 12 weeks and paternity leave to three days.[3][66][36][58] The timing supports leave as one component of an improved work–family package, but the rise occurred alongside mobility reform, anti-discrimination measures, wage subsidies, sectoral opening and stronger labour demand.[20][32] India’s maternity expansion improves legal protection for covered mothers, but its direct relationship with aggregate female participation remains unsettled; a credible answer requires estimates comparing eligible and ineligible women before and after the reform.

Paid, job-protected leave can preserve the connection to a pre-birth employer, while father-specific leave can redistribute care; however, entitlement alone does not ensure use, and very long leave can weaken later labour-market attachment. Cross-country evidence finds positive participation effects of paid leave overall but negative marginal effects beyond the equivalent of 20 weeks of full-pay leave.[22][61][24]

The evidence instead attributes the post-2018 employment gains to overlapping pro-women legal reforms, changing norms, wage subsidies, a higher de facto minimum wage under Nitaqat, mobility reforms, sectoral opening and post-pandemic labour demand.[32]

Inheritance reform is not identified as a measured driver of women’s participation in any of the four countries. The more immediate legal constraints documented for Saudi Arabia concerned guardianship, workplace segregation, transport, restricted occupations and discrimination, while the Indian, Korean and Icelandic labour-market links are mediated through broader household resources and gender norms.[12][55][32]

Saudi Arabia has a direct experimental test of practical access to the driving reform. In Riyadh, women randomly offered free and immediate driver training, examination fees and transport to training were 8.6 percentage points more likely to be employed at follow-up than control-group women, a 41% increase relative to the 21.0% control employment mean.[74] They were also 10.6 percentage points less likely to be unemployed or searching for work.[74]

Employment and labour-force participation are not interchangeable in this experiment. The treatment did not produce a statistically detectable overall change in nonparticipation: the estimated effect was a 1.9-percentage-point increase, with p = 0.643.[74] Effects also differed sharply by family circumstance: never-married and widowed women recorded employment gains, whereas married or divorced women with children were 9.5 percentage points less likely to be in the labour force after gaining driving access.[75][74] The results therefore show that mobility can increase employment for women already close to the labour market while household bargaining may constrain or reverse participation responses for others.

The national contribution of licensing was necessarily limited by early take-up. Two years after the driving ban ended, only 2% of Saudi women had a licence; the study’s back-of-the-envelope calculation implies that licensing could explain 0.34 percentage points, or 4.1%, of the national female-employment increase from 2018 to 2020.[75] Even an extreme upper bound, assuming every licensed woman had been prevented from working solely by lack of a licence, implies only a 2-percentage-point employment increase, or 24% of the total rise.[75]

Mobility reform was a real employment enabler in Saudi Arabia, but it explains only part of the aggregate post-2018 increase because legal opening, wage subsidies, sectoral access, employer demand and changing social expectations moved at the same time.[32][75][74]

### What legal reforms around mobility occurred during 1970–2025 in each

Saudi Arabia is the only country in this comparison with a dated, women-specific legal mobility reform in the evidence. A decree issued in September 2017 ended the 61-year prohibition on women driving when it took effect in June 2018.[74] In 2019, women aged over 21 gained the right to obtain passports and travel abroad without male guardianship.[32] The country also introduced WUSOOL transport subsidies in 2018 and the Labour Reform Initiative in 2021, which reduced workers’ dependency on sponsors; the Job Mobility Freedom service permits a worker to change employer without the prior employer’s consent.[74][32][3]

The 2018–20 employment reforms widened women’s occupational access. They lifted restrictions on women working in mining, construction and manufacturing, removed bans on night work, strengthened employment-discrimination protection and equalised women’s retirement age with men’s at 60.[32] Saudi Arabia also equalised women’s access to all job types and eliminated the ban on women’s night work in reforms implemented during 2020.[83]

The draft establishes no dated comparable repeal of a female driving ban or guardian-permission rule for Korea, Iceland or India.[74][32] Mobility barriers in India instead commonly arise through safety, transport quality, distance and social restrictions on women’s movement; in Korea and Iceland they are more often practical work–family and transport constraints than formal sex-specific restrictions on travel.

India has evidence that public-safety intervention can increase women’s mobility. Police patrols focused on sexual harassment reduced harassment and improved women’s mobility, although the cited result does not report a labour-force-participation effect.[84] This shows that mobility policy includes safety in public space, not only formal travel rights.

**Saudi Arabia’s 2018–19 reforms directly removed legal barriers to women’s commuting and independent travel, while India illustrates that safety can remain a mobility constraint even without equivalent legal restrictions.** Mobility policy matters when it lowers the monetary, time and social cost of reaching work.[32][74][84]

### What cultural shifts relevant to women's work occurred in each

Saudi Arabia’s cultural trajectory has been uneven. The 1970s–80s oil boom expanded national income, education and some work opportunities; girls’ schools, which began in the 1950s, spread more widely during the 1970s boom.[85] Educated women from wealthy and supportive families gained access to university, overseas study, professional work and business opportunities, while poorer and less-educated women benefited much less.[85] Wealth, domestic service and repeated childbearing also reinforced domestic roles for some women.[85]

The Sahwa, or Islamic revival, strengthened conservative norms from the mid-1980s through the 1990s. It placed greater value on women’s roles as mothers and wives, widened reluctance toward mixed-gender workplaces, reinforced gender-segregated environments and intensified constraints on women’s activity and mobility.[85] Its influence began to weaken around 2000, alongside internet and satellite-media diffusion and the King Abdullah scholarship programme from 2006.[85]

Saudi public discourse shifted further after 2001 and especially from 2011, when social media expanded public debate and reforms opened new occupations.[85] Jobs such as cashier and receptionist initially faced hesitation but became socially accepted over subsequent years.[85] By 2022, a Saudi government report described women working in large numbers as an emerging social norm, although it stressed that traditional norms continued to create barriers.[12] Family authority remained substantial: 62% of surveyed women said they would be unlikely to take a job their parents disapproved of, and 67% said family strongly influenced their choice of industry.[12]

Korea has experienced a different tension: greater education, autonomy and work aspirations coexist with patriarchal norms and a highly unequal division of unpaid care.[15] In 2014, Korean women spent more than 3.5 hours daily on unpaid housework and childcare, compared with 49 minutes for men.[15] World Values Survey data for 2017–22 found that 59% of men and 47% of women agreed that men should have more right to a job when jobs are scarce.[15] Yet younger Koreans express more egalitarian views: a 2025 survey found support for women’s labour-force participation among nearly 80% of women and around 70% of men aged 25–44.[15] Korea’s #MeToo movement gained national traction in early 2018, increasing public attention to sexual harassment and gender-based violence.[15]

India’s evidence shows durable but locally variable norms. Higher husbands’ education is associated with lower female participation beyond household income, and survey evidence records persistent support for male job priority when work is scarce.[31] In Odisha, Hindu women in Adivasi-majority villages reported weaker caste-purity norms concerning female seclusion and were more likely to report paid outside-home work.[46] Field experiments show that many women accept home-based work but remain reluctant to work outside the home, even where outside work is safe, nearby and more remunerative.[47]

Iceland’s cultural shift is most visible in the normalisation of fathers’ caregiving through reserved leave. Nordic father-specific leave was designed as non-transferable, take-it-or-leave-it leave, and its wider purpose was to shift care norms as well as redistribute time.[23][61]

**Cultural change is neither uniform nor separate from policy: Saudi Arabia moved from stronger restrictions toward wider acceptance; Korea combines more egalitarian younger cohorts with persistent care inequality; India varies sharply across communities; Iceland institutionalised fathers’ care as a social norm.**[12][15][46][61]

### How do identified cultural shifts correlate with changes in women's

Saudi Arabia provides both aggregate and experimental evidence. Growing acceptance of women’s work accompanied the post-2018 participation rise, especially among women without university education, after legal reforms expanded mobility and entry into previously restricted sectors.[20][32] A 2017 Riyadh randomized experiment found that men commonly underestimated peer support for women’s employment: 72% did so, although 87% privately agreed that women should be allowed to work outside the home.[86] Providing accurate information increased wives’ reported applications for outside-home jobs from 6% to 10% within three to five months and interviews from 1% to 5%.[86]

India likewise has evidence that perceived norms can affect work behaviour. In Madhya Pradesh, correcting men’s and women’s exaggerated beliefs about community disapproval increased women’s participation-related behaviour.[46] In West Bengal, flexible home-based data-entry jobs increased take-up; after that experience, women became more willing to accept less-flexible outside-home work and reported more liberal gender attitudes.[46] These interventions show mechanisms through which norms and work experience can reinforce each other.

Korea’s participation path reflects competing cultural forces. Traditional male-provider and female-caregiver expectations, unequal unpaid work and workplace inflexibility help explain continued motherhood-related exits, including among highly educated women.[15] At the same time, rising female work aspirations, increased paternal leave use and more egalitarian younger cohorts can support continued participation.[37][38][15]

**Culture affects participation through care allocation, employer expectations, household approval and women’s own perceived feasible choices; participation can in turn make women’s paid work more socially normal.** The Saudi and Indian experiments show that changing perceived approval can alter job-search behaviour, while Iceland’s leave model shows how institutions can normalise fathers’ care.[23][86][46][61]

### Synthesis

The four countries show that women’s participation responds to packages, not isolated policies. Korea built the most extensive dated childcare and individual-leave architecture, yet care expansion initially produced only modest participation gains among women in their thirties. Saudi Arabia’s rise after 2018 coincided with childcare and transport support, legal mobility, occupational access, wage subsidies and changing acceptance; mobility experiments show real employment effects but not a uniform participation effect. Iceland’s father quotas most clearly shifted care from mothers to fathers. India shows that care provision, flexible work and norm change can alter women’s work choices locally, but that childcare alone may not raise overall participation.[60][32][74][44][47][46]

1. Treat childcare, leave, mobility, labour demand and norms as a combined work–family system, not as interchangeable interventions.
2. Measure employment, unemployment, labour-force participation and hours separately.
3. Design policies around mothers’ actual care preferences and fathers’ care responsibilities, not formal entitlement alone.

## Employment patterns by education, sector and hours

Women’s labour-force participation rate counts women who are employed or actively seeking and available for work. This section separates that entry measure from the composition of employment: education-specific participation asks which women enter the labour force; sectoral distribution asks where employed women work; and hours distinguish reduced-hours from standard-hours work. Part-time work still counts as employment, including under the ILO approach when a person worked at least one hour in the reference week.[13]

India shows a different, non-linear pattern. National Sample Survey estimates for 2011–12 put women’s participation among those with higher-secondary education at 11.9% in urban areas and 19.3% in rural areas, compared with 27.4% and 32.7% respectively for women with undergraduate degrees.[33][5] This fits the U-shaped education–participation relationship identified for India: participation is relatively high among women with little schooling or completed tertiary education and lower at intermediate education levels, partly because less-educated women may undertake subsistence or informal work while suitable higher-wage jobs matter for graduates.[33][31] India’s education expansion could therefore coexist with weak participation when many women moved into intermediate schooling without a commensurate expansion of acceptable paid work.[31]

Korean women’s educational attainment rose sharply, but high education has not eliminated non-participation. Among women aged 25–34, tertiary attainment rose from 27% in 1997 to 51% in 2005 and 77% in 2023, above the 63% recorded for men in 2023.[15] Yet 21% of tertiary-educated Korean women aged 25–34 were economically inactive in 2023, and tertiary-educated women were 7 percentage points less likely to be employed than similarly educated men at ages 25–34 and 18.3 points less likely at ages 25–64.[15] **Korea illustrates that high female education is not equivalent to strong labour-force attachment.**[15]

### How does women's employment break down by sector in each

A sectoral employment distribution is the proportion of employed women working in agriculture, industry, or services; it is not a labour-force participation rate.[13] Saudi Arabia’s modelled ILO series placed 0.56% of employed women in agriculture, 1.61% in industry, and 97.83% in services in 2017.[56]

Saudi women’s employment then diversified within and beyond services. The share working in education and health fell from over 65% in 2016 to around 37% in 2022, while women increasingly entered manufacturing, information technology, finance, transportation, and food services.[32] From the beginning of 2019 to the end of 2020, Saudi women’s public-sector employment rose 5%, while private-sector employment grew in most activities; women’s employment rose 9% in construction, 14% in manufacturing, and 40% in accommodation and food.[11] **Saudi Arabia therefore shows the clearest measured sectoral reallocation: an overwhelmingly service-based female workforce became less concentrated in education and health as private-sector opportunities widened.**[56][32][11]

Korea’s female employment remains shaped by occupational segregation. Women have gained access to professional and technical jobs, but the earlier World Bank account described a narrow range of such jobs and women’s absence from managerial positions.[37] The OECD reports that Korean women are under-represented in STEM and over-represented in health, welfare, education, and clerical occupations, where pay and career progression tend to be weaker.[15] This pattern links education choices to the types of employment available, rather than implying that higher attainment automatically produces managerial or technical careers.[15]

In South Korea’s 2020 distribution of employed women by occupation, 22.8% worked in expert and related occupations, 20.6% in office occupations and 17.6% in service occupations.[106]

India combines agricultural work, informal production, and growing service employment. A United Nations account reports that the informal economy accounted for nine in every ten women working outside agriculture in India in the figures it cites; it also identifies home-based beedi rolling, feminised small-scale production, offshore back-office work, and call centres as important forms of women’s employment.[26] Studies also identify growth in Indian IT call centres as a source of increased demand for women’s work, whereas declining agricultural opportunities and limited export-oriented manufacturing constrained women’s employment opportunities.[23][31] Iceland has no female agriculture–industry–services distribution in the material used here.

The cross-country sector comparison is consequently asymmetric: Saudi Arabia has dated three-sector shares and measured diversification, Korea has a clear occupational-sorting pattern, and India shows a mix of informality, agriculture, production, and IT-enabled services.[56][15][26][31]

### How does women's employment break down between part-time and full-time

Part-time employment is a share of employed women, not a share of all women or an LFPR. A commonly used research definition is usual work of fewer than 30 hours per week in the main job, but national definitions differ.[24][56]

Saudi Arabia is the only country in this comparison with a dated national female part-time series. In 2015, female part-time employment was 2.81% of total female employment under the indicator’s regular-employment classification; it defines part-time work as working time substantially below normal without assigning a fixed hourly threshold.[56] Separately, the 2022 survey of young employed Saudis reports weekly-hours bands for women: 42% worked 35 or more hours, 26% worked 20–34 hours, and 33% worked fewer than 20 hours. These are young employed respondents’ hours bands, not national part-time and full-time shares.[12]

Korean women are more likely than men to work part-time or only part of the year.[15] Full-time work remains the norm, while part-time jobs often carry lower pay, weaker protections, and atypical contracts; flexible-work uptake is among the OECD’s lowest because workers report concerns about income, job security, promotion, and performance evaluation.[15] Among full-time, full-year workers, Korean women earned 73% of men’s income with tertiary education, 72% with upper-secondary or post-secondary non-tertiary education, and 74% with below-upper-secondary education; the OECD reports a larger gap when all workers are included because women are more often part-time or part-year workers.[15] **Korea’s hours pattern therefore adds an earnings and career dimension to participation: entering employment does not ensure access to stable, standard-hours work.**[15]

South Korean law protects part-time employees and requires additional pay for work beyond their contractual hours; its broader non-regular employment category also includes fixed-term and temporary-agency workers.[108]

In August 2019, non-regular workers—including fixed-term, part-time and temporary-agency workers—accounted for 36.4% of South Korea’s wage workers of both sexes; this is not a female part-time employment share.[108]

India and Iceland have no dated female part-time/full-time split in the material used here. This is especially consequential for India because employment can include informal, home-based, self-employed, and contributing-family work that may not map neatly onto standard weekly-hours categories.[13][26] Participation rates alone cannot identify the volume, regularity, or security of women’s paid work, since rising participation can coexist with falling average weekly hours per woman.[13]

In Iceland’s register-based employment measure, taxable income equivalent to at least one hour of work per week at the minimum-wage level can qualify a person as employed; this employment threshold is not a part-time/full-time hours split.[114]

An undated pre-2009 Icelandic account reported average weekly working hours of 37 for women and 47 for men; these averages do not provide a female part-time/full-time split.[110]

### Synthesis

Across education, sector, and hours, Saudi Arabia’s recent change is the most visibly broad-based: participation rose fastest among women with less education, and employment diversified away from education and health into private-sector services, manufacturing, IT, transport, and food services.[32][11] India’s central pattern is instead a U-shaped education relationship embedded in informal and unevenly expanding employment opportunities, while Korea combines world-leading young women’s tertiary attainment with continued inactivity, occupational sorting, and more frequent part-time or part-year work.[33][31][26][15] Iceland cannot be numerically compared on these three breakdowns from the observations presented here.

The comparison shows why headline participation rates should be read alongside education, sector, and hours: the same participation rate can represent very different opportunities and constraints.[13][16]

## Demographic outlook

### What demographic trends are relevant to future women's labor force

Judgment — Demography changes both the number of women who could work and the care demands that can constrain their employment. The key measures are the working-age population, fertility, population ageing, household composition, and urbanisation; none mechanically determines the female labour-force participation rate.

Saudi Arabia combines a large youth cohort, continuing population growth, ageing, urbanisation and migration-shaped population composition. In 2024, Saudi women numbered 9,807,663: 33.1% were aged 0–14, 35.7% aged 15–34 and 6.2% aged 60 or over; Saudi women’s total fertility rate was 2.0.[89] Saudi Arabia’s total population was estimated at 35.3 million in mid-2024, up 4.7% on 2023, while the Saudi population grew 2.0%; non-Saudis accounted for 75.6% of the total increase and males for 70.8%.[90] More than 18 million people were under age 30 in the cited population estimate; positive natural change is projected to drive population growth in 2021–2040, while urbanisation continues as cities attract internal migrants for economic opportunities.

The population aged 65 and over is also expected to increase. This can enlarge the potential female workforce but can increase elder-care needs within households.[19][91][92]

**Historical observations, South Korea —** South Korea’s old-age dependency ratio—people aged 65 or over per 100 people aged 20–64—rose from 7.3 in 1964 to 29.3 in 2024.[15] KOSIS records a total fertility rate of 0.799 in 2025, 254,341 live births, 362,968 deaths, and 240,326 marriages; deaths exceeded births by 108,627.[93] Fertility fell below one child per woman by 2018, and a growing number of younger Koreans have postponed or forgone courtship, marriage and childbearing.[15][38]

Demographic projections, South Korea — The OECD projects the old-age dependency ratio to reach 122 in 2084, driven primarily by increasing longevity and declining fertility.[15] The OECD also projects a 49% decline in the population aged 20–64 over the next 40 years.[94] Smaller cohorts will enter working age while pension, health-care and elder-care demands rise.[15][94]

Historical observations and demographic projection, India — India’s total fertility rate fell from 4.5 births per woman in 1980–1985 to 3.3 in 1995–2000.[26] India’s relatively favourable working-age structure creates potential for a demographic dividend—a period in which the working-age share is high while dependency is relatively low—but only if working-age adults participate in paid work.[31] The UN projects India’s population to continue growing through 2054, potentially peaking later in the century or beyond 2100.[95]

Projection, Iceland — Statistics Iceland projects a total fertility rate of 1.4 children per woman aged 13–55 in 2075 and female life expectancy at birth of 89 years, up from 84 years in 2024.[96] These trends increase the importance of age composition, migration, and care arrangements for the future female labour force.[96]

Derived — South Korea’s old-age dependency ratio rose 4.01-fold from 7.3 people aged 65+ per 100 people aged 20–64 in 1964 to 29.3 in 2024; the stated projection to 122 in 2084 is a further 4.16-fold increase from 2024. This quantifies the scale of the demographic pressure underlying the case for sustaining women’s employment over the life course.[15]

Saudi Arabia’s projected ageing is substantial. A United Nations 2017-revision projection puts the population aged 65 or over at 1,806.272 thousand in 2025 and 7,516.493 thousand in 2050; the 60-and-over share rises from 5.2% in 2015 to 11.0% in 2030 and 22.9% in 2050. The same projection places total fertility at 2.29 children per woman in 2020–2025 and 1.78 in 2045–2050, and population growth at 1.43% and 0.46%, respectively.[97]

Migration makes Saudi Arabia’s potential labour supply especially sensitive to nationality and sex composition. The General Authority for Statistics reports that, in 2024, people aged 15–64 represented 89.9% of non-Saudis and 62.7% of Saudis; the total population pyramid had more men than women aged 15–64.[90] The UN ESCWA demographic profile identifies labour migration as a source of age-structure distortion.[97]

Projection, South Korea — The OECD projects Korea’s population aged 20–64 to decline by 49% over the next 40 years.[94]

Projection, Saudi Arabia, 2025–2050 — The 2017-revision projections combine growth in the 25–64 population, from 20,387.466 thousand in 2020 to 24,717.699 thousand in 2050, with much faster growth of the population aged 65 or over, from 1,303.458 thousand to 7,516.493 thousand.[97] This expands the pool of potential workers while increasing prospective elder-care needs.

### How will demographic trends affect future women's labor force participation

**Projection, Saudi Arabia, 2025–2040 —** Under fixed age-specific participation rates, the aggregate female rate changes only as the age composition changes; youth, urbanisation and population growth increase the number of Saudi women potentially available for work.[19][91][22][23] Under improved entry and retention, higher participation among young women and women continuing through care-intensive ages raises the aggregate rate above the fixed-rate scenario.

Saudi women’s employment gains in 2017–2021 were 70% attributable to the participation effect, while the demographic effect was minimal.[32] Growth in the older population increases household elder-care demands and makes retention conditional on care arrangements.[92][97]

Projection, South Korea, 2025–2040 — Low fertility and ageing will reduce the number of potential workers and increase the economic importance of retaining women through marriage, childbirth and later life. Around 70% of Koreans aged 55–79 said in a 2023 government survey that they wished to continue working, and respondents aged 55–64 preferred retirement at around age 70, compared with an official pensionable age of 63.[15] A cited conditional projection places women’s employment rate at 74.1% in 2040 if trends hold; this is a conditional employment projection rather than a female labour-force-participation projection.[38] **The central demographic pressure is toward greater reliance on women’s continued and later-life employment, though the female participation-rate response depends on job quality and care arrangements.** Older re-entrants frequently work in insecure, low-paid positions with limited social protection, so higher participation need not mean stronger employment security.[15]

Projection, India, 2025–2060 — Under fixed age-specific participation rates, India’s favourable working-age structure does not itself create a demographic dividend. A discrete-time microsimulation through 2060, incorporating population size and composition, demographic characteristics, education and secular tendencies, finds that the non-worker-to-worker ratio is very unlikely to become favourable with participation rates held constant.[4] Under improved entry and retention, higher female age-specific participation raises the aggregate female participation rate and the number of women workers relative to the fixed-rate scenario, helping realise the demographic dividend.[31][4] Lower fertility can free time for paid work, but does not itself determine the participation response.[13][73]

Fertility is an important mechanism but a poor standalone forecast rule. Fewer children can free time for paid work, and studies summarized in one review find a causal link from higher fertility to lower participation; however, worldwide fertility decline has not produced a corresponding overall increase in female participation, because effects vary with development and the jobs available to women.[13][73]

Common scenario method — For each country and scenario, the aggregate female labour-force participation rate is calculated as \(LFPR=\sum_a s_a r_a\), where \(s_a\) is female age group \(a\)’s share of the female population in the rate’s age denominator and \(r_a\) is that group’s assumed participation rate. This separates changes in the aggregate rate caused by population age composition from changes in women’s participation within age groups.[22][23] A fixed-age-specific-rate scenario holds every \(r_a\) constant at its base-year value; an improved-entry-or-retention scenario raises relevant \(r_a\) values for younger entrants, mothers returning after childbirth, or older women remaining in work.

The number of women in the labour force is separately \(\sum_a P_a r_a\), where \(P_a\) is the female population in age group \(a\): it can rise even where the aggregate rate falls, and vice versa.[22][23]

Projection, Iceland, 2035 — Under unchanged female age-specific participation rates, population ageing puts downward pressure on Iceland’s aggregate female participation rate as the working-age share declines.[96][22][23] Higher migration at young ages produces a younger age composition and increases labour-force numbers relative to lower-migration variants; lower migration produces an older age composition and fewer women in the labour force.[96] Under improved entry or retention, higher age-specific participation offsets the ageing-related pressure on the aggregate rate.[22][23] This is a qualitative conditional scenario, not a numerical forecast.[96]

### Synthesis

**The demographic outlook expands Saudi Arabia’s potential female labour supply, puts downward compositional pressure on aggregate participation in South Korea and Iceland through ageing, and leaves India’s demographic dividend conditional on higher women’s participation.** Saudi Arabia’s 2017–2021 employment gains were predominantly participation-driven rather than demographic-driven.[32] Korea’s falling cohort inflows and rising old-age dependency make women’s employment retention increasingly consequential.[15][94] Iceland’s migration direction changes its age composition and labour-force numbers.[96] India’s demographic dividend remains unrealised with unchanged participation rates.[4] Across all four countries, the fixed-age-specific-rate scenario isolates age-composition effects, while the improved-entry-or-retention scenario changes age-specific participation; fertility, care needs, labour-market institutions and available jobs shape the difference between them.[13][92][73][22][23]

1. Use age-specific female participation rates, rather than only headline rates, to separate demographic composition from changes in women’s propensity to work.
2. Model 2025–2040 care demand alongside fertility and working-age population projections, especially for Saudi Arabia and South Korea.
3. Treat India’s rising female participation as a necessary condition for capturing its demographic dividend, not as an automatic demographic outcome.

### Open questions

The precise causal effect of each childcare subsidy, leave reform, inheritance or mobility reform on women’s participation remains open in all four countries. Country-specific causal estimates would require comparable policy uptake, eligibility, local childcare supply, fertility, wages, household composition, and age-specific employment data over time. Long historical education, sector and hours series are also not fully harmonised across the four countries, particularly for informal and unpaid family work in India and for Saudi citizens versus all residents in Saudi Arabia.
