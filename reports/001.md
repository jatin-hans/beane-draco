# Staggered-Adoption DiD Estimators and Their Uptake in Applied Economics

## Executive summary

No single newer estimator is methodologically dominant across 2020–2024 labor and health articles in AER, QJE, and JPE on the evidence presented here; the defensible choice depends on the estimand and on whether the application needs cohort-time, event-time, or observation-level treatment effects.

Goodman-Bacon’s critique establishes that conventional staggered two-way fixed effects can use already-treated units as controls and thereby misstate an average effect when effects evolve after treatment, even under parallel trends.[1] Callaway–Sant’Anna estimates clean cohort-by-calendar-time effects and makes aggregation explicit, Sun–Abraham estimates and cohort-weights effects at each relative time, and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess imputes untreated potential outcomes from untreated observations before averaging treated-minus-imputed gaps.[2][3][4][5] All three permit heterogeneous effects that conventional pooled TWFE need not accommodate, but none makes anticipation, credible untreated counterfactual trends, or changing cohort composition at long event horizons disappear.[2][3][6][7][5] They correct contamination of event-study coefficients, including spurious leads, but do not by themselves solve Roth’s concern that insignificant pre-trend tests may have low power or that conditioning on passing such tests can distort inference.[7]

## Staggered adoption and alternative estimators

Staggered adoption occurs when units—such as states, firms, hospitals, or workers—start receiving a treatment in different calendar periods. It matters because the familiar two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) Difference-in-Differences regression can then combine several different comparisons into one coefficient, even though those comparisons may not estimate the same causal effect. The modern estimators compared here respond by making the comparison group and the final aggregation of effects explicit.

### What is Goodman-Bacon (2016, National Bureau of Economic Research, 'The Long-Run Effects of Childhood Insurance Coverage: Medicaid Implementation, Adult Health, and Labor Market Outcomes') [8]’s (2021) staggered-adoption critique?

Andrew Goodman-Bacon’s 2021 *Journal of Econometrics* article, 《Difference-in-Differences with Variation in Treatment Timing》, shows that the usual staggered-adoption TWFE DiD coefficient is a weighted average of all available two-group, two-period—or “2×2”—DiD comparisons.[1][9] A 2×2 DiD compares one group’s before-after outcome change with the corresponding change for another group.

In staggered settings, the component comparisons include treated-versus-never-treated comparisons, earlier-treated versus later-treated comparisons while the latter are still untreated, and later-treated versus earlier-treated comparisons after the earlier cohort has already been treated.[1][10][11]

The first two comparison types can be clean under the usual DiD logic: the control group is untreated over the comparison window. The third type is different. In a multi-period TWFE regression with varying treatment timing, units whose treatment status does not change can serve as comparisons for units whose status changes; thus, when a later-treated group begins treatment, an earlier-treated group may serve as its effective control even though the earlier group’s outcome already includes its own treatment effect.[12][1]

Goodman-Bacon’s key point is therefore not that staggered dates are intrinsically invalid. It is that staggered TWFE mechanically mixes comparisons that use untreated controls with comparisons that use already-treated controls. The latter are “forbidden comparisons” in the TWFEDD causal interpretation when the earlier cohort’s treatment effect changes with time since treatment, because already-treated units act as controls in some 2×2 DD terms and the comparison group’s outcome change is no longer an untreated counterfactual trend.[1][10][5]

The decomposition weights on the component 2×2 comparisons are non-negative and sum to one. They depend on timing-group sizes and on the variance of the treatment indicator within a comparison, with groups treated nearer the middle of the sample often receiving greater weight.[1][13][14]

The frequently invoked “negative weights” issue needs a separate statement. Goodman-Bacon’s weights on the underlying 2×2 comparisons are positive; potentially negative weights arise when the pooled regression coefficient is expressed as a weighted combination of the underlying group-period treatment effects.[1][15][16]

This distinction matters because a positive-weight average of contaminated comparisons need not be a sensible average of causal effects. If an early cohort’s treatment effect grows after adoption, the late-versus-early comparison subtracts that growth from the later cohort’s estimated effect. The aggregate TWFE coefficient can then be smaller than the relevant average effect, or even have the opposite sign.[1][17]

Goodman-Bacon formalizes this point by showing that the causal estimand identified by TWFEDD is a variance-weighted average treatment effect on the treated (VWATT). Identification requires both a zero variance-weighted average of changes in untreated potential outcomes (VWCT = 0) and no change over time in average treatment effects within each timing group (∆ATT = 0); the latter condition addresses changes in treatment effects among already-treated groups used as controls.[1]

The unilateral-divorce-law illustration makes the point concrete. Goodman-Bacon reports a TWFE estimate of −3.08, whereas the average of the post-treatment event-study estimates is approximately −4.92; later-treated versus earlier-treated comparisons were positive on average, 3.51, and accounted for the difference. Treatment-timing comparisons accounted for 37 percent of the fixed-effects estimate’s weight; comparisons between the 1973 states and non-reform or pre-1964-reform states accounted for 18 percent, and the ten highest-weight 2×2 DDs accounted for over half.[1][11]

**The central critique is that parallel trends alone does not make a pooled staggered TWFE coefficient a clean average treatment effect when already-treated units are used as controls and treatment effects evolve over exposure time.**[18][10]

The result is stronger than a warning about an inconvenient weighting scheme. Goodman-Bacon states that a causal interpretation of the conventional TWFE DiD coefficient requires both parallel trends and treatment effects that are constant over time.[1][11] Even if effects are stable over time but differ across adoption cohorts, the regression can identify a variance-weighted average that differs from the sample-weighted ATT because its weights are driven by timing and treatment variation, not necessarily by the population to which a researcher wants to generalize.[1][19]

A related literature describes the same problem in group-period terms. de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille (2020, American Economic Review, 'Two-Way Fixed Effects Estimators with Heterogeneous Treatment Effects') show that a TWFE regression can attach negative weights to group-period average treatment effects, so the coefficient can be negative even if every group-period effect is positive.[20][21] Sun and Abraham show that the problem also affects dynamic TWFE event studies: a coefficient labelled as the effect at one event time can include effects from other event times.[22][7]

There is an important qualification to the strongest version of the critique. A broader treatment-switching framework finds that heterogeneous contemporaneous effects need not always make the TWFE estimand non-convex: if treated potential outcomes, as well as untreated potential outcomes, evolve similarly across groups, some comparisons otherwise called forbidden can be valid. That result qualifies a universal claim that effect homogeneity is the only route to a positive-weight causal interpretation; it does not restore the usual staggered-adoption TWFE coefficient as a generally transparent ATT estimator.[21]

Goodman-Bacon’s decomposition of the static TWFE coefficient is diagnostic rather than corrective. It reports weights on two-group, two-period DiD comparisons, allowing researchers to assess the contribution of “forbidden” comparisons of already-treated units and how removing them would change the estimate; it does not itself remove the contaminated comparisons.[23][24][25]

The implication is practical: a single staggered TWFE coefficient is often an opaque, sample-composition-dependent summary. Its meaning changes with cohort sizes, adoption dates, and the number of periods in the panel, as well as with the treatment effects being summarized.[1][6][26]

Dynamic TWFE is a partial modification, not a general solution to the critique. It allows the effect to differ by time since adoption—for example, year two versus year three—but still assumes that the effect at a given exposure duration is the same for every adoption cohort. It therefore remains inappropriate when effects differ both by cohort and by exposure duration.[27][28]

Goodman-Bacon also documented the empirical relevance of staggered timing: 46 of 93 DiD papers published in 2014–2015 in five general-interest or field journals had variation in treatment timing.[1][3]

The 2016 NBER working paper 《The Long-Run Effects of Childhood Insurance Coverage: Medicaid Implementation, Adult Health, and Labor Market Outcomes》 is a distinct study; its published American Economic Review record is separate from the 2021 methodological article.[14][8][29]

In the unilateral-divorce illustration, the absolute gap between the TWFE estimate (−3.08) and the average post-treatment estimate (−4.92) is 1.84 suicides per million women; the TWFE magnitude is only 62.6% of the post-treatment average magnitude. This quantifies how the contaminated timing comparisons materially attenuated the pooled summary in that application.[1]

### What precisely is the two-stage aggregation approach

In this report, **two-stage aggregation** means the Callaway–Sant’Anna identify-then-aggregate framework, not Gardner’s distinct “Two-Stage Difference-in-Differences” residualization regression. The terminology is important because both procedures have stages, but they construct estimates differently and should not be conflated.[10][30][31]

Callaway and Sant’Anna’s central causal building block is the **group-time average treatment effect on the treated**, written ATT(g,t). Group \(g\) is the cohort first treated in period \(g\); \(t\) is the calendar period in which the effect is measured. In ordinary language, ATT(g,t) asks: among units that first adopted treatment at date \(g\), what was the average causal effect in date \(t\)?[2][6][32]

The first stage estimates many such cohort-by-calendar-time effects rather than forcing all treated observations to share one coefficient. For a post-treatment time \(t\), the usual comparison contrasts cohort \(g\)’s outcome change from the final clean pre-treatment period, \(g-1\), to \(t\), with the corresponding change among units that are never treated or are not yet treated at \(t\).[33][34][23]

The second stage aggregates those ATT(g,t) estimates into a summary chosen to answer the substantive question. Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) propose a guided and transparent alternative to standard TWFE specifications that explicitly separates identification of disaggregated causal parameters, aggregation into summary effects, and estimation and inference.[35][2][6]

The possible summaries include an overall treatment effect, adoption-cohort effects, calendar-time effects, and dynamic event-study effects indexed by time since adoption. The choice is not merely presentational: each aggregation places different weights on different cohort-period effects and therefore targets a different causal estimand.[2][3][36]

For an overall effect, one useful aggregation first averages the post-treatment ATT(g,t) values within each adoption cohort and then averages those cohort summaries using cohort shares. This gives an overall ATT-like quantity for the population that ever receives treatment, with each cohort’s contribution made explicit.[18][32]

For a dynamic event-study effect at event time \(e=t-g\), the procedure averages ATT(g,g+e) among cohorts observed \(e\) periods after adoption. The resulting coefficient is an average effect at a stated exposure duration, not a conventional TWFE event-study coefficient that can combine effects from other exposure durations.[3][12][32]

Callaway–Sant’Anna’s method permits outcome-regression, inverse-probability weighting (IPW), and doubly robust (DR) estimation of each group-time effect. Outcome regression models the comparison group’s counterfactual outcome evolution; IPW reweights observations using estimated treatment-group probabilities; DR combines both and remains consistent if either the outcome-evolution model or the propensity-score model is correctly specified.[3][6][36][32]

The two-stage aggregation label should not be used for Gardner’s estimator. Gardner’s (2022) first stage fits group and period fixed effects using untreated or not-yet-treated observations only. Its second stage subtracts those fitted effects from observed outcomes and regresses the adjusted outcome on treatment status in the full sample, producing an overall sample-weighted treatment effect.[31][30]

Gardner’s estimator is closely related to the Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess imputation estimator. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess state that Gardner’s (2021) two-stage estimator, Liu et al.’s (2020) counterfactual estimator, and the version of Athey et al.’s (2021) matrix-completion estimator without factors or regularization coincide with their imputation estimator for the specific class of estimands those papers consider. In particular, Gardner’s (2021) and Liu et al.’s (2020) estimators coincide with imputation for the overall ATT and the average effect at a given number of periods after treatment under the relevant cohort-and-period-fixed-effects specification.[5][44]

Callaway–Sant’Anna is therefore not a synonym for Gardner’s two-stage regression, even though both avoid the conventional TWFE bad-control problem. Callaway–Sant’Anna begins with explicitly identified cohort-time causal effects and then chooses a target aggregation; Gardner/imputation first estimates the untreated outcome structure and then averages treated-minus-counterfactual gaps.[2][31][5]

The two frameworks can coincide in special cases. Sun and Abraham state that, without covariates and with never-treated units available, their estimator coincides with Callaway–Sant’Anna’s approach. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess similarly note that a never-treated-only Callaway–Sant’Anna specification can equal efficient imputation based on the observations where parallel trends is imposed.[7][5][19]

The implication is that labels alone do not determine the estimand. A paper should state whether it is estimating cohort-time effects and aggregating them, residualizing an untreated-outcome model, or fitting a saturated cohort-by-event-time regression—and should report the weights and control group that turn those calculations into a particular causal quantity.[3][31][4]

Callaway and Sant’Anna describe the framework at two distinct levels. Conceptually, it has three steps: identify disaggregated causal parameters, aggregate them into summary measures, and estimate those target parameters and conduct inference. Computationally, their group-time estimator has two steps: estimate the cohort-period nuisance functions—such as propensity-score and outcome-regression functions—and plug those fitted values into the sample ATT(g,t) formula. Thus, this report’s two-stage aggregation label refers to the identify-then-aggregate structure, not merely to the nuisance-function plug-in implementation.[28]

The Callaway–Sant’Anna framework is designed for an absorbing treatment and limited anticipation. For a short-lived treatment, it can be used by restricting the sample through the end of treatment for the first-treated cohort, so treatment is effectively irreversible in the analysis window, or when treatment effects persist after treatment ends and the cohort consequently remains as-if treated.[45]

Callaway and Sant’Anna define ATT(g,t) as the average treatment effect in period \(t\) for units first treated in period \(g\), and characterize their summary parameter as a researcher-specified weighted sum of these effects, \(\theta=\sum_g\sum_t w(g,t)ATT(g,t)\), chosen to answer a “well-posed empirical/policy question.”[46]

There is also an inner two-step procedure within estimation of each ATT(g,t). Callaway and Sant’Anna first estimate the relevant nuisance functions—propensity-score and/or comparison-group outcome-evolution functions for each group and period—and then plug the fitted values into the ATT(g,t) sample analogue; the resulting ATT estimates are subsequently combined into summary treatment-effect parameters. This nuisance-estimation sequence is distinct from the report’s use of two-stage aggregation to mean identify cohort-time effects and then aggregate them.[46]

Callaway and Sant’Anna’s identify-then-aggregate framework applies to repeated cross-section data as well as panel data: their repeated-cross-section identification results imply an analogous two-step ATT(g,t) estimation procedure, followed by aggregation into causal-effect summaries.[46]

### How does the two-stage aggregation approach handle heterogeneous treatment effects

Callaway–Sant’Anna handles heterogeneity by treating the cohort-time effect ATT(g,t), rather than one pooled coefficient, as the primitive causal object. That permits effects to differ across adoption cohorts, calendar periods, observed covariates, and time since adoption.[2][3]

For a focal cohort \(g\) and period \(t\), the estimator uses a comparison group that remains untreated through the focal period. This can be the never-treated group or the set of not-yet-treated groups. It therefore excludes the key bad comparison in staggered TWFE: using an already-treated early cohort as the control for a newly treated late cohort.[10][47][48]

A group-time effect is more informative than a pooled post-treatment coefficient because it keeps two sources of heterogeneity separate. First, treatment effects may differ because cohorts adopt under different institutional or economic conditions. Second, effects may evolve because units have been exposed to treatment for different lengths of time.[10][3]

The aggregation stage then determines the policy question being answered.

- **Overall aggregation:** The analyst can average all available ATT(g,t) values, use treated-observation weights, or first form cohort-level post-treatment averages and then weight cohorts by their shares. These are different summaries because early cohorts commonly contribute more post-treatment periods. [18][26][49]

- **Cohort aggregation:** The analyst can average a cohort’s ATT(g,t) values over its observed post-treatment periods. This makes variation by adoption date visible rather than burying it in a pooled average. [12][32]

- **Calendar-time aggregation:** The analyst can average effects in a common calendar period. This is useful when the causal question concerns how the effect differs across macroeconomic, policy, or epidemiological environments. [2][37][50]

- **Dynamic or event-time aggregation:** The analyst can average ATT(g,g+e) across eligible cohorts at common exposure duration \(e\). This produces a sequence of effects at, for example, impact, one period after treatment, and two periods after treatment. [3][12][49]

The dynamic aggregation answers a different question from a static overall estimate. The latter asks for an average effect across selected treated cohort-periods. The former asks how effects vary with elapsed treatment exposure. The Callaway–Sant’Anna minimum-wage application illustrates the distinction: estimated group-time effects range from 0.9 percent lower teen employment for states first treated in 2006, measured in 2006, to 7.1 percent lower teen employment for states first treated in 2004, measured in 2007; three of seven group-time estimates are statistically significant, the average effect is a 3.1 percent reduction, and the estimated magnitude tends to increase with exposure length.[3]

That example identifies a central interpretive issue: the composition of contributing cohorts can change with event time. Later event-time estimates are often based only on earlier adopters, because later adopters have not yet accumulated enough post-treatment periods. An apparent dynamic pattern can therefore reflect both changing effects within a cohort and changing composition across cohorts.[3][6][49]

Callaway and Sant’Anna provide a **balanced event-study aggregation** to address this composition issue. The researcher selects an event-time window and retains only cohorts observed over the entire required window, so that the same cohort composition contributes at each event time; included cohorts are then weighted by their sample sizes.[10][3][6][24]

Balancing changes the target population. It improves comparability across event times by focusing on cohorts with enough observed exposure, but it excludes late-adopting or short-follow-up cohorts. This is a substantive trade-off, not a technical nuisance: the balanced event-study profile pertains to the selected cohorts rather than automatically to all treated units.[3][6][37][49]

The package implementation makes the choice operational. Its `balance_e` option restricts dynamic aggregation to cohorts observed for at least a selected exposure duration and the corresponding event-time range; in the illustrative minimum-wage example, `balance_e = 1` retains the 2004 and 2006 treated groups, drops the 2007 group, and reports instantaneous effects and effects one period after treatment.[49][36][37][51]

For example, `balance_e=2` retains only cohorts observed at event times 0, 1, and 2.[132]

**Callaway–Sant’Anna fixes the Goodman-Bacon bad-control problem by estimating each cohort-time effect against units untreated at the relevant time, then aggregating those clean effects rather than asking one TWFE coefficient to summarize them implicitly.**[10][33][19]

This does not mean that every aggregate event-study curve automatically describes a common causal dynamic. If one compares unbalanced event-time averages across horizons, a pure dynamic interpretation requires either balanced cohort composition or a cross-cohort homogeneity condition that the effect at each exposure duration does not vary with adoption cohort. Callaway and Sant’Anna explicitly describe that homogeneity condition as potentially too strong.[3][6]

The method’s flexibility therefore has a corresponding reporting obligation. Researchers should report the group-time estimand or the explicit aggregation weights, identify the control group, state the event-time window, and explain whether dynamic estimates are balanced across cohorts.[3][37][49]

The conclusion is that the approach allows arbitrary cohort and time heterogeneity at the group-time level, but the researcher—not the regression software—must define the aggregate effect that serves the substantive question.[2][33][52]

The framework identifies these heterogeneous group-time effects under **conditional parallel trends**: after conditioning on observed covariates, the selected untreated comparison group supplies the treated cohort’s untreated outcome path. This assumption permits covariate-specific outcome trends and does not restrict the relationship between treatment timing and potential outcomes. The framework offers outcome-regression, inverse-probability-weighted, and doubly robust estimands for this purpose.[28][53]

The choice of pre-treatment base period affects the diagnostic pre-treatment estimates but not the post-treatment ATT(g,t) estimates. With a varying base period, each pre-treatment pseudo-ATT compares the change from t−1 to t for the focal cohort and its comparison group; with a universal base period, each pre-treatment comparison is against the fixed period immediately before treatment, adjusted for anticipation.[54][55]

For an unbalanced panel, each ATT(g,t) requires the same units to be observed both at t and at the comparison period g−1. Dropping units not observed in all required periods can bias estimates when attrition is related to treatment or outcomes.[45]

Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) also allow **conditional parallel trends**: groups with the same values of time-invariant covariates must have the same evolution in their untreated outcomes. This replaces an unconditional requirement that every group have identical untreated-outcome evolution.[56][57]

The available control group determines the longest estimable horizon. When not-yet-treated groups are controls and every group is eventually treated, ATT(g,t) can be estimated only through the last period in which at least one group remains untreated.[57][58]

When not-yet-treated units are the comparison group and every cohort eventually adopts, the last-treated cohort has no later untreated cohort against which to identify its treatment effect.[28][47][6][46]

The simple Callaway–Sant’Anna estimand weights treated cohort-period ATT(g,t) cells in proportion to cohort size \(N_g\); the cohort estimand averages within-cohort effects and then weights those cohort averages by \(N_g\); and the calendar estimand weights treated units by \(N_g\) within each calendar period.[59]

Callaway and Sant’Anna’s pre-tests based on group-time effects, including effects aggregated into an event-study plot, remain valid in the presence of selective treatment timing.[60]

### How do Sun and Abraham’s interaction-weighted estimators handle heterogeneous treatment

Sun and Abraham’s interaction-weighted (IW) estimator is designed for the dynamic event-study setting. Its building block is the **cohort average treatment effect on the treated**, CATT(e,ℓ): the average treatment effect for the cohort first treated in calendar period \(e\), measured ℓ periods relative to that cohort’s treatment date.[7][38]

CATT(e,ℓ) is the same causal object as Callaway–Sant’Anna’s group-time ATT(g,t), expressed in event time rather than calendar time. The difference is notation and estimation architecture: CATT indexes a cohort and time since adoption, whereas ATT(g,t) indexes a cohort and calendar period.[22][7]

Sun and Abraham estimate cohort-by-relative-time effects in a saturated regression containing interactions between adoption-cohort indicators and event-time indicators. This replaces the conventional event-study regression, which includes only a common lead or lag dummy and therefore imposes a structure that can be contaminated by effects from other relative times.[18][4][23]

The estimator proceeds in three steps:

1. It estimates the full set of cohort-specific relative-time effects, CATT(e,ℓ), using cohort-by-event-time interactions in a regression. [18][38]

2. It calculates each eligible cohort’s sample share at relative time ℓ. [38][4]

3. It averages the estimated CATT(e,ℓ) values at that same relative time using those cohort shares. [22][4]

For every event time ℓ, the IW coefficient is therefore a non-negative weighted average of cohort-specific treatment effects at ℓ, with weights summing to one. This is the key contrast with conventional dynamic TWFE event-study coefficients, which may combine effects at the nominal event time with effects from other event times and may fall outside the convex hull of the underlying effects.[22]

Sun and Abraham’s estimator permits treatment effects to differ across adoption cohorts and over event time. It does not require that the impact effect, one-period effect, or long-run effect be the same for all cohorts in order to obtain an interpretable cohort-share-weighted event-time average.[22][18][4]

The conventional dynamic TWFE event study fails precisely on this point. Sun and Abraham show that a coefficient for one relative period can be contaminated by cohort-specific effects from other relative periods; a pre-treatment coefficient can consequently be nonzero even when parallel trends and no anticipation both hold.[7]

This is not simply a cosmetic issue in plotting leads and lags. In a conventional event study, a nonzero “lead” can reflect heterogeneous post-treatment effects elsewhere in the panel, while a zero lead can coexist with an actual pre-trend. Such coefficients cannot reliably be read as either dynamic treatment effects or pre-trend tests without strong additional assumptions.[7][23]

The IW estimator removes this cross-relative-time contamination by estimating cohort-specific effects first and aggregating only effects at the same target event time. The post-treatment coefficient at ℓ is therefore interpretable as the specified cohort-share-weighted average treatment effect ℓ periods after adoption.[22][38]

Sun and Abraham’s dynamic estimates still have an event-time-composition feature. At longer horizons, only cohorts treated sufficiently early can contribute. Thus a movement in the IW event-study curve can arise from genuine within-cohort treatment dynamics, differences across cohorts, or both. The estimator solves the forbidden-comparison and non-convex-weighting problem; it does not make composition changes disappear.[7][61][4]

The control-group choice differs from Callaway–Sant’Anna in some designs. In the described Sun–Abraham implementation, controls are never-treated units or the last-treated cohort, and always-treated units are dropped.[18] If no never-treated group exists, Sun and Abraham use the last-treated cohort as the control, whereas Callaway–Sant’Anna can use the set of not-yet-treated cohorts.[7][62]

Those choices imply different parallel-trends restrictions. Sun and Abraham explicitly characterize the two sets of assumptions as different and non-nested in the no-never-treated setting. Neither method can therefore be declared universally weaker solely from the estimator label; the plausibility of the relevant control group is an empirical-design question.[7][62]

The methods coincide in an important special case. Sun and Abraham state that, without covariates and with never-treated units available, their method coincides with Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020a); Sun and Abraham also note that Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020a) propose other aggregations of cohort-specific effects.[7][62] They differ more materially when covariates or alternative comparison groups matter, because Callaway–Sant’Anna accommodates conditional parallel trends and time-varying covariate conditioning through its broader procedures.[22][18][62]

**The IW estimator’s decisive contribution is to make each event-study coefficient a convex average of cohort-specific effects at the stated event time, rather than an implicitly contaminated coefficient from a pooled dynamic TWFE regression.**[22][4]

The implication is that IW is especially natural when the central estimand is a dynamic event-study profile. It is less naturally framed as a single universal ATT estimator because its primary aggregation is by relative time, not first by cohort and then across all post-treatment periods.[18][4][63]

The relationship between IW and Callaway–Sant’Anna is especially close when a never-treated cohort exists: Sun and Abraham’s estimator is equivalent to the Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator in that setting. When there is no never-treated cohort, Sun–Abraham instead uses the last-treated cohort as the comparison group, while Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) consider an estimator that replaces the never-treated group with an average of cohorts not yet treated in period t.[64][57]

In the formal comparison of the two methods, Sun–Abraham’s cohort-specific interaction estimate is consistent for the corresponding ATT(g,t) under its all-groups parallel-trends assumption, and its second-stage cohort weighting targets the event-study effect at the selected exposure duration.[40]

With never-treated units as controls, Sun and Abraham’s cohort-period estimators are identical to Callaway–Sant’Anna’s estimators using that same control group. Sun and Abraham also show that these estimates can be computed by linear regression.[57]

Sun and Abraham do not propose the Callaway–Sant’Anna options of not-yet-treated controls or conditional parallel trends.

When every cohort eventually adopts, the last-treated cohort can serve as a comparison group only before its own treatment date. This restricts the cohort-relative-time effects that can be identified without a never-treated group.

In the 500-replication simulation reported in *An Anatomy of Event Studies*, parallel trends and no anticipation hold by construction, and the five treatment-effect paths are constant, linear growth, exponential decay, lagged kick-in, and hump-shaped. Under cohort-heterogeneous effects, IW remains centered near the target in all five designs, whereas dynamic TWFE is biased in all five; the reported TWFE bias ranges from 0.017 to 0.070 and is about 70% of the target effect in the lagged-kick-in design.[65]

A simulation in which parallel trends and no anticipation hold by construction finds that, under cohort-heterogeneous treatment effects, IW remains centered near its target in all five effect-path designs while dynamic TWFE is biased in all five. For the aggregate lag-two target, IW likewise remains centered near the aggregate target under cohort heterogeneity, whereas dynamic TWFE does not.[65]

Sun and Abraham’s estimator, like Callaway and Sant’Anna’s framework, assumes staggered adoption of an irreversible treatment.[18]

### How do Borusyak et al.’s imputation-based methods handle heterogeneous treatment

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s imputation approach starts from the untreated potential outcome, often written Y(0): the outcome a treated observation would have had without treatment. Instead of comparing a newly treated cohort with a selected control cohort in one 2×2 contrast, the procedure models untreated outcomes using observations that are untreated.[5]

In the baseline two-way-fixed-effects implementation, the first step estimates unit and period fixed effects from untreated observations only. The second step uses those estimates to predict each treated observation’s untreated counterfactual outcome. The third step computes the treated observation’s estimated effect as its observed outcome minus that imputed counterfactual and averages those effects with weights corresponding to the selected causal target.[5]

This sequence is:

```mermaid

flowchart LR
    A[Untreated observations] --> B[Estimate untreated-outcome model]
    B --> C[Impute Y(0) for each treated observation]
    C --> D[Observed outcome minus imputed Y(0)]
    D --> E[Aggregate treated-observation effects with target weights]

```

The method permits unrestricted treatment-effect heterogeneity in the target effects. It does not require one common treatment effect across cohorts, calendar periods, units, or exposure durations; it can estimate a weighted sum of treated-observation effects, with weights chosen to represent an overall ATT, a subgroup effect, or an event-time effect.[5][42]

For an event study at horizon \(h\), the target can be the average treatment effect among observations exactly \(h\) periods after their own treatment dates. Thus staggered timing is handled by aligning treated observations relative to treatment and then averaging the observation-level effects at the desired horizon.[5]

This differs conceptually from Callaway–Sant’Anna and Sun–Abraham. Those approaches begin with cohort-period effects identified through explicit DiD comparisons against selected untreated reference groups. Imputation instead uses all untreated information permitted by its untreated-outcome model to predict Y(0) for treated observations and then aggregates the resulting individual observation gaps.[5][66]

The use of all untreated observations can improve precision. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess argue that their imputation estimator uses the full set of untreated observations, whereas compared cohort-based estimators often rely on the single period immediately before a focal cohort’s treatment for that cohort-time effect.[5]

Their simulation evidence reports that imputation is the most efficient of the compared robust estimators in the stated baseline design at every horizon. Under homoskedasticity, reported standard deviations for de Chaisemartin–D’Haultfœuille were 35–39% above imputation’s, while Sun–Abraham standard deviations were 39–290% above imputation’s; the paper reports broadly similar relative efficiency under several alternative error processes, with one stated exception.[42]

Under the simulation’s alternative heteroskedastic, AR(1), and wild-cluster-bootstrap error specifications, imputation remains the most efficient of the three robust estimators in the reported comparisons, except that dCDH has a 2 percent lower standard deviation at h = 0 under AR(1) errors. In the paper’s tax-rebate application, the authors also stress a separate identification issue: effects more than 12 weeks after rebate receipt cannot be estimated through DiD without restrictive treatment-effect heterogeneity assumptions because treated and untreated households are observed together for at most 13 weeks.[42][5]

This illustrates a valuable feature of the imputation approach: it avoids extrapolating to event-time horizons without an explicit comparison basis. A regression can always return a coefficient, but a coefficient at a horizon where untreated potential outcomes cannot be recovered under the maintained model is not thereby identified.[5][67][68]

The method’s heterogeneity robustness should not be confused with freedom from assumptions about untreated outcomes. In the baseline version, the unit-and-period fixed-effects model fitted on untreated observations is the model used to construct counterfactual Y(0). The estimator avoids imposing homogeneity on treatment effects, but it still depends on the specified untreated-outcome structure and the associated parallel-trends logic.[5]

The relation to Gardner’s two-stage DiD is especially close. Gardner’s method fits group and period effects on untreated observations, subtracts those estimates, and regresses adjusted outcomes on treatment. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess state that Gardner’s estimator and their imputation estimator coincide for the overall ATT and average effects at a given post-treatment duration under the relevant fixed-effect setup.[31][5]

The imputation approach can also be related to Callaway–Sant’Anna. With never-treated units only, and with a specified imputation model that uses the relevant never-treated observations and base-period construction, the Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator can be written as observed outcomes minus imputed untreated outcomes and can be numerically equivalent to an efficient imputation estimator.[5][19]

Anticipation and dynamics remain important. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess show that if anticipation effects are not ruled out in a fully dynamic specification and there are no never-treated units, the combined anticipation-and-treatment path is not point identified. They therefore distinguish testing assumptions about pre-trends from estimating dynamic treatment effects under those assumptions.[5]

The software implementation reflects the same distinction. Its `shift` option treats treatment as occurring a specified number of periods earlier when anticipation is expected, and explicitly says that the option should not be used for pre-trend testing.[68]

**The imputation approach solves the staggered-TWFE heterogeneity problem by predicting untreated outcomes from untreated data and averaging treated-minus-imputed gaps, rather than by using treated cohorts as controls for one another.**[5]

Its practical implication is that researchers can define flexible overall, subgroup, and event-time targets without assuming common effects, but they must report the untreated-outcome model, the treated observations retained at each horizon, and the target weights that define the reported average.[5][42][68]

In the stated homoskedastic simulation, Sun–Abraham’s standard deviation was 1.39 to 3.90 times the imputation estimator’s standard deviation across horizons; this is the ratio form of the reported 39%–290% excess.[42]

The imputation framework is part of the same heterogeneous-effects class as Callaway–Sant’Anna and Sun–Abraham: it permits effects to vary across cohorts and over time, but constructs them by using the average of never-treated and not-yet-treated observations to recover untreated outcomes rather than by estimating a separate cohort-by-relative-time interaction regression.[27][45]

The untreated sample used to fit the baseline counterfactual model can contain both never-treated units and not-yet-treated units in their pre-treatment periods. The treated-observation residuals can then be averaged across all treated observations or within a selected relative-time window to form the reported ATT.[69]

An equivalent saturated representation estimates group fixed effects, time fixed effects, and a fixed effect for every treated group-time cell. Under parallel trends, the coefficient for treated cell (g,t) equals that cell’s average treatment effect; cohort-relative-time effects are then averages of the relevant cell effects. Borusyak et al.’s linear estimators of these cell effects have the lowest variance.[57][70]

The imputation estimator requires never-treated units or sufficiently many not-yet-treated observations to fit the untreated-outcome model.[71]

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess propose a parallel pre-trends test that estimates fixed effects and covariate coefficients on untreated observations, computes treated observations’ actual-minus-imputed outcomes, and constructs a weighted average of those gaps.[72]

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess test parallel trends and no anticipation with OLS regressions estimated only on untreated observations.[5] This separation prevents treatment-effect heterogeneity from contaminating the diagnostic test and avoids the power loss associated with testing assumptions in specifications that include treated outcomes.[5]

The imputation procedure estimates its untreated-potential-outcome model from untreated observations, including never-treated observations and pre-treatment observations from eventually treated units; it then imputes untreated outcomes for treated observations and averages observed-minus-imputed outcomes. This construction exploits the connection between parallel trends and the model for untreated potential outcomes.[19]

Relative to Callaway–Sant’Anna’s last-pre-treatment-period baseline, imputation uses average outcomes across all pre-treatment periods as its baseline and requires parallel trends in all time periods.[34]

Imputation is meaningful only for treated observations whose unit and calendar-time fixed effects can be learned from untreated data: treated units require untreated observations before adoption, and every calendar period containing treated observations requires untreated observations.[73]

The imputation result is efficient and unbiased under parallel trends and no anticipation when errors are homoskedastic.[71]

Imputation can permit heterogeneous treatment effects while its specified untreated-outcome model restricts how covariates relate to untreated outcomes over time. In the stated two-period condition, the conditional mean change in untreated outcomes must be linear in the change in covariates; equivalently, the effects of time-varying and time-invariant covariates on untreated potential outcomes are constant across time periods.[74]

Its precision advantage is conditional: using a longer pre-treatment history can improve precision, but departures from parallel trends can increase bias, and serial correlation removes the guarantee that imputation is more efficient than Callaway–Sant’Anna or Sun–Abraham estimators.[57]

### The spread of staggered treatment adoption in applied Difference-in-Differences work

The methodological debate has become important because DiD itself has diffused widely in applied economics and related policy research. In a published-journal sample, the share of applied-micro papers mentioning DiD rose from 27% in 2011–2017 to 41% in 2018–2024.[75]

Staggered timing is common among DiD applications because policies and organizational changes often roll out across places or groups at different dates. In top-five finance and accounting journals from 2000 to 2019, one study identified 744 DiD papers; 407, or 55%, used staggered DiD, and 394 of those 407, or 97%, were published after 2010.[18]

Health-policy research shows a similar expansion. A review of medical DiD studies in three *JAMA Network* journals published from January 2022 through November 2024 found 42 studies, of which 20, or 48%, had staggered treatment designs. Nine of the 20 staggered studies, or 45%, still used static TWFE as their main estimator; ten used an estimator accounting for staggered timing.[76]

That review found Callaway–Sant’Anna and stacked DiD each in three of the ten staggered-timing-adjusted applications, while Gardner two-stage DiD appeared in one. These counts concern the review’s medical-journal sample rather than the AER, QJE, and JPE denominator considered in the following section, but they show that the methodological response has reached applied work.[43][76]

The growth of broad DiD use does not imply that all applied work has adopted heterogeneity-robust estimators. The same health-policy review documents continuing use of static TWFE under staggered timing, precisely the setting in which the Goodman-Bacon critique is relevant.[43][76]

The core methodological tension is therefore not theoretical only. Applied researchers commonly face designs in which cohorts adopt at different dates, treatment effects may grow, fade, or differ by cohort, and a single static TWFE coefficient can combine clean and contaminated comparisons.[10][24][26]

The three approaches examined here share one broad response to bad comparisons in TWFE DiD regressions: they avoid comparing newly treated units with units treated earlier and make the aggregation of heterogeneous effects more transparent. They differ in their effective comparison observations, covariate treatment, and whether they begin with cohort-time DiD contrasts, saturated cohort-event-time regression coefficients, or treated-observation counterfactual imputations.[18][19][5]

This difference determines what a reported estimate means. Callaway–Sant’Anna is most naturally described as a menu of explicit cohort-time aggregations; Sun–Abraham is an interaction-weighted solution for dynamic event studies; Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess is a counterfactual-imputation framework that can target arbitrary weighted averages of treated-observation effects.[2][38][5]

The following section, **Parallel trends, treatment-effect homogeneity, and anticipation**, compares the identification assumptions that make each of these estimators causal rather than merely computationally well-defined.

The increase from 27% in 2011–2017 to 41% in 2018–2024 is 14 percentage points, or a 51.9% relative increase in the applied-micro share of published-journal papers mentioning DiD. This quantifies a substantial expansion in the method’s visibility over the two periods.[75]

Goodman-Bacon reported that 46 of 93 DiD papers published in 2014–2015 in five general-interest or field journals had variation in treatment timing. This earlier count establishes that staggered timing was already a common applied design before the 2021 methodological articles formalized the current critique and estimator families.[1][77]

An earlier measure likewise found staggered timing in 46 of 93 DiD papers published in 2014–2015 in five general-interest or field journals.[1][3]

This earlier tally directly documents that staggered adoption was already common in applied DiD before the 2020–2024 publication window examined elsewhere in this report.[1][3]

The practical distinction between the newer estimators is visible when all units eventually adopt. Callaway–Sant’Anna comparisons stop once no unit remains untreated, whereas the imputation framework estimates the untreated-outcome structure from untreated observations and then aggregates treated-observation gaps using the target weights.[58][5]

No adoption counts or rates for Callaway–Sant’Anna aggregation, Sun–Abraham interaction weighting, or Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess imputation are established for labor or health economics applications in *AER*, *QJE*, and *JPE* published in 2020–2024. No eligible using-paper has a verified classification of whether its authors justified estimator choice with Monte Carlo simulations, sensitivity analyses, both, or neither. Keyword mentions cannot supply these numerators or the eligible-paper denominator because they measure mentions rather than verified method use.[78][79]

Accordingly, methodological dominance among the three approaches in the specified journals, fields, and publication years cannot be determined. The verdict would change only with a verified census that defines the eligible staggered-DiD paper denominator separately for each journal-field cell, records every paper’s use of each named estimator allowing multiple use, and classifies each using paper’s estimator-choice justification.[78][79]

### Synthesis —

Goodman-Bacon’s critique identifies the central failure of conventional staggered TWFE: it mixes valid untreated-control comparisons with later-versus-already-treated comparisons, which become contaminated when effects change after treatment. Callaway–Sant’Anna addresses that by estimating cohort-time effects with untreated controls and explicitly aggregating them; Sun–Abraham estimates cohort-by-event-time effects and forms convex cohort-share-weighted event-time averages; Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess predicts untreated outcomes from untreated data and then aggregates treated-minus-imputed gaps. All three permit heterogeneous treatment effects, but they differ in their primitive estimand, control construction, and dynamic aggregation. The choice should follow the desired causal summary—overall ATT, cohort effects, event-time profile, or weighted subgroup effect—rather than software convenience.[1][10][2][38][5]

## Parallel trends, treatment-effect homogeneity, and anticipation

Parallel trends, treatment-effect homogeneity, and anticipation are distinct assumptions. Parallel trends concerns the untreated outcome path: how outcomes would have changed without treatment. Treatment-effect homogeneity concerns the causal effect itself: whether it must be the same for every cohort and exposure duration. Anticipation concerns whether a future treatment already changes behaviour before its recorded start date. The newer staggered-adoption estimators relax the second requirement, but none makes the first or third disappear.

The central distinction is therefore between a method that prevents already-treated observations from contaminating the comparison and a design that supplies a credible untreated counterfactual. All three approaches do the former. They differ in how much structure they place on the latter.

### What assumptions does the two-stage aggregation approach make concerning parallel

Here, the two-stage aggregation approach means Callaway and Sant’Anna’s strategy of estimating a group-time average treatment effect, ATT(g,t), for each adoption cohort and period using a never-treated or not-yet-treated comparison group and the period before adoption as the baseline, and then aggregating those effects into a chosen summary.[15] Callaway and Sant’Anna describe this as a two-step strategy that first estimates group- and time-specific nuisance functions and then plugs the fitted values into the sample analogue of the ATT estimand.[3][6] Gardner’s two-stage regression-based procedure is a distinct approach designed to be robust to treatment-effect heterogeneity when treatment timing varies.[80] Callaway and Sant’Anna instead identify group-time average treatment effects, ATT(g,t), for each cohort first treated in period g and each calendar period t, then aggregate those effects into an overall, cohort, or event-time estimand selected by the researcher.[2][3][33]

For every ATT(g,t), Callaway and Sant’Anna require conditional parallel trends: after conditioning on pre-treatment covariates X, cohort g’s change in untreated potential outcomes must match the change for the selected clean comparison group.[6][3] The researcher chooses either a fixed never-treated group or units not yet treated by t + δ.[6][3] The never-treated version requires a sufficiently large and comparable never-treated group; the not-yet-treated version uses more comparison observations but, when δ = 0, also restricts observed pre-treatment trends across groups.[6][3]

The control-group choice determines the relevant counterfactual claim. Never-treated units remain the same comparison group for every cohort and date. Not-yet-treated controls change over time and are eligible only while they are untreated through t + δ.[3][6][82] Thus, later-treated units supply additional clean comparisons only if their own untreated-outcome trends satisfy the selected conditional parallel-trends assumption and they have not entered their anticipation window.[6][82]

Let a group-time effect mean the average causal effect for cohort g in calendar period t. Gardner allows those effects to differ across cohorts and periods. The second-stage coefficient for a static specification then estimates the overall average treatment effect on the treated, with each treated group-period cell weighted by its share of post-treatment observations.[30][31] Thus, earlier cohorts need not have the same effects as later cohorts, and effects may grow, fade, or reverse over exposure duration.

For dynamic effects, the second stage replaces a single treatment indicator with leads and lags relative to treatment. A lag coefficient identifies the average effect for units treated for that many periods, averaging across units observed at that exposure duration. A lead coefficient is an average deviation from the fitted untreated counterfactual among units approaching treatment; under the maintained model, it should be zero.[31] The set of cohorts contributing to the estimate can vary with exposure duration, because later event times are unavailable for cohorts treated near the end of the sample. This is an aggregation fact, not an assumption that dynamics are common across cohorts.[30]

The baseline is no anticipation, δ = 0: future treatment does not affect a cohort’s outcome before its treatment date.[6][12] Callaway and Sant’Anna instead permit limited anticipation when the researcher specifies a known horizon δ. The reference period becomes g − δ − 1, the latest period in which untreated potential outcomes are observed for cohort g.[6][3]

The limited-anticipation rule also changes control eligibility. A later-treated unit leaves the not-yet-treated control group once it enters its own anticipation window.[82] A larger δ moves the baseline farther back and strengthens the parallel-trends assumptions; each anticipated period removes a usable pre-treatment period, and early cohorts can lack a clean base period.[6][3][82]

**Callaway–Sant’Anna two-by-two aggregation solves staggered-adoption contamination by estimating each cohort-time effect only against never-treated or not-yet-treated clean controls before aggregating.** It permits arbitrary heterogeneous and dynamic effects, while retaining the conditional parallel-trends assumption for the selected controls and either no anticipation or a specified anticipation horizon.[2][3][6][33][23]

Gardner’s event-study formulation defines a cutoff R* such that parallel trends is required only when relative time is earlier than −R*. The usual R* = 0 requires parallel trends throughout the pre-treatment period; R* > 0 allows parallel trends to fail in a specified near-treatment window and thereby relaxes the assumption of unanticipated treatment.[81]

Gardner’s first stage can include time-varying covariates when parallel trends holds conditional on them, or interact time-invariant covariates with time indicators when covariate-specific trends are required.[81] When trend differences increase over time, estimating the first stage using untreated observations near treatment or including group-specific linear trends reduces potential bias.[44]

In Callaway and Sant’Anna’s group-time framework, both the never-treated and not-yet-treated parallel-trends assumptions can hold conditional on covariates, permit covariate-specific trends, and place no restriction on the relationship between treatment timing and the potential outcomes Yt(g).[46] This does not make homogeneous treatment effects necessary: it is a conditional specification of untreated trends.

Callaway and Sant’Anna’s covariate-adjusted population-regression coefficient equals ATT(g,t) only if treatment effects are homogeneous conditional on X and covariate-specific trends are ruled out; their ATT(g,t) identification characterizations do not require either restriction.[46]

With anticipation allowance δ, Callaway and Sant’Anna identify ATT(g,t) only for cohorts in Gδ, which can exclude early-treated cohorts, and only through calendar period T − δ. When δ = 0, Gδ = G.[46]

Each ATT(g,t) additionally requires an eligible untreated comparison group in calendar period t. If no never-treated or sufficiently later-treated units remain available for a cohort-time cell, that cell is not identified and cannot validly enter an aggregate.

Here, the two-stage aggregation approach means Callaway and Sant’Anna’s procedure, not John Gardner’s separate two-stage regression procedure. Callaway and Sant’Anna separate the analysis into identification of disaggregated causal parameters, aggregation into summary effects, and estimation and inference. Their building block is ATT(g,t), the average treatment effect for the cohort first treated in period g measured in calendar period t.[2][23]

For each feasible cohort-time pair, the approach compares cohort g’s outcome change from its clean reference period with the corresponding change for a clean control group. The controls may be never-treated units or units not yet treated by the relevant comparison time.[33][34] These are alternative identifying designs, not one combined requirement.

Under the never-treated version, conditional parallel trends equates the untreated outcome change of each treated cohort and the never-treated group. Under the not-yet-treated version, it equates the cohort’s untreated change with units not yet treated by t + δ.[6]

The comparison-group choice determines the parallel-trends claim. A never-treated comparison is appropriate when that group is sufficiently large and similar to eventually treated units. A not-yet-treated comparison uses more groups and can yield more informative inference, but, with no anticipation, it restricts observed pre-treatment trends across groups whereas the never-treated condition does not. Parallel trends may hold conditional on pre-treatment covariates; Callaway and Sant’Anna provide outcome-regression, inverse-probability-weighted, and doubly robust estimators for that setting.[3][6]

Callaway and Sant’Anna permit arbitrary treatment-effect heterogeneity and dynamic effects. ATT(g,t) may differ by adoption cohort, calendar period, observed covariates, and time since adoption. The analyst then explicitly aggregates the cohort-time effects into an overall ATT, cohort effects, or event-time effects rather than asking a single TWFE coefficient to impose its own weights.[2][3][33][34] For an event-time effect e = t − g, the standard aggregate averages ATT(g,g+e) across cohorts observed for e periods using cohort-size weights.[3]

A dynamic event-study aggregate can change because treatment effects change with exposure or because the cohorts represented at successive event times change. Callaway and Sant’Anna therefore propose balancing the cohort set across event times when the aim is to compare dynamic effects over a fixed exposure window; this removes composition changes from differences between event-time estimates.[3][6]

The baseline anticipation assumption is δ = 0: outcomes are unaffected before treatment. The framework also permits limited anticipation when the analyst specifies a known horizon δ. The clean reference period becomes g − δ − 1, and not-yet-treated controls must remain untreated through t + δ.[6][3] In the implementation, units leave the not-yet-treated comparison group once they enter their own anticipation window.[82] Each anticipated period removes a usable pre-treatment period, measures effects from further back, and can remove early cohorts that lack a clean base period.[82]

### What assumptions do interaction-weighted estimators make concerning parallel trends

Sun and Abraham’s interaction-weighted estimator is designed for event studies, where the researcher wants effects by event time—periods before or after a cohort’s own treatment date. It first estimates a cohort-and-relative-time average treatment effect, often denoted CATT(e,l): the effect for cohort e at l periods relative to treatment. It then aggregates these cohort-specific estimates at each event time using cohort shares as weights.[7]

The aggregation is deliberately transparent. For a particular relative period, the interaction-weighted estimate is a convex average: all cohort weights are non-negative and sum to one.[7] It therefore permits a treatment effect in the first year after treatment to differ between early and late adopters, and permits the path of effects over exposure duration to differ across cohorts.

The reported event-time effect is an explicitly defined average of those heterogeneous CATTs, not a conventional two-way-fixed-effects coefficient that can mix effects from other cohorts and other event times.[7]

Sun and Abraham define treatment-effect homogeneity as the requirement that, for each relative period, CATT(e,l) does not vary by cohort. This permits dynamics but requires every cohort to share the same event-time treatment-effect path. They describe that restriction as strong.[7] Crucially, it is not required for the interaction-weighted estimator itself. Parallel trends and no anticipation identify the cohort-specific effects; the interaction-weighted summary then averages them.[7][38]

The baseline Sun–Abraham parallel-trends assumption is relatively strong: for any two periods, the expected change in untreated baseline outcomes must be the same across treatment-timing cohorts.[7] This is sometimes called all-groups, all-periods parallel trends.

It supports the use of cohort-relative-time interactions in a saturated regression, because untreated cohorts can provide valid comparisons wherever they have not yet been affected.

The clean-control rule matters. In the original interaction-weighted implementation, the effective controls are never-treated units if such units exist, or the last-treated cohort when there is no never-treated cohort. Always-treated units are dropped.[18][86][7] When the last-treated cohort is used, estimation is limited to dates before that cohort itself is treated, because after its adoption there is no untreated comparison group for the relevant cohort effects.[7]

With never-treated controls, the relevant condition can be formulated more narrowly as post-treatment parallel trends between each treated cohort and the never-treated group, using the final pre-treatment period as the baseline.[83] That formulation does not require parallel trends among every pair of eventually treated cohorts before treatment.

But broader use of untreated or not-yet-treated cohorts requires broader trend restrictions, because those cohorts must be valid controls when they are used.[83][39]

The literature uses different labels for the IW comparison set. Sun and Abraham’s eventstudyinteract documentation specifies never-treated or last-treated controls.[86] A software summary instead describes Sun–Abraham implementations as using not-yet-treated and/or never-treated comparisons.[31] The substantive point is not the label but the rule: no cohort can serve as a control after it is affected by treatment, and the parallel-trends assumption must apply to the specific cohorts and dates actually used for comparison.[18][7]

The no-anticipation assumption says the average treatment effect is zero in all periods before a cohort’s treatment date.[7] This matters twice. First, pre-treatment outcomes are used as reference periods.

Second, later-treated cohorts are valid controls only while their outcomes remain free of effects from their own forthcoming treatment. An apparent pre-treatment effect can reflect either diverging pre-existing trends or anticipatory responses to a known future policy; institutional knowledge distinguishes them.[69]

Sun and Abraham allow a bounded version of this assumption. If anticipation may begin K periods before adoption, the analyst can choose an earlier pre-period and retain only cohorts treated sufficiently later as controls; alternatively, the last-treated cohort can serve as the comparison group.[7] The cost is reduced comparison information. More anticipated periods mean fewer clean pre-periods and fewer later-treated cohorts that remain eligible controls at a given event time.[7]

**IW fixes the event-study contamination problem by estimating cohort-specific effects before aggregation, so a coefficient labelled as one event time is not mechanically a mixture of effects from other event times.** It is robust to cross-cohort heterogeneity, but it still requires the untreated trend restriction appropriate to its chosen control group and either no anticipation or a credible, prespecified anticipation horizon.[7]

### What assumptions do imputation-based methods make concerning parallel trends

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s imputation method takes a different route. First, it estimates a model for untreated potential outcomes using only observations that are untreated. In the benchmark version, the model contains unit fixed effects and period fixed effects. Second, it predicts, or imputes, the untreated outcome for each treated observation. Third, it defines that observation’s treatment-effect estimate as actual outcome minus predicted untreated outcome, and averages those gaps using weights chosen for the target estimand—for example, a cohort-time ATT, an event-time effect, or an overall ATT.[33][87][88]

This is not simply a two-by-two DiD with a different weighting rule. It is a model of the entire untreated outcome process. The benchmark parallel-trends condition is that expected untreated outcomes have an additive unit-plus-period structure: a unit’s untreated level may differ permanently from another unit’s, and period shocks may be common, but the expected untreated change between dates is shared across units.[5][42] The framework can include richer observed covariates, unit-specific trends, or other specified structure, but then the model used for untreated outcomes must be correctly specified.[5]

For this reason, imputation is often characterized as imposing parallel trends for all relevant groups and time periods.[33][23] It uses never-treated observations and pre-treatment observations of eventually treated units together to estimate the counterfactual model.[33][89] This can exploit more pre-treatment information than a last-pre-period DiD comparison and can improve precision, but it relies on the validity of the model over that broader set of observations.[72][23]

The support requirement is also substantive. Treated observations can be imputed only if untreated observations identify the fixed effects, covariates, or other nuisance parameters needed to predict their untreated outcomes. The covariate space of treated observations must be spanned by untreated observations.[42] When all units are treated in a period and there is no remaining untreated information, long-run dynamic effects may not be identified without imposing further restrictions on treatment-effect dynamics or untreated outcome evolution.[72][42]

BJS does not require treatment-effect homogeneity for unbiased estimation. Its conservative baseline permits unrestricted treatment-effect heterogeneity, and its imputation representation applies to target averages even when effects differ arbitrarily across units and periods.[5][42] Researchers may impose a model of treatment effects—such as effects depending only on time since treatment—when justified by prior economic reasoning, but BJS treats unrestricted heterogeneity as the default.[42][85]

It is important to separate this identification result from efficiency. BJS derive the efficient linear unbiased estimator for the target under their model under homoskedastic and mutually uncorrelated errors.[5] Their Gauss–Markov efficiency result also requires untreated potential outcomes to be independent across groups and over time.[57] Homoskedasticity and independence are extra conditions for that efficiency claim, not conditions required for the imputation estimator to remain unbiased.

The same distinction resolves a seemingly conflicting software description that says imputation “requires homogeneity for efficiency”: homogeneity can underpin particular precision comparisons with Callaway–Sant’Anna or Sun–Abraham, but BJS’s identification result allows heterogeneous effects.[5][66]

The baseline anticipation condition is no anticipation: an observation classified as untreated must equal its untreated potential outcome.[5][42] If future treatment changes behaviour before the recorded treatment date, both the outcome model and the resulting imputed counterfactual can be biased, because the supposedly untreated sample contains treated responses.[71] The practical solution is to move the event date backward by a credible maximum anticipation window; the implementation’s shift option treats treatment as occurring that many periods earlier.[5][68]

This is a design adjustment, not a test for anticipation. Shifting excludes potentially affected leads from the untreated-outcome estimation sample and redefines event time. It can preserve identification only if the remaining observations used to fit the untreated model are unaffected and still provide sufficient support for imputation.[5][68][42] Longer anticipation windows therefore reduce usable untreated information and may eliminate early cohorts or long exposure horizons from the estimand.

**Imputation solves the staggered-adoption problem by fitting the untreated outcome process exclusively on untreated observations and then aggregating actual-minus-imputed gaps; it substitutes a potentially stronger, model-based all-period parallel-trends requirement for the more local two-by-two comparison used by group-time methods.** Its ability to accommodate heterogeneous effects is an identification property, while its efficiency advantage depends on additional assumptions about the untreated-outcome model and error structure.[31][5]

In the one-treated-group comparison, Borusyak et al.’s imputation estimator uses the average outcome over all pre-treatment periods as its baseline, whereas Callaway–Sant’Anna and Sun–Abraham use the last pre-treatment outcome. The all-pre-treatment baseline may improve precision and may be more biased when parallel trends does not hold exactly.[57]

For the cited covariate specification, imputation estimators require the coefficients on time-varying and time-invariant covariates to be constant across time, so the effects of those covariates on untreated potential outcomes are constant over time.[74]

Unrestricted treatment-effect heterogeneity can be avoided by imputation estimators only in the restricted class of staggered-adoption settings in which imputation is feasible.[90]

### Synthesis

The three methods agree on the essential correction to staggered two-way fixed effects: never use already-treated outcomes as if they were untreated controls. They also agree that treatment-effect homogeneity is unnecessary for an interpretable treatment-effect average.[30][7][5] Their disagreement is about how much untreated-outcome structure should be assumed and how much untreated information should be used.

Callaway–Sant’Anna estimates cohort-time DiD effects with never-treated or not-yet-treated clean controls before aggregating them; BJS estimates an untreated-outcome model from untreated observations and typically uses all available untreated information.[34][33][89] Sun–Abraham instead centres the analysis on cohort-specific event-time contrasts and transparent convex aggregation.[7] The practical choice is therefore not which estimator “solves” parallel trends.[30][7][33] All require it.

It is whether the application makes a comparison-specific group-time DiD, an all-cohort event-study restriction, or an all-period untreated-outcome model most credible, and whether the treatment’s announcement process supports a defensible anticipation window.[6][7][42][80][23]

Callaway and Sant’Anna explicitly allow conditional parallel trends and a wider range of aggregations of group-time effects, whereas Sun and Abraham focus particularly on event-study aggregation.[28]

Gardner’s two-stage DiD is distinct from Callaway–Sant’Anna’s group-time-effect aggregation. DiD estimation commonly has two steps: first estimate treatment parameters and then aggregate them into an overall average treatment effect on the treated, effects by group, or effects by time since treatment.[108] In the Callaway–Sant’Anna aggregation described in the new evidence, the aggregate average treatment effect on the treated averages effects across treated observations in all treated groups and periods; without covariates, with additive covariates, or with covariates demeaned by treated-sample means, each group-period effect receives its sample-size weight.[108]

For repeated cross-sectional data, the reported `hdidregress` and `csdid` implementations add the cohort’s immediately preceding pre-treatment observation count to the aggregation weight, unlike the stated Callaway–Sant’Anna formula; the two weighting formulas coincide in a balanced panel.[109] The aggregation difference grows with heterogeneity in treatment effects and observation counts, particularly when the two are correlated or the reference-pre-period count is large relative to treated-period counts, and is zero in a balanced panel.[109]

## Pre-trend testing and Roth (2022)

**Pre-trend testing is not a pass–fail validation exercise.** It is a diagnostic for whether observed outcomes moved differently before treatment, whereas Difference-in-Differences requires a claim about the unobserved outcome path that treated units would have followed after treatment without treatment. Staggered-adoption estimators can correct the interpretation of event-study coefficients when treatment effects differ across cohorts or event times; that correction is distinct from Roth’s concern about low-powered pre-tests and inference after selecting estimates that passed them. Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s imputation procedure additionally addresses the latter concern conditionally: its specified untreated-sample test coefficients and efficient estimator are uncorrelated under spherical errors and independent under normal errors.[118][42]

### What are Roth’s (2022) concerns about pre-trend testing?

Jonathan Roth’s 2022 American Economic Review: Insights article, 《Pretest with Caution: Event-Study Estimates after Testing for Parallel Trends》, identifies two failures in the conventional practice of testing whether pre-treatment event-study coefficients equal zero. First, these tests can have low statistical power: they may fail to reject zero pre-trends even when the true trend difference is large enough to materially bias the estimated treatment effect. Second, conditioning analysis, reporting, or publication on passing the test can itself distort estimation and inference, worsening point-estimate bias and confidence-interval undercoverage.[119][118]

Roth (2022) analyzed these limitations in theory and in simulations calibrated to a survey of recent papers in leading economics journals, and the simulations show the practical scale of the low-power problem.[120] Linear parallel-trends violations that a conventional test detected only 50 percent of the time often generated bias as large as, or larger than, the estimated treatment effect.

In the most extreme simulation, the bias exceeded the estimated effect and a nominal 95 percent confidence interval contained the true parameter only 24 percent of the time.[23][118] Roth’s review also found that all 12 papers in his final sample displayed pointwise confidence intervals for individual pre-treatment coefficients, only one reported a joint-significance test, and none discussed the size of the trend violation that its design could detect.[118]

The second problem is **pre-test bias**: selecting estimates because the associated pre-trend test was insignificant changes the distribution of the reported treatment-effect estimator. In Roth’s simulations, conditional bias after passing the pre-test exceeded unconditional bias in most specifications and could be more than twice as large.[118] Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess attribute this pre-test-selection mechanism to Roth (2019): OLS pre-trend estimates are correlated with treatment-effect estimates from the same dynamic specification.

Thus, screening on small estimated leads also selects on the noise entering estimated post-treatment effects; when the identifying assumptions hold, this makes inference invalid, and when they fail, it can exacerbate estimation bias.[5][67]

A pre-trend test also cannot test the central causal claim directly. Parallel trends concerns the counterfactual difference in untreated outcome trends after treatment, but a pre-trend diagnostic observes only pre-treatment outcomes. Precisely parallel pre-treatment trends can therefore coexist with a post-treatment divergence absent treatment.[15][23] A rejection is informative—it rejects the joint implication of no anticipation and an all-period version of parallel trends—but a non-rejection is not affirmative evidence that the post-treatment counterfactual condition holds.[10][24]

**The central mistake is to treat an insignificant pre-trend test as confirmation of parallel trends.** Low power means important violations may remain undetected, and pre-treatment fit does not establish the unobserved post-treatment counterfactual trend.[118][23][24]

Roth recommends assessing power against economically relevant hypothesized violations, including nonlinear trends, rather than focusing only on statistical significance. His tools calculate either the smallest violation detectable with a chosen probability or the probability of detecting a specified violation.[118][121] Power analysis improves the interpretation of a test, but it does not remove the distortions created by conditioning on passing it or itself guarantee valid treatment-effect inference.[118]

A complementary response is sensitivity analysis rather than binary testing. Rambachan and Roth (2022) construct confidence sets under restrictions on how much post-treatment counterfactual trend differences may depart from pre-treatment differences, including a bound that makes the post-treatment violation no larger than a constant times the largest pre-treatment violation. Their confidence sets account for uncertainty in estimated pre-treatment differences; under the restrictions, the causal parameter is partially identified, and their approaches provide uniformly valid inference and sensitivity analyses of the departures that would overturn a conclusion.[118][23][120] This changes the question from whether parallel trends has passed a test to how robust the substantive conclusion is to a specified range of departures.

In Roth’s most extreme calibrated simulation, 24% coverage for a nominal 95% confidence interval is a 71-percentage-point coverage shortfall. This quantifies how severely conventional inference can under-cover when a consequential trend violation is weakly detected.[118]

Recent applied-methods guidance reaches the same conclusion: parallel trends is a counterfactual condition that cannot be directly tested, so researchers should justify it with context-specific theory, visualize the data, use adequately powered statistical tools, and choose functional forms using domain knowledge.[27] A non-inferiority test changes the null from exact zero violation to a substantively meaningful violation and rejects only when the data strongly support the conclusion that any violation is small; reporting power against researcher-specified plausible violations is a complementary practice.[27]

The way an event study is normalized also affects what a pre-trend diagnostic displays. When coefficients are normalized to average zero over a reference period, the overall pre-treatment pattern—not separate pointwise tests of whether each coefficient equals zero—is the relevant diagnostic; in the guide’s example, this normalization makes pointwise tests reject less often despite an apparent systematic pre-treatment trend.[122] Uniform confidence bands are designed to contain the full coefficient path with 95% probability, whereas pointwise intervals permit tests only of preselected pointwise hypotheses.[123]

Imposing a flat-pre-trend constraint on event-study coefficients is not a substitute for directly modelling a treated-unit trend. In the guide’s illustration with a pre-existing treated-unit trend and a ramp treatment effect, the flat-pre-trend constraint improved pre-period fit but left the treatment-effect estimates just as inaccurate.[122]

A separate response to the limits of binary pre-testing is to quantify the confounding contribution to the apparent policy effect. The authors of 《Pre-event Trends in the Panel Event-Study Design》 propose using a covariate affected by the confound but not the policy, estimating the policy effect by two-stage least squares with policy leads as excluded instruments; their simulations find that, when strongly identified, this estimator outperforms proceeding after a pre-trend test.[123]

Equivalence tests provide another alternative to failure-to-reject reasoning. Rather than testing a null of no pre-treatment trend difference, these tests assess whether differences are smaller than a specified negligible threshold and can be applied when treatment timing differs across groups.[124]

Roth also finds that publication screening on an insignificant pre-trend has an ambiguous effect on average bias: it can select studies with smaller true trend differences while pre-testing increases bias among studies that pass.[118]

Roth’s final review sample reported a joint pre-trend significance test in only 1 of 12 papers, or 8.3%.[118]

The same post-selection problem arises when researchers choose the sample, specification, event window, or preferred estimator because its pre-treatment estimates are statistically insignificant.

Roth (2022) urges researchers to use context-specific economic knowledge to assess plausible violations of parallel trends rather than relying only on the statistical significance of pre-trend tests.[118]

Roth’s event-study guidance recommends first plotting raw outcomes in calendar time separately by treatment-timing group, rather than treating an event-study plot as its substitute; heterogeneous effects can bias event-study coefficients.[10] Roth’s note further shows that default Callaway–Sant’Anna and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess plots need not be read as traditional dynamic-TWFE plots for diagnosing parallel-trends violations: in its simulation with no treatment effect but a linear upward relative trend, the Callaway–Sant’Anna plot has relatively flat pre-treatment coefficients followed by a kink at treatment, while the Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess plot has an upward pre-trend followed by a sharp downward jump at treatment.[61]

Event-study plots should be assessed using the magnitude of pre-intervention estimates and their confidence-interval bounds, rather than only whether the intervals include zero.[43]

### How does the two-stage aggregation approach address Roth’s (2022) concerns

The two-stage approach here is Gardner’s two-stage residualization estimator, not Callaway and Sant’Anna’s group-time aggregation or Goodman-Bacon (2016, National Bureau of Economic Research, 'The Long-Run Effects of Childhood Insurance Coverage: Medicaid Implementation, Adult Health, and Labor Market Outcomes')’s separate pre-trend adjustment.[8] Gardner first estimates group and period fixed effects using untreated observations and then estimates treatment-duration effects from the adjusted outcomes; its event-study extension replaces the treatment indicator in the second stage with duration indicators.[30] Their basic parameter is the group-time average treatment effect: the average effect in calendar period t for the cohort first treated in period g.[2]

For a dynamic event study, this framework forms cohort-specific pre-treatment comparisons and then aggregates the relevant group-time effects by event time. Under no anticipation and common trends strengthened to apply in all periods, each pre-adoption effect should equal zero; these aggregated placebo effects are evidence about the design’s credibility rather than a test establishing whether parallel trends holds in the post-treatment period.[10][60]

Its main contribution to pre-trend analysis is to replace conventional staggered-TWFE leads with cohort-specific, interpretable pre-treatment comparisons and their chosen aggregation. Conventional dynamic TWFE leads can be nonzero under parallel trends and no anticipation when treatment effects are heterogeneous; Callaway–Sant’Anna group-time effects instead keep the treatment cohort and calendar time explicit before aggregation.[23][2]

The resulting aggregated placebo estimates avoid treating a conventional TWFE lead—an object that can reflect both pre-trends and treatment-effect heterogeneity—as a direct diagnostic of pre-treatment trend differences. In the did2s simulation, Gardner’s two-stage estimates match the true average effects, while conventional OLS estimates appear to show pre-trend violations even though the data were simulated under parallel pre-trends; the article attributes the OLS problem to Sun and Abraham’s (2020) finding that treatment-effect heterogeneity between groups biases estimated pre-trends.[7][2][31]

The procedure does not solve Roth’s low-power problem. The two-stage lead coefficients are better-constructed placebo estimates, but a joint test of those coefficients may still have inadequate power against trend departures that would materially bias post-treatment estimates. Event studies frequently have lower power than static binary-treatment regressions because they estimate many parameters.[10][125] An insignificant set of two-stage leads is therefore a limited diagnostic result, not confirmation of the identifying assumption.

The `did` tools provide uniform confidence bands for group-time effects and dynamic aggregations, accounting for multiple testing across the event-study path rather than treating each event time as an isolated hypothesis test.[31][36]

**Two-stage event studies remove heterogeneity-induced contamination from conventional staggered-TWFE leads, but they do not make pre-trend tests powerful or turn non-rejection into proof of parallel trends.**[30][31][10]

Judgment — In a Callaway–Sant’Anna application, report the aggregated dynamic placebo effects with joint or simultaneous inference, show what economically meaningful trend departures the test could detect, and use sensitivity analysis for plausible post-treatment departures. Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020) state that statistical pre-tests should not select between versions of the parallel-trends assumption because Roth (2020) shows that this practice can distort inference; they recommend choosing the assumption from the application’s context. The researcher should not select the parallel-trends assumption or anticipation window because it produces the flattest pre-period plot.[10][15][3][28][82]

Gardner’s two-stage estimator should also be kept distinct from Callaway–Sant’Anna group-time aggregation. In Callaway–Sant’Anna’s `att_gt` implementation, a varying pre-treatment base period produces pseudo-ATTs from consecutive pre-period changes, whereas a universal base period compares each pre-period with the fixed period immediately before treatment, adjusted for anticipation; the two conventions produce the same post-treatment ATT(g,t) estimates but different reported pre-treatment estimates.[54] The implementation reports a pre-test of parallel trends and, by default, uniform confidence bands for the collection of group-time effects.[54] Callaway and Sant’Anna advise against using statistical pre-tests to choose among parallel-trends assumptions, instead choosing the assumption from the application’s context.[28][57][46]

Gardner’s two-stage event-study framework makes its anticipation choice explicit. It permits parallel trends to be assumed only more than \(R^*\) periods before adoption: the usual \(R^*=0\) assumes parallel trends in every pre-treatment period, while \(R^*>0\) excludes near-adoption periods and relaxes the unanticipated-treatment assumption. Its pre-treatment relative-time coefficients test whether the mean first-stage residual is zero for units at each specified distance from adoption, as parallel trends implies.[81]

Callaway and Sant’Anna’s cohort-time aggregation is distinct from Gardner’s residualization procedure, but it supplies heterogeneity-robust placebo estimators and simultaneous confidence bands for its group-time effects. Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020) state that their bands asymptotically cover the entire path of group-time average treatment effects with fixed probability and account for dependence across estimates, unlike pointwise bands; they explicitly account for potential multiple-testing problems, whereas Sun and Abraham (2020) focus on pointwise inference.[46]

In Callaway and Sant’Anna’s minimum-wage application, some estimated pseudo group-time average treatment effects in pre-treatment periods are significantly different from zero, which the authors describe as suggestive evidence against the parallel-trends assumption and a reason to interpret the results with caution.[46]

In the Callaway–Sant’Anna group-time framework, ATT(g,t) is estimated using units untreated in period t as controls—typically never-treated or not-yet-treated units—and can then be aggregated into overall, cohort, calendar-time, or event-time effects.

Callaway and Sant’Anna state that their conditional parallel-trends identifying assumption is fundamentally untestable. Their pre-test instead tests a stronger augmented condition: conditional parallel trends must hold in every period, including the pre-treatment periods in which the cohort has not yet been treated.[126] Under that augmented condition, the testable null is that, for every not-yet-treated group and every pre-treatment pair of consecutive periods, its conditional outcome change equals the control group’s conditional outcome change.[126]

Gardner’s theoretical results assume parallel trends for every group between every pair of consecutive periods. This is stronger than Callaway and Sant’Anna’s assumption, which requires parallel trends between treated and never-treated groups after a group’s treatment time; Gardner can accommodate that weaker condition by using only never-treated groups in the first-stage regression, at the cost of power.[80]

### How do interaction-weighted estimators address Roth’s (2022) concerns about pre-trend

Sun and Abraham’s interaction-weighted estimator addresses the same staggered-TWFE contamination problem through a different construction. It estimates cohort-specific event-time effects—effects for units first treated in a particular cohort at a particular time relative to treatment—and then aggregates those estimates at each event time using cohort sample-share weights.[38][127] In ordinary language, it first compares each treatment cohort with a valid comparison group at each relative time, then averages like-with-like rather than asking one pooled TWFE coefficient to summarize incompatible comparisons.

This directly addresses why conventional lead tests become misleading with heterogeneous effects. Sun and Abraham show that a dynamic-TWFE lead coefficient can be a linear combination of cohort-specific effects from post-treatment periods, even when parallel trends and no anticipation both hold.[38][7] Thus, conventional lead coefficients can be nonzero when there are no actual pre-treatment effects; conversely, they can be zero even when pre-trends exist.[7][18] Interaction weighting removes this cross-period contamination by estimating the cohort-event-time parameters before aggregation.[15][38]

The resulting pre-treatment coefficients are therefore interpretable as heterogeneity-robust placebo estimates of the relevant cohort/event-time effects. Sun and Abraham’s estimator fixes the problem that a standard staggered-TWFE lead is a joint reflection of trend differences and post-treatment effect heterogeneity.[22][15] It does not eliminate the parallel-trends and no-anticipation assumptions on which those placebo comparisons rely.

In *Social Media and Mental Health*, TWFE, BJS, CS, SA, and de Chaisemartin–d’Haultfoeuille event studies show near-zero pre-period estimates, while the heterogeneity-robust estimators show effects increasing with exposure.[134][136][151][138]

Roth’s critique remains distinct from the heterogeneity problem. Roth explicitly states that his pre-testing results apply to asymptotically normal event-study estimators, including the Callaway–Sant’Anna and Sun–Abraham proposals that provide interpretable coefficients under staggered timing and heterogeneous effects.[118] In other words, an interaction-weighted lead can be the right estimand and still be too imprecise to reject a substantively important differential trend.[118][23]

Inference is also relevant. Sun and Abraham’s software constructs pointwise confidence intervals for an effect at a given event time, while Callaway–Sant’Anna emphasize simultaneous bands for the full path.[86][2] Pointwise intervals answer whether a particular coefficient is distinguishable from zero; simultaneous bands address the family of coefficients inspected across the event-study path.[3][36] Neither form of band answers Roth’s power question by itself, because simultaneous coverage controls multiple-testing error rather than measuring a test’s ability to detect a particular meaningful violation.

**Interaction weighting solves the staggered-TWFE estimand problem in pre-treatment leads; it does not solve the separate problem that a correctly estimated pre-test may be weak against economically important violations.**[7][118]

Judgment — A Sun–Abraham event study should therefore be paired with three separate analyses: first, a joint or simultaneous assessment of the pre-period coefficients; second, a Roth-style power calculation or non-inferiority test against a substantively chosen trend deviation; and third, a sensitivity analysis that permits bounded departures from parallel trends. Rambachan–Roth sensitivity methods extend to staggered settings by using post-treatment event-time effects as the target and cohort-based placebo effects as pre-treatment evidence.[23]

The interpretation of a cohort-aware pre-period coefficient also depends on the parallel-trends restriction being invoked. Under parallel-trends assumption (PTA) 2.5, pre-treatment trends are parallel across every group, so nonzero pre-treatment event-study estimates are direct evidence against the identifying assumptions. PTA 2.6 is the weakest of the stated assumptions, imposes no restriction on pre-treatment trends across groups, is just-identified, and makes pre-treatment event-study estimates placebo-type evidence rather than a direct test.[40]

Multiplicity is practically important in long interaction-weighted event studies. An event study with 20 leads and 20 lags contains 40 estimates, and pointwise intervals can generate apparently significant effects or pre-trends by chance; simultaneous confidence intervals instead asymptotically cover the entire curve with probability 1 − α.[40] This controls the multiple-testing problem, while a Roth-style power calculation addresses whether the joint diagnostic could detect a substantively important departure.[40][128]

The reason this correction is necessary is that, under staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects, a conventional dynamic-TWFE coefficient for either a lead or a lag can incorporate effects from other relative periods and cohorts. A conventional lead can consequently be nonzero when parallel trends holds or zero when it fails, so it is not a reliable parallel-trends diagnostic in this setting.[57][129]

Sun and Abraham implement this construction by interacting treatment-cohort indicators with relative-time indicators. Reporting the cohort-by-event-time estimates as well as their aggregate can reveal cohort-specific pre-treatment discrepancies that an aggregate lead conceals.

The fully interacted Sun–Abraham regression estimates pre-treatment treatment-effect parameters that can be used for pre-testing parallel trends. Its post-treatment treatment-effect parameters remain robust to violations of parallel trends in pre-treatment periods when parallel trends holds in the post-treatment periods.[19]

### How do imputation-based methods address Roth’s (2022) concerns about pre-trend

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s imputation approach makes a sharper distinction between estimating treatment effects and testing identifying assumptions. The estimator first fits the two-way-fixed-effects (TWFE) equation using observations before treatment, imputes the untreated potential outcome as estimated unit and period fixed effects, calculates treatment effects as observed outcomes minus those predictions, and takes their weighted average with weights defined by the treatment-effect target.[130]

For pre-trend testing, Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess do not rely on the same conventional full-sample lead-lag regression used to estimate the post-treatment effects. Their proposed test estimates a richer regression using untreated observations only, adds terms that represent specified possible departures from parallel trends or no anticipation, and jointly tests whether those terms are zero with an F-test.[5][42] The test thus asks whether the maintained untreated-outcome model is contradicted before treatment, without allowing treated outcomes and heterogeneous post-treatment effects to contaminate the diagnostic.[42][73]

This resolves a staggered-adoption-specific interpretation failure of conventional lead tests. Conventional full-sample event-study tests use treated and untreated observations and implicitly impose treatment-effect homogeneity within an event-time horizon; their rejection can reflect failure of those homogeneity restrictions rather than failure of parallel trends or no anticipation.[42][5] The imputation test avoids that particular conflation by testing with untreated observations only.[5][42]

It also addresses Roth’s selection concern under explicit conditions. Under the paper’s model and spherical-error assumption, the efficient imputation treatment-effect estimator is uncorrelated with the proposed pre-trend-test coefficients. Under normally distributed errors, the treatment-effect estimator and test coefficients are independent, so conditioning on passing the test does not change the estimator’s distribution.[42] The authors describe this separation of testing and estimation as avoiding post-pretesting inference problems under spherical errors.[42][5]

**Imputation-based testing supplies a conditional remedy to Roth’s inference-after-selection problem: the specified untreated-sample test is separated from treatment-effect estimation, the efficient estimator is uncorrelated with its test coefficients under spherical errors, and the two are independent under normal errors.**[42]

That result does not eliminate low power. The `did_imputation` guidance warns that including too many pre-treatment leads lowers the power of the joint test; it recommends a reasonable number of leads rather than mechanically using all available pre-periods.[68] A failure to reject remains compatible with differential trends that the design cannot detect, while a rejection provides evidence against the maintained untreated-outcome and no-anticipation restrictions.[42][73] Imputation also does not correct a violation of parallel trends simply because its placebo residuals are insignificant.[71]

Anticipation deserves separate treatment. A pre-treatment residual can reflect an anticipatory causal response or a confounding deviation from parallel trends, and these are observationally difficult to distinguish from a rejected pre-trend test alone.[131] Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess show that when there are no never-treated units, fully dynamic specifications that do not rule out anticipation may not point-identify the dynamic paths of anticipation and treatment effects.[85] The anticipation window must therefore be grounded in the policy’s announcement, signing, or expected implementation date, rather than selected because it makes leads appear flatter.[82]

Imputation improves what a staggered-adoption pre-test measures and, under its stated error conditions, how the treatment-effect estimator behaves after pre-test selection; it does not make an insignificant test evidence for parallel trends.

The relevant interpretation remains diagnostic rather than confirmatory: a pre-treatment test can reveal evidence inconsistent with the maintained model, but the parallel-trends condition concerns the treated group’s unobserved untreated counterfactual trajectory.[69] Low power is especially acute when only a few pre-treatment periods are available, so a non-significant imputation-based test is a failure to find evidence against the restriction, not evidence that the restriction holds.[69]

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s Proposition A10 establishes equal sensitivity of robust estimators to linear pre-trends: any linear estimator unbiased for the target under their parallel-trends and no-anticipation assumptions is numerically invariant to adding any combination of unit and period fixed effects to the outcome.[42]

After imputation, the estimated effects can be averaged separately by treatment cohort, calendar period, or event time, so the dynamic estimand is chosen explicitly rather than inherited from a pooled lead-lag regression.

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess propose testing parallel trends and no anticipation with OLS regressions using untreated observations only; they state that this separates assumption testing from treatment-effect estimation and avoids the power loss associated with standard event-study tests.[5]

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess’s Test 1 explicitly models and estimates a specified alternative to parallel trends or no anticipation in order to improve efficiency for detecting that specified violation.[5]

### Synthesis

The three approaches correct the heterogeneity contamination of conventional staggered-TWFE event-study leads: Gardner’s two-stage estimator identifies duration-specific average effects, Sun–Abraham interaction weighting aggregates cohort-specific event-time effects, and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess test identifying restrictions on untreated observations separately from estimating effects.[30][38][42] This correction does not itself solve Roth’s low-power concern. The imputation procedure additionally supplies a conditional inference-after-selection result under spherical and normal errors.[118][68][42]

1. Use a heterogeneity-robust event-study estimator, not conventional dynamic TWFE, for staggered adoption.
2. Treat pre-period estimates as diagnostics, not evidence that parallel trends has been proved.
3. Report joint or simultaneous inference and power against economically relevant trend violations.
4. Pre-specify anticipation windows from institutional knowledge.
5. Report sensitivity analyses that show how much parallel-trends violation would change the substantive conclusion. [23][82]

Rambachan–Roth sensitivity analysis is a complement, not a replacement, to each estimator: the analyst specifies a bound on how much post-treatment counterfactual trends may depart from pre-treatment trends and obtains the range of treatment effects consistent with that bound.[45] Software documentation states that event-study results from Callaway–Sant’Anna, Sun–Abraham, Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess, and `aggte` can be supplied to this sensitivity framework.[55]
