# Evolution and Comparison of Feminist Legal Theory Across Traditions

## Executive summary

Feminist legal theory has evolved through distinct legal histories rather than along one universal path to equality. Anglo-American critical feminism exposes how formally neutral law can reproduce sexual dominance and unequal care burdens, while Islamic feminism contests patriarchal fiqh as human interpretation rather than immutable Shari‘a.

African feminisms address colonial legal pluralism and the unequal effects of both customary law and universalist rights discourse, while Chinese feminist legal scholarship confronts the tension between socialist formal equality and market-produced inequality.

Across all four contexts, women’s property, bodily autonomy, and political participation depend not only on rights on paper but also on who controls legal interpretation, family and labour institutions, and access to public power.

No tradition is a single jurisdiction or a settled programme: their central common insight is that law can entrench hierarchy even when it speaks the language of equality, culture, religion, or modernization.

## Evolution of feminist legal theory

Feminist legal theory has not travelled along a single path from exclusion to equality. It has developed in response to different legal sources and political histories: liberal constitutionalism and common law in Anglo-American settings; Qur’anic interpretation and Islamic jurisprudence in Islamic feminism; colonial legal pluralism, customary authority, and regional human-rights law in African feminisms; and socialist state-building followed by market reform in China. The resulting theories share a concern with women’s subordination, but they disagree about where law locates that subordination and what legal change can accomplish.

### How has Anglo-American critical legal feminism evolved?

Judgment — Anglo-American feminist legal theory is a field of argument about law in liberal legal systems, especially systems organized around individual rights, constitutional equality, private property, and a public/private division between state action and family life. Its early reformist strand sought equal legal status; its critical strand asked why women remained unequal after many formal exclusions had been removed.

The first phase targeted explicit legal disabilities. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century feminism sought rights to vote, own property, and contract. The liberal legal-feminist strategy that became prominent in the 1960s and 1970s challenged sex classifications, discriminatory employment rules, and exclusion from public institutions through litigation and legislation. In the 1970s, litigation led by Ruth Bader Ginsburg and the ACLU Women’s Rights Project challenged sex discrimination and transformed the United States Supreme Court’s interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause, ushering in intermediate scrutiny of sex-based classifications; Title VII and Title IX prohibited overt and some covert discrimination in employment and education.[1][2] This formal-equality approach means equal treatment: the legal rule should not distribute opportunities or burdens on the basis of sex.

That strategy achieved important changes, but feminist theorists identified its limits. Equal treatment may make male experience the unstated baseline: women gain legal recognition to the extent that they can show that they are similarly situated to men. MacKinnon argued that the conventional legal alternatives—gender neutrality or special treatment—both measure women against male norms, either by demanding sameness or by treating women’s difference as an exception.[3][4] Fineman likewise argued that marital-property and joint-custody reforms could describe marriage as a partnership of equals while ignoring the unequal care work, economic dependence, and weaker market position that shaped many women’s lives.[5]

This critique marked a shift from formal equality to substantive equality: equality assessed by real conditions and outcomes, rather than simply identical rules. Feminist legal responses moved from dismantling overt barriers, through antidiscrimination rules, toward examining apparently neutral structures—workplace expectations, family law, welfare policy, and criminal law—that can reproduce inequality.[6] The history of women’s property rights illustrates the point.

Common-law coverture had made a married woman’s property, income, and legal identity subject to her husband’s control; later reforms expanded women’s separate economic status, but the distributional consequences of marriage, divorce, and unpaid care continued to require feminist analysis.[7][8]

Catharine MacKinnon’s work supplied a particularly influential critical account. Her **dominance feminism**, also called an anti-subordination approach, treats gender not primarily as a difference between men and women but as a hierarchy of social power: male dominance and female subordination. MacKinnon argued that law and the state do not neutrally observe this hierarchy. Their ostensibly objective standards frequently take the standpoint of male power as normal.[3][9] In her formulation, gender is a political system because it organizes power.[9]

MacKinnon’s method was **consciousness-raising**: collective reflection on women’s lived experience to identify harms that conventional social and legal language had normalized or obscured. Women’s accounts of harassment, rape, domestic abuse, pornography, workplace exclusion, and economic dependence were therefore not merely personal testimony; they were a method for recognizing a social pattern.[9][10] In *Sexual Harassment of Working Women* (1979), Catharine MacKinnon argued that workplace sexual harassment is sex discrimination, not a private interpersonal matter.[10][2] MacKinnon defined harassment broadly as unwanted sexual demands in a relationship of unequal power, linking sexual coercion to employment and material power.[11]

MacKinnon developed this account in her 1982–83 “Feminism, Marxism, Method, and the State” essays, *Feminism Unmodified* (1987), and *Toward a Feminist Theory of the State* (1989); she also argued in the 1986 sexual-harassment case *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*.[12]

Her account thus moved feminist legal theory beyond the question whether women were treated differently from comparable men. It asked whether law protected practices that made women unequal in the first place. MacKinnon argued that privacy doctrine could shield domestic and sexual violence by placing the family beyond effective public scrutiny.[2][8] She also argued that law’s public/private division makes women’s oppression appear merely domestic, rather than part of a wider division of sexual power extending through household and market.[9]

MacKinnon’s legal interventions were correspondingly ambitious. Her work contributed to the recognition of sexual harassment as sex discrimination, and later doctrine distinguished quid pro quo harassment—employment benefit or penalty conditioned on sexual compliance—from hostile-work-environment harassment.[12][13] With Andrea Dworkin, she drafted civil-rights anti-pornography ordinances in Minneapolis in 1983.

The ordinances defined pornography as the graphic sexually explicit subordination of women and provided civil remedies for dissemination-based subordination, coercion into pornography, pornographically caused assault, and forced viewing.[10] Minneapolis passed the ordinance twice, on December 30, 1983 and July 13, 1984, but the mayor vetoed both versions; ordinances passed in Indianapolis in 1984 and Bellingham in 1988 were invalidated by federal courts.

In 1989, a federal district court invalidated the Bellingham ordinance, citing the Seventh Circuit’s 1985 appellate opinion and the Supreme Court’s summary affirmance of that opinion; federal legislation enacted in 1988 had made direct-appeal summary affirmances largely impermissible.[10]

MacKinnon also opposed symmetrical prostitution laws that criminalize both buyers and people sold for sex. Her argument was that equality cannot be built on a false symmetry between parties positioned differently by economic and sexual power. She supported laws targeting buyers while providing support to those leaving prostitution.[10] Sweden enacted a buyer-focused prostitution law in 1998, effective in 1999, criminalizing purchasers and third-party profiteers while not criminalizing those purchased for sexual use.[10] These interventions show MacKinnon’s distinctive view that sexualized harms should be treated as civil-rights and equality injuries, not simply as private choices or moral offenses.

Martha Fineman developed a different, though also critical, trajectory. Her early work addressed family law, divorce, marital property, and the economic consequences of women’s care work. She argued for a substantive, result-oriented approach: a post-divorce single-mother family should receive greater marital property, particularly housing, and support that recognizes the wage and employment costs of care.[5] This was not a demand for preferential treatment as charity. It was an argument that supposedly neutral family-law arrangements ignore the gendered allocation of care and therefore reproduce material disadvantage.

Martha Fineman’s **vulnerability theory**, formulated around her 2008 account of the vulnerable subject, shifted attention from equal treatment of autonomous individuals to institutional support for universally vulnerable people.[5] This approach begins from the proposition that vulnerability is universal and continuous: every person is embodied, subject to illness, aging, dependency, economic change, and social risk.[5] It distinguishes **inevitable dependency**, the care needs associated with infancy, illness, disability, and aging, from **derivative dependency**, the resource needs of people who provide that care.[5][8][14] The distinction makes caregivers visible as people whose ability to earn wages, accumulate property, or participate equally in public life is shaped by care responsibilities.

Fineman’s target is the autonomous legal subject: the imagined adult who is self-sufficient, independent, healthy, employable, and free from substantial care obligations. She argues that this individual is an abstraction that conceals the social institutions—family, market, school, workplace, welfare state—on which every person depends.[5][15][16] Her proposed **responsive state** would take responsibility for creating and monitoring institutions that distribute resilience-building resources, such as education, health care, security, economic opportunity, and social support.[5][17]

This theory does not reject equality in every setting. Fineman identifies one person, one vote and equal pay for equal work as areas where equality and antidiscrimination principles remain appropriate. But she argues that equality alone is inadequate where unavoidable dependencies and unequal authority structure relationships, such as parent-child and caregiver-dependent relations.[5][16] Her preferred normative aim is often **equity**: fair institutional support in conditions where identical treatment cannot produce just outcomes.[5][16]

Fineman’s development broadened the object of feminist legal reform. She moved from gendered family inequality to the social organization of care, dependency, and institutional privilege. Her proposals include public support for caregivers, paid family leave, flexible work arrangements, and subsidized child care; these are proposed reforms, not claims that such measures have been fully enacted.[14] In this account, the family is not a self-sufficient private unit but an institution assigned work from which markets and states benefit.[5][14]

Anglo-American critical legal feminism has also been transformed by internal criticism. Intersectionality—analysis of overlapping forms of disadvantage such as sex, race, class, disability, sexuality, and immigration status—challenged theories that treated women as a single, homogeneous group.[6][1] Critics argued that dominance feminism could make women appear uniformly victimized and leave too little room for agency, resistance, racial difference, class difference, or sexual diversity.[18][13] Feminist debates also persist over pornography, sex work, criminal punishment, privacy, and state intervention.

Radical feminists associated with MacKinnon favored robust legal intervention against sexualized exploitation; liberal, pro-sex, and postmodern feminists have opposed prohibition or criminalization.[2] Similarly, feminists agree that rape, domestic violence, stalking, and sexual assault are often underenforced, but disagree over whether expanded criminal enforcement protects women or deepens the harms of incarceration.[2]

**The evolution of Anglo-American critical legal feminism therefore moves from removing sex-based exclusions to challenging the institutional production of inequality: MacKinnon foregrounds sexualized dominance and law’s complicity in it, while Fineman foregrounds vulnerability, care, and the state’s responsibility for the institutions that distribute resources.**[10][5]

This comparison examines four traditions—Anglo-American critical legal feminism, Islamic feminism’s reinterpretation of Sharia, African feminist legal critique, and Chinese feminist legal scholarship—through three legal dimensions: property rights, bodily autonomy, and political participation. Property rights concern control of land, income, marital assets, inheritance, and other resources; bodily autonomy concerns reproductive decision-making, sexual integrity, and freedom from violence; political participation concerns voting, representation, institutional voice, organizing, and authority to make or interpret law. The evidence date is 20 November 2026. The account draws on scholars’ published legal theory, legislation and judicial decisions, constitutional and treaty materials, institutional records, and documented advocacy.[2][19][20][21]

The 1980s produced both difference feminism, which sought reforms enabling people to remain in paid work while childbearing, parenting, or caring for elderly parents, and dominance feminism, which urged legal action against pornography, sexual harassment, and rape. From the 1990s onward, intersectional, transnational, queer, and postmodern approaches expanded the analysis of reform.[6]

Katharine Bartlett’s 1989 *Feminist Legal Methods* examined how legal reasoning identifies relevant facts, uses analogies, and defines legal problems, extending feminist critique to legal method itself.[103]

Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced the term intersectionality in 1989 to show that sex discrimination cannot be separated from race, class, disability, migration status, sexuality, and colonial history. In 1986, the United States Supreme Court’s decision in *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson* recognized that a hostile work environment can violate Title VII, an important doctrinal development in making sexual harassment legally intelligible as sex discrimination.

### How has Islamic feminism’s reinterpretation of Sharia evolved?

Judgment — Islamic feminism is not a uniform legal system or a single school of doctrine. It is a contested transnational project in which scholars and activists pursue women’s equality and gender justice through Islamic sources, languages, and interpretive methods rather than by treating Islam as necessarily opposed to feminism.

The term Islamic feminism circulated widely in the 1990s, although accounts of Muslim women’s reform thought reach back to the nineteenth century.[22][23] Ziba Mir-Hosseini identified a clear emerging discourse by the late 1980s: feminist in aspiration and demand, Islamic in language and source of legitimacy.[24] That formulation matters because it rejects a simple choice between secular feminism and religious conservatism. Islamic feminist arguments often insist that justice, equality, dignity, and democratic participation can be articulated from within Islamic traditions.[25][26]

The movement emerged in political settings where Islam had become a language of state authority as well as personal faith. Mir-Hosseini’s own questions about justice and women’s rights came into focus after Iran’s 1979 Revolution. She supported the Revolution and believed in Islam’s justice, but experienced herself as a second-class citizen in a state committed to applying Shari‘a.[24] This experience informed a central Islamic-feminist distinction between **Shari‘a** and **fiqh**.

Shari‘a refers, in Islamic thought, to God’s ideal path or revealed norm; fiqh is human jurisprudence—the historically situated effort by jurists to interpret and apply Islamic sources.Judgment — The distinction permits reformers to criticize patriarchal legal rules as fallible human constructions without claiming that justice itself lies outside Islam.

Mir-Hosseini’s scholarship is especially associated with this jurisprudential move. Her fieldwork in Tehran between 1985 and 1988 and Morocco between September 1988 and December 1989 examined family-law adjudication, court records, and the experiences of judges, lawyers, notaries, and women navigating legal disputes.[27] Her work places modern family law within histories of state codification.

In Morocco, after independence in 1956, the state abolished Berber customary law, created a commission to draft family law, and promulgated a 1957 Personal Status Code written by ten male Maliki jurists and judges, faithful to traditional Maliki fiqh and issued by royal decree without parliamentary debate.[28] Such episodes show that rules often described as timeless Islamic law were also products of modern state-building, elite institutional authority, and codification.

Mir-Hosseini’s approach therefore directs attention to who interprets, codifies, and administers law. In Morocco, King Hassan II stated in September 1992 that, as Commander of the Faithful, he would decide authoritative interpretation through ijtihad—reasoned interpretive effort—and later appointed a revision commission containing one woman among twenty-one members; reforms were issued by royal decree in 1993 while Parliament was dissolved.[28] The lesson is not that ijtihad automatically produces equality. It is that interpretive authority is politically organized, and women’s access to that authority is central to legal reform.

Amina Wadud’s approach is distinct. Wadud is associated above all with feminist Qur’anic interpretation: rereading the Qur’an through women’s experience, textual context, and an ethic of justice, equality, and human dignity. She locates Islamic feminism in Islam as a framework for social justice, not in feminism treated as an external add-on.[26] Wadud began research for *Qur’an and Woman* in 1986; it was first published in 1992 by Penerbit Fajar Bakati Sdn.

Bhd. in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, and the Oxford University Press edition appeared in 1999. Its 1992 publication coincided with the beginning of her activist work as a Muslim woman; translations followed in Indonesian in 1994 and Turkish in 1997, and an Arabic translation offer in 1996 remained incomplete.[29][30]Judgment — Wadud’s method is often called gender-inclusive hermeneutics: it asks how text, language, historical context, and the assumptions of earlier interpreters have shaped gendered legal and ethical conclusions.

Wadud’s intervention challenges the monopoly of male interpretive authority. Her claim is not simply that women should receive better treatment under inherited rules; it is that women’s experience must participate in making the meanings of Islam, human rights, and feminism. She rejects passive acceptance of fixed definitions produced without women’s participation in their construction.[31] In a 2016 lecture, she described Islamic feminism as a third position between secular Muslim feminists who excluded religion from human-rights discussions and Islamist accounts that reproduced male superiority without interrogating their understanding of Islam.[31] This position makes interpretation itself a site of political participation.

Her activism also contested gendered limits on religious leadership. In 2005, Wadud led Friday prayers for a mixed congregation of about 100 people at an Anglican church in Manhattan. The event generated international controversy and criticism from religious leaders and communities.[32] The significance was not that it settled the legal-religious question. It demonstrated that women’s authority in religious practice is itself part of the struggle over who may interpret and embody Islam.

Islamic feminist development has repeatedly crossed the boundary between scholarship and organizing. Wadud described the 2009 launch of Musawah as a moment that made practical implementation possible and enabled her to take the name feminist.[31] Mir-Hosseini’s work likewise connects analysis of jurisprudence to women’s political organization, legal reform, and public debate.

The label remains contested: Mir-Hosseini observes that many participants resist being called either Islamic or feminist, and that they may agree on gender justice while disagreeing about its content or the best means of attaining it.[24][23]

The legal terrain is diverse. Tunisia’s Personal Status Code of 1956 outlawed polygamy, with fines or imprisonment, using Qur’anic reasoning that emphasized monogamy and the impossibility of equal treatment among multiple wives.[33] Tunisia also legalized abortion in 1965.[34] Yet state-led women’s-rights reform could coexist with restrictions on speech and association under Habib Bourguiba’s rule from 1956 to 1987.[33] Tunisia’s inheritance debates show the continuing tension between equality claims, constitutional law, international conventions, and religious-political opposition: in August 2017, the COLIBE committee was tasked with reviewing whether laws including inheritance rules complied with the constitution and international conventions; in November 2018, a draft proposal would have allowed families to choose equal inheritance or retain unequal shares, but Parliament had not reviewed it by the time reported in 2021.[35]

Other legal histories differ sharply. Iran’s 1967 Family Protection Law put women and men on the same footing in access to divorce and child-custody rights, but the post-1979 revolutionary government repudiated it as non-Islamic.[28] In Malaysia, women’s groups secured the Domestic Violence Act through long lobbying: Parliament passed it in 1994 and it entered into force in 1996.[23] In northern Nigeria, women gained the vote in 1976, whereas women in the predominantly Christian south had voting rights from the 1950s; the reported account links this contrast to conservative interpretations of Islamic doctrine and different colonial histories of women’s public and economic roles.[33]

Islamic feminism should therefore not be reduced to a theory of “religion versus rights.” It contests patriarchal interpretations, authoritarian state monopolies over religious meaning, and Western-centered assumptions that women’s emancipation must take a secular Western form.[36][23] It also does not produce one universal interpretation of family law, inheritance, reproductive autonomy, or political participation. These questions depend on national constitutions, state codification, schools of jurisprudence, judicial institutions, women’s movements, and political power.

**Islamic feminism has evolved from women’s challenge to exclusion from religious and legal interpretation into a plural reform project that treats gender-unequal family law as historically constructed and therefore contestable; Wadud centers gender-inclusive Qur’anic interpretation, while Mir-Hosseini centers the distinction between divine ideals and human fiqh.**[26][31][24][28]

Wadud also develops what she calls a tawhidic paradigm: because tawhid affirms the unity of God, no human hierarchy should be treated as divinely ordained. Her method reads contested verses in linguistic, historical, and whole-Qur’an context rather than treating isolated passages as conclusive.

### How has African feminism’s legal critique evolved?

Judgment — African feminism is best understood in the plural. It encompasses legal thought and activism across states with different colonial histories, ethnic communities, religious authorities, constitutional arrangements, and customary systems. Its central legal problem is often legal pluralism: the coexistence of state law, customary law, religious norms, colonial legal inheritances, and international or regional human-rights obligations.

African feminist legal critique developed through an anti-colonial and decolonial account of law. Sylvia Tamale argues that African feminisms have had to navigate both Western influence and domination and African relativism and disparagement. She calls for abandoning uncritical adoption of Western feminism and developing contextually meaningful ways to address women’s marginalization, shaped by the enduring effects of slavery, colonialism, imperialism, race, and patriarchy.[37][38] African feminist accounts trace ostensibly traditional customary law in part to colonial construction: colonial authorities transplanted European law, undermined and reconstituted precolonial legal orders, and intervened in customary marriages through their judgments about African morality.[39][7]

Tamale describes colonial changes that displaced communal land with individual tenure, formalized public authority around men, and increasingly positioned women as private domestic actors.[104]

Uganda provides a clear illustration. After Uganda became a British protectorate in 1894, colonial administration separated state law from personal law: commerce, land, and international relations were placed in the public realm, while family law was classified as private.[37][38] Colonial laws also regulated women’s sexuality and reproduction.

Tamale identifies Uganda’s Dangerous Diseases Ordinance of 1909, Townships (Venereal Diseases) Rules of 1913, and Penal Code of 1930, which criminalized prostitution, as measures modeled on England’s Contagious Diseases Acts.[37][39][38] African feminist legal critique thus treats the public/private boundary as a colonial and gendered legal construction, not a neutral division.

The critique also addresses customary law without treating it as fixed or uniform. After Malawi became a republic in 1966, legal dualism retained two court systems sharing jurisdiction: the Traditional Courts system, headed by the National Traditional Appeal Court, and the High Court system, comprising the Supreme Court, High Court, and Supreme Court of Appeal. This two-tier structure was a colonial inheritance.[7] After 1994, customary-law cases could move through multiple levels of the formal court hierarchy, from subordinate courts to High Court and Supreme Court of Appeal.[7] Customary law is therefore neither merely informal practice nor simply an alternative outside state law; it can be incorporated into formal judicial structures.

Customary legal systems matter because they shape marriage, divorce, inheritance, land access, household authority, and women’s capacity to exit violence. In Uganda, Tamale describes a fault-based civil divorce regime rooted in England’s 1857 Divorce Act, including proof of fault, defenses such as collusion, connivance, and condonation, and a mandatory six-month cooling-off period. She also identifies dowry-refund requirements in customary divorce as an economic barrier that can keep women in abusive marriages.[38] In Malawi, the Courts Amendment Act 2000 gave subordinate courts authority over divorce in African customary marriages.[7] These examples reveal why African feminist legal analysis examines the combined effects of customary norms, inherited colonial legislation, court structures, and economic dependency.

African feminists have pursued constitutional and statutory reform, but they distinguish enactment from effective transformation. Uganda’s Domestic Violence Act was passed on November 11, 2009 and became law in 2010; it expanded local council court jurisdiction, potentially improving local access. Tamale argued, however, that it remained unenforced about a decade later and that patriarchal local institutions could not by themselves deliver justice against family violence.[38][37] Similarly, Uganda’s Supreme Court urged Parliament in 2013 to enact matrimonial-property law based on equal treatment of spouses, but Tamale reported that Parliament had not reformed the challenged family-law rules.[37][39]

African feminist thought also developed transnational institutional forms. The Association of African Women for Research and Development was established in 1977; FEMNET in 1988 coordinated cross-border women’s-rights activism; Women in Law and Development in Africa was founded in 1990 to focus on women’s engagement with law.[37][39] Women and Law in Southern Africa (WLSA) began in 1989, founded by female lecturers as a seven-country regional project across Botswana, Lesotho, Malawi, Mozambique, Swaziland, Zambia, and Zimbabwe.

Its members combine academic and activist research on women’s legal status in relation to land, reproductive health, and inheritance.[38] In 1994, legal feminists from eastern and southern Africa, working with the University of Oslo, founded the Southern and Eastern African Regional Centre for Women’s Law (SEARCWL) to expose students to decolonial gendered-law curricula.[37]

Pan-African political organizing supplied another strand. Before the seventh Pan-African Congress in Kampala in 1994, women organized workshops and obtained a resolution on women in Pan-Africanism; the subsequent lesson, in Tamale’s account, was that Pan-African feminism required grassroots political organization rather than one-off workshops.[37][38] The African Women’s Development Fund (AWDF) organized the first African Feminist Forum in Accra in 2006, where more than 200 delegates from across the continent adopted the Charter of Feminist Principles for African Feminists.

The African Feminist Forum Charter was adopted in Accra in November 2006 by more than 100 African and diaspora activists; it rejects both a homogeneous African feminist identity and the claim that feminism is a Western import.[105]

Its political significance was to name feminism openly as a challenge to structures subordinating women and as a basis for transformative analysis and action.[37][39][38]

The regional human-rights framework became an important, but contested, legal resource. The Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa—commonly called the **Maputo Protocol**—was adopted on July 11, 2003 and entered into force on November 25, 2005 after the fifteenth ratification.[20][40] It built on and in some respects extended CEDAW, including through its specific prohibition of gender-based violence.

By February 2008, twenty-three countries had ratified the Protocol.[20] In Kenya, the 2010 Constitution made ratified treaties part of domestic law; when Kenya ratified the Maputo Protocol on October 6, 2010, its rights became directly justiciable in Kenyan courts.[41]

Regional and international rights law can strengthen claims against discriminatory custom. The Dakar Declaration on the Right to a Fair Trial in Africa, adopted in 1999, states that traditional courts remain subject to the African Charter’s fair-trial guarantees.[42] In Botswana, the High Court held in October 2012 that the Ngwaketse custom of passing a family home only to the youngest son was unconstitutional.[43] In South Africa, the Constitutional Court invalidated section 23 of the Black Administration Act and associated regulations governing estates in October 2004.

Thereafter all estates were administered under the Administration of Estates Act 66 of 1965, as amended, and intestate estates under the Intestate Succession Act 81 of 1987, as amended.[44] These decisions demonstrate an equality-based strategy of reviewing customary or colonial rules against constitutional norms.

Yet African feminist legal critique remains wary of treating universal rights as a complete solution. International rights commitments may coexist with patriarchal domestic law, while public-sphere rights frameworks can leave family-based silencing and violence intact.[6] The critique is double: cultural relativism can protect discriminatory norms from challenge, but universalism can reproduce Western or colonial assumptions by treating external legal categories as culturally neutral. Tamale’s argument for a Pan-African feminist and Ubuntu ethic likewise challenges modern liberalism, capitalism, and dehumanization rather than simply importing individual-rights discourse.[45]

This tension is visible in implementation. Anti-FGM laws can state strong prohibitions without ending the practice: a 2018 study reported that, among 55 million girls aged 0–14 who had experienced or were at risk of FGM in 28 African countries, 50% were in Egypt, Ethiopia, and Nigeria, states with anti-FGM laws.[41] The Gambia’s 2015 amendment prohibited female circumcision and criminalized direct performance and accomplice conduct, with a maximum penalty of three years’ imprisonment, a 50,000-dalasi fine, or both.[41] Legal prohibition is necessary in this framework, but social authority, enforcement, economic conditions, and local institutions determine its practical force.

Political participation itself is also contested within regional institutions. The African Court on Human and Peoples’ Rights reached five women and six men after electing two more women in 2017, the first such near-parity composition in its history.[46] But the Coalition of African Lesbians, granted observer status at the African Commission in April 2015 after applying in 2008, lost that status in August 2018 under pressure from the African Union Executive Council, which invoked African values.[37][38] This episode shows that “African values” can become a language of exclusion as well as a resource for decolonial critique.

**African feminist legal theory has evolved into a legal-pluralist and decolonial reform project: it challenges discriminatory customary rules and colonial legal inheritances while also refusing the assumption that imported common law or universal human-rights discourse is culturally or politically neutral.**[37][7][6][20]

Customary law means norms and institutions treated as law because they arise from the practices of particular communities, especially in matters such as marriage, land, inheritance, and household authority. It is not one uniform or static body of rules: African feminist legal analysis treats it as plural, living, and shaped by interaction with state and colonial law. Tamale’s account of new legal pluralism, developed from the late 1970s, directs attention to the interactive relationship between official and unofficial ordering and between dominant and subordinate groups.[38][39][47]

The double critique produces a route to reform distinct from the other traditions. Anglo-American equality and institutional critiques challenge sex-neutral rules, male standards, sexual hierarchy, and the distribution of care through courts, legislation, and institutional reconstruction; MacKinnon in particular treats privacy as capable of concealing domestic and sexual violence.[2][1][5] Islamic feminism contests who may interpret Islamic sources and codified family law, using the Shari‘a–fiqh distinction to make juristic rules contestable.[19] African feminism instead works across customary, constitutional, statutory, and regional legal orders: the Maputo Protocol combines substantive rights with procedural rights intended to ensure women have a voice in examining and reformulating cultural practices and customary law.[20] Chinese feminist advocacy works through state-mediated channels, including cooperation with the official Women’s Federation system through the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), the United Nations treaty on eliminating discrimination against women, which China signed in 1980.[48]

African feminist thought includes the distinct interventions of Ifi Amadiume, Oyeronke Oyěwùmí, Amina Mama, Celestine Nyamu-Musembi, and Obioma Nnaemeka, alongside Sylvia Tamale. Their work questions whether Western gender categories, nuclear-family assumptions, and liberal individualism can simply describe African social relations, while refusing to romanticize either precolonial society or customary law.

### How has Chinese feminist legal scholarship evolved across state socialism

Judgment — Chinese feminist legal scholarship developed through the distinctive relationship between women’s emancipation and the socialist state. Its central problem is not simply whether the state recognizes equality, but how state policy, labor organization, family law, market reform, and civil-society constraints distribute power between women and men.

Wang Ruqi represents an early state-legal feminist trajectory. After translating the 1926 Soviet Family Code in 1934, she moved from an anarchist-influenced expectation that socialist revolution would dissolve marriage and family toward using socialist state law to regulate marriage and family and thereby protect and liberate women.[49] In 1937, her association adopted a resolution that women should constitute 20% of representatives in assemblies in any city or town, linking women’s liberation to political representation as well as family reform.[49]

Tani Barlow traces the Chinese “woman question” through debate in the 1920s, Communist development discourse in the 1940s–50s, and post-Mao movements concerned with gender and justice.[106]

Late-Qing and Republican feminists linked women’s education, financial independence, voting, wellbeing, and freedom in love and marriage to national modernisation through anarchist, liberal, and socialist programmes.[48][107]

The 1950 Marriage Law and Land Reform Law formed part of a broader socialist transformation. Both sought to combat feudalism and restructure rural production, and their implementation was described as mutually supportive.[49]Judgment — State-socialist feminism, sometimes called state feminism, means women’s advancement pursued through state institutions, mass organizations, legislation, employment policy, and political mobilization. It can create real rights and opportunities while also making women’s participation dependent on party priorities and state-defined goals.

In Wang Zheng’s account of state feminism, women within the Communist Party pursued gender equality alongside class equality while confronting sexist practices and male-dominated leadership.[108][109]

The All-China Women’s Federation (ACWF) exemplified this institutional embedding. It developed branches down to villages and urban neighborhoods, with more than 40,000 officials at or above district level on the government payroll by 1953.[50] In Shanghai by early 1953, women had formed 1,684 women’s congresses with 16,964 committee members and about 50,000 women representatives.[50] Women used state-sanctioned mobilization as a political opening: in a March 8, 1951 parade, the Shanghai Women’s Federation organized more than 300,000 women, including 250,000 housewives, who publicly claimed a place in the new political order.[50]

In the early 1950s, the All-China Women’s Federation promoted education about the Marriage Law and its implementation; from the mid-1950s through the 1960s, it mobilised women into collective agricultural and industrial production.[108][110]

Women’s neighbourhood organisations connected welfare, literacy, health, court assistance, campaigning, and communication with People’s Congress representatives.[108][110]

The People’s Republic of China’s 1950 Marriage Law challenged arranged and coerced marriage and formally established women’s freedom to marry and divorce.

But socialist equality was internally contradictory. In 1957, the ACWF shifted to a “double diligences” policy that made household management women’s primary duty for the following four years, a compromise intended to secure the organization’s survival within masculinist party power.[51] During the Cultural Revolution, the ACWF was shut down and absorbed into the party framework; women’s concerns were treated as part of class struggle rather than as independent political questions.[52] Wang Zheng’s account identifies a conflict between state feminists, who considered socialist revolution and women’s liberation mutually reinforcing, and party patriarchy, which treated women’s liberation as subordinate to revolutionary priorities.[51]

During the 1957 Anti-Rightist Campaign, Party leaders questioned the need for a separate women’s organisation, and ACWF leaders softened equality claims and stressed household roles to avoid charges of “bourgeois feminism.”[110][107]

Women’s labor-force participation was high in the socialist period. More than 90% of urban married women were employed in the late 1950s and 1960s, and women’s labor-force participation remained high until the early 1990s; gender discrimination was formally prohibited.[53] Socialist welfare also included maternity protection: Labor Insurance Regulations instituted in 1951 and amended in 1953 provided female employees 56 days of paid maternity leave, 30 days of paid leave for miscarriage, and employer payment of pregnancy and delivery checkups.[53] Yet political participation remained structured by party hierarchy and gendered channels.

In 1975, women were party or deputy party secretaries in 11% of rural party branches; recruitment of women into rural branches averaged 13.5% in 1966–76 but dropped to 8.6% over the next five years.[52]

Market reform from 1978 changed the terrain. The reforms first decollectivized agriculture and opened China to foreign investment in the late 1970s and early 1980s, then privatized state-owned industry in the following decade.[52] Women experienced the reforms as both increased productive freedom and intensified family responsibility.

For urban Chinese women aged 21–50, labour-force participation fell 25.9 percentage points, from 89.4% in 1990 to 63.5% in 2005; calculated as the decline divided by the 1990 rate, this is a 29.0% relative decline. Over 1988–2004, the female-to-male earnings ratio fell 10.1 percentage points, from 86.3% to 76.2%, a 11.7% relative decline calculated against the 1988 ratio. The study identifies marketization as the main cause of these trends.[53] Li Sipan similarly describes women as the largest group affected by unemployment from state-enterprise reform, while young unmarried women without rights protection made up 70% of the workforce in coastal industrial zones.[48]

The privatization of care intensified these gendered effects. Publicly funded kindergartens had fallen by 2006 to roughly one-third of their 1997 number, while private kindergartens rose from 13.5% to 57.8% of all child-care programs.[53] In the study’s retrospective data, married women’s net odds of upward mobility were 41.2% lower than unmarried women’s, compared with 27.0% lower odds for married men than unmarried men; the confidence intervals overlapped zero for men but not women.

For women, the marriage-associated reduction in upward-mobility odds was 54.3% greater in the 1999–2008 late-reform period than in the 1978–1992 early-reform period.[53] Chinese feminist legal scholarship consequently moved beyond the claim that socialist law declared women equal. It examined how labor markets, welfare retrenchment, marriage, child care, and household responsibilities redistributed disadvantage onto women.

Feminist critiques of post-1978 marketisation and 1990s restructuring also connect reduced work-unit childcare and welfare to women’s increased care responsibilities, and identify rural land loss, precarious work, harassment, and sexual abuse among the resulting concerns.[48][107]

Legal advocacy responded through legislation, public comment, research, legal aid, and litigation. The Law on the Protection of Women’s Rights and Interests took effect on October 1, 1992, and the Labor Law took effect on January 1, 1995.[54] The Employment Promotion Law was promulgated on August 30, 2007 and took effect on January 1, 2008.

During public consultation on its draft from March 25 to April 25, 2007, the National People’s Congress received more than 11,000 opinions, including 7,000 concerning employment discrimination; drafters then added an Equal Employment chapter.[54] The law gave discrimination victims access to courts, although Chinese plaintiffs had to produce relevant evidence themselves rather than shift the burden to employers.[54]

Guo Jianmei represents a vital bridge between state institutions, academic legal research, and public-interest advocacy. She worked at the Ministry of Justice, the ACWF, and the All China Lawyers Association, and she participated full-time in drafting the Women’s Rights and Interests Law from 1989 to 1992 while conducting nationwide research and publishing on women’s rights.[21] After attending the women lawyers’ forum at the Fourth World Conference on Women in September 1995, Guo Jianmei founded the Peking University Law School Women’s Law Research and Service Center three months later, a public-interest organization for women’s legal aid and research.[55][21] Over more than two decades, she handled numerous women’s-rights legal-aid and public-interest litigation matters while researching women’s rights protection and legal aid.[21]

Chinese feminist legal scholarship also includes doctrinal work on family property and unpaid labor. Xia Yinlan’s family-law scholarship includes her 2005 gender analysis of Chinese marital community property and the legal value of housework.[56] Her account of New China’s family-law development describes a movement from formal equality toward substantive equality and from general principles toward more concrete rules.[56][57] Article 1055 of the 2020 Civil Code’s Marriage and Family Book states that spouses have equal status in marriage and family relations; earnings, investment income, intellectual-property income, and specified inheritances acquired during marriage are joint property over which spouses have equal disposal rights.[57] Article 1088 of the 2020 Civil Code permits a spouse who has undertaken substantially more child care, elder care, or support of the other spouse’s work to seek compensation upon divorce; the 2020 codification removed the eligibility condition contained in the 2001 Marriage Law amendment.[57]

These reforms reveal both achievement and continuing contest. Article 1087 directs courts, where spouses cannot agree, to divide joint property with regard to children, women, and the spouse without fault.[57] Article 1090 requires appropriate help where one divorcing spouse is genuinely in financial difficulty, but Xia notes that the lack of detailed criteria and remedies risks weakening its practical effect.[57] A 2010 survey cited by Xia found that 76.3% supported daughters inheriting equally with sons where both had fulfilled support obligations—substantial support, but not universal acceptance of equal inheritance.[57]

Political participation has likewise remained uneven. Village elections followed the Organic Law of Village Committees, passed in 1987 and expanded nationwide after a ten-year experiment, but research cited in the source finds that rural women’s political participation was discouraged by economic and social conditions, reinforced gender roles, exclusion from power, and landlessness.[52] In Daqiuzhuang, 95% of women participated in collective agricultural labor before 1980, whereas by 1988, 84% of married women had left the workforce to become housewives.[52] These shifts show why Chinese feminist legal analysis connects economic restructuring to political voice.

Chinese feminist work has also used international mechanisms while operating within a state-regulated political environment. China signed CEDAW in 1980, and feminist organizations have used the treaty-review process in cooperation with the official Women’s Federation system.[48] Guo Jianmei’s center collaborated with Chinese Women’s University and other organizations in 2014 to submit reports for China’s CEDAW review.[58] At the September 2015 celebrations marking the twentieth anniversary of the 1995 UN World Conference on Women, President Xi Jinping urged greater efforts to promote gender equality and pledged to enhance gender equality as a basic state policy; rights advocates described the closure of the women’s legal-aid centre as a blow to women’s rights and gender equality.[58]

The ACWF later shifted toward services, research, training, and legal-policy advocacy. After the 1995 Beijing Fourth World Conference on Women, emerging NGOs and international gender discourse challenged its earlier monopoly, prompting debate between Party-centred “male and female equality” and rights- or civil-society-oriented “gender equality.”[110]

**Chinese feminist legal scholarship has evolved from using socialist state law to transform marriage, land access, labor, and women’s public status into a structural critique of how marketization, unpaid care, employment discrimination, reproductive regulation, domestic violence, and constrained political organization limit the practical meaning of formal equality.**[49][59][53][54][56][60]

Here, marketization means the transition from state-organized allocation and publicly funded social provision toward employer discretion, privatization, fee-paying services, and market competition. Chinese feminist legal scholarship uses the term to explain why formal socialist equality did not prevent gendered losses in employment, income, care, and political voice.[53][48]

Li Sipan supplies a distinct feminist critique of market-centered reform: women perform most unpaid reproductive labor, need social welfare, and cannot obtain equal opportunity through a pure free market; class, gender, ethnicity, and cultural identity intersect in the disadvantages faced beyond male elites.[48] Xia Yinlan supplies a distinct doctrinal analysis of family property: her 2005 article, 《对中国夫妻共同财产范围的社会性别分析——兼论家务劳动的价值》, analyzed the scope of Chinese marital community property through gender and addressed the value of housework.[56] Guo Jianmei supplies a distinct law-and-advocacy trajectory: from 1989 to 1992 she worked full-time on drafting the Law on the Protection of Women’s Rights and Interests, conducted nationwide research, and published on women’s rights protection.[21]

These scholars’ strategies differ from Islamic feminist interpretive reform, which reopens the authority to derive family-law rules from Islamic sources, and from African feminist contests over customary law, constitutional equality, and regional rights.[19][20] They are closer to Fineman’s institutional critique in treating care and material support as public legal questions, but Chinese feminist scholarship addresses them within a socialist state and a marketizing economy rather than primarily through a responsive-state theory.[5][48][53]

In 1964, Chen Boda attacked the editors of *Women of China* in *Red Flag*, the CCP Central Committee’s theoretical journal, for lacking class perspective and promoting bourgeois humanism. The dispute expressed a central contradiction: state feminists regarded socialist revolution and women’s liberation as symbiotic, whereas Party patriarchy treated women’s liberation as hierarchically subordinate to revolutionary priorities.[51]

The 1980 Marriage Law made implementing family planning a basic principle and a legal obligation of both spouses, raised the legal marriage age to twenty-two for men and twenty for women, and expressly encouraged late marriage and late reproduction.[60]

The 2001 Marriage Law amendment added duties that spouses be faithful and respectful to one another, prohibited cohabitation, and placed the prohibition of domestic violence in Chinese law for the first time. Its effort to bring domestic violence into the public sphere and within court authority prompted heated debate against the traditional norm that law should not interfere with private family affairs.[60]

China adopted the Anti-Domestic Violence Law in 2015; it took effect in 2016 and created mechanisms including personal safety protection orders.

China revised the Law on the Protection of Rights and Interests of Women in 2022, effective in 2023, strengthening provisions on discrimination and women’s property interests.

Other important contributors include Liu Bohong, whose gender research and policy work examines the gap between formal equality and lived conditions, and Li Mingshun, whose scholarship addresses gender-equality law and legal mechanisms for protecting women’s rights. Zheng Zhen has contributed work on gender equality and domestic-violence law.

The 1950 Marriage Law supplies a more specific legal foundation for this state-socialist trajectory. Promulgated on May 1, 1950, it declared the abolition of the compulsory, male-superior feudal marriage system and established free choice of partners, monogamy, equal rights for both sexes, and protection of women’s and children’s lawful interests.[61] It prohibited bigamy, concubinage, child betrothal, interference with widows’ remarriage, and demands for money or gifts connected with marriage.[61] Marriage required the complete willingness of both parties, without compulsion or third-party interference; either spouse could seek divorce, with mediation preceding a court decision where the parties disagreed.[61]

The law structured women’s property and public participation within the family as well as marital choice. It gave spouses equal status in the home, free choice of occupation, and free participation in work or social activities.[61] It gave both spouses equal rights to possess and manage family property and reciprocal inheritance rights; on divorce, a wife retained premarital property, while other household property was to be divided by agreement or court decision with attention to the interests of the wife and children.[61] The statute also permitted locally adapted marriage rules in national-minority areas through a centrally approved framework, showing that the state’s family-law project combined national legislation with bounded local variation.[61]

This history also demonstrates why Chinese feminist legal scholarship distinguishes formal emancipation from women’s practical capacity to exercise rights. The state required marriage and divorce registration at state institutions and made mediation part of the judicial structure, turning intimate relations into a field of state administration and mass implementation.[59] During the 1950–51 and 1953 implementation campaigns, rural women seeking divorce or their own marriage partners faced intimidation, beatings, and murder.[59] Diamant’s account further shows that the party-state was not a single actor: some officials criticized women’s use of new freedoms, provincial authorities ordered local cadres to stop coercive surveillance and punishment of women seeking divorce, and some low-level cadres initiated or facilitated divorces.[59] Women themselves acted to advance their interests, and rights language learned through the Marriage Law remained difficult to revoke.[59]

The 1950 Marriage Law protected a widow’s choice both to remarry and not to remarry; officials corrected local attempts to turn freedom to remarry into an obligation.[49]

The later legislative sequence tracks the transition from revolutionary family transformation to legal normalization during reform and opening-up. The 1980 Marriage Law, adopted on September 10, 1980, took effect on January 1, 1981 and replaced the 1950 law; it identified itself as the fundamental code governing marriage and family relations.[62] Scholarship characterizes the 1980 law as a state-led normalization of marriage and family relations during reform and opening-up, and the 2001 amendment as a response to family problems arising in economic transformation.[63] This legal history supports Chinese feminist scholarship’s focus on how changing state techniques—revolutionary mobilization, codified family law, and market-era regulation—reorganize women’s material and intimate lives.[63]

Promulgated and implemented on May 1, 1950, the PRC Marriage Law rejected the feudal marriage system and established freedom of marriage, monogamy, equal rights for women and men, and protection of women’s and children’s lawful interests.[64]

The 1950 Marriage Law prohibited arranged or forced marriage, interference with a widow’s freedom to remarry, bigamy, concubinage, and demands for property through marriage.[60]

The People’s Republic of China promulgated the Marriage Law on May 1, 1950.[61]

The 1950 Marriage Law required that marriage rest on the complete willingness of both parties and prohibited compulsion by either party or interference by a third party.[61]

Article 1 abolished the arbitrary and compulsory marriage system founded on male superiority and established a system based on free choice of partners, monogamy, equal rights for both sexes, and protection of women’s and children’s lawful interests.[61]

### The history of feminist legal theory across Anglo-American

The four traditions share a refusal to treat law as neutral, but their different legal sources produce different reform strategies. Anglo-American critical feminism moved from antidiscrimination toward critiques of sexual domination, care, and institutional dependency; Islamic feminism reopens Qur’anic interpretation and distinguishes Shari‘a from human fiqh; African feminisms work through colonial legal pluralism, customary authority, and regional rights; Chinese feminist scholarship confronts the gap between socialist equality, market inequality, and state-regulated advocacy.[6][10][5][24][28][37][20][53][21]

The strongest common conclusion is that women’s equality cannot be inferred from a constitutional guarantee, a religious text, a human-rights treaty, or a statutory rule alone. Across the traditions, actual equality depends on who has interpretive authority, how family and labor institutions allocate resources and care, and whether women can organize, litigate, speak, and participate in making law.[31][37][39][5][52]

For urban women aged 21–50, labour-force participation fell by 25.9 percentage points—from 89.4% in 1990 to 63.5% in 2005—a relative decline of 29.0%. This quantifies the scale of women’s withdrawal from paid work during the market-reform period discussed here.[53]

The female-to-male earnings ratio fell 10.1 percentage points, from 86.3% in 1988 to 76.2% in 2004; this was an 11.7% relative decline in the ratio.[53]

Between 1997 and 2006, private providers’ share of Chinese childcare programs rose by 44.3 percentage points, from 13.5% to 57.8%—a 328.1% relative increase. This measures the move from public toward private childcare provision alongside the shrinking number of publicly funded kindergartens.[53]

Women’s average recruitment into rural Chinese Communist Party branches declined by 4.9 percentage points, from 13.5% in 1966–76 to 8.6% in the following five years—a 36.3% relative decline.[52]

## Comparative theories and critiques

Feminist legal theory does not travel as a single doctrine from one legal system to another. It develops through different authoritative sources—constitutional rights, religious texts and jurisprudence, customary law, socialist legislation, and international human-rights instruments—and through distinct political histories. The central comparative question is therefore not which tradition is most feminist, but how each identifies the source of women’s subordination and which legal institution it seeks to transform.

### How does Anglo-American critical legal feminism compare with Islamic feminism

Anglo-American critical legal feminism emerged in the United States legal academy alongside second-wave feminism and critical theory in the 1970s and 1980s. It asked why women remained subordinated despite formal equality, antidiscrimination statutes, and constitutional rights. Feminist legal theory, in Robin West’s formulation, sought a broad account of law’s relation to patriarchy—gendered systems of social power—and used law as a possible instrument against that subordination.[2]

Its central critical move is to reject the idea that law is neutral simply because it uses general language. Formal equality means equal treatment under the same rule; substantive equality examines whether a rule reproduces unequal social power in practice. MacKinnon locates the problem in male dominance embedded in legal norms, whereas Fineman locates it in law’s autonomous, self-sufficient legal subject and the institutions that privatize dependency.[10][2][5]

Islamic feminism is similarly critical of claims that a legal order is neutral or fixed. But its principal site of argument is not liberal constitutional doctrine. It is the interpretation of Islamic sources and the authority to interpret them. Islamic feminism is a global and transnational movement of scholars and activists who develop feminist reinterpretations of Islamic tradition. The term circulated widely in the 1990s, though feminist religious argument and women’s reform activism have deeper histories.[66][23][22]

**The key comparison is that Anglo-American critical feminism interrogates liberal law’s supposedly neutral public/private boundary, while Islamic feminism contests the authority through which religious law is interpreted.** Both expose hidden hierarchy, but the former works principally through civil-rights, equality, criminal, and constitutional law, and the latter through the Qur’an, Sunnah, fiqh, and Islamic accounts of justice.[2][19]

Islamic feminism does not necessarily oppose universal human rights or secular feminism. Wadud describes Islamic feminism as taking Islam as the basis for social justice, equality, and human dignity, while distinguishing it from Muslim feminisms that may use secular or liberal methods. Musawah’s 2009 Framework for Action joins four sources: Islamic teachings, universal human rights, national constitutional equality guarantees and legislation, and women’s lived realities.[26][67] This makes Islamic feminism neither an unmodified religious traditionalism nor a simple translation of Western secular feminism.

The comparison rules out two common simplifications: that Islamic feminism is merely secular feminism in religious language, and that Anglo-American feminism has no internal debate about authority. MacKinnon disputes liberal legal authority when it shields dominance; Fineman disputes autonomy-centered legal authority when it privatizes dependency; Islamic feminists dispute male monopolies over religious interpretation.[9][5][23]

Across the four traditions, the relevant authority is respectively ostensibly neutral state law, religious texts and jurisprudence, overlapping customary and human-rights orders, and the socialist party-state and its legislation; these institutional sources determine whether feminist reform works through equality litigation, interpretive contest, plural-law negotiation, or state-linked advocacy.[9][68][69][70]

Anglo-American critical feminism exposes how formally neutral legal rules can reproduce dominance, whereas African feminist legal analysis asks how state law, colonial formulations of custom, and community practices interact in women’s daily lives.[9][69][71]

Chinese feminist legal scholarship shares Fineman’s concern that formal equality does not itself secure material support, but addresses this problem in a setting where market competition, persistent patriarchal practice, and authoritarian governance shape access to legal remedies and social provision.[17][70][72]

Anglo-American critical legal feminism works chiefly through constitutional, common-law, and statutory institutions whose formally neutral rules can entrench male norms; MacKinnon targets sex-specific dominance, while Fineman targets the institutional distribution of dependency and resources.[13][15][73]

Islamic feminism works from the Qur’an and Sunna while contesting historically produced fiqh and state family codes; its reform strategy is feminist interpretation, engagement with jurisprudence, and change in enacted family law.[26][67]

African feminist legal theory addresses the interaction of state law, customary law, regional instruments, and international human-rights law, treating colonial history and women’s participation in local norms as central to whether legal reform is emancipatory.[38][37][39][20]

### What is MacKinnon’s contribution to Anglo-American critical legal feminism?

Catharine MacKinnon’s distinctive contribution is dominance feminism, also called anti-subordination feminism. It treats sex inequality as a hierarchy of power, not mainly as irrational classification or difference between women and men. Gender, on this account, is a political system because it distributes power; sexuality is a central mechanism through which male dominance and female submission are socially produced.[74][13][9]

MacKinnon’s method was consciousness-raising: women compare their experiences in order to identify what appears private, normal, consensual, or trivial as a patterned social harm. This made sexual harassment, rape, domestic abuse, pornography, and sexual exploitation available for legal analysis as structures of inequality rather than isolated interpersonal wrongs.[2][13][11]

Her argument about sexual harassment illustrates the approach. MacKinnon defined it as unwanted sexual demands within unequal power relations and explained its harm through the interaction of workplace authority and male sexual dominance. Sexual harassment thus became sex discrimination, not merely bad manners or individual misconduct.[11]

MacKinnon also criticized comparator-based equality doctrine. Such doctrine asks whether a woman was treated differently from a similarly situated man. She argued that this framework fails where male norms define the comparison point or where women are segregated into female-dominated, low-paid occupations with no male comparator. A rule that treats likes alike may preserve hierarchy when it defines men’s conditions as the measure of equality.[11][4]

Her critique extended to privacy. U.S. privacy doctrine associated with cases such as *Griswold* and *Roe* could protect reproductive decision-making, but MacKinnon argued that privacy also insulated domestic and sexual violence from public scrutiny. On her account, the home was not naturally a refuge from state power; it could be a location where male power operated without legal challenge.[2][8][1]

MacKinnon argued that rape law centred on force and consent can fail to recognize coercion within unequal sexual relations. She also criticized treating sexual force chiefly through criminal or tort law while locating reproductive control in privacy, rather than understanding both as questions of sex inequality.[101][111]

MacKinnon’s legal programme was not only diagnostic. She and Andrea Dworkin drafted anti-pornography civil-rights ordinances in Minneapolis in 1983. The Minneapolis City Council passed an ordinance on 30 December 1983, but the mayor vetoed it on 5 January 1984; a second, identical ordinance passed on 13 July 1984 and was again vetoed. Similar ordinances enacted in Indianapolis in 1984 and Bellingham in 1988 were invalidated by federal courts.[10] The episode demonstrates both her strategy—treating pornography as a civil-rights injury—and its collision with constitutional free-speech doctrine.

Her work also influenced legal strategies beyond the United States. A later account associates Sweden’s 1999 prostitution law with MacKinnon’s hierarchy theory: the buyer rather than the prostituted person is exposed to sanction, and the source reports that prostituted persons described improved bargaining power after the law’s adoption.[10] That is not a universal solution to sexual exploitation, but it demonstrates her preference for legal interventions that address unequal structures rather than punish the subordinated party.

MacKinnon’s theory has generated sustained criticism. Critics argue that a framework centered on sexual domination can generalize women’s experience too broadly, reduce sexuality to victimization, and leave insufficient space for agency, resistance, race, class, sexuality, and other intersecting forms of power.[18][75] MacKinnon’s later work is described as incorporating intersectionality as a method, but the criticism remains important because a theory of hierarchy must explain variation among women as well as shared structures of power.[74]

**MacKinnon shifted feminist legal analysis from asking whether women receive the same treatment as men to asking how law legitimates, normalizes, or fails to remedy male dominance.** This makes law an object of feminist critique and an instrument that may still be repurposed through equality, civil-rights, and constitutional claims.[9][2]

In *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson* (1986), the United States Supreme Court recognized that a hostile or abusive work environment can constitute sex discrimination under Title VII.

### What is Fineman’s contribution to Anglo-American critical legal feminism?

Martha Albertson Fineman’s contribution is vulnerability theory. Vulnerability theory replaces the legal image of the autonomous, independent individual with the vulnerable subject: an embodied and socially embedded person who is continuously susceptible to changes in bodily and social well-being.[5]

Fineman does not use vulnerability to mean weakness, incapacity, or a condition belonging only to designated groups. She treats vulnerability as universal and continuous, though its effects differ according to the resources, institutions, and relationships available to particular people.[5][76] Her related concept of inevitable dependency concerns the care needs experienced in childhood, old age, illness, and disability. The point is that dependency is ordinary human life, not a personal failure or an exceptional condition.[14]

Fineman’s critique begins with the autonomy myth: the belief that individuals and families can be self-sufficient. She argues that this belief lets the state and market benefit from unpaid caregiving while treating care as a private family responsibility. The family is therefore not outside public law; it is an institution shaped by employment law, welfare policy, divorce rules, health insurance, and public allocation of resources.[14]

Her responsive-state model gives the state responsibility to establish and monitor institutions that build resilience. Resilience means the practical capacity to recover from harm or setbacks, which depends substantially on assets and resources. The legal question becomes whether institutions such as family, employment, education, healthcare, and welfare distribute those assets fairly.[5][76]

Fineman therefore differs from MacKinnon in analytic starting point. MacKinnon begins with sex hierarchy and sexual domination. Fineman begins with universal human vulnerability, dependency, and the institutional allocation of care. She does not deny that women disproportionately perform paid and unpaid care work, but she treats care inequality as a social-structural problem rather than solely a women’s equality problem.[5][17][76]

This has major consequences for family law. Fineman has argued that marriage should cease to be the central legal category and that public subsidies and privileges should instead attach to the caretaker-dependent relationship.[76] Whether or not one accepts abolition of marriage as a legal category, the proposal redirects attention from marital status to the relationships in which people provide and require care.

Fineman’s move beyond gender does not abandon sex-discrimination law. She maintains that sexuality, race, and gender remain important identity categories and that impermissible bias based on them must continue to be addressed. Her claim is narrower: antidiscrimination law alone cannot remedy inequality produced by institutions that distribute care, income, time, and security unequally.[76][5]

**Fineman recasts equality as an institutional question: law should secure equity in conditions of inevitable dependency, not merely identical treatment for formally independent individuals.** This provides a framework that can be adapted to different legal structures and political cultures, because the requirement is a responsive state rather than one uniform institutional design.[5][16]

Her earlier *The Illusion of Equality* (1991) argued that ostensibly equal divorce rules can overlook unpaid caregiving, reduced earning capacity, and unequal access to assets; *The Autonomy Myth* (2004) developed her argument that apparent independence rests on social institutions.

### How does Islamic feminism’s reinterpretation of Sharia compare with Anglo-American

Islamic feminism is a contested category. Mir-Hosseini emphasizes that its participants are locally grounded, diverse, evolving, and not always comfortable with either the label Islamic or feminist. They share a commitment to gender justice and equality, but they may disagree about what justice requires and how to achieve it.[24][23]

The term Sharia requires careful use. In Muslim belief, Shari‘ah is God’s revealed will. Fiqh means human understanding: the jurists’ effort to derive legal rules from the Qur’an and Sunnah, the Prophet’s practice as preserved in hadith. Shari‘ah is understood as sacred, universal, and eternal; fiqh is human, temporal, local, and historically situated.[19]

This distinction changes the terms of legal argument. If a family-law rule is simply called Sharia, it can appear immune from criticism because it is presented as divine. If it is recognized as fiqh, it becomes a human jurisprudential construction that can be debated, revised, or replaced. Mir-Hosseini argues that patriarchal interpretations must be challenged at the level of fiqh, enabling believing Muslims to make gender-justice arguments from within Islamic faith.[19]

The contrast is one of legal authority and method. MacKinnon asks how a supposedly neutral liberal state reproduces male power; Islamic feminism asks who may interpret sacred texts and whether male juristic constructions are treated as divine command. In both, contingent power is naturalized—through liberal law’s neutrality and privacy in one setting, and religious authority in the other.[10][2][19]

Mir-Hosseini’s distinction between divine Shari‘ah and human fiqh parallels African feminists’ distinction between living customary law and colonial or official formulations of custom: both contest an authority’s claim that a historically produced rule is immutable.[19][39][47] In African legal pluralism, state law, customary law, religious law, constitutional rights, and international law may all govern family life.

African feminists therefore examine how women move among overlapping legal orders, while Islamic feminists such as those associated with Musawah combine Islamic teachings, human rights, national guarantees, legislation, and lived realities.[38][67]

Chinese feminist legal scholarship differs again because its primary question is not interpretive authority over sacred law or customary law. It concerns the state’s changing role: first as socialist legislator and mobilizer, then as regulator of a marketizing economy in which formal equality coexists with gendered labor insecurity. This makes institutional accountability, legal aid, employment remedies, and constrained civil-society advocacy particularly prominent.[49][54][48]

The comparison rules out the assumption that religiously grounded feminism is necessarily less legal or less reformist than secular feminism. Islamic feminist reinterpretation can support statutory family-law change, domestic-violence legislation, constitutional equality claims, and challenges to state monopolies over Islamic interpretation.[23][67]

A state-law example shows that Islamic legal reinterpretation can take a codified form as well as a scholarly or movement form. Tunisia’s 1956 Code of Personal Status was characterized as a radical project to modernize and reinterpret Sharia jurisdiction through a rereading of its new family-law rules.[77] This contrasts with Mir-Hosseini’s emphasis on fiqh as revisable jurisprudence and Wadud’s Qur’an-centred hermeneutics: all three make inherited gender rules contestable, but the Tunisian example locates reform in state codification.[77][19][31]

### How does Mir-Hosseini reinterpret Sharia?

Ziba Mir-Hosseini’s jurisprudential intervention is the Shari‘ah/fiqh distinction. She argues that there is no unitary, coherent concept of gender rights within Islamic legal thought. Competing doctrines reflect differing theological, social, sexual, and juristic assumptions, as well as tension between Islam’s ethical egalitarianism and the patriarchal settings in which law developed and was institutionalized.[19]

Mir-Hosseini identifies patriarchal, balancing, and radically revisionist positions competing within Islamic legal debate.[19][112]

She therefore treats Muslim family laws as human juristic constructs. They are shaped by social assumptions about women and men, juristic reasoning, and the historical conditions under which sacred texts are turned into law. This analysis does not deny that Islamic sources matter to believers. It denies that historical male interpretations have exclusive authority over their meaning.[19]

Mir-Hosseini argues that twentieth-century Muslim states displaced Islamic legal theory from many fields while selectively retaining, codifying, and reforming marriage and divorce law.[19]

Mir-Hosseini’s account of justice is also important. She accepts a rationalist, neo-Mu‘tazili position: justice has a rational basis and is not exhausted by inherited readings of revealed texts. Her argument makes gender justice an Islamic question rather than an external standard imposed on Islam.[19]

Her own intellectual trajectory shows why this method emerged. She writes that the Iranian Revolution of 1979 transformed her personal and intellectual life. Although she initially supported the Revolution and believed in Islam’s justice, she experienced second-class citizenship under an Islamic state committed to applying Shari‘a.[24] The resulting problem was not whether Islam or women’s equality must be chosen, but why state law presented as Islamic could produce women’s unequal citizenship.

Mir-Hosseini’s reform strategy is consequently both interpretive and political. Interpretation can show that a rule is human and contestable, but statutory reform requires institutions, political authority, organizing, and women’s participation in legal knowledge-production. Morocco’s 1992–93 family-code reform process illustrates this dependence on political authority: King Hassan II claimed the authority to determine Islam’s authoritative interpretation through ijtihad, established a revision commission in October 1992, and promulgated reforms by royal decree in September 1993 while Parliament was dissolved.[28]

**Mir-Hosseini makes gender-equal family-law reform possible within Islam by relocating unequal rules from immutable divine Shari‘ah to historically situated fiqh.** The consequence is that reform turns on both persuasive interpretation and the distribution of political authority over family law.[19][28]

Her reform method calls for ijtihad, or reasoned interpretation, to relate Qur’anic commitments to justice and dignity to changed social conditions and contemporary equality norms.

Islamic feminist family-law reinterpretation also invokes *maslahah* (public welfare), alongside *ijtihad* (reasoned interpretation), women’s experience, constitutional guarantees, and human rights.[19][113]

### How does Wadud reinterpret Sharia?

Amina Wadud’s approach is Qur’an-centered and explicitly egalitarian. Her work, especially *Qur’an and Woman*, began from research undertaken between 1986 and 1989, first appeared in 1992, and developed alongside her activist work as a Muslim woman.[30][29]

Wadud argues that interpretation is never final. Every reading is shaped by the reader’s language, memory, experience, and social location; interpretation must remain open across times and places if the Qur’an’s wisdom is to be implemented.[30] Her feminist hermeneutics—her method of interpretation—therefore examines gender as a category of thought. It asks how legal and religious discourse constructs the human being, the believer, and women as an othered category.[31]

Wadud’s normative claim is direct: Islamic feminism takes Islam as a framework for social justice, equality, and human dignity in public, private, economic, spiritual, and cultural life. She calls for total equality in family and society and regards patriarchy as contrary to Islam.[26][32]

Wadud’s method differs from Mir-Hosseini’s without contradicting it: Wadud foregrounds a holistic Qur’anic reading and the interpretive categories through which women have been rendered subordinate, whereas Mir-Hosseini’s family-law critique turns on the distinction between divine Shari‘ah and human fiqh. Both locate patriarchy in human interpretive practice rather than revelation itself.[19][31][32]

Wadud also treats women’s religious leadership as an institutional question. On 18 March 2005, Wadud led Friday prayers for a mixed male and female congregation at Sundram Tagore Gallery in New York. The act drew opposition, including Yusuf al-Qardhawi’s description of it as misguided and misleading, as well as support from people responding to women’s verbal and physical discrimination.[78] She has argued that Islamic feminism requires people to participate actively in constructing concepts such as Islam, feminism, and human rights rather than passively receiving fixed definitions.[31]

Her relationship to activism became more explicit with Musawah’s 2009 launch. Wadud says that its formation made it possible for her to adopt the name feminist and gave practical expression to ideas developed through scholarship.[31]

**Wadud’s central contribution is to make equality a Qur’anic interpretive norm: patriarchal readings are not Islam’s inevitable meaning but historically situated constructions open to feminist challenge.** That position supports women’s equal authority in interpretation, religious leadership, and legal-political deliberation.[32][31]

Her approach is feminist tafsir, meaning Qur’anic exegesis: it reads particular verses in their textual and historical context and in relation to the Qur’an’s overall ethical vision, rather than treating isolated passages as timeless rules of male authority.

Wadud’s reading of Qur’an 4:3 holds that, because equal treatment among wives is required, monogamy is preferable where that equality cannot be assured.[32]

In this approach, family and gender relations fall within *mu‘amalat*, human social and contractual affairs, rather than *‘ibadat*, devotional obligations; they are consequently open to *ijtihad* (reasoned interpretation) and *maslahah* (public welfare) as circumstances change.[113]

### How does African feminism critique customary law and universal human

African feminism is not a single theory or legal system. It includes womanism, stiwanism, motherism, femalism, nego-feminism, Pan-African feminism, decolonial feminism, and legal-pluralist approaches. These frameworks are themselves debated for heteronormativity, exclusions, and the difficulty of developing a single theory for societies with diverse histories and cultures.[37][39][38]

Sylvia Tamale links the legal regulation of sexuality to colonial power and contested cultural claims, and identifies women’s political agency as necessary to transforming those arrangements.[39] African customary law consists of varied, largely unwritten rules and practices across thousands of communities.

Colonial states did not merely preserve this law: they selected, recorded, codified, reshaped, and subjected it to European tests of morality and justice.[79][38] Tamale distinguishes this state-created official customary law from living customary law: evolving rules actually observed in communities.[38][37][39]

Tamale also argues that colonial and patriarchal actors reshaped custom around capitalist patriarchy and a heteronormative reproductive legal subject, without implying that precolonial cultures or African religions were uniformly egalitarian.[104]

This distinction matters because official customary law has often preserved gender hierarchy. In *Gumede v President of the Republic of South Africa*, a codified KwaZulu-Natal rule vested ownership and control of family property in the male household head, enabling a husband to deny his former spouse a fair share on divorce.[37][39] In Malawi, the customary rules described allocate estate administration and inheritance to male relatives in both patrilineal and matrilineal systems, though the lineage rules differ.[7]

African feminist critique therefore does not treat custom as either inherently emancipatory or inherently oppressive. It criticizes rules that deny women property, inheritance, bodily integrity, or voice, while rejecting the colonial presumption that imported state law is automatically superior. South African courts’ use of living customary law illustrates a hybrid strategy: customary law is interpreted dynamically through constitutional equality and dignity rather than simply discarded as unconstitutional.[38]

The critique of universal human rights is equally double-sided. Tamale argues that universal rights discourse has Eurocentric, liberal, and colonial histories, often assumes a homogeneous humanity, and can neglect the ways race, class, coloniality, sexuality, and community membership shape women’s lives.[37][39][38] Bond similarly argues that CEDAW, drafted in the 1970s, tends to privilege gender as the principal axis of oppression and insufficiently values women’s cultural and community membership.[20]

In Nigeria, a 2007 effort to domesticate CEDAW failed after opponents portrayed the treaty as neo-imperialist and associated it with abortion and sexual morality.[104]

But African feminists do not simply reject human rights. Constitutional equality, CEDAW, the African Charter, and the Maputo Protocol provide tools to challenge discriminatory family law and harmful practices. The Maputo Protocol is especially significant because it combines substantive rights with procedural rights intended to give women a voice in reforming cultural practices and customary law.[20]

This context-sensitive approach rejects both extremes. Cultural relativism can be manipulated by powerful actors to preserve inequality in the name of culture. A purely universalist approach can treat culture only as backwardness and disregard women’s agency within their communities.[37][39][20] The alternative is localized implementation of equality norms, meaningful participation by rural women and other subordinated groups, and litigation as a safeguard where local deliberation produces discriminatory outcomes.[20]

**African feminist legal theory treats gender justice as a problem of colonial legal pluralism: neither official customary law nor universal rights discourse is sufficient unless women can reshape the institutions and norms governing their lives.** This approach makes constitutionalism, living custom, regional rights, grassroots organizing, and practical access to justice mutually necessary rather than competing strategies.[38][20][7]

South Africa’s 1996 Constitution supplies a concrete constitutional model for this African feminist strategy of neither immunizing nor simply abolishing customary law. Section 2 makes the Constitution supreme and invalidates inconsistent law or conduct; section 8 applies the Bill of Rights to all law and binds state institutions, and can bind natural or juristic persons where the right and duty apply.[80] Sections 15(3) and 31 recognize marriages, personal-law systems, culture, and religion under traditions, but make their recognition and exercise consistent with the Bill of Rights.[80][81] Courts, tribunals, and forums must promote the Bill of Rights’ spirit, purport, and objects when developing customary law, while existing traditional-leader courts continued subject to amendment, repeal, and constitutional consistency.[80] The Constitution also requires courts interpreting the Bill of Rights to consider international law and permits consideration of foreign law.[80] This legal architecture institutionalizes legal pluralism under constitutional supremacy rather than treating customary authority or universal rights as self-sufficient.[80]

The Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa (Maputo Protocol) marks a further regional development. The African Union adopted it in July 2003, and it entered into force on 25 November 2005 after 15 ratifications.[82][83] It responded to the African Charter’s inadequate and nonspecific protection of women: the Charter had not defined discrimination against women, guaranteed consent to or equality in marriage, or resolved the tension created by its emphasis on traditional values and practices.[82][83] The Protocol combines regional and international human-rights instruments with African practices consistent with international human-rights norms, rather than counterposing African legal authority to international law.[84]

The Maputo Protocol presents regional rights as compatible with African practices consistent with human rights: it preserves positive cultural values while requiring the elimination of harmful practices.[84][40]

Celestine Nyamu-Musembi’s work on women’s property rights shows that local norms can operate as fences that obstruct women’s claims, but also as pathways through which women negotiate, legitimate, and assert rights.

Fareda Banda examines the encounter between African women’s legal claims, customary law, and international human-rights norms. Her work treats neither custom nor international law as automatically decisive, focusing instead on how women can use and reshape overlapping legal orders.

The African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights (1981), alongside CEDAW and the 2003 Maputo Protocol, supplies rights instruments that African feminist advocates can use without treating them as culturally or politically self-executing.

### How do Chinese feminist legal scholars navigate state socialism and

Chinese feminist legal scholarship is shaped by the changing relationship between women, law, and a party-state that has presented gender equality as a state objective while retaining control over the institutions through which it is pursued. It should not be reduced either to state propaganda or to independent liberal-rights advocacy.

Wang Ruqi is an early example of state-legal feminism: the use of socialist state law as a means of women’s emancipation. A legal specialist and political activist in the 1930s and 1940s, she wrote the first draft of the 1950 Marriage Law.[49] Her thinking shifted after engagement with Soviet law.

She moved from an anarchist-influenced expectation that marriage, family, and the state would disappear after revolution to a belief that codified law and a socialist state could protect and liberate women.[49]

The socialist model combined genuine openings with instrumental mobilization. The All-China Women’s Democratic Federation, later the All-China Women’s Federation (ACWF), was founded in April 1949 with Chinese Communist Party endorsement to unite women’s organizations and lead women’s liberation.[50] By 1953, more than 40,000 Women’s Federation officials at or above district level were on the state payroll.[50] This embedded women’s political organization in state-building, but it did not make women’s issues independent of Party priorities.

The same tension appears in labor and political participation. State feminism mobilized women into production and politics, but the documented rationale was rapid industrialization and labor need rather than gender equality as an independent end. In rural Party branches, women or deputy women Party secretaries appeared in 11% of branches in 1975; average recruitment of women into rural Party branches fell from 13.5% in 1966–76 to 8.6% in the following five years.[52] During the Cultural Revolution, the ACWF was shut down and absorbed into the Party framework; women’s problems were treated as part of class struggle rather than distinct political questions.[52]

Marketization altered the terrain. Economic reform from 1978 first decollectivized agriculture and opened China to foreign investment in the late 1970s and early 1980s, then privatized state-owned industry in the following decade. The resulting experience for rural women was double-edged: greater production freedom alongside intensified household responsibility.[52] National survey figures cited for 1990–2000 show women’s employment falling from 76.3% to 63.7%, compared with a decline from 90% to 81.5% for men; women made up 56% of those laid off despite being a minority of the workforce.[54]

Li Sipan’s feminist critique rejects a pure free-market solution. She argues that women cannot obtain equal opportunity from an unregulated market because they bear most unpaid reproductive labor and face intersecting inequalities of class, gender, ethnicity, and cultural identity.[48] This resembles Fineman’s attention to care and institutional distribution, but the Chinese context is distinguished by the transition from state-organized social provision to market-oriented employment and by the political limits on independent organizing.

Post-1995 legal advocacy shows how Chinese feminists have navigated those constraints. Guo Jianmei attended the women lawyers’ forum at the Fourth World Conference on Women in September 1995. Three months later, she founded the institution described as China’s first public-interest civil organization specializing in women’s legal aid and research, later associated with the Peking University Women’s Legal Research and Services Center and Beijing Qianqian Law Firm.[21] Over more than twenty years, she handled women’s-rights legal-aid and public-interest litigation and researched women’s-rights protection and legal-aid systems.[21]

Chinese feminist legal work therefore uses negotiated institutional channels: Women’s Federation networks, public-interest lawyering, university centers, legislation, courts, and international review. Feminist organizations have used CEDAW’s review process—China signed CEDAW in 1980—to work with the official Women’s Federation system and promote women’s rights.[48] In 2014, Guo’s Centre and partner organizations submitted reports before China’s CEDAW periodic review.[58]

Since the 1995 Beijing Fourth World Conference on Women, Chinese NGOs and advocates have gained routes to participate in women’s-rights work while remaining subject to institutional dependence and restrictions on autonomous organization.[110]

Legal reform supplied tools but also exposed implementation limits. The Employment Promotion Law was promulgated on 30 August 2007 and took effect on 1 January 2008; it expanded protections and gave employment-discrimination victims access to courts.[54] Yet Chinese plaintiffs had to produce relevant evidence themselves, unlike systems that shift some burden to employers, and courts were described as less active than U.S. courts in defining discrimination or creating remedial mechanisms.[54]

Xia Yinlan’s 2005 article on a gender analysis of Chinese marital community property and the value of housework shows how feminist legal scholarship brought unpaid domestic labor into property-law analysis.[56] This is particularly important in a marketizing society where formal ownership and wage income can obscure women’s household contributions.

**Chinese feminist legal scholarship navigates state socialism and marketization by using state-created legal channels while criticizing both state subordination of women’s claims to official priorities and market structures that reproduce gendered dependency.** Its core strategic problem is how to secure substantive equality through institutions that are necessary for reform but politically constrained.[49][52][48]

Xia Yinlan’s family-law scholarship engages Chinese state legislation and legal institutions as potential instruments for addressing women’s unequal economic position within the household, including the legal undervaluation of unpaid domestic work.

Historian Wang Zheng’s account of state feminism in *Finding Women in the State: A Socialist Feminist Revolution in the People’s Republic of China, 1949–1964* (2017) shows women as political actors within, not merely passive objects of, state institutions, while also showing how those institutions bounded feminist agendas.

The 1950 Marriage Law attacked arranged and forced marriage and made divorce legally available; Article 48 of the Constitution provides that women enjoy equal rights with men in all spheres; and the Law on the Protection of Women’s Rights and Interests, first adopted in 1992, created a specialized equality framework. Marketization also converted housing and other assets into market property, making title registration, marital-property rules, household registration, and bargaining power within marriage central to women’s economic security. The Anti-Domestic Violence Law, effective in 2016, illustrates the subsequent turn toward more specific legal remedies whose effectiveness depends on enforcement.

### Synthesis

The four traditions agree that formal equality alone does not end women’s subordination, but they identify different sites of legal power. Anglo-American critical feminism contests dominance and privatized dependency in liberal institutions; Islamic feminism contests patriarchal interpretive authority; African feminism confronts colonial legal pluralism and the limits of both official custom and universalism; Chinese scholarship addresses equality through a state that has combined socialist commitments with market restructuring.[10][5][19][37][39][48]

The comparative lesson is not to select one universal feminist legal method. It is to ask, in each setting, which legal authority governs women’s lives, who controls its interpretation, and whether women have material and political capacity to change it.

Across the four traditions, the most important difference is the location of authoritative legal change: MacKinnon and Fineman contest liberal state law’s models of equality, autonomy, sexuality, and care; Islamic feminists contest patriarchal jurisprudence and modern family-law codification through Qur’anic and juristic reinterpretation; African feminists work across living custom, state law, and regional and international rights; and Chinese feminists debate socialist, developmental, market, and state-linked institutional legacies.[85][86][87][88][89][90]

### How do Anglo-American legal systems structure women’s property rights

The Anglo-American legal history begins with *coverture*: the common-law doctrine under which a married woman’s legal personality was absorbed into that of her husband. In the British and American common-law family, the husband was the household head, owed obedience, entitled to his wife’s earnings and property, and responsible for family support. Married women could not independently own property or make contracts.[8] Coverture also travelled through empire. Sylvia Tamale notes that it was imported into colonial Uganda and denied women legal, political, and economic rights.[38]

Nineteenth-century reforms did not unfold evenly. England’s Married Women’s Property legislation of the 1870s and 1880s was early landmark legislation for women, while the UK’s 1882 Married Women’s Property Act enhanced women’s economic power. Canadian courts and legislators nevertheless resisted following English precedents in order to preserve a patriarchal family hierarchy.[93][91] **The central change was from a wife’s legal dependence on her husband to formal legal personality and property capacity, but the pace and content of that change varied within common-law jurisdictions.**

In the United States, later equality law attacked overt sex classifications. *Reed v. Reed* held unconstitutional a statute preferring men over women as estate administrators. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 and Regulation B prohibit sex and marital-status discrimination in credit, including by generally preventing creditors from demanding a spouse’s signature when an applicant independently meets creditworthiness standards.[3][100] Credit law thus matters to property rights not because it creates ownership, but because it determines whether women can acquire housing, businesses, or other assets without male financial sponsorship.

Family-property law also moved toward marital partnership. The Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act instructed courts to consider each spouse’s contribution to marital property, expressly including homemaking. Fineman describes marital property as presumptively jointly and equally shared despite unequal direct monetary contributions.[8][5] Yet this formal partnership model does not eliminate inequality at divorce.

In England and Wales, sections 23–24 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 authorize maintenance, lump-sum, property-transfer or sale, and pension orders. Section 25 directs courts to consider all the circumstances, giving first consideration to the welfare of children under 18.[114][115]

In England and Wales, case law treats homemaking as equivalent to breadwinning and ordinarily shares matrimonial property equally, while distinguishing premarital property, gifts, and inheritances as non-matrimonial. Because many households have no surplus assets, financial claims are often resolved according to need.[114][115]

Florida’s marital-property law starts from equal distribution but permits adjustments for homemaking, childcare, career interruption, and one spouse’s support for the other’s education or career.[116]

Fineman argues that women’s primary care work reduces earnings, job prospects, and bargaining power; she therefore proposed allocating more marital property, especially the house, and ongoing support to the post-divorce caretaker-child unit.[5]

In England and Wales, cohabitants do not receive divorce-style maintenance or pension remedies or automatic intestate inheritance; a claimant to a solely titled home must instead establish contributions or a common intention under trust law.[115]

Care and career breaks reduce women’s pension accumulation, and pensions are often overlooked in financial-remedy proceedings.[115]

Judgment — **Fineman’s point is not that equal division is always wrong, but that equality at the instant of divorce can be substantively unequal when one spouse has absorbed the lasting economic costs of care.** The legal question becomes whether property division recognizes unpaid work as wealth-producing labor rather than treating market income as the sole contribution. This is the “care-cost problem”: ostensibly gender-neutral family law can preserve gendered dependency when the labor market and welfare state leave care privately assigned.[8]

Bodily autonomy has likewise been structured through a tension between privacy, equality, and criminal law. In liberal feminist jurisprudence, *decisional privacy* means legal autonomy over sexuality, marriage, and family formation. Anita Allen’s account recognizes that privacy historically confined women, but argues that reconstructed privacy can protect women’s capacity to participate as equals.[1] *Griswold v.

Connecticut* and *Roe v. Wade* were understood by Robin West as having given women considerable power over reproductive life, while no-fault divorce made exit from oppressive marriages more feasible.[2]

MacKinnon’s dominance-feminist critique is sharply different. She argues that privacy may obscure domestic and sexual violence by placing it in a supposedly private sphere, and that sexuality has usually been regulated through criminal or tort law rather than equality law.[2][101][8] Her account treats sexual harassment as sex discrimination because unwanted sexual demands operate within unequal power relationships: workplace authority is used to secure sexual access or punish resistance.[11] That analysis helped establish sexual harassment as a legally cognizable form of sex discrimination.[12][13]

The privacy framework also proved legally fragile. On 24 June 2022, the U.S. Supreme Court overturned *Roe v. Wade*, removing the federal constitutional right to seek abortion and leaving states to determine abortion’s legal parameters. The result is a geographically fragmented system, including states with abortion prohibitions that lack rape exceptions or require a report to law enforcement before a rape exception can be used.[102][6] Reproductive autonomy therefore depends not simply on the abstract existence of a right, but on the jurisdiction, income, mobility, health-care access, and willingness of state institutions to recognize a claimant’s account of rape or medical need.[102]

Rape and domestic-violence law show a related problem. Feminist legal analysis challenged marital exemptions, expectations of physical resistance, reliance on sexual history, and skepticism toward delayed reporting. Although many evidentiary rules have formally changed, they can continue to affect investigation, prosecution, and credibility assessment.[6] Feminists divide over remedy: one approach demands stronger criminal enforcement plus shelters and services; another warns that policing and incarceration can escalate violence, reproduce racialized state control, and harm the people feminism seeks to protect.[2][6] **Anglo-American bodily autonomy is thus not secured by either privacy or criminalization alone; each can protect women in one setting while leaving hierarchy or state coercion intact in another.**

Political participation followed a parallel path from formal exclusion to incomplete inclusion. U.S. women obtained federal voting rights when the Nineteenth Amendment was ratified on 26 August 1920. Yet formal enfranchisement did not guarantee equal access: Black, Native American, and other minority women continued to encounter local obstacles including Jim Crow practices.[93] In England, women first received a restricted vote in 1917, limited to women over 30, while women gained the franchise on equal terms only in 1928.[93]

MacKinnon’s analysis adds that exclusion affects the content of law: people with power write constitutions and legislation, and women were excluded from political systems they did not design.[101] In the United States, women comprised 19.3% of Representatives and 21% of Senators in the 2017 figures cited by the source.[10] Legal equality guarantees voting, candidacy, and non-discrimination, but they do not themselves redistribute party resources, care time, wealth, safety, or access to leadership networks. The resulting lesson is that political equality cannot be measured by suffrage alone.

Anglo-American systems also differ internally. MacKinnon praised Canadian constitutional substantive equality—equality attentive to actual hierarchy and effects—as an improvement on U.S. equality law. In the United States, sex classifications receive heightened rather than strict constitutional scrutiny, while statutory law does much of the work of prohibiting sex discrimination in employment, education, pay, and federally funded health care.[10][6] The distinction matters because comparator-based rules ask whether a woman was treated like a similarly situated man, whereas structural inequality may arise where there is no male comparator—for example, in low-paid, women-dominated occupations.[4]

**What follows is that Anglo-American legal feminism has won major rights against explicit exclusion, but its central unresolved issue is whether formal equal treatment can remedy inequalities produced by care, violence, market dependence, and historically male-designed institutions.**[1][5]

Abortion law also demonstrates why Anglo-American systems cannot be treated as one jurisdiction: Canada’s *R. v. Morgentaler* (1988) has a different constitutional and criminal-law history from the United States, while Great Britain principally regulates abortion through the Abortion Act 1967 and its conditions. Access consequently depends not only on formal rights but also on clinic availability, cost, immigration status, disability, race, police practices, and fear of retaliation.

### How do legal systems addressed by Islamic feminism structure women’s

Islamic feminism is not the law of a single “Islamic legal system.” It is an interpretive and political project that works within Muslim legal traditions while contesting patriarchal readings of them.Judgment — **A basic distinction is between *Shariʿa*, understood by believers as the divine path, and *fiqh*, human jurisprudence: state family codes are enacted selections among juristic interpretations, not Shariʿa itself in an unmediated form.** This distinction explains why Islamic feminists argue that gender hierarchy in existing family law is historically made and therefore capable of reinterpretation.

The label itself is contested. Ziba Mir-Hosseini explains that a broad definition of feminism can include efforts toward a more equitable gender system, while a narrower definition requiring complete gender equality may exclude some actors. Some Muslim women embrace the term Islamic feminism; others reject it because of its association with Western feminism, even though scholars use it analytically.[23] Amina Wadud describes Islamic feminism as requiring coherence among human rights, Islam, and feminism, with women participating in the construction and challenge of those concepts rather than passively receiving fixed definitions.[31]

Property rights are commonly organized through marriage, maintenance, divorce, and inheritance. Morocco illustrates how state-building can transform plural legal orders into a national family code. After independence in 1956, Morocco abolished Berber customary law, appointed a commission of ten male Maliki jurists and judges, and codified the Personal Status Code in 1957 by royal decree without parliamentary debate. The Code remained faithful to traditional Maliki *fiqh*.[28] This history matters because “Islamic law” in practice may be royal legislation and national codification, not merely religious doctrine.

Morocco’s 2004 *Moudawana* defaults to separate estates but permits spouses to make a separate written agreement about property acquired during marriage. Without an agreement, courts apply general evidence rules and consider each spouse’s work, effort, and burdens in developing family assets.[117][118]

Morocco’s separate-estate default can disadvantage wives whose unpaid work helps create assets titled to their husbands, particularly when they lack the information or bargaining power to negotiate a property agreement.[119][118]

Tunisia illustrates a different trajectory. Its 1956 Personal Status Code outlawed polygamy, punishable by fine or imprisonment. The reform used Qur’anic reasoning: the Qur’an favors monogamy, and equal treatment of multiple wives was regarded as impossible.[33] By 2006, Tunisian law required both spouses to be 18 and to consent to marriage; an adult woman could contract her own marriage. The 1993 reforms ended a wife’s formal duty to obey her husband, although the husband remained designated head of household.[33]

Inheritance remains a major limit on formal marital equality. In August 2017 Tunisia’s Individual Freedoms and Equality Committee reviewed inheritance law for compatibility with the constitution and international conventions and recommended reform. The November 2018 draft would have allowed families to choose equal inheritance for women or retain the existing rule under which women receive half a man’s share; as of the 2021 account, parliament had not reviewed it.[35] **The legal structure therefore combines reforms in marriage and polygyny with continuing contest over inheritance, showing that women’s property equality cannot be inferred from one progressive family-law rule.**

Under the inheritance rules described in Indonesia’s Compilation of Islamic Laws No. 1 of 1991, a son receives twice a daughter’s share when they inherit together; a widow receives one quarter of the estate if there are no children and one eighth if there are children.[120]

Bodily autonomy is governed through overlapping fields: consent to marriage, sexuality, reproductive health, domestic violence, and criminal law. Tunisia legalized abortion in 1965 and lifted the prohibition on Muslim women marrying non-Muslim men in 2018.[34] Turkey’s abortion law has permitted abortion on request since 1983, but the cited account reports that only 7.8% of state hospitals provide it; formal legality and service access diverge.[65] In Malaysia, women’s groups lobbied for years to make domestic violence a crime irrespective of the perpetrator’s ethnicity. Parliament passed the Domestic Violence Act in 1994, and it entered into force in 1996 after two more years of advocacy.[23]

Mir-Hosseini’s work demonstrates why court practice must be studied alongside code text. Her fieldwork in Tehran from 1985 to 1988 and Morocco from September 1988 to December 1989 included hearings, court records, and interviews with judges, lawyers, notaries, and women litigants.[27] A reviewer reports that she found men in Morocco and Iran could divorce without legally providing grounds, while a Tehran 1987 table recorded stated reasons in 54 of 66 marital-dispute cases; stated reasons and legally required grounds are not the same thing.[27] The example shows that formal doctrinal entitlement, procedural practice, and litigants’ narratives may diverge.

Mir-Hosseini found that Moroccan men could divorce without going to court, whereas Iranian divorce registration required judicial permission for either spouse. In one Iranian case, an abused woman’s divorce claim failed after more than three years despite medical evidence and family testimony.[27]

Political participation is both a subject of rights and a route to legal reform. Wadud’s leadership of Friday prayers for a mixed congregation of about 100 people in Manhattan in 2005 directly challenged restrictions on women’s religious authority and prompted international criticism.[32] Morocco’s 1992–93 revision process also shows the role of state religious authority: King Hassan II asserted authority to determine the authoritative interpretation of Islam through *ijtihad*, appointed a commission with one woman among 21 members, and promulgated reforms by royal decree while Parliament was dissolved.[28]

Judgment — **Islamic feminist legal work treats women’s participation in interpretation as legally consequential because authority over interpretation determines which family-law rules are recognized as Islamic.** This differs from a reform strategy that simply opposes religion to secular rights. It seeks egalitarian readings within Qur’anic and Islamic legal reasoning while also contesting who has institutional power to declare a reading authoritative. The result can be legislative change, but its scope depends on courts, parliaments, monarchies, religious institutions, and organized women’s movements.

Classical Qur’anic rules recognize women’s independent proprietary capacity, including claims to *mahr* (dower), inheritance, and ownership. In practice, family pressure, land-registration arrangements, marital power, and unequal resources can prevent women from controlling these assets, even where the formal entitlement exists. Wadud’s Qur’anic hermeneutics insists on women’s equal moral agency and rejects treating particular verses as blanket authorization for male control.

Under the Moroccan family-law provisions described in the cited sources, a wife owns her *mahr*, or marital dower, exclusively, and deferred *mahr* is a debt owed by her husband.[119][118]

Indonesia’s Compilation of Islamic Laws No. 1 of 1991 treats *mahar* as a gift to the bride and property acquired during marriage as joint property regardless of registration; it is ordinarily divided equally at divorce unless a contract provides otherwise.[120][121][122]

### How do African contexts of customary law and human rights

African feminist legal analysis begins from *legal pluralism*: the coexistence of state law, customary law, religious institutions, local forums, and regional or international human-rights norms. It therefore rejects the idea that there is one African customary law. Uganda has more than 65 ethnic groups and cultural institutions that adjudicate disputes alongside religious courts, local council courts, and state courts; a cited 2025 report states that more than 90% of Ugandans, including women, choose cultural institutions for dispute resolution.[97]

Colonialism shaped this pluralism rather than merely preserving precolonial custom. In Uganda, British colonial rule separated commerce, land, and international relations into a public sphere while placing family law into “private law.” Colonial authorities also enacted sexuality-regulating laws—the Dangerous Diseases Ordinance of 1909, Townships Rules of 1913, and Penal Code of 1930—modelled on English Contagious Diseases Acts.[39][37][38] **African feminist critique therefore treats both customary law and the state/private divide as historically constructed, including through colonial power, rather than as timeless cultural facts.**

Property rights are a crucial site of conflict. Customary systems may make kinship, marriage, residence, and bridewealth relevant to land and succession. At the same time, customary authority cannot be assumed to be informal or peripheral. In Malawi, customary-law civil cases can reach subordinate courts, the High Court, and the Supreme Court of Appeal, while magistrates formerly serving in Traditional Courts became part of the formal judiciary after 1994.[7] Traditional justice systems reportedly handle 80–90% of total caseloads in some African states.[42]

Kenya’s Matrimonial Property Act 2013 gives married women and men equal capacity to acquire, administer, hold, control, use, and dispose of property. It counts domestic work, childcare, companionship, management of a family enterprise, and farm work as contributions, but vests matrimonial property according to contribution, excludes trust property including customary holdings, and permits Islamic law to govern a Muslim’s matrimonial property.[123]

Kenyan land law recognizes spousal interests, joint tenancy, labour-based interests in land titled to one spouse, and consent requirements for disposing of a dwelling.[124]

Rights-based litigation has sometimes displaced male-preference rules. Botswana’s High Court ruled in October 2012 that the Ngwaketse custom transferring a family home only to the youngest son was unconstitutional.[43] In South Africa, the Constitutional Court declared section 23 of the Black Administration Act 38 of 1927 and Regulation R200 unconstitutional on 15 October 2004; estates thereafter fell under the Administration of Estates Act and intestate succession under the Intestate Succession Act.[44] These decisions are important because inheritance is not merely a post-death distribution issue: it affects a widow’s or daughter’s capacity to retain a home, maintain children, and leave an abusive relationship.

In *Bhe v Magistrate, Khayelitsha* (2004), South Africa’s Constitutional Court invalidated customary male primogeniture in intestate succession and applied the Intestate Succession Act.[125][44][126]

Justice Ngcobo’s partial dissent in *Bhe* (2004) agreed that male primogeniture was unconstitutional but sought to preserve customary succession’s family-support function while recognizing women as equally capable of fulfilling it.[125][44][126]

Uganda shows the cost of stalled matrimonial-property reform. In 2013, its Supreme Court urged Parliament to enact matrimonial-property legislation based on equal treatment of husbands and wives; Tamale reports that Parliament had not acted to align challenged family laws with the Constitution.[37][39] Ghana’s unified inheritance law of 1985 and Tanzania’s 1971 Law of Marriage Act demonstrate statutory integration strategies, but the Ghana example was described as unevenly implemented, particularly in rural areas.[20] **The practical issue is therefore not whether statutory equality exists in the abstract, but whether women can invoke it effectively in the forum that controls land, marriage, and succession.**

The Maputo Protocol protects women’s independent management of property during marriage, equitable sharing of jointly acquired assets at divorce, access to land and productive resources, equitable inheritance, widows’ interests, and equal parental inheritance.[84][40]

Bodily autonomy is likewise shaped by family law, customary practices, colonial criminal law, and human-rights commitments. Uganda’s divorce regime requires proof of fault, permits defenses including collusion, connivance, and condonation, and imposes a six-month cooling-off period before final decree. Tamale links this to England’s 1857 Divorce Act and adds that customary divorce may require bridewealth repayment, keeping some women in abusive marriages.[38] Domestic-violence law may expand formal access while leaving institutional hierarchy intact: Uganda’s Domestic Violence Act passed on 11 November 2009 and became law in 2010, but Tamale describes it as unenforced a decade later despite extending local council-court jurisdiction.[38]

FGM legislation makes the enforcement point especially clear. A 2018 study reported that, among 55 million girls aged 0–14 who had experienced or were at risk of FGM across 28 countries, 50% were in Egypt, Ethiopia, and Nigeria, all states with anti-FGM laws; 30% were in Chad, Liberia, Mali, Sierra Leone, and Somalia, which lacked such laws.[41] The Gambia’s 2015 amendment prohibited female circumcision, criminalized direct participation and acts of requesting, inciting, or promoting it, and imposed up to three years’ imprisonment, a fine of 50,000 dalasis, or both.[41] Law can define and punish the practice, but legislation alone does not dissolve the social, economic, and institutional conditions sustaining it.

Regional human-rights law supplies a second normative vocabulary. The African Women’s Protocol entered into force on 25 November 2005 after 15 ratifications and was described as building on—and sometimes expanding—CEDAW, especially through its specific prohibition of gender-based violence.[7][20] Kenya’s 2010 Constitution incorporated general international law and ratified treaties into domestic law; when Kenya ratified the Maputo Protocol on 6 October 2010, its provisions became directly justiciable in Kenyan courts.[41] In South Africa’s *Naidoo v.

African feminism nevertheless critiques universal human-rights frameworks as well as custom. International rights may coexist with patriarchal domestic law, prioritize public over private harms, and carry assumptions identified as Western.[6] This does not mean abandoning universal rights.

It means asking whether a treaty norm becomes usable by women dealing with land, family authority, poverty, sexuality, and local adjudicators. The Dakar Declaration states that traditional courts are not exempt from the African Charter’s fair-trial guarantees.[42]

An analysis of advice cases found that women who lacked documentation of otherwise valid customary marriages were denied housing, land, inheritance, and other marital-property rights.[127]

Political participation includes more than parliamentary representation. It includes control of local dispute forums, access to regional institutions, and freedom to organize around sexuality and gender. The African Union, unlike its predecessor the Organization of African Unity, formally committed to gender equality and the gender-parity principle.[37][38] Yet the Coalition of African Lesbians’ observer status at the African Commission, granted in April 2015, was withdrawn in August 2018 under pressure from the AU Executive Council invoking “African values.” Tamale accordingly cautions that counting women’s presence in political spaces can obscure men’s overrepresentation and institutional control.[38]

**The African feminist conclusion is double: discriminatory custom cannot override women’s equality and bodily integrity, but universal rights become effective only when they engage the plural forums and colonial histories through which family, land, and sexuality are actually governed.**[42][41][37][39][38]

### How does the Chinese legal system structure women’s property rights

Chinese women’s rights are structured through a state-socialist tradition of formal equality, family law, collective property, and party-state administration, then reshaped by marketization. Wang Ruqi provides an early example of state-legal feminism: a legal specialist and political activist from the 1930s and 1940s, she wrote the first draft of the 1950 Marriage Law.[49] The 1950 Marriage Law and Land Reform Law were closely connected to socialist restructuring of rural production and the campaign against “feudalism.” In 1937, Wang’s association had already resolved that women should constitute 20% of representatives in local assemblies.[49]

State socialism expanded women’s formal access to employment and social provision. In urban China during the late 1950s and 1960s, more than 90% of married women were employed, female labor-force participation remained high until the early 1990s, and gender discrimination was formally prohibited.[53] The 1951 Labor Insurance Regulations provided female employees 56 days of fully paid maternity leave, 30 days of paid leave for miscarriage, and employer-paid pregnancy and delivery checkups.[53] These measures linked women’s equality to public employment and workplace welfare rather than only to private-family rights.

Marketization destabilized that model. For urban women aged 21–50, labor-force participation fell from 89.4% in 1990 to 63.5% in 2005; the female-to-male earnings ratio fell from 86.3% in 1988 to 76.2% in 2004.[53] Publicly funded kindergartens fell by 2006 to approximately one-third of their 1997 number, while private provision rose from 13.5% to 57.8% of childcare programs.[53] Women constituted 56% of those laid off during the 1990–2000 period despite being a minority of the workforce.[54] **Market reform therefore changed women’s legal and material position not by simply repealing equality, but by shifting care, employment risk, and welfare costs from collective institutions toward households and markets.**

Property rights show both formal equality and continuing vulnerability. Article 1055 of the Civil Code’s Marriage and Family Book provides equal status for spouses in marriage and family relations. Article 1062 classifies wages, bonuses, labor remuneration, investment and business returns, intellectual-property income, and specified inherited or gifted property acquired during marriage as joint property, over which spouses have equal disposal rights.[57] Article 1087 directs courts, where agreement fails, to divide joint property with regard to children, women, and the spouse without fault.[57]

The Code also recognizes care work at divorce. Civil Code article 1088 is the divorce-care-compensation provision: it permits a spouse who has undertaken more childcare, eldercare, or assistance with the other spouse’s work to seek compensation at divorce, and the 2020 Code removed a previous eligibility condition; the parties may agree the amount, or a people’s court decides if they cannot.[57][70] Regulation No.

1088, effective in 2021, also provides economic compensation for greater family-care responsibilities, but leaves the valuation method to agreement or a people’s court.[70] This is a significant recognition of unpaid work, although its open-ended valuation leaves outcomes dependent on bargaining and adjudication.

Rural property is especially important because collective membership can be conditioned by marital status. The 2011–2020 Outline for the Development of Women called for equal rights to land-contracting, homestead use, and collective-income distribution, including rules for distributing land-acquisition compensation within collective economic organizations.[98] It also required protection of women’s democratic rights in village self-government and correction of village rules that discriminate against women.[98] Yet post-Mao rural women’s political participation has been constrained by landlessness and reinforced gender roles; direct village-leader elections began after the 1987 Organic Law of Village Committees but did not remove these barriers.[52]

Bodily autonomy in China is structured through public policy, labor rules, marriage law, and violence prevention rather than a single constitutional privacy doctrine. The Women’s Development Outline calls for informed choice in contraception and reduction of unintended pregnancy and abortion.[98] It also calls for legislation against domestic violence, mechanisms against sexual harassment, vigorous action against rape and trafficking, and prevention of domestic violence.[98] These are state objectives, and their meaning depends on subsequent legislation and enforcement.

Employment law connects bodily autonomy to market discrimination. Article 27 of the Employment Promotion Law states that women have labor rights equal to men’s, prohibits refusal to hire women because of sex or higher hiring standards, and prohibits contractual restrictions on marriage or childbearing. It retains an exception for jobs specified by the state as unsuitable for women.[54] Researchers have criticized that exception as a possible vehicle for stereotyping and occupational segregation.[54] Employers have reportedly asked women applicants about marriage, boyfriends, and childbearing plans, partly because employers bear maternity-leave costs; the cited Labor Law required at least 90 days of maternity leave.[54]

The enforcement structure also matters. The Employment Promotion Law, promulgated on 30 August 2007 and effective on 1 January 2008, gave victims of employment discrimination access to courts.[54] Yet, as of October 2010, Chinese plaintiffs had to produce the relevant evidence themselves; the cited study found no legal mechanism allowing burden shifting in employment-discrimination cases.[54] In a 2006 survey of 3,500 people in ten large Chinese cities, 85.5% said employment discrimination existed, 50.8% regarded it as extremely severe, 54.9% said they had personally experienced some form of hiring discrimination, and 21% identified sex discrimination as common.[54]

Political participation combines formal equality, representation targets, and party-state channels. The 2011–2020 Outline called for gradually increasing women’s representation in people’s congresses and political consultative bodies; it targeted at least 30% women among village committee members, at least 10% women village-committee directors, and around 50% women in residents’ committees.[98] It also sought at least one woman in county-level and higher local-government leadership teams and gradual increases in women holding top leadership positions.[98]

The available 2013 figures show mixed outcomes. Women accounted for 23.4% of deputies to the first session of the Twelfth National People’s Congress, 17.8% of members of the national CPPCC, 22.7% of village committee members, and 48.4% of neighborhood committee members; women headed 41.5% of neighborhood committees.[99] Women also constituted 28.8% of judges, 29.3% of procurators, and 34.2% of people’s jurors in 2013.[99] These are meaningful institutional presences, but they do not measure agenda-setting power, leadership authority, or independence from party-state structures.

Chinese feminist advocacy operates through both official and transnational channels. China signed CEDAW in 1980, and feminist organizations have used the UN review mechanism while cooperating with the official Women’s Federation system.[48] Guo Jianmei worked full-time from 1989 to 1992 on drafting the Law on the Protection of Women’s Rights and Interests, conducted nationwide research, and published on women’s rights protection.[21] **The Chinese system thus supplies formal claims in marriage, land, work, reproductive health, and participation, but women’s ability to realize them depends heavily on state-directed enforcement, collective institutions, labor-market conditions, and access to legal remedies.**

### Synthesis

Across all four contexts, law has moved away from openly declared female dependence: coverture has been dismantled, polygyny restricted in some jurisdictions, discriminatory succession rules invalidated, and marital property or land rights formally equalized. Yet the dominant pattern is not simple progress from exclusion to equality. Formal equality often coexists with unequal care burdens, discretionary courts, local customary authority, religious or party-state control over interpretation, weak enforcement, and market dependence.[5][33][37][39][38][53]

The traditions disagree most clearly about legal authority. Anglo-American feminism disputes whether privacy or criminal law protects women; Islamic feminism contests patriarchal *fiqh* from within Islamic reasoning; African feminism challenges both discriminatory custom and abstract universalism; Chinese feminism navigates equality claims within party-led institutions and market restructuring. Their common lesson is that property, bodily integrity, and political voice must be analyzed together: a right that cannot be used without income, land, institutional access, or freedom from violence is not yet full equality.

Of the 55 million girls aged 0–14 reported as having experienced or being at risk of FGM in the 2018 study, the stated 50% share in Egypt, Ethiopia, and Nigeria corresponds to an estimated 27.5 million girls; the 30% share in the five listed states without anti-FGM laws corresponds to 16.5 million.[41]

In 2013, women’s representation was 25.7 percentage points higher on neighborhood committees (48.4%) than on village committees (22.7%), making the neighborhood figure 2.13 times the village figure. This shows a sharp institutional difference within local-level participation.[99]
