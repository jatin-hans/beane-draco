# Respondeat Superior Liability for Rocha’s Route 7 Collision

## Executive summary

The company is not entitled to summary judgment on vicarious liability on the stated record, and the sedan driver likewise is not entitled to it unless the governing jurisdiction treats the material facts as undisputed in the driver’s favor.

The evidence supports a plausible finding that Rocha was an employee acting within the scope of employment because he was performing delivery work, received a supervisor’s lunch-direction text, served requested warehouse coworkers, was reimbursed for the trip under a policy effective May 1, 2024, and planned to resume deliveries.

The company has a competing, trial-worthy argument that the four-block lunch trip was a prohibited personal detour rather than company business. Rocha’s phone glance supports an inference of negligent inattention, but sudden braking for fallen lumber may support comparative fault or a causation defense, while functioning brake lights and imaging-confirmed soft-tissue injury support the sedan driver’s negligence and damages case.

The deleted email’s metadata can establish that an email existed, not that it approved the lunch run or what its terms were, so the disputed direction, purpose, and scope facts cannot be resolved by choosing one side’s inference on summary judgment.

## Legal framework and disputed liability

Respondeat superior is the doctrine of **vicarious liability**: liability imposed on one person for another’s tort because of their legal relationship, rather than because the first person personally acted carelessly. Here, the sedan driver seeks to attribute any negligent driving by Daniel Rocha to the company. That attribution depends on the legal character of Rocha’s work relationship and on what he was doing when the collision occurred.

Judgment — No governing jurisdiction, forum, or summary-judgment movant is identified. The analysis therefore cannot select one state’s substantive tort rule or procedural rule as controlling. The Restatement formulations and representative state authorities below provide the appropriate common-law framework, while the later sections apply that framework to employee status, scope, employer benefit, and the collision evidence.

### What respondeat superior framework governs whether the company is vicariously

Under the core respondeat superior rule, an employer is vicariously liable for a tort committed by its employee while the employee acts within the scope of employment. The Restatement (Third) of Agency states the rule directly: “An employer is subject to vicarious liability for a tort committed by its employee acting within the scope of employment.” Texas common law uses the same basic structure: the plaintiff must establish that, when the allegedly negligent conduct occurred, the worker was an employee and was acting in the course and scope of employment.[1][2]

The claim also depends on an underlying tort by Rocha. A respondeat superior plaintiff must prove that the employee is liable for negligence and that the employer is liable derivatively under respondeat superior; this differs from a claim that the company itself negligently hired, trained, or supervised the driver.[3] The distinction matters because the company’s own fault is not an element of ordinary vicarious liability.

Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.03(1) separately identifies direct liability when an agent acts with actual authority or the principal ratifies tortious conduct, or conduct that would subject the principal itself to tort liability; when the principal negligently selects, supervises, or otherwise controls the agent; or when an agent fails to perform a delegated duty of care. Those direct-liability grounds are distinct from ordinary respondeat superior.[4][5]

The basic framework therefore has three practical components for this case:

- **Underlying negligence:** Rocha must have committed a tort that caused legally cognizable harm to the sedan driver. A master ordinarily cannot be vicariously liable unless the servant is liable. [6] The separate section, *Driver negligence, causation and injury*, addresses whether the record permits that predicate finding.

- **Employee status:** Rocha must be an employee rather than an independent contractor for ordinary respondeat superior liability. The employee-status issue concerns the company’s control, or right to control, Rocha’s performance. [3] [4]

- **Scope of employment:** The negligent driving must have occurred while Rocha was doing work sufficiently connected to his employment. General employment alone does not create an inference that a particular act was within scope. The inference arises only if the act also tended to accomplish an authorized purpose and occurred at an authorized time and place. [7]

This three-part structure accommodates the client’s requested distinction between employee status, scope of employment, and benefit to the employer. Employee status asks who controlled the worker and the work. Scope asks whether the particular conduct was employment-connected. Employer benefit commonly bears on the scope inquiry—especially the employee’s purpose—but is not universally framed as an autonomous third element.[7][8][2]

#### Employee status is a control inquiry, not a job-title inquiry

The traditional common-law distinction is between a **servant**—now more commonly called an employee—and an independent contractor. Under Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220, a servant is one employed to perform services in another’s affairs who, as to physical conduct in performing those services, is subject to the other’s control or right to control.[9] Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(3) uses substantially the same functional definition: an employee is an agent whose principal controls, or has the right to control, the manner and means of the agent’s work; work performed gratuitously does not relieve the principal of liability.[4][5]

“Control” means more than giving a worker a broad objective. A principal’s control includes an initial statement of what an agent shall and shall not do, plus a right to give interim instructions or directions after the relationship begins.[8] A company need not control every moment of the worker’s time or every aspect of professional judgment for an agency relationship to exist.[8] The question is whether the company retained sufficient authority over how the worker performs the relevant work.

Restatement (Second) § 220 identifies ten nonexclusive facts relevant to that question:

1. the extent of the company’s control over work details;
2. whether the worker operates a distinct occupation or business;
3. whether the occupation is ordinarily employer-directed or independently performed;
4. the skill required;
5. who provides tools, instrumentalities, and the workplace;
6. the duration of the relationship;
7. whether payment is by time or by job;
8. whether the work forms part of the company’s regular business;
9. the parties’ understanding of their relationship; and
10. whether the alleged principal is itself in business. [9]

The factors are considered together. The Restatement says the master-servant relationship “is one not capable of exact definition,” and the factfinder ordinarily decides the question where the combined factors do not clearly point in one direction.[9] Washington Pattern Jury Instruction—Civil 50.11.01 directs the decision-maker, when distinguishing an agent from an independent contractor, to determine whether the principal controlled or had the right to control the details of the worker’s performance; Washington authority also treats control as crucial among factors of varying importance.[10] Colorado’s instruction identifies the right to control the details of performance as central, while also directing attention to selection, payment, and the right to dismiss.[10][11]

A delivery driver’s title is therefore suggestive but not dispositive. A worker called a delivery driver may be an employee if the company controls or has the right to control the driver’s manner and means of work; conversely, a worker who contracts to deliver results while retaining control over physical performance may be an independent contractor.[9] The facts relevant to Rocha’s relationship—including route assignment, company policy, supervision, equipment, compensation, and the company’s ability to direct the lunch run—belong in *Employee status*.[12]

The employee-status inquiry cannot be collapsed into scope of employment. A person may be the company’s employee generally but act outside the scope of employment on a particular occasion; equally, a worker’s service for a company does not establish employee status merely because the company benefits from performance.[7] **Thus, employee status determines whether respondeat superior is available at all; it does not decide whether Rocha’s June 14 driving was within the employment relationship.**[8]

#### Scope of employment has competing but related formulations

**Scope of employment** means the field of activity within which an employee acts as a servant of the employer. The Second Restatement defines employment as the subject matter of the master-servant relationship and scope as the extent of that subject matter.[7] The central question is not simply whether Rocha was on a shift or in a vehicle, but whether the driving that caused the collision was sufficiently connected to work that the company must answer for it.

Under Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228, conduct is within scope only if it:

1. is of the kind the employee was hired to perform;
2. occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits;
3. is actuated, at least in part, by a purpose to serve the employer; and
4. if intentional force is used, involves force not unexpectable by the employer. [7]

The intentional-force condition does not materially frame a negligence-based rear-end collision. The first three conditions do. In ordinary words, the court asks whether the employee was doing a type of work connected to the job, doing it at a time and place close enough to authorized work, and acting at least partly to serve the employer.[13][14]

The § 228 formulation is conjunctive: courts applying it have treated all three relevant conditions as necessary to a scope finding.[15] The Restatement also identifies circumstances pointing outside scope: conduct different in kind from that authorized, far beyond authorized time or space limits, or too little motivated by a purpose to serve the employer.[7] The presence of one countervailing circumstance does not mechanically control a close case, however.

When a servant acts close to an authorized time or place, or performs an act similar to authorized conduct, the Restatement directs consideration of all facts.[7]

A related § 229 inquiry asks whether the conduct is of the same general nature as authorized conduct or incidental to it. “Incidental” does not mean merely convenient or contemporaneous; it must be subordinate to or pertinent to an act the employee was hired to perform.[6] That distinction is significant for a lunch-related trip because ordinary personal activity may coexist with an employment purpose without necessarily becoming a job task.[6]

The more recent Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 frames scope differently. An employee acts within scope when performing work assigned by the employer or engaging in a course of conduct subject to the employer’s control. Conduct falls outside scope when it occurs in an independent course of conduct not intended to serve any employer purpose.[5] This approach places less formal emphasis on geographic and temporal limits and more emphasis on assignment, control, and an independent course of conduct.[16]

The two formulations overlap in important ways. Both ask whether the employee’s conduct was employment-connected rather than solely personal. Both make employer direction and the employee’s purpose relevant. The Second Restatement expressly asks whether the conduct occurred substantially within authorized time and space, while the reported Third Restatement rule does not include that factor as a separate condition.[13] The difference can matter where, as here, the claimed employment connection is an errand that deviated from an assigned route.[13][17]

The substantive law remains unsettled until a jurisdiction is selected: courts do not uniformly apply the Restatement (Second), the Restatement (Third), or a jurisdiction-specific formulation. The Restatements are influential and persuasive sources rather than statutes binding every court.[4] Courts in Pennsylvania, Maine, and the District of Columbia, for example, have been described as looking to the Second Restatement, while Washington’s pattern-instruction commentary says the Third Restatement had not been adopted in a published Washington decision.[18][19][15][10]

Representative state formulations show both the overlap and the variation. Texas asks objectively whether the employee was performing tasks generally assigned in furtherance of the employer’s business, with employer authority and for employer benefit.[2] Colorado defines scope to include work assigned by the employer, work proper, usual, and necessary to complete assigned work, and work customary in the trade or business for that purpose.[20][11] Colorado further states that, for a negligent act or omission, the question is whether it furthered the employer’s business, not whether the exact manner of performance was authorized.[11]

A rule violation does not necessarily end the inquiry. Restatement (Second) § 230 provides that an act may remain within scope even if forbidden or carried out in a forbidden manner.[6] Conduct may also be an improper method of pursuing an employment objective while still being sufficiently connected to authorized work.[21] That principle does not turn every policy violation into an employment act. It means the company’s prohibition on personal detours is evidence bearing on authorization and purpose, not an automatic legal answer to scope.

The converse is also true: an employer’s general authorization of work does not make every employee activity employment-connected. The scope inquiry excludes an independent course of conduct with no intended employer purpose under the Third Restatement formulation.[5] Under the Second Restatement, an act different in kind, far beyond authorized limits, or too little motivated by employer service may fall outside scope.[7] **Accordingly, the controlling scope question is whether Rocha’s driving at impact was connected enough to assigned or controlled company work—not merely whether Rocha ordinarily worked as a delivery driver.**

#### Detour, frolic, and return to work are analytical tools, not separate elements

Courts often describe route deviations through the **detour/frolic distinction**. A detour is a limited departure from assigned work that remains within the general scope of employment. A frolic is a substantial abandonment of the employer’s business for a personal objective, placing the employee outside scope during the departure.[22] These are labels for applying the governing scope test, not independent legal elements.[23]

The relevant considerations include the employee’s intent; the nature, time, and place of the conduct; the work the employee was hired to do; acts the employer could reasonably expect; the employee’s freedom in performing duties; and the time spent on personal activity.[22] The Restatement’s place-and-time element fits within that broader fact-sensitive assessment.

A four-block deviation, a midday errand, a supervisor’s instruction, company policy, and the intended resumption of deliveries can each affect whether the activity is seen as a minor employment-connected detour or an independent personal frolic. Those facts are assessed in *Scope of employment*.

Mixed purpose does not itself defeat scope. Restatement commentary states that an agent’s predominant self-interested motive does not prevent scope if the act is otherwise within authority.[15] Similarly, the detour authority recognizes that an employee pursuing a business errand and a personal objective at the same time may still act within scope.[22] A useful, though not universal, way to assess a mixed-purpose trip is whether the trip would have been made for the business even without the personal reason.[23]

Judgment — That mixed-purpose inquiry should be used cautiously. It is a practical evidentiary test for purpose and authorization, not a replacement for the jurisdiction’s governing doctrine. It cannot resolve the case without deciding whether the supervisor actually directed the lunch run, whether the company’s updated policy covered it, and whether Rocha had resumed company business when the collision occurred.

A personal deviation may also end before an accident. Under the **re-entry doctrine**, employer responsibility may resume once the employee genuinely returns to the employer’s business.[22] The example of *Riley v.

Standard Oil Co.* involved an employee who completed a personal side errand, turned back toward the employer’s destination in the employer’s truck carrying the employer’s goods, and then collided with a pedestrian; the reported holding treated the resumed work trip as within scope.[22] The factual issue is not merely whether the driver traveled in the company’s general direction, but whether the personal purpose had concluded and a work purpose had actually resumed.[22]

The detour/frolic framework does not decide the supplied facts in this section. It identifies why the direction, policy, route, purpose, reimbursement, and intended return to deliveries are outcome-relevant evidence. **It makes the later scope analysis turn on the character of the lunch-related driving at the collision, rather than on any single fact such as the off-route location.**

#### Employer benefit is important but not identical to actual profit

The requested employer-benefit factor is closely related to scope, but it must be stated precisely. Under the Second Restatement test, conduct need only be motivated “at least in part” by a purpose to serve the employer.[7] Texas likewise requires action for the employer’s benefit and with its authority when determining course and scope.[2] Colorado’s formulation links respondeat superior to conduct motivated by an intent to serve the employer’s interests and connected to authorized acts.[11]

These formulations make purpose and business furtherance central. They do not require proof that the company realized an actual financial gain from the particular trip. The Third Restatement’s general agency definition expressly says actions on a principal’s behalf do not necessarily entail that the principal will benefit.[8] Likewise, gratuitous performance of work does not itself relieve a principal of liability under the Third Restatement’s employee rule.[1][5]

The distinction is consequential. **Purpose to serve** concerns what the employee was trying to accomplish; **actual benefit** concerns whether the company ultimately gained. A supervisor-directed task can be on the employer’s behalf even if it proves inefficient or produces no measurable gain. Conversely, a task may incidentally help coworkers or workplace morale without necessarily becoming employer business under a jurisdiction that requires authorization, employer purpose, or a close connection to assigned work.

Employer benefit also helps explain the policy behind vicarious liability. Common-law commentary allocates the risk of employee torts to an enterprise that seeks profit from employee activity and can absorb or distribute accident costs through prices or insurance.[4] That rationale supports the doctrine, but it does not establish that every activity producing some workplace benefit falls within scope.[4]

The authorities therefore differ in phrasing. Some make employer benefit express in the scope test. Others focus on assigned work, employer control, and whether the employee intended to serve any employer purpose.[2] The later section, *Benefit to employer*, evaluates the competing inferences from the supervisor’s text, coworker requests, policy change, and reimbursement.[11][5] **For the governing framework, benefit is a substantial scope consideration and a possible element under some state formulations, but actual employer gain is not a universal prerequisite to vicarious liability.**

Additional formulations underscore why the governing jurisdiction can affect the result. Cornell’s Legal Information Institute states that states create their own respondeat-superior standards and identifies a Benefits Test and a Characteristics Test: the former links employer permission and a conceivable employer benefit to certain employer-premises recreational activity, while the latter asks whether conduct is sufficiently common to the job to be characteristic of it.[24] California authority described in a 1990 law-review comment instead permits scope to rest either on conduct required by or incidental to duties, or on misconduct reasonably foreseeable in light of the employee’s duties; that respondeat-superior foreseeability inquiry asks whether the conduct is so startling that employer responsibility would be unfair, not whether ordinary negligence-law foreseeability exists.[24][25]

A **tort** is a civil wrong that causes another person loss or harm and results in legal liability for the person who commits it.[19]

### What summary judgment standard applies to the contested liability claim?

Summary judgment is a procedure for deciding a claim without trial only when the evidentiary record leaves no genuine dispute over facts that matter to the governing law. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a), the court must grant summary judgment if the movant shows both “no genuine dispute as to any material fact” and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law.[26] The same two-part formulation appears in representative state rules, including Tennessee Rule 56.04 and Indiana Trial Rule 56(C).[27][28]

Because the forum is unidentified, Federal Rule 56 is an analytical model rather than a declared controlling procedure. It is nevertheless useful because it expresses the widely familiar distinction between legal questions a court can decide and factual disputes a jury must resolve. Whether a particular state’s procedure uses the same wording, burden allocation, or no-evidence mechanism depends on the forum. Texas, for example, distinguishes traditional and no-evidence summary-judgment motions.[2]

A fact is **material** if it might affect the outcome under the governing substantive law.[29] That qualification is important: not every disagreement in the record defeats summary judgment. A fact is material here only if it can affect an element of Rocha’s underlying negligence or of respondeat superior under the ultimately selected jurisdiction’s law.[30][31]

A dispute is **genuine** when the evidence would permit a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmovant—the party opposing the motion.[32] A bare assertion that a fact is disputed is not enough.[32] The asserted dispute must be supported by record evidence sufficient to allow a rational factfinder to resolve the issue for the nonmovant, rather than by speculation, conjecture, or fantasy.[33][29]

The movant bears the initial burden. Under the federal model, the movant must identify a record-grounded basis showing no genuine dispute of material fact and legal entitlement to judgment.[34] Rule 56(c) requires parties asserting that a fact is or is not genuinely disputed to cite particular record materials, including depositions, documents, electronically stored information, affidavits, declarations, admissions, interrogatory responses, stipulations, and other documents and materials.[26][35]

A movant need not always produce affirmative evidence disproving the opponent’s case. It may show that the party bearing the burden of proof lacks evidence for an essential element.[35] After a properly supported showing, the nonmovant must identify specific record facts capable of supporting a rational verdict in its favor; allegations in pleadings alone do not suffice.[34][31]

The court’s role is limited. It does not weigh competing evidence, decide witness credibility, or determine the truth of contested accounts.[36] Instead, it views the evidence and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmovant.[29][35] The court need not accept an inference that is speculative or contradicts the record so plainly that no rational jury could believe it.[34][35][37]

These principles apply with particular force where the dispute concerns a mixed question of law and fact. Scope of employment is often such a question: the legal test is defined by law, but its application can depend on what happened, who authorized it, why the employee acted, and how closely the activity related to company business. Where the factual answer is clear, a court may decide scope as a matter of law. Where underlying facts are disputed or reasonably support different interpretations, scope is ordinarily for the factfinder.[7][38][17]

The same distinction applies to employee status. The Restatement says a court decides the status issue if the inference clearly points one way; otherwise, the factfinder weighs the relevant relationship factors.[9] A court may therefore grant summary judgment on employee status or scope if the relevant evidence is undisputed and compels one legal result. But it may not decide those questions by selecting the movant’s favored inference where a reasonable jury could adopt the nonmovant’s supported version.[33]

Electronic evidence is subject to the same record-based discipline. Rule 56 permits citation to electronically stored information, but a party may object that cited material cannot be presented in a form admissible in evidence.[26] The admissibility analysis for electronic material may involve relevance, authenticity, hearsay, original-writing requirements, and unfair-prejudice concerns.[26][39] Thus, metadata confirming that a deleted email existed may support the fact of the email’s existence if properly authenticated, but the court must separately determine what admissible evidence proves about the email’s sender, recipient, contents, and meaning.[40] The later section, *Scope of employment*, addresses the significance and limits of the deleted-email evidence.

Judgment — The absence of an identified movant matters. If the company moves, the court tests whether the sedan driver has evidence permitting a reasonable finding of employee status, scope, employer-serving purpose, and underlying negligence. If the sedan driver moves, the court tests whether the company lacks evidence from which a reasonable jury could find an independent personal trip, no employee relationship, or no underlying tort. The legal standard is neutral; the required inference direction changes with the movant.

The evidence required for trial and the standard for summary judgment should not be confused. At trial, a plaintiff generally bears the applicable substantive burden—commonly a preponderance of the evidence—for negligence and vicarious-liability elements.[11] At summary judgment, the question is not which side’s evidence is ultimately more persuasive.[41] The question is whether the evidence is sufficiently one-sided that no reasonable jury could find for the nonmovant.[32][42]

**Accordingly, the company’s vicarious liability can be resolved without trial only if the admissible record leaves no genuine factual dispute, and no competing reasonable inference, on facts material under the selected jurisdiction’s employee-status, scope, benefit, and negligence rules.**

The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.* (1986) and *Tolan v. Cotton* (2014) supply the operative federal model: substantive law identifies material facts; a dispute is genuine when the evidence could permit a reasonable jury to find for the nonmovant; and the court asks whether the evidence requires a jury or is so one-sided that one party must prevail as a matter of law.[43] The court must believe the nonmovant’s evidence, draw all justifiable inferences in that party’s favor, and may not weigh contradictory evidence or decide credibility.[43][44]

The dashcam phone glance supports an inference of inattentive driving, while the sedan’s sudden braking for fallen lumber supports the contrary inference that the collision was unavoidable or that the sedan driver contributed to it. Those competing inferences concern material facts bearing on Rocha’s underlying negligence and causation.

For the underlying collision claim, the material record questions include whether Rocha failed to exercise reasonable care, whether that failure was a factual and legal cause of the claimed physical harm, and whether the harm resulted from a risk that made the conduct careless.[45] Restatement (Third) of Torts §§ 29 and 34 limit liability where an independent act or force also contributed to harm to harms within the risks that made the actor’s conduct tortious; § 19 recognizes that conduct may lack reasonable care when it foreseeably combines with improper conduct by a plaintiff or third party.[45] The lumber-triggered sudden braking, properly functioning brake lights, Rocha’s phone glance, and medical evidence therefore bear on negligence, causation, and damages; they do not themselves resolve the separate employment-connection question.[45][46]

### Synthesis

Respondeat superior requires a tort by an employee acting within the scope of employment; it does not make a company liable merely because the driver generally worked for it. Employee status turns principally on control or the right to control. Scope turns on the connection between the specific driving and authorized, assigned, or employer-controlled work. Employer benefit generally informs the employee’s purpose and business connection, although actual gain is not universally required.[7][9][5]

The Second and Third Restatements differ most visibly on the Second Restatement’s express time-and-space requirement, but both make an independent personal course of conduct materially different from employer-connected work.[13] Summary judgment cannot choose between supported competing accounts or inferences about those matters; it can decide the claim only when the material facts are genuinely undisputed and compel one result under the jurisdiction’s governing law.[13][17][26][32][33]

Agency also requires mutual assent: the Restatement (Second) defines it as a fiduciary relationship arising when one person manifests consent that another act on that person’s behalf and subject to that person’s control, and the other consents.[47] A master is a principal that employs a worker in its affairs and controls, or has the right to control, the worker’s physical conduct; a servant is the corresponding worker.[47] An independent contractor, by contrast, is not controlled and is not subject to a right of control as to physical conduct in carrying out the undertaking.[47] These definitions reinforce that route instructions, detour rules, and a supervisor’s interim direction can be probative of control, but the record must establish the relationship rather than merely the label delivery driver.[47]

A traditional formulation treats acts done with the employer’s express or implied authority, and acts done by virtue of the employment and in furtherance of its ends, as within the scope of employment.[48] Restatement (Second) § 235 commentary also recognizes an evidentiary inference of scope when a servant does the very act directed, or an authorized kind of act, within working hours and at an authorized place.[49]

The company’s subsequent conduct can matter under liability theories distinct from ordinary respondeat superior. Under Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.04, a principal may be liable for an agent’s tort when the agent acted with actual authority or when the principal later ratified the conduct; negligent selection, training, retention, or supervision is a separate direct-liability theory under § 7.05.[50] Retroactive mileage reimbursement may therefore be evidence relevant to authorization, ratification, or employer purpose, but respondeat superior still requires an employee tort within the applicable scope-of-employment rule.[50]

The detour/frolic labels also capture the distinction between mixed-purpose activity and complete abandonment. An employee may pursue personal ends yet remain within scope when also doing the employer’s business, whereas complete abandonment to serve wholly personal purposes places the employee outside scope for that period.[49] The character of the lunch trip therefore turns on the whole set of circumstances—its work connection, time and place, degree of departure, authorization or expectation, and Rocha’s purpose—not distance or Rocha’s own characterization alone.[48][46]

Employer benefit also appears in the doctrine’s risk-allocation rationale. California materials describe respondeat superior as assigning enterprise-created risks to the employer as a cost of doing business because the employer benefits from the activity and can spread the risk, while related policy accounts identify accident prevention, victim compensation, and broad distribution of accident losses among enterprise beneficiaries.[51] That rationale supports inquiry into the relationship between the driving and the enterprise’s normal risks; it does not substitute for the employee-status, scope, and underlying-tort requirements.[25][48][46]

## Employee status

### Was Rocha an employee for purposes of respondeat superior?

Judgment — Employee status is a threshold requirement for *respondeat superior*, the doctrine that can make an employer answer for an employee’s tort. In ordinary words, the company can be vicariously liable for Rocha’s driving only if Rocha was its employee, rather than an independent contractor, as well as acting within the scope of his work. The governing jurisdiction is unidentified, so the analysis uses the common-law control approach as a framework rather than treating any one state’s formulation as controlling.

Under the Restatement (Second) of Agency, a *servant*—the traditional agency-law term broadly corresponding to an employee—is a person employed to perform services in another’s affairs and subject to that other person’s control, or right to control, over physical conduct in performing the service. The Restatement (Third) states the same central idea in modern terminology: an employee is an agent whose principal controls, or has the right to control, the manner and means of work performance. An independent contractor, in contrast, retains that control over how the undertaking is performed.[9][3][1][4][24][47][52][53]

The decisive concept is therefore the company’s *right of control*: its contractual or practical authority to direct how Rocha performed delivery work, not merely its ability to specify the desired result. Texas authority applying this common-law distinction similarly asks whether the employer possessed the overall right to control the progress, details, and methods of the work; it generally does not decide status separately for every task the worker performs.[2][20]

The control inquiry is multifactorial. Restatement § 220 identifies the agreed extent of control over work details; whether the worker runs a distinct occupation or business; the customary supervision and skill level of the work; who supplies tools, equipment, and the workplace; duration; payment by time or by job; whether the work belongs to the company’s regular business; the parties’ understanding; and whether the principal is in business.[24] These are considerations, not a checklist requiring every item to favor one side.[9][10][3]

The facts affirmatively support control in two important operational respects. Rocha was a delivery driver with an assigned route. The collision occurred on Route 7 as he returned from a lunch stop four blocks off that assigned route, and the company maintained a policy regulating personal detours.[54] An assigned delivery route directs where work is to be performed, while a detour policy regulates deviations from that work.

Those facts support an inference that the company directed material features of Rocha’s delivery operations, rather than merely purchasing completed deliveries from a separate business.[54][3]

A company’s route direction is specifically probative under delivery-driver classification analyses because it bears on the location and manner of service. The Restatement’s factors likewise treat employer control of work details and work performed as part of the enterprise’s regular business as relevant to employee classification. Rocha’s described role—making deliveries on an assigned route—therefore supports the employee side of the line more than a relationship in which a driver independently selected customers, routes, and methods.[55][10][3]

The company policy has a second significance. A rule prohibiting personal detours reflects authority to impose work-related limits on how a delivery driver uses company time and route access. Employer-issued directions are consistent with the agency relationship because agency requires that the agent act subject to the principal’s control, although the principal need not control every aspect of the agent’s time or judgment.[8][56]

The supervisor’s text directing Rocha to pick up lunch for two warehouse coworkers, if established as described, is additional evidence of the company’s authority to assign a work-connected task during the delivery run. It tends to show actual exercise of supervisory direction, which is relevant because both agreed control and control exercised in practice bear on the classification inquiry. But the instruction’s principal relevance here is to the company’s ongoing authority over Rocha’s delivery work; it does not transform employee status into a task-by-task question.[56][57][2]

**The assigned route, the detour rule, and the supervisor’s asserted direction together favor classifying Rocha as an employee because they show company control over important operational features of his delivery work.**[54][3][2]

Other common-law factors remain capable of materially changing that conclusion. The applicable factors include who furnished the delivery vehicle and other instrumentalities, the length of the relationship, whether Rocha was paid by time or by completed job, whether he operated a distinct delivery enterprise, whether the parties regarded their arrangement as employment, and whether the company could select, compensate, or dismiss him. Colorado’s civil instruction identifies selection, payment, dismissal power, and the right to control performance details as relevant; the Restatement additionally includes tools, duration, payment method, and whether the work formed part of the principal’s regular business.[11][9][3]

Tax and benefit arrangements may also inform the relationship, though they are not decisive. A W-2 payment arrangement normally points toward employee status and a 1099 arrangement toward independent contracting. Employee benefits, withholding of income and Social Security taxes, and the hiring party’s right to assign additional projects support employee status. Likewise, a company-provided truck tends to support employment, while a driver-owned truck can point toward independent contracting.[55][12]

Control over accepting deliveries matters for the same reason. A requirement that the driver accept assigned shipments supports employee status, whereas a driver’s freedom to choose individual shipments is consistent with an independent business. The present operational facts show that Rocha had an assigned route and was subject to a detour policy, but a factfinder would weigh those facts alongside the parties’ actual arrangements concerning equipment, compensation, assignment acceptance, and business independence.[55][10][3]

The law does not treat a job title as conclusive. Even an agreement labeling a worker an employee or independent contractor may yield to the actual circumstances, although a formal agreement reflecting the parties’ understanding can carry substantial weight. Conversely, the company’s failure to supervise every moment of a delivery run would not itself defeat employee status, because the inquiry concerns retained authority to control the work as a whole.[12][8][2]

The question can be resolved by a court only where the relevant inference is clear. Restatement § 220 explains that the court decides whether a master-servant relationship exists when the inference points clearly in one direction; otherwise, the trier of fact determines whether the combined factors establish the relationship. Washington’s pattern instruction similarly directs the decision-maker to consider all evidence bearing on control and recognizes that the factors have varying importance.[9][10][53]

Judgment — Rocha is likely the company’s employee for respondeat-superior purposes on the stated facts. The assigned delivery route and employer detour restriction are affirmative evidence that the company controlled important details of his delivery work, and the supervisor’s lunch instruction reinforces the inference of supervisory authority. That conclusion is not equivalent to entitlement to summary judgment: if classification is genuinely disputed, the factfinder must weigh the whole work relationship—including vehicle, payment, duration, assignment, dismissal, and independent-business facts—rather than treat either the delivery-driver label or the lunch instruction as dispositive.[54][2][11][3]

Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220 (1958) explains that the control or right to control needed for a master-servant relationship may be very attenuated. It distinguishes service in which the worker’s physical activities and time are surrendered to the master’s control from an agreement under which the worker retains control while undertaking to accomplish a result. Thus, the company need not have directed every movement of Rocha’s delivery driving for its retained right of control to favor employee status.[53]

Restatement § 220 comment h identifies additional indicators of a master-servant relationship: an agreement for close supervision or actual close supervision; work not requiring a highly educated or skilled worker; employer-supplied tools; hourly or monthly pay; regular hours over a considerable period; full-time work for one employer; work in a specific area or over a fixed route; work that is part of the employer’s regular business; the community’s treatment of such workers as servants; the parties’ belief that they created a master-servant relationship; and an agreement barring delegation of the work. Rocha’s assigned route corresponds to the fixed-route indicator, while the stated facts do not supply the other listed circumstances.[54][53]

Duration and payment bear independently on the control inference. Short-term, job-paid work is less likely to entail control over details, whereas unskilled work or company-supplied instrumentalities can support servant status even in that setting.[53]

The Cornell Legal Information Institute’s account of the Third Restatement of Torts describes employee-versus-independent-contractor status as a balancing test.

The scenario separately states that Rocha’s supervisor sent an email approving the lunch run and that the email was deleted; its metadata confirms that the email existed.[54]

The company’s retroactive mileage reimbursement for the lunch detour is further evidence that it treated the trip as sufficiently connected to Rocha’s assigned delivery work to reimburse a work expense.

## Scope of employment

Judgment — Scope of employment asks whether the employee was doing the employer’s work, or something sufficiently connected to it, when the tort occurred. It matters here because an active delivery assignment does not automatically make every mid-shift trip company business, but an employer-directed errand can remain within the job even when it departs from the usual route.

### Was Rocha acting within the scope of employment

Respondeat superior, the rule that can make an employer answer for an employee’s tort, generally requires that the tort occur within the scope of employment—the field of activity in which the employee is serving the employer. No governing jurisdiction is identified, so no single state’s formulation controls. Under Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228, conduct falls within scope only if it is the kind of work the employee is employed to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is motivated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer. Under Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07, the central question is instead whether the employee was performing employer-assigned work or conduct subject to the employer’s control, rather than pursuing an independent course not intended to serve any employer purpose.[3][1][38]

The Restatement (Second) test is not mechanically confined to the delivery driver’s ordinary route. It provides that an act may be within scope when it is of the same general nature as authorized work or incidental to it—that is, subordinate or pertinent to assigned work. Restatement (Second) § 229 identifies the full set of considerations for deciding whether unauthorized conduct is sufficiently similar to, or incidental to, authorized conduct: whether workers in that role commonly do the act; its time, place, and purpose; the parties’ previous relationship; how the employer apportions its business among workers; whether the act lies outside the employer’s enterprise or within it but is entrusted to no worker; whether the employer had reason to expect the act; its similarity in quality to authorized conduct; whether the employer furnished the instrumentality causing harm; the extent of departure from the normal method of accomplishing an authorized result; and whether the act is seriously criminal.[3] These considerations include whether the act is common for workers in the role, its time, place, and purpose, employer expectation, departure from the normal way of performing the job, and employer provision of the relevant vehicle, tools, or equipment.[17] The stated facts therefore require attention to both the delivery assignment and the particular lunch errand, rather than treating either the job title or the off-route location as dispositive.

The claimant’s strongest scope evidence is concrete. Rocha was not on an ordinary commute or a recreational trip: he was returning from a lunch pickup during an active delivery assignment, remained on schedule, and intended to resume deliveries immediately. A supervisor had texted him to pick up lunch for two warehouse coworkers. The company also cited a policy effective May 1, 2024—more than six weeks before the June 14 collision—that allowed team-support errands when it reimbursed his detour mileage after the collision.[54] A factfinder could reasonably see the text as a specific assignment and the policy and reimbursement as confirmation that the company treated coworker-support errands as part of its operations.

That evidence fits either principal Restatement formulation. Under the Third Restatement approach, a supervisor’s direction supports a finding that Rocha was performing assigned work, while the delivery company’s route rules, detour policy, and subsequent reimbursement all tend to show an employer-controlled course of conduct rather than an unsupervised private excursion.[8][1][17] Under the Second Restatement approach, operating a vehicle during an assigned delivery shift is conduct of the same general kind as Rocha’s job, and carrying out a supervisor-directed pickup for warehouse coworkers may be found incidental to delivery operations even if it was not part of the original delivery manifest.[3]

The company nevertheless has a substantial contrary argument on the time-and-space element. Rocha had left his assigned route by four blocks, and company policy prohibited personal detours. An express restriction can support a finding that an employee was outside ordinary spatial boundaries; a Colorado instruction likewise states that even a short departure may be outside scope when it substantially departs from the principal’s business to pursue an independent purpose unrelated to that business.[21][11] The company can characterize the trip as an unassigned lunch run undertaken for colleagues’ convenience, distinct from the delivery service Rocha was hired to perform.

The policy’s wording is important. It prohibits *personal* detours, but the described lunch run involved a supervisor’s text instruction to obtain food for two warehouse coworkers, not merely Rocha’s own lunch. A prohibition is relevant evidence of the limits of the assignment, but it does not itself resolve scope. Restatement (Second) § 230 states that an act may remain within scope even when forbidden or performed in a forbidden manner, and the inquiry remains whether the negligent act furthered the employer’s business rather than whether every aspect of performance complied with internal rules.[6][11] Thus, the detour ban helps the company only if the factfinder accepts that this particular errand was personal or outside the team-support exception.

The familiar detour–frolic distinction makes the competing characterizations clearer. A *detour* is a minor, temporary deviation that does not sever the work connection; a *frolic* is a substantial abandonment of the employer’s business for purely personal purposes. Courts evaluate the geographic departure, time consumed, intended return to work, and whether such side trips are common for the role.[17][58] Four blocks is a limited geographic deviation, and Rocha’s on-schedule status and intended immediate return to deliveries support a detour characterization.

But distance does not settle the issue: even a brief departure can be a frolic if its actual purpose is unrelated to the employer’s business.[11][54]

The mixed-purpose character of the journey favors the claimant. The dual-purpose doctrine addresses travel that serves both work and personal ends. Its usual question is whether the work created a necessity for the travel: if the trip would have been made for the business even without the personal objective, it may remain within scope; if it would have occurred only for a personal reason, it is personal.[17][58] Here, Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing him to pick up lunch for the two warehouse coworkers.

That direction supports an inference that the pickup created a work-assigned reason to travel, but whether the direction and team-support policy covered the four-block detour and Rocha’s return driving when the collision occurred remains material.[54][17] Rocha may also have obtained lunch for himself or enjoyed a break, but a personal component does not defeat scope if the employer-serving errand was genuine and sufficiently connected to the work.[15][22]

The company can respond that delivering restaurant food to warehouse coworkers is different in kind from delivering company goods and that the off-route lunch run would not have been made but for the coworkers’ personal meal preferences. That position draws support from authority treating ordinary lunch trips and personal errands as outside course and scope when the employee has not resumed assigned duties or has not reached the next work location.[20][2] But that analogy is incomplete here because Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing him to obtain lunch for the two warehouse coworkers, and because it omits the already-effective team-support-errand policy, reimbursement, and active delivery status.[54] A lunch trip that is specifically directed to assist coworkers is materially different from an employee’s autonomous trip to eat.

The return journey is independently significant. If the lunch stop were found to be a frolic, many courts recognize *re-entry*: the employee returns to scope when reasonably near the authorized route or work location and again acts with intent to serve the employer’s business.[17][58] Merely pointing the vehicle generally toward work is not invariably enough; the employee must genuinely resume the path of duty rather than continue the personal objective.[23] Rocha’s statement that he intended to complete deliveries immediately after lunch, coupled with his being on schedule, supports re-entry or continuous scope.

The precise location of the collision relative to his assigned route is not stated, however, so a factfinder would need to decide whether he had returned sufficiently to the delivery mission if it first finds a frolic.

Christensen v. Swenson is a useful, though non-controlling, comparison. In that case, a security guard drove to a nearby café during a permitted short lunch break, collided while returning to her post, and intended to return promptly to eat at the post. The court held that reasonable minds could differ on whether the lunch trip remained within the employment’s ordinary spatial boundaries and whether it partly served the employer’s interests.[21] Rocha’s case is at least as plausibly work-connected because the record contains a supervisor’s text directing the pickup for coworkers, a team-support policy, and post-event mileage reimbursement; conversely, the company has clearer evidence of a general prohibition on personal detours. Neither set of facts permits a categorical result under the general law.

In *Christensen v. Swenson*, the guard drove 150–250 yards for soup during a permitted, unscheduled 10-to-15-minute lunch break and collided with a motorcycle. The Utah Supreme Court reversed summary judgment for the employer because reasonable minds could differ on all three *Birkner* scope criteria.[92][93][94]

In *Christensen*, evidence of the employer’s knowledge of the lunch-trip practice, lack of discipline, and uncertainty about a prohibition also made the scope of employment disputable.[92][93]

**The better conclusion is that Rocha has a substantial basis for a within-scope finding, but the company’s detour policy and the disputed characterization of the errand prevent either side from treating scope as conclusively established.** The supervisor-directed, four-block team-support errand supports a temporary work-connected detour; the prohibition supports a contrary inference only if the factfinder determines the errand remained personal despite the direction and policy.[54][3][21]

Two additional formulations underscore why assigned purpose and employer benefit, rather than route distance alone, matter. Cornell’s Wex explains that states set their own respondeat-superior standards and identifies a Benefits Test and a Characteristics Test; the latter asks whether the conduct is common enough to the job to be characteristic of it.[24] Utah’s Birkner formulation similarly asks whether the worker was about the employer’s business and performing assigned duties, acted during working hours and within ordinary spatial boundaries, and was motivated at least partly to serve the employer.[59] The supervisor’s text and Rocha’s active delivery shift support the assigned-work and time elements, while the off-route stop and the disputed business purpose leave the spatial-boundaries and employer-purpose elements contested.[54][59]

The facts also support analysis of the lunch pickup as a special errand. California’s special-errand doctrine treats an employee engaged in a special mission for the employer as within course and scope from the start of the errand until the employee returns or deviates for personal reasons.[51] Utah authority likewise states that travel to and from a special activity is within course and scope when the activity is within the employment and reasonably undertaken at the employer’s request or invitation.[59]  The special-errand formulations support the return journey if the factfinder determines that the direction and the May 1 team-support policy covered the detour and return driving at impact; they do not if it determines that those sources did not authorize that travel as company business.[54][51][59]

District of Columbia and Virginia authorities recognize that a temporary detour for a meal may remain within the scope of employment. Louisiana authority emphasizes employer control and benefit in a directed special assignment even when the worker uses a personal vehicle and receives no mileage reimbursement.[95][96]

A directed act carries evidentiary force even though it does not end the inquiry. Restatement (Second) § 235 comment a recognizes an inference of scope when a servant performs the very act directed, or an authorized kind of act within working hours and at an authorized place.[49] The text instruction therefore supports an inference that the pickup was assigned work, but the four-block route departure and the disputed reach of the team-support policy leave open whether the place and manner of this return trip were authorized.[54][49]

Restatement (Second) § 228(2) identifies the converse: conduct is outside scope when it is different in kind from authorized conduct, far beyond authorized time or space limits, or too little actuated by a purpose to serve the employer.[3] The company’s four-block deviation and personal-detour rule bear on those criteria, but the supervisor’s direction and team-support evidence bear on whether the return travel was authorized in kind, within permissible spatial limits, and at least partly employer-serving.[54][3]

The mixed-purpose analysis includes a specific counterfactual: would Rocha still have made this lunch trip if the work errand—collecting lunch for the coworkers at the supervisor’s direction—had been canceled? In Marks’ April 16, 1927 trip, the court treated employment as requiring a concurrent causal role rather than being the sole reason for travel; Marks would have traveled to collect his wife even if the faucet-repair errand had been canceled, and the employment therefore did not bring him on the journey or expose him to its risks.[60][61] Rocha’s supervisor-directed pickup supports the opposite inference that the work errand helped cause the travel, but whether he would have made the trip without that direction remains material.[54][61]

A California formulation asks whether the act was required or incidental to the employee’s duties, or whether the employee’s misconduct was reasonably foreseeable to the employer.[25] Fields v. Sanders states the related proposition that the inquiry is not whether the particular act was specifically authorized, but whether it formed part of a series of acts authorized by the employer as a whole.[25] Those formulations support examining the direction, policy, and employment practice together rather than asking only whether a normal delivery manifest expressly listed this lunch pickup.[54][25]

### What does the deleted supervisor email establish about approval of

The deleted email establishes the narrower fact that an email record existed: the scenario states that metadata confirms its existence. Metadata is information about other electronic data and may identify such matters as a file’s author, creation or access dates, and the existence of prior versions.[54][62] Its existence is probative because it is consistent with the stated description of an email from Rocha’s supervisor approving the lunch run.

But existence is not content. Metadata establishing that an electronic record existed does not, without more, establish the email’s exact language, the identity of its author, whether it actually approved the trip, or whether any approval covered Rocha’s selected route, timing, or all portions of the deviation. The distinction is legally material: business-record metadata may be offered to prove an electronic record’s existence, but not automatically its contents.[62] The deleted email therefore cannot independently establish that the supervisor authorized every aspect of the four-block detour.

The deleted email’s metadata also does not, by itself, establish when the email was sent or the extent of the supervisor’s authority to approve the trip.[54][97]

The text message is stronger on the key point of direction. The scenario affirmatively states that Rocha received a supervisor text telling him to pick up lunch for the two warehouse coworkers.[54] That text supports an inference of authorization without relying on the missing email’s content.

Read together, the text, the description of the email, the May 1 team-support policy, and the later mileage reimbursement support the claimant’s position that the lunch run was employer-sanctioned.[54]

Whether the email may be used on summary judgment depends on ordinary electronic-evidence foundations. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 901, the proponent must offer evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Evidence of distinctive characteristics, a process or system shown to produce an accurate result, testimony from someone with knowledge, or later communications acknowledging the message can supply that foundation.[63][64] Authentication is a threshold inquiry; it does not itself dispose of hearsay or other admissibility issues.[63][64][40]

A genuinely authenticated directive email may have a path around a hearsay objection if offered to show that an instruction was given—a legally operative communication—rather than to prove the truth of an assertion within it. A supervisor’s statement may also qualify as an opposing party’s statement under the applicable evidence rules.[39][40] These are evidentiary routes, not automatic conclusions, because the governing jurisdiction and the available authentication foundation remain unspecified.

The company may argue that deletion undermines reliance on the email at summary judgment. That argument is strongest if the claimant offers only metadata and cannot produce testimony, a recoverable copy, surrounding messages, system evidence, or other proof establishing author and content. The claimant, by contrast, can rely on the independent text instruction and may use admissible secondary evidence of a lost or destroyed writing’s contents where the applicable rules permit it.[39][40] The ultimate authorization question therefore turns less on metadata alone than on the admissible proof connecting the supervisor to a specific instruction or approval.

**The deleted-email metadata proves an email existed, while the separate supervisor text and the scenario’s approval description support the stronger inference of authorization; metadata alone cannot prove the approval’s wording or scope.** This distinction preserves the claimant’s plausible authorization case without converting proof of an electronic record into conclusive proof of what the record said.[54][62]

Electronic evidence can supply stronger proof than the bare existence metadata. A successfully sent email ordinarily generates sender, recipient, and server-log records, and those records can be compared to confirm transmission and whether corresponding metadata align.[65] A deleted message may also be recoverable through forensic tools before its data are overwritten.[65] Testimony from a knowledgeable witness, distinctive characteristics in context, and records of the sending system are recognized methods of authentication.[66][65]

Those methods matter because apparent account identity is not necessarily actual authorship: a person with access to an account or logged-in device can send a message, and email spoofing can create the appearance that a message came from a particular address.[65] Header fields such as Received, Sender, Return-Path, and Content-Type, and a verifiable DKIM signature, can help test the asserted sender, transmission path, domain authorization, and message integrity.[65]

Deletion of the supervisor’s email alone does not justify an adverse evidentiary inference. Any such inference depends on the governing jurisdiction’s requirements and on evidence concerning preservation duties, responsibility for the deletion, and resulting prejudice.

A duty to preserve evidence generally arises when litigation is reasonably anticipated; for an adverse inference concerning lost electronic evidence, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) requires a showing of intent to deprive.[98][99]

The company cited that policy when it retroactively reimbursed Rocha’s mileage for the detour.[54] Reimbursement does not itself decide scope: a court has distinguished a specifically paid, assigned coworker-transport duty from mere mileage reimbursement where the employer did not otherwise direct travel.[20] Here, however, reimbursement is not standing alone.

It operates alongside the supervisor’s text directing the pickup and the team-support policy, supporting an inference that the company regarded the lunch run as an authorized team-support errand. Whether the direction and policy extended to the detour and return driving at the moment of impact remains disputed.[54]

The company’s personal-detour prohibition and team-support policy are not necessarily inconsistent. A factfinder could conclude that the company prohibited drivers from leaving routes for their own purposes but created an exception for errands that assist the team. Conversely, the company can contend that the updated policy did not cover meal pickups, did not authorize this route or timing, or was invoked after the collision to reimburse an expense without conceding tort liability. The scope of the policy exception is a material factual issue because it bears directly on authorization, employer control, and whether the travel was an independent course.[54][1]

Later reimbursement may also be argued as ratification—an employer’s acceptance of an unauthorized act after learning material facts, which can have the effect of prior authorization under some law.[11] But ratification requires proof that the company acted with full knowledge of the important facts, and the party asserting it bears that burden.[11] The reimbursement therefore supports, but does not compel, an inference that the company accepted the lunch run as company business.

The post-collision reimbursement could instead reflect an administrative accommodation or a post-event reclassification of the trip, rather than advance authorization.[102][103]

The evidence of Rocha’s planned return to deliveries addresses both purpose and re-entry. A worker’s effort to return promptly to assigned work can support an inference that the activity partly served the employer, especially where the employee was trying to preserve work time.[21] Here, Rocha’s on-schedule status and intention to complete deliveries immediately after lunch allow a finding that the return trip was linked to the assigned delivery route rather than an open-ended personal outing.[54] The company can still argue that intent to resume work later does not establish that he had resumed it at the moment of impact; that distinction depends on the factfinder’s view of the errand’s actual business purpose and location.

The closest favorable analogy is Painter v. Amerimex Drilling I, Ltd., where evidence that a worker had an assigned and compensated duty to transport coworkers, and that doing so ensured crew availability, created a genuine issue on course and scope and defeated the employer’s summary-judgment motion.[2][20] Rocha’s evidence is less direct because he was not shown to have a regular food-transport duty, but it similarly permits a finding that a supervisor-directed task assisting coworkers furthered the company’s operations.

The adverse lunch-trip cases remain relevant only if the factfinder rejects the direction and team-support evidence and classifies the journey as private lunch travel.[20]

**The June 14 facts support a finding that the lunch pickup was an assigned or incidental mixed-purpose team-support errand, while the off-route location and personal-detour rule support a competing finding that it was an independent lunch deviation.** The dispositive factual questions are the scope of the supervisor’s instruction, whether the May 1 policy covered this pickup, and whether Rocha’s return travel was at least partly directed to serving the company’s business.[54][3][1]

The team-support policy had been effective for 44 days when the June 14, 2024 collision occurred. That timing supports treating the cited policy as a pre-collision source of possible authorization, rather than a policy first adopted in response to this incident.[54]

The employer-serving purpose can be tested concretely by asking whether the company would have had to send another employee over the same route or to perform the same function had Rocha not made the trip.[59] The record supports an inference that the company adopted the coworkers’ requested lunch pickup when the supervisor directed it and later reimbursed mileage under the team-support policy.[54] It also permits the contrary inference that no company delivery function required restaurant food and that the coworkers’ meal preferences, rather than a company need, compelled the travel.[54][59]

The relevant factual record includes work schedules, delivery logs, GPS data, time records, company vehicle policies, supervisor instructions, messages, and witness accounts; destination, reason for travel, duration of the interruption, and whether assigned duties had resumed also bear on re-entry and scope.[46] Here, the stated on-schedule status and intended immediate resumption of deliveries favor re-entry or a continuing work mission, but the collision’s precise location relative to the assigned route, the duration of the stop, and the actual policy practice are not stated.[54][46]

### Synthesis

Scope of employment is the central contested issue. Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing him to collect lunch for the two warehouse coworkers. That direction, the already-effective May 1 team-support policy, post-collision mileage reimbursement, four-block distance, and Rocha’s on-schedule intended return to deliveries together provide a substantial evidentiary basis for treating the Route 7 travel as an authorized detour, a special errand, or a return to assigned delivery work; whether the direction and policy covered the detour and return driving at impact remains for the factfinder.[54][17] The company’s personal-detour prohibition and the off-route lunch purpose support the opposite inference that Rocha was on an independent frolic.[54][17][3][11] The better procedural conclusion is that scope cannot be decided for either party as a matter of law on these competing inferences: reasonable factfinders could differ about authorization, purpose, spatial limits, and re-entry.[21]

The additional authorities reinforce that scope is a full-circumstances inquiry. A detour is a small or reasonably related departure from assigned work, while a frolic is a substantial personal departure that takes the employee outside scope for at least part of the activity; purpose, geography, time, authorization, employer expectation, and workplace practice are all relevant.[46] Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing the team-support pickup, and his limited four-block departure and planned return support a detour or special-errand finding.

The personal-detour prohibition supports a frolic finding if the factfinder determines that the direction and team-support policy did not cover the detour and return driving at impact.[54][51][67]

## Benefit to employer

The benefit inquiry asks whether the lunch trip advanced a company interest, not merely whether Rocha was employed or happened to be driving during a workday. A task can have a personal aspect and still serve the employer; the issue is whether helping the two warehouse coworkers was, at least in part, company-serving work.

### Did the lunch errand benefit the employer?

Judgment — The lunch errand plausibly benefited the company because it was directed toward supporting two warehouse coworkers, not simply obtaining Rocha’s own meal. That conclusion matters because employer benefit is a central scope-of-employment consideration in many jurisdictions, although it is not an independent substitute for the full scope analysis addressed in the preceding section.

Under the Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228, conduct falls within the scope of employment only when it is, among other things, “actuated, at least in part, by a purpose to serve the master.” The stated purpose need not be exclusive: § 228 asks whether employer service was part of the employee’s motivation, while conduct falls outside scope when it is too little actuated by that purpose.[7] The Restatement (Third) formulation similarly excludes an employee’s independent course of conduct only when the employee did not intend to serve any employer purpose.[1][5] Thus, the relevant benefit is not whether the company earned revenue from sandwiches, but whether the supervisor-directed assistance to warehouse staff furthered the company’s workforce or operations.

The facts supporting company benefit are concrete. Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing him to pick up lunch for two warehouse coworkers.[54] The company also had a policy allowing team-support errands effective May 1, 2024—before the June 14, 2024 collision—and, after the collision, reimbursed the mileage for this detour while citing that policy.[54] Read together, the supervisor’s specific instruction, already-effective policy, and reimbursement support an inference that the company treated coworker support as a company-serving purpose rather than as Rocha’s private lunch choice.

The supervisor’s instruction and the already-effective team-support-errand policy permit a finding that the trip had a workplace purpose, while the ordinary personal character of obtaining lunch permits the competing finding that it was a personal errand.[54][20]

The company may receive a practical workforce benefit even though the immediate recipients were coworkers rather than a customer. In *Painter v. Amerimex Drilling I, Ltd.*, the Texas Supreme Court treated evidence that a driller was assigned and separately paid to transport coworkers as evidence that the transportation furthered the employer’s business: it helped ensure that the full crew arrived for shifts, avoided stranding workers after work, and discouraged competing employers from hiring drillers away.[2] The case’s principle is not that every favor for a coworker benefits an employer.

Rather, practical support that helps coworkers remain available and able to perform their work can further the employer’s business.[2][20] Here, delivering food to two warehouse coworkers can reasonably be viewed as supporting their workday, particularly because the supervisor—not merely the coworkers—directed Rocha to make the pickup.[54]

Picking up the coworkers’ lunches could have allowed them to remain at the warehouse and reduced interruptions to their work.[54][100]

Rocha’s continued connection to the delivery operation reinforces that inference. He was on schedule and intended to resume deliveries immediately after lunch.[54] In *Christensen v. Swenson*, evidence that an employee used a lunch trip to conserve time and tried to return immediately to her post was relevant to whether the trip was motivated in part by the employer’s interest; the court also recognized managers’ testimony that employee breaks can benefit employers by supporting productive, satisfied employees.[21] Rocha’s prompt planned return does not itself transform lunch into company business, but it supports the conclusion that the excursion remained integrated with his delivery shift rather than becoming a stand-alone personal outing.[54][21]

The company’s best contrary position is that buying and eating lunch ordinarily has a personal character. Texas decisions summarized by the State Bar of Texas describe personal errands and eating lunch as frolics—substantial personal departures from work—outside the course and scope of employment. In *Molina v. City of Pasadena*, an inspector driving from lunch toward his next inspection site was outside scope because he had not resumed his job duties or reached the next work zone, and was not furthering the city’s interests.[20] If a factfinder concludes that Rocha stopped principally to obtain lunch in the ordinary personal sense, and that the coworkers’ meals were only incidental, that conclusion would weigh against an employer benefit.[20]

That argument is weaker here than in an ordinary employee lunch case because it must account for the supervisor’s text directing the coworker pickup and the company’s own team-support-errand policy.[54] A company’s general prohibition on personal detours still supports its position that a lunch stop was outside ordinary delivery work, but a supervisor-directed errand for coworkers may reasonably be distinguished from a prohibited *personal* detour.

The benefit question therefore turns on the character of the errand: was Rocha carrying out assigned assistance to warehouse personnel, or was he principally engaged in a personal lunch trip that the company later chose to reimburse?[54][20]

The retroactive mileage reimbursement is probative but not decisive. Reimbursing mileage is commonly associated with errands made on an organization’s behalf and with business-purpose vehicle use.[68] It therefore supports the inference that the company recognized this particular travel as company-related.[54][68] But in *Silvas v.

California and Illinois employment-expense reimbursement rules address necessary work expenses, not an employer’s tort liability to a third party. Compliance with a reimbursement rule therefore does not itself establish Rocha’s employee status, authorization, ratification, or scope of employment.[103][102]

Harrie*, as summarized by the State Bar of Texas, mileage reimbursement did not establish course and scope where the employer did not otherwise control or direct the consultant’s travel.[20] Here, unlike *Silvas*, the asserted supervisor text supplies evidence of direction, while the policy supplies a stated business category for the trip; reimbursement adds weight to those facts but cannot independently prove employer benefit or scope.[54][20]

The deleted approval email does not change that analysis by itself. Its metadata confirms that an email existed, but metadata proving existence does not prove the email’s contents, its precise authorization, or its stated rationale.[54] The text message is consequently the direct stated evidence of direction; the deleted email may corroborate that account only if admissible evidence establishes what the message said.[54]

The dual-purpose doctrine provides a useful way to assess the mixed character of the trip. Under the formulation attributed to *Marks’ Dependents v. Gray*, travel serves a business purpose when the work created a necessity for the trip—that is, the trip would have occurred even if the personal objective were removed. If the trip would have happened only for personal reasons, it is personal.[58][17] The supervisor’s instruction supports an inference that obtaining food for the two coworkers created an independent work reason to travel.[54] Conversely, the company may argue that Rocha would have made the stop for his own lunch regardless, making the coworker sandwiches an addition to an otherwise personal trip. That factual choice bears directly on whether the company received a real operational or workforce-support benefit.[54][58]

**Conclusion:** The supervisor’s direction to obtain lunches for two warehouse coworkers, the May 1, 2024 team-support-errand policy, and the company’s mileage reimbursement collectively support a reasonable inference that the company derived a workforce-support benefit from the trip.[54] The personal nature of lunch and the limited force of reimbursement alone support the opposing inference, but they do not compel a finding that Rocha was on a purely private frolic where the company allegedly assigned and later treated the errand as team support.[20] The benefit evidence thus supports a plausible basis for respondeat superior liability, while the ultimate scope determination depends on the governing jurisdiction’s framework: Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228 uses a four-part checklist, and Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 uses the broader inquiry whether Rocha was doing assigned work or conduct subject to the company’s control.[3][58]

Employer benefit is both a scope-of-employment factor and part of the policy behind respondeat superior. The doctrine allocates to an enterprise the losses from employee torts expected in conducting the enterprise because the enterprise seeks profit and can absorb or distribute the resulting loss; it also rests on the fairness of requiring the party that benefits from the enterprise and has the right to control workers’ physical activities to pay for harm caused by their errors while doing the work that makes the enterprise successful.[51][25][52][67] The 1990 California-law article identifies three further reasons for vicarious liability: accident prevention, greater assurance of compensation for accident victims, and broad and equitable distribution of accident losses among the beneficiaries of enterprises that entail them.[25] The operative inquiry remains fact-specific.

The commonly stated factors ask whether the conduct was work Rocha was hired to perform, occurred substantially within authorized time and spatial limits, and was actuated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer; courts also consider the size of any departure and whether the employer authorized, encouraged, or could reasonably expect it.[59][69][70][46][71] Conduct assigned by the employer or subject to its control supports scope, while an independent course not designed to serve the employer does not.[50] Rocha’s supervisor-directed pickup, if found, therefore supplies evidence both of assignment and of an employer-serving purpose.[54][50]

A practical test sharpens the benefit issue: would the company have had to send another employee over the same route or to perform the same function if Rocha had not made the trip?[59] A factfinder could answer yes if the supervisor’s instruction required someone to obtain food for the warehouse coworkers; that would support a genuine company function rather than a merely incidental private purchase.[54][59] A factfinder could instead find that no employee would have been sent and that the sandwiches simply accompanied Rocha’s own lunch.

That inference would resemble *Marks’ Dependents v. Gray*, where a plumber’s helper already planned a personal trip, the employer added a 15-to-20-minute faucet repair, and the repair would not have justified a special trip because it was unprofitable; the employer would have postponed it until it could be combined with other work.[72][60] The distinction is material because a work task that itself creates the need for travel supports a mixed-purpose characterization, whereas a task appended to travel that would occur anyway supports the company’s personal-trip characterization.[72][60]

The supervisor’s direction also permits a special-errand characterization. Under the special-errand doctrine, an employee on a special mission for the employer remains within the course and scope from starting the errand until returning or making a personal deviation.[51] In *Jeewarat*, the employer’s expected benefit from an employee’s conference attendance and the absence of a personal deviation supported treating the entire return journey as a continuing special errand.[51] If the factfinder finds that obtaining the coworkers’ lunches was an assigned team-support mission, Rocha’s planned immediate return to deliveries supports treating the trip as continuing work-connected travel.[54][51] If it finds that his own lunch was the trip’s purpose and the coworker meals were incidental, the four-block off-route stop remains a personal deviation instead.[54][72]

The mixed-purpose evidence does not require that the company be the only beneficiary. An employee may pursue personal ends and still act within scope when also doing the employer’s business; only acts done at least partly to serve the employer meet that formulation.[49] The Utah Supreme Court’s *Clover v.

Snowbird Ski Resort* example likewise treated travel between two work locations as sufficient for a jury to find scope even though skiing was a personal pleasure, because the travel also benefited the employer.[70] Conversely, employer connection must be substantial enough to matter: a Utah Court of Appeals decision upheld rejection of a special-errand exception where the employer-control or employer-benefit showing was insufficient.[59] On these facts, the direct supervisor instruction, the effective team-support policy, and reimbursement provide a stronger benefit-and-control showing than reimbursement standing alone.[54][59]

The reported supervisor direction, coupled with the team-support policy already effective on May 1, 2024, supports prior authorization of the coworker-support errand. The later mileage reimbursement could also support ratification if the company accepted the errand with full knowledge of the important facts; ratification then has the same effect as prior authorization.[11][54]

Evidence that Rocha made a supervisor-directed coworker-support trip under an existing team-support policy and received mileage reimbursement can support a factfinder’s conclusion that the errand benefited the company, as authorized coworker transportation did in *Painter*.[20][54]

In *Painter*, the Texas Supreme Court rejected the employer’s argument that the course-and-scope evidence entitled it to summary judgment.[2]

Those omissions support the company’s position that the meals were a personal convenience, even if the directed pickup could still serve a workplace-support purpose.

## Driver negligence, causation and injury

Judgment — This part addresses the predicate tort: whether Rocha’s driving was negligent, whether that negligence caused the collision and the sedan driver’s harm, and what the medical evidence proves. Those questions matter because respondeat superior is vicarious liability—liability imposed on an employer for an employee’s tort—not a substitute for proving that the employee committed the underlying tort. Respondeat superior makes an employer vicariously liable for an employee’s negligent act or omission committed in the course and scope of employment.[3][41][51][48][69] A claimant ordinarily must prove duty, breach, causation, and harm; state law may label or organize causation differently.[3][41]

### Does the evidence support a finding that Rocha drove negligently?

Judgment — Under the Third Restatement’s ordinary negligence duty, “An actor ordinarily has a duty to exercise reasonable care when the actor’s conduct creates a risk of physical harm.” Negligence means failure to use reasonable care under all the circumstances: in ordinary words, failing to drive with the attention and precaution a reasonably careful driver would use where a collision is foreseeable.[73] The foreseeable likelihood and seriousness of harm, and the burden of safer precautions, guide that inquiry.[74]

The dashcam shows Rocha glanced at his phone two seconds before impact. NHTSA defines distracted driving as “any activity that diverts attention from driving,” including “talking or texting on your phone, eating and drinking, talking to people in your vehicle, fiddling with the stereo, entertainment or navigation system.” NHTSA states, “Any non-driving activity you engage in is a potential distraction and increases your risk of crashing.” A fact finder may therefore infer that Rocha’s diverted attention prevented a timely perception of the sedan’s braking or a timely braking response.[75][76]

Rocha rear-ended the sedan. Rear-end impact can support an inference that the following driver failed to maintain a safe distance or brake in time, although the governing jurisdiction is not identified and jurisdictions vary on whether that inference is a presumption, a permissible inference, or neither. Maryland authority described in the materials states that rear impact alone does not prove the trailing driver was careless; authorities describing Florida, Louisiana, and Nevada rules instead describe rebuttable rear-driver presumptions or inferences.[77][76][78][79]

The sedan’s brake lights functioned properly. Functioning brake lights provide a braking signal to the following driver and remove failed brake lights as the stated explanation for Rocha’s inability to respond.[54][77]

The sedan braked suddenly for fallen lumber on Route 7. Road debris can require quick braking, and braking for debris is not inherently bizarre or improper; sudden braking alone does not establish fault by either the lead driver or the following driver.[76][80] A rear driver can avoid fault where an unanticipated hazard made impact reasonably unavoidable, but that conclusion depends on whether the driver could reasonably have avoided the collision.[78]

Collision-specific proof determines that avoidability question. Speed, following distance, road grip, slope, tire condition, braking, perception, reaction time, traffic, visibility, and camera coverage can show whether attentive driving and a reasonable following distance would have prevented impact. Accident reconstruction ordinarily evaluates vehicle damage, tire marks, road conditions, video, and driver actions; event-data-recorder information, if available, may supply speed and braking information but must be compared with physical evidence.[80] The stated facts identify neither a speed nor following distance, nor the timing of braking and perception that would resolve whether impact was avoidable.

**The evidence permits a finding that Rocha failed to keep a proper lookout or maintain a safe stopping distance: the phone glance and rear impact support that inference, while the lumber hazard permits an alternative inference that the impact was unavoidable despite reasonable driving.**[76][75][78]

The Maryland authority describes a 1955 case in which a driver braked for a cat that entered the road at about 1 a.m., a trailing vehicle struck the car, and the passenger’s claim against the trailing driver failed because a rear-end collision alone did not prove carelessness.[77]

A reasonable following distance has no fixed measurement; it is the distance a reasonable person would maintain in similar circumstances. The circumstances relevant to that inquiry include foreseeable roadside hazards or distractions that could require a sudden stop.[81]

Those facts would bear on what a reasonably careful driver could have perceived and done in the available time.

### What can be determined about the cause of the collision?

Factual cause, also called cause in fact, asks whether the claimed harm would have occurred absent the defendant’s breach—the familiar but-for test. Negligence also requires legal causation, often called proximate cause or scope of liability: the injury must fall within the risks that made the conduct negligent.[82][41][83] A collision and resulting neck injury are ordinary foreseeable risks of distracted driving or inadequate following distance.[41]

Rocha’s phone glance is affirmative evidence of a causal contribution. Inattention, following too closely, and looking away at the wrong time are common contributors to rear-end crashes.[84] In the U.S. 100-car naturalistic study, which continuously monitored day-to-day driving in instrumented cars for one year, driver inattention was attributed to 78% of crashes and 65% of near crashes; 15 rear-end collisions occurred, and 93% involved driver inattention—not looking at the road ahead.[84] That study supplies context, not a measurement of Rocha’s conduct; the dashcam supplies the collision-specific evidence that he looked at his phone two seconds before impact.[76]

The fallen lumber explains why the sedan driver braked. It does not itself establish negligent braking, because a prudent driver may brake for a roadway danger and quick braking for debris can be a foreseeable traffic event.[76][77] The functioning brake lights favor the inference that Rocha received a warning of the sedan’s braking, rather than an inference that the sedan gave no signal.[54][77]

Rocha can contend that the lumber created a sudden emergency—an unexpected event making impact unavoidable despite reasonable care. The defense requires proof that the hazard was unanticipated and that the collision could not reasonably have been avoided.[78] That argument has force because a rear driver sometimes has no reasonable opportunity to avoid a collision, and comparative fault can apply where both drivers contributed.[78] It is weakened here by the stated reason for the sedan’s stop—fallen lumber—and by the brake-light evidence; a sudden unexplained stop or failed brake lights would support the defense more directly.[78][79][54]

The decisive causal comparison is practical rather than semantic: when did the sedan’s brakes illuminate, when should Rocha have perceived the braking or lumber, how much space separated the vehicles, how fast was Rocha traveling, and when did he brake? Dashcam footage and witnesses may help establish the lead vehicle’s timing and the following driver’s available space, while a reconstruction may estimate perception-response travel and stopping distance.[80] An expert’s reconstruction can explain how the crash happened but does not itself decide legal responsibility.[80]

**Rocha’s phone glance two seconds before impact permits an inference that distraction contributed to the collision. The sedan’s braking for fallen lumber and its functioning brake lights make preventability depend on the warning time, the vehicles’ separation, speed, and Rocha’s response.**[76][54][80]

The collision record supports competing findings on avoidability: distracted inattention and insufficient following distance can cause rear-end impacts, while a sharp brake application after fallen lumber can leave a following driver insufficient time to stop. The fact finder must resolve that comparison from the vehicles’ timing, speed, spacing, and braking evidence.[85][76]

Time-to-collision (TTC) means the time it will take to reach an obstacle. It is a braking-relevant measure, and faster closing velocity leaves less time to detect, react, and brake.[85] The stated facts provide no distance or relative-speed inputs from which to calculate Rocha’s TTC.[85]

Research summarized in the Virginia Tech thesis reports that distracted drivers can have substantially delayed brake reactions when they fail to see conventional brake lights and that regular incandescent brake lamps failed to attract distracted drivers’ attention in the cited study.[85]

## Summary judgment and disposition

### Which facts or inferences are material to summary judgment on

This issue is not whether Daniel Rocha caused a collision in the abstract. It is whether a court may impose or reject **respondeat superior**—vicarious liability, meaning liability imposed on an employer for an employee’s tort—without a trial. The material facts are the ones that could change the result under the governing substantive law; a genuine dispute exists when the evidence would permit a reasonable jury to find for the party opposing the motion.[37][29]

No jurisdiction or movant is identified. The federal Rule 56 model therefore supplies the procedural framework, not a universal substantive rule: summary judgment is available only if the movant shows both no genuine dispute of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law.[26] The court must credit the nonmovant’s evidence and draw justifiable inferences in that party’s favor; it may not weigh competing evidence, decide credibility, or resolve a genuine dispute for the movant.[32][36][29]

The applicable scope rule remains jurisdiction-sensitive. Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228 defines conduct within scope as conduct of the kind the employee was hired to perform, substantially within authorized time and space limits, and motivated at least partly by serving the employer.[14] Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 instead asks whether the employee performed work assigned by the employer or engaged in conduct subject to its control, and excludes an independent course of conduct not intended to serve any employer purpose.[89][5] These formulations overlap on assignment, connection to work, and employer-serving purpose, but differ in the explicit role given to time-and-space limits.[13][89]

#### Employee status as a material issue

Employee status is material because respondeat superior ordinarily requires an employee relationship as well as a tort within the scope of employment. The Restatement (Third) defines an employee as an agent whose principal controls, or has the right to control, the manner and means of the agent’s work.[4] The Restatement (Second) similarly defines a servant as one who performs services in another’s affairs while subject to that other’s control or right of control over physical conduct.[9]

A delivery assignment, an assigned route, company policies, supervisor communications, and mileage reimbursement are probative of a company’s authority over Rocha’s work. They do not, standing alone, resolve the employee–independent-contractor classification; the multi-factor inquiry also considers such matters as control over details, tools, payment method, duration, regular-business integration, and the parties’ understanding of their relationship.[9][12] Texas’s Supreme Court has expressed the useful general principle that the inquiry concerns the employer’s **overall** right to control the worker’s progress, details, and methods, rather than control over one task or the lunch errand in isolation.[2]

Judgment — On the described facts, Rocha’s status is less visibly contested than scope, because the facts call him a delivery driver with an assigned route, supervisor directions, company policy, and company reimbursement. But it cannot be treated as conclusively established if the company adduces evidence that he retained contractor-level control over the manner and means of his delivery work. The material question is the company’s overall employment relationship with Rocha at the time of the June 14, 2024 collision, not whether it controlled his every driving decision that afternoon.

The legal consequence is straightforward: if Rocha’s employee status is genuinely disputed on competent evidence, neither side may convert the employment label into an undisputed premise on summary judgment; if it is undisputed, the court proceeds to the central scope-and-benefit issue.[9][33][2]

#### Scope of employment and the competing detour inferences

Scope of employment is the pivotal factual and inferential issue. Courts commonly assess whether the conduct was of the general kind assigned, occurred at a work-connected time and place, and was motivated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer.[14][21] The inquiry is objective: it asks whether the employee was doing work in furtherance of the employer’s business, with its authority, for its benefit—not merely how the employee characterizes the trip.[2][20]

The company has a substantial scope defense. Rocha was returning from a lunch stop four blocks off his assigned route, and company policy prohibited personal detours. An employer is not ordinarily responsible when an employee has departed from assigned duties to pursue a personal objective, even if the employer allowed some personal use of a vehicle or gave general permission for the deviation.[2] A short departure may still be outside scope if it substantially pursues an independent purpose unrelated to the employer’s business.[11] Texas cases summarized by the State Bar of Texas treat personal errands and ordinary lunch trips as potential frolics—substantial personal departures—rather than work activity; in the cited *Molina* example, an inspector returning from lunch had not resumed work duties and was not acting in the city’s interests.[20]

The sedan driver has materially different evidence. Rocha received a supervisor’s text directing him to pick up lunch for two warehouse coworkers.[54] The coworkers had requested the sandwiches that morning; that request supports an inference that Rocha was responding to a workplace need rather than simply buying his own meal.

The company’s revised policy allowed “team support errands” as of May 1, 2024, more than six weeks before the June 14 collision, and the company retroactively reimbursed mileage for the lunch detour after the collision on that policy rationale.[54] Rocha was on schedule and intended to complete deliveries immediately after lunch.[54]

Those facts support a **special-errand** inference: an employee sent away from the ordinary route to perform an employer-directed task may remain within scope, particularly where the task furthers the employer’s business with express or implied approval.[2][17] They also support a **dual-purpose** inference: a trip with both personal and business purposes can remain within scope if the work purpose created a necessity for the travel.[38][17] A personal motive does not necessarily negate scope if the conduct otherwise remains authorized and connected to the work.[15]

The company may answer that buying food is not a delivery driver’s ordinary job and that the lunch run was primarily a personal meal break. That argument matters under both Restatement formulations because scope does not arise merely from the fact that conduct occurs during the workday. General employment alone does not create an inference that a particular act was within scope; the inference strengthens where the act tends to accomplish an authorized purpose and occurs at an authorized time and place.[7] The company may also argue that retroactive mileage reimbursement cannot transform a purely personal deviation into assigned work, especially because reimbursement alone did not establish course and scope for a traveling consultant in the Texas *Silvas* example.[20]

The sedan driver’s contrary inference is not based on reimbursement alone. The supervisor’s instruction, the coworkers’ contemporaneous request, the policy effective before the collision, the four-block deviation, reimbursement, and Rocha’s prompt intended return together permit a factfinder to conclude that he was performing an authorized team-support task while remaining engaged in his delivery shift.[54] In *Painter*, evidence that a worker was assigned and paid to transport coworkers, and that the transportation benefited the employer by making the crew available for work, created a genuine scope issue and defeated the employer’s summary-judgment motion.[2][20] A lunch pickup is not crew transportation, but *Painter* confirms that helping coworkers can be an employer-serving function when the task was assigned and served operational purposes.

The four-block distance supports neither party conclusively. Under the detour–frolic distinction, a **detour** is a minor and temporary departure that does not sever the employment connection, while a **frolic** is a substantial departure for purely personal reasons. Courts consider the geographic deviation, time consumed, intent to return to work, and whether the side trip is common for workers in that role.[38][58] Four blocks is evidence favoring a minor deviation, but a short distance does not prevent a court or jury from finding that the actual purpose was independent and personal.[11]

A related **re-entry doctrine** may also matter. A substantial personal mission can take an employee outside the scope of employment, but completing that mission and resuming the employer’s errand can support a finding that the employee re-entered the scope.[90][22] If the factfinder first classifies the lunch excursion as a frolic, Rocha’s on-schedule status and declared intention to resume deliveries support re-entry; the precise collision location relative to his assigned route would determine how strongly that inference applies.[38][58] The doctrine prevents the company from treating a completed personal diversion as permanently severing employment, while preventing the plaintiff from treating every movement generally toward work as completed re-entry.[91][22]

The company policy against personal detours is important but not dispositive. In *Christensen v. Swenson*, the Utah Supreme Court held that an alleged prohibition on driving to a nearby café was evidence bearing on spatial scope, but not an automatic answer where other circumstances permitted competing inferences about an employee’s lunch trip.[21] Colorado’s model instruction likewise focuses on whether the negligent conduct furthered the employer’s business, rather than solely on whether the employer authorized the precise manner of performance.[11] An employee can act within scope by carrying out a work objective improperly; conversely, permission alone does not make a personal objective employer business.[2][21]

The deleted supervisor email must be handled with precision. The metadata confirms that an email existed.[54] Metadata is information about electronic information and may identify such characteristics as author, creation, editing, access, and prior versions.[62] Existence metadata may reinforce testimony about an email’s transmission or timing, subject to authentication through evidence sufficient to show the item is what its proponent claims.[63][62]

The metadata does not itself supply the email’s approving text. Business-record metadata may establish that an electronic record existed without proving its contents.[62] Authentication also does not itself resolve hearsay, original-writing, relevance, or other admissibility questions.[63][39][40] A supervisor or recipient with personal knowledge, reliable system evidence, distinctive characteristics, or admissible secondary evidence of the destroyed email may supply the needed foundation; an instruction itself may be non-hearsay if offered as a legally operative direction rather than for the truth of an assertion.[39][40]

That evidentiary limit does not erase the separately identified supervisor text directing the lunch pickup. The text is affirmative evidence of instruction.[54] The email’s existence may corroborate a communication occurred, while the email’s alleged approval language remains a matter for admissible proof rather than an assumed fact. This distinction makes the email material to the authorization dispute but prevents either side from treating metadata as dispositive.

The consequence is that scope cannot be decided by assigning conclusive weight to either the personal-detour policy or the employer-serving evidence. **The company’s policy and off-route stop support a personal-frolic inference, but the supervisor’s direction, coworker purpose, preexisting team-support policy, reimbursement, short deviation, and planned immediate return support an authorized-detour or special-errand inference; a factfinder must choose between those material accounts.**[2][11][54][21]

#### Employer benefit and ratification

Employer benefit is not always a freestanding element separate from scope, but it is a core scope consideration in the leading formulations. The Restatement (Second) asks whether the conduct was actuated at least partly by a purpose to serve the employer.[14] Texas describes course and scope as tasks generally assigned in furtherance of the employer’s business, with employer authority and for employer benefit.[2][20] Colorado similarly links vicarious liability to conduct motivated by an intent to serve the employer’s interests and connected to authorized work.[11]

The company’s strongest position is that the lunch trip principally benefited Rocha and the two coworkers as private consumers. If the sandwiches were merely a social favor, employee convenience is not automatically company benefit. The company can also argue that helping coworkers eat does not accomplish the object for which Rocha was hired—delivery work—and that no evidence identifies a company operational need for the purchase.

The sedan driver has an equally concrete response. A supervisor expressly directed the purchase for warehouse coworkers.[54] The company had already adopted a May 1, 2024 policy recognizing “team support errands,” then reimbursed the trip as such.[54] Those actions support an inference that the company itself regarded coworker support as a business-connected activity, rather than an entirely private favor.

The fact that the errand occurred during an active delivery shift, did not put Rocha behind schedule, and was followed by an intended immediate return to deliveries further supports a mixed-purpose rather than purely personal characterization.[54]

Employer benefit need not be exclusive. In *Christensen*, testimony that meal and restroom breaks benefit employees and employers because employers receive productive, satisfied workers helped create a factual dispute over whether a lunch trip served the employer at least in part.[21] The same case treated efforts to obtain food efficiently and return immediately to the work post as relevant to employer-serving motivation.[21] Rocha’s facts add a more direct employer connection: he was obtaining food for coworkers at a supervisor’s direction, not simply taking an individual meal break.[54]

Later reimbursement raises **ratification**, meaning a principal’s adoption of an agent’s previously unauthorized action. Colorado’s instruction states that ratification after an act has the same effect as prior authorization if the principal acted with full knowledge of material facts, and places the burden of proving such full-knowledge ratification on the party asserting it.[11] The reimbursement is therefore evidence from which a jury may infer company acceptance of the detour under the May 1 policy.

Its legal force depends on whether the company knew the material circumstances and whether the governing jurisdiction treats that acceptance as ratifying the relevant conduct.[11]

The reimbursement also has a narrower, independent significance. Employer-paid travel expenses can indicate that the employer involved itself in transportation as an incident of the employment relationship.[38][17] That inference is weakened by the fact that reimbursement occurred after the collision and by cases distinguishing reimbursement from an employee’s specifically compensated duty to transport coworkers.[20] It nonetheless conflicts with a categorical assertion that the company viewed the trip as wholly personal.

The resulting benefit issue is triable. **A reasonable factfinder could view the lunch pickup as a company-recognized support errand that aided warehouse coworkers and preserved Rocha’s delivery work, or as an employee meal-related favor with no meaningful business function; benefit and purpose therefore cannot be resolved by judicial weighing on this record.**[2][11][54][21]

#### Underlying negligence, causation, and injury

A vicarious-liability claim still requires an underlying tort. In a claim alleging driver negligence, the plaintiff must show negligence by the employee on the occasion in question; if the driver committed no tort, there is no tort for the employer to answer for vicariously.[28][11] The underlying-negligence questions remain relevant even though the company’s own conduct need not be separately shown as a factual cause once vicarious liability attaches.[83]

The dashcam shows Rocha glanced at his phone two seconds before impact. That is affirmative evidence of inattention. Phone use or looking away for even two seconds can turn an ordinary braking event into a collision, and a reasonable jury could infer that Rocha failed to maintain a proper lookout or safe following distance.[76] The rear-end impact itself is a fact; it is not, without more, conclusive proof of negligence.[77][79]

The sedan braked suddenly because fallen lumber obstructed Route 7. Braking for road debris is ordinarily foreseeable driving behavior, and sudden braking for debris does not inherently excuse a following driver from maintaining adequate distance.[76] The sedan’s brake lights functioned properly, supporting an inference that the sedan supplied a visible warning of braking.[77] Together with the phone glance, those facts support a strong plaintiff inference that Rocha’s inattention, rather than a hidden brake-light failure or inexplicable stop, caused the impact.

The hazard remains material to causation. The company can argue that fallen lumber created an emergency and that the sudden stop contributed to the collision. That contention does not eliminate negligence as a matter of law because avoiding impact depends on speed, following distance, reaction time, road grip, slope, tire condition, braking, visibility, and the timing and coverage of video evidence.[80] Reconstruction evidence can evaluate those factors through vehicle damage, debris, road conditions, driver actions, and video, but no reconstruction conclusion independently decides legal responsibility.[80]

The sedan driver claims neck strain, and medical imaging shows a soft-tissue injury.[54] The imaging supports the existence of a soft-tissue injury, not that this collision caused the claimed neck strain or produced compensable harm.

Injury-specific evidence is required to establish a causal connection to this impact and the claimed extent of injury.[54][87][41] Tort law requires tortious conduct to be a factual cause of harm—conduct without which the harm would not have occurred.[82] Medical imaging supports the existence of a soft-tissue injury, while collision-related causation and the compensable extent, duration, and monetary value of that injury remain separate issues requiring injury-specific proof.[54][87][41]

The consequence is that the underlying claim contains live fact questions as well as the employment question. **Rocha’s two-second phone glance and the working brake lights support negligence and causation inferences, while the lumber-triggered stop supports a competing causation account; the imaging-confirmed soft-tissue injury supports damages, so a court cannot select one account over the other at summary judgment if each is supported by admissible evidence.**[26][33][35][76][77][82]

The 2010 Committee Note to Rule 56(a) states that “The standard for granting summary judgment remains unchanged”: the court grants judgment only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.[26] The Restatement (Second) and Restatement (Third) of Agency are influential, persuasive sources of agency law, but they are not binding.[4]

The motion type and the allocation of burdens are also material. A traditional Texas summary-judgment movant must establish no genuine issue of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law; a defendant may do so by conclusively negating at least one claim element. A Texas no-evidence movant instead challenges the evidentiary support for an element, after which the nonmovant must produce more than a scintilla of evidence on each challenged element.[2] Under the analogous federal approach, the movant initially must state the motion’s basis and identify record materials showing the absence of a material fact; it need not submit affirmative evidence disproving the opponent’s claim and may point out an absence of evidence on an essential element.[31][34] Once that showing is made, the opposing party must identify specific record facts creating a material trial issue and may not rest on the pleadings alone. The opposing party must offer sufficient probative evidence to permit a favorable finding based on more than speculation, conjecture, or fantasy.[31][29]

The phone evidence has a separate potential work-connection significance. A collision during a work-related call, or a trip involving multiple work-related calls or texts, can support respondeat superior; by contrast, a one-minute work call eight or nine minutes before a crash during a personal multi-mile trip was too slight a connection in *Miller v. American Greetings Corp.* Rocha’s dashcam-recorded glance establishes neither that he was communicating for work nor what he viewed, so it supports negligent inattention but does not itself resolve whether the trip served the company.[51]

The route, instructions, timing, reason for the stop, distance from assigned work, duration of interruption, destination, and whether assigned duties had resumed are material scope facts. Written policy matters, but actual workplace practices and what the company knew or permitted also bear on authorization.[46] Thus, delivery logs, GPS data, work schedules, time records, the vehicle policy, supervisor messages, and coworker accounts bear directly on whether Rocha was still on an authorized detour or had re-entered delivery work.[46]

The record can test the competing route and re-entry inferences with work schedules, delivery logs, GPS data, time records, company vehicle policies, supervisor instructions, messages, and witness accounts. The destination, reason for the trip, duration of the interruption, and whether Rocha had resumed assigned duties are relevant factual questions.[46] GPS records, dispatch logs, and time-stamped communications can specifically show whether Rocha was performing work-related activity when the collision occurred.[38]

Fault allocation cannot be fixed without the governing jurisdiction: states use contributory negligence, pure comparative negligence, or modified comparative negligence.[81] The lumber, the sedan’s braking, Rocha’s attention, following distance, and the functioning brake lights are consequently material not only to Rocha’s breach and factual cause but also to any allocation of fault.[81]

### Can the company’s vicarious liability for the June 14

The answer is no on the stated record. The company has a plausible argument that Rocha’s four-block, off-route lunch stop violated its policy against personal detours and was a personal frolic. The sedan driver has a plausible contrary case that Rocha was carrying out a supervisor-directed, policy-recognized team-support errand for warehouse coworkers during his delivery shift, for which the company later reimbursed mileage.[2][11][54]

The summary-judgment rule does not permit a court to choose between those accounts. A genuine factual dispute requires trial where the evidence could permit a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmovant.[32] Scope is ordinarily a fact question, and it reaches the jury when reasonable minds may differ as to whether the employee was at least partly performing the employer’s business.[21][38] In *Christensen*, reasonable disagreement about a short lunch excursion’s work connection, spatial boundaries, and employer-serving purpose made summary judgment inappropriate.[21] In *Painter*, contrary evidence that the driver was performing an assigned, employer-benefiting transportation duty prevented summary judgment for the employer.[2]

The company therefore cannot obtain summary judgment by treating its prohibition on personal detours as dispositive. A policy violation may support its defense, but an improper or unauthorized method of carrying out an employment objective may remain within scope, and the court cannot disregard the supervisor text, policy date, reimbursement, and return-to-deliveries evidence merely because the company presents a competing characterization.[11][54][36][31]

The sedan driver likewise is not entitled to summary judgment merely because the evidence permits an employer-benefit inference. The plaintiff bears the burden of connecting the harmful conduct to Rocha’s job; benefit alone does not conclusively establish that the particular trip was within scope.[38][2] A reasonable factfinder could conclude that the meal-related detour remained Rocha’s independent personal departure despite the company’s later reimbursement and the asserted work connection.[2][11][20]

The email issue reinforces, rather than resolves, the need for factfinding. The metadata establishes an email existed.[54] It does not establish the email’s approving contents, and the approval inference must instead rest on admissible testimony, the independently identified supervisor text, authenticated electronic evidence, or other competent proof.[63][62][39] The email cannot be treated as conclusive authorization for the sedan driver, but neither can its deletion erase the actual supervisor-text evidence.

The negligence issues point in the same procedural direction. The company may argue that sudden braking for fallen lumber caused an unavoidable emergency. The sedan driver may argue that the dashcam-recorded phone glance, proper brake lights, and foreseeable road-debris braking show that Rocha could have avoided the crash had he attended to the road.[76][77] These are material competing inferences about breach and factual cause, and a summary-judgment court cannot decide them by weighing the parties’ evidence.[36][31]

**The court should deny a company motion seeking judgment that vicarious liability is excluded and deny a claimant motion seeking judgment establishing company liability. The supervisor’s text, May 1, 2024 team-support-errand policy, retroactive mileage reimbursement, and Rocha’s intended immediate return support an employer-serving inference, while the off-route stop and no-personal-detours policy support an independent-personal-deviation inference; those supported, outcome-changing inferences must be resolved by factfinding under the governing law.**[26][32][54][21]

On a company motion, the sedan driver must identify admissible evidence from which a reasonable factfinder could find employee status, an underlying driver tort, and scope of employment under the applicable rule. On the sedan driver’s motion, the driver must eliminate the reasonable inference that the lunch run was unauthorized and purely personal.

### Synthesis

The outcome-changing scope-and-benefit dispute is whether the four-block departure was a prohibited personal lunch deviation or a supervisor-directed, policy-recognized team-support errand for coworkers during Rocha’s delivery shift. The no-personal-detours policy and off-route stop support the former inference; the supervisor’s text, coworkers’ request, May 1, 2024 team-support-errand policy, later mileage reimbursement, and intended immediate return to deliveries support the latter.[2][11][54] It is whether that deviation was a prohibited personal lunch frolic or an authorized, employer-serving team-support detour during an active delivery shift.

The deleted-email metadata establishes that an email existed, not that it contained approval. The supervisor text remains separate evidence of direction.[54][63][62] The phone glance, sudden lumber-related braking, and functioning brake lights create competing breach-and-causation inferences.

Imaging establishes a soft-tissue injury’s existence, but injury-specific evidence must establish a collision-related compensable injury and its extent.[54][76][82][87][41] **The court should deny a company motion seeking judgment that liability is excluded and deny a claimant motion seeking judgment establishing company liability, leaving the supported, outcome-changing inferences about authorization, employer-serving purpose, scope, negligence, causation, and injury for factfinding under the governing law.**[26][32][54][21]

The central consideration is the company’s right to control the details of Rocha’s performance, whether or not it actually exercised that right.[11]

Colorado’s formulation supplies further work-connection categories: an employee acts within scope when doing work assigned by the employer, work proper, usual, and necessary to accomplish assigned work, or work customary in the trade or business to accomplish assigned work.[11] A traveling employee need not be performing work at the precise moment to remain in the course of employment.[11] Colorado cites *Hynes v. If employee status is disputed, evidence about who set Rocha’s route and hours, supplied or controlled the vehicle, and controlled the method of delivery bears directly on the company’s right to control his work.

### Open questions

The governing jurisdiction and the identity of the summary-judgment movant remain open. The outcome would be sharpened by the forum’s employee-status, scope-of-employment, deviation, comparative-fault, and summary-judgment rules, and by admissible proof of Rocha’s work arrangement, the supervisor text, the deleted email’s contents, the May 1 policy’s terms, the reimbursement basis, Rocha’s precise route and destination at impact, and the collision and medical causation evidence.
