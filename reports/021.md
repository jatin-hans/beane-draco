# Deepfake Detection Research, Performance, Ethics and Regulation Since 2022

## Executive summary

Deepfake detection is useful as a probabilistic triage and forensic-support capability, not as a universal proof of authenticity or falsity. Controlled benchmarks can produce strong cross-dataset results, such as TALL-Swin’s 90.79% AUC when trained on FaceForensics++ and tested on CelebDF, but contemporary in-the-wild evaluation found a highest open-source AUC of only 0.58 and average relative AUC declines of 50% for video and 48% for audio.[1][2] The most credible technical direction combines video, speech, and audio-visual consistency analysis with transferable pretrained representations, continual adaptation, provenance signals, and calibrated human review rather than reliance on a single artifact detector.

Enacted EU and US measures increasingly require transparency, labelling or removal processes, and remedies for harmful synthetic intimate media, but their effectiveness must not depend on detectors making error-free automated judgments.[3][4][5]

This review covers deepfake-detection research published from 2022 onward. It distinguishes a study’s publication date from the release date of any older benchmark dataset used in its evaluation.

## Research landscape since 2022

### What is the current state of deepfake detection research since

Deepfake detection is now a moving-target problem. A **deepfake** is manipulated existing media or newly generated synthetic image, video, or audio made with deep-learning methods; the term therefore covers face swaps, face reenactment, entirely generated video, synthetic speech, voice conversion, and audio-visual composites rather than one single technical object.[6] Since 2022, the central task has shifted from recognizing the artifacts of a small set of known generators to deciding whether a detector can recognize unfamiliar generators, post-processing, languages, modalities, and deployment contexts.

Judgment — Detection research has three linked layers. First, *content forensics* asks whether the pixels, frames, waveform, or cross-modal relationships contain evidence of synthesis or manipulation. Second, *provenance* asks whether trustworthy metadata, signatures, or watermarks identify the origin and history of media. Third, *operational assessment* asks whether a system’s output is calibrated and explainable enough for a platform, journalist, investigator, court, or user to act on it. The strongest present approach is therefore layered: detection is necessary, but it cannot by itself establish authenticity.

The visual field remains large but unevenly distributed. A review of more than 400 studies published from 2022 to 2025 found that 98% of detection publications it identified concerned synthetic images and only 2% concerned videos, even though generation work was less imbalanced, at 68% images and 32% videos.[7] A separate 2026 corpus review found that, among 1,108 papers with dataset information, image-based datasets appeared in 71.1%, video datasets in 33.0%, multimodal datasets in 11.2%, text-associated resources in 10.4%, and audio datasets in 5.9%; the categories overlap because a paper may use more than one modality.[8] This means that progress reported for face crops and still images should not be assumed to transfer to full video, speech, or synchronized audio-video media.

The video-detection literature has moved beyond a predominantly frame-level formulation. Gu, Chen, Yao, and Ding’s 2022 AAAI work, *Delving into the Local: Dynamic Inconsistency Learning for DeepFake Video Detection*, argued that sparse frame sampling misses informative local motion between adjacent frames. Its method uses a short successive-frame unit called a *snippet*, with bi-directional temporal differences to learn short-term motion and alternating intra- and inter-snippet modules that can be attached to two-dimensional convolutional neural networks (CNNs).[9] Later work has extended this logic to pixel-wise temporal-frequency analysis: Kim and colleagues’ ICCV 2025 approach applies a one-dimensional Fourier transform along time at each pixel, targeting temporal inconsistencies that ordinary spatial-frequency detectors can miss.[10]

A **CNN** is a neural network that learns local spatial patterns through convolutional filters; it remains a common visual backbone. A **transformer** is a neural-network architecture built around self-attention, a mechanism that weights relationships among distant tokens, patches, or frames. Transformers are attractive for deepfake video analysis because synthetic defects may be sparse, non-local, and temporally inconsistent. For example, the Spatiotemporal Dropout Transformer reorganizes a video as a bag of patches for a vision transformer, while another recent system uses separate spatial and temporal branches within a visual-transformer architecture.[11][12] The later “Transformer-based architectures” section examines these models in technical detail; their landscape significance is that attention-based models have become a major alternative to CNNs rather than a wholesale replacement for them.

This diversification is visible in a 1,410-paper corpus review: CNN-based approaches accounted for 266 papers (18.9%), transformer-based methods for 207 (14.7%), and foundation-model-based approaches for 121 (8.6%). The same review counted 28 foundation-model papers in 2024 and 50 in 2025, while observing that classic CNNs remained larger in aggregate.[8] These counts describe research attention, not a controlled architecture comparison; nevertheless, they show a field adding pretrained and attention-based representations to, rather than abandoning, conventional forensic classifiers.

A **foundation model** is a large model pretrained on broad, diverse data and then adapted to a narrower task. In visual detection, this has produced parameter-efficient systems that retain a large pretrained encoder and tune only small task-specific modules. A 2025 CVPR framework, for example, uses a CLIP image encoder—CLIP is a pretrained model linking visual and textual representations—with a side-network decoder to extract spatial and temporal cues from video.[13] A separate GenD method adapts only Layer Normalization parameters, stated as 0.03% of the model’s parameters, while using L2-normalized features and metric-learning losses to shape a transferable feature space.[14] The “Foundation model integration” section addresses the methods and evidence separately; at landscape level, their rise reflects the attempt to learn broader visual or speech representations before learning the real/fake distinction.

The corresponding audio field treats synthetic or converted speech as both a media-authenticity problem and an anti-spoofing problem for **automatic speaker verification (ASV)**, systems that decide whether a claimed speaker is genuine. Speech deepfakes primarily include **text-to-speech (TTS)**, which generates speech from text, and **voice conversion (VC)**, which changes a source speaker’s voice to resemble a target speaker.[15][6] Deep-learning-based methods increasingly learn representations directly from raw waveform or spectrogram data; the 2023 survey groups these representations into learnable spectral features, supervised embeddings, and self-supervised embeddings.[16]

AASIST, introduced at ICASSP 2022, is a representative audio anti-spoofing architecture. It combines sinc convolution, a RawNet2-based encoder, graph learning, and graph attention across spectral and temporal representations before classifying an utterance as bona fide or spoofed.[17][18] **Self-supervised learning (SSL)** is also now central: it pretrains a model on unlabelled speech, allowing it to learn broad speech structure before fine-tuning for spoof detection.

The 2025 audio-detection review describes SSL representations as the prevailing direction for adaptation to unseen attacks, while retaining handcrafted features as useful for low-resource settings.[19]

Speech synthesis has changed the problem faster than many legacy benchmarks. Audio Language Models (ALMs), which combine advances in large language models and neural audio codecs, lower the practical barrier to producing varied synthetic speech.[20] The 2025 Codecfake work describes conventional countermeasures trained only on vocoded audio as vulnerable to ALM-based speech and constructed Codecfake, an open-source collection comprising over 1 million English and Chinese audio samples focused on ALM-based audio detection.[21] This is the audio equivalent of the visual transition from earlier GAN-driven face manipulation toward diffusion-based and wholly generated media: detectors trained to recognize a generator family’s incidental artifacts may fail when the generative mechanism changes.[22][23]

The field’s principal scientific problem is therefore **generalization**: retaining performance when media are generated, processed, recorded, distributed, or expressed in a language differently from the training data. This is the shift from recognizing artifacts of a known generator to learning spatial, temporal, and audio-visual regularities that persist across unfamiliar generators, datasets, languages, and conditions.[24][25] Video papers pursue it through self-supervision, manipulation-invariant features, synthetic hard negatives, temporal artifacts, adapters, and frequency or spatial cues.

UCF, for example, separates forgery-irrelevant, method-specific, and common forgery features through multi-task learning and contrastive regularization, so that the classifier can rely more heavily on features shared across methods.[24] RealForensics instead learns from large quantities of authentic talking-face video without labels; it used the 500,000-video LRW dataset and reported benefits from increasing the amount of real video, with competitive results also reported using VoxCeleb2’s approximately one million talking-face videos.[26]

Researchers have also moved from whole-item real/fake classification toward localization and attribution. **Forgery localization** identifies where and when manipulation occurs, while **source attribution** aims to identify the generating model or method. In a 717-paper subset with explicit formulation labels, binary real-versus-fake classification appeared in 499 papers (69.6%), localization in 64 (8.9%), source attribution in 37 (5.2%), and multi-class manipulation classification in 17 (2.4%).[8] AV-Deepfake1M illustrates why this matters: it was designed for temporal localization of short manipulated audio, visual, or audio-visual segments within otherwise authentic videos, rather than assuming a full clip is entirely real or fake.[27]

**Benchmark infrastructure has become a research object in its own right.** DeepfakeBench (Yan et al. (2023, neural information processing systems, 'DeepfakeBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark of Deepfake Detection'), 2023) standardized comparison of 15 visual deepfake detectors across nine datasets through consistent inputs, implementations, metrics, and protocols, and reported within-domain and cross-domain evaluations.[28][29][30] It supplied a unified data-management system, common implementations, and standardized metrics and protocols across 15 methods and nine datasets.[29][28] Its reported full evaluation trained 15 detectors on FaceForensics++ c23 and tested them across 14 datasets using area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC), Average Precision (AP), and Equal Error Rate (EER).[31] AUC measures how well a scoring system ranks fake above real examples over all thresholds; AP summarizes precision-recall performance, particularly useful when classes are imbalanced; EER is the point where false-accept and false-reject rates are equal.

DeepfakeBench’s initial protocol sampled 32 frames per video, aligned detected faces, resized them to 256 × 256 pixels and reported frame-level results.[95][29][139]

Yet standardization has limits. DeepfakeBench originally concentrated on frame-level algorithms and did not incorporate temporal-based detectors, although its later repository states that version 2 supports 36 methods, including 28 image and eight video detectors.[31][32] Its evaluation procedure also selected the model with the highest test-set performance rather than using a validation set for selection, which is important context for interpreting headline results.[31] The benchmark resolves inconsistency between implementations, but it does not by itself make familiar academic data representative of platform media, live calls, or forensic casework.

This distinction appears in controlled cross-dataset testing. TALL-Swin reported 90.79% AUC on the FaceForensics++ to CelebDF transfer task, and the 2022 Self-Blended Images paper reported gains over its baseline of 4.90 percentage points on DFDC and 11.78 points on DFDCP under cross-dataset evaluation.[1][33] These are meaningful results because their test distributions differ from their training distributions. They nevertheless remain curated benchmark transfers rather than direct evidence of live deployment performance, an issue developed in “Benchmark performance versus real-world deployment.”

The practical stakes have broadened with media creation. The 2024 review of deepfake definitions notes that more than 10,000 deepfake-generation tools were available by 2024.[6] A 2024 UK survey of 1,403 adults found 82.7% had heard of deepfakes and 8.1% self-reported creating one for education, entertainment, or other purposes.[34] The same survey recorded reported exposure to entertainment-oriented deepfakes, political deepfakes, misinformation or propaganda, non-consensual pornographic deepfakes, and fraud or scams.[34] These findings do not measure online prevalence, but they show why the detection agenda now extends beyond face-swap video to voice fraud, intimate-image abuse, election communications, and synthetic evidence.

The research agenda has also become more explicitly safety-conscious. Datasets can reproduce harmful media, expose identities, and enable misuse. Deepfake-Eval-2024 restricts access: prospective users must accept terms, provide an institutional or company email and evidence of relevant work, and the authors state that gated access will continue until May 2027.[2] SynthForensics screened 1,363 prompts during construction, revising 70 flagged by human reviewers and 38 flagged by automated screening.[35] These practices make benchmark access and dataset construction part of responsible research design, not merely administrative detail.

Fairness is another live research question. The 2022 study *Analyzing Fairness in Deepfake Detection With Massively Annotated Databases* released more than 65.3 million labels over 47 demographic and non-demographic attributes for five datasets and evaluated three detector backbones across four datasets and 31 attributes.[36] Its tables show that error can vary across both demographic and appearance attributes; for example, for the “Big Nose” attribute, reported balanced error was 35.97% for the negative group and 28.28% for the positive group, while pristine error was 65.55% and 37.96%, respectively.[36] A detection system that unevenly flags or clears people across groups can create unequal burdens in moderation, verification, journalism, and criminal investigation.

The state of the field is thus advanced but not settled. The 2024 meta-review identifies robust, scalable, generalizable, and explainable detection as persistent priorities in an arms race between generation and detection.[6] The 2025 video-generalization review similarly identifies overfitting to available datasets as a major limitation because it reduces effectiveness on unseen deepfake types.[37] **The resulting research direction is clear: useful detectors must learn signals that survive change, report uncertainty honestly, and operate alongside provenance and human review rather than claim a permanent, universal artifact signature.**[7][38]

This establishes why the following method sections distinguish video, audio, generalization, transformers, multimodal analysis, foundation models, and privacy-preserving detection: they are complementary responses to different parts of the same generalization and deployment problem.

Evidence current as of 19 November 2026. Since 2022, deepfake detection has become a media-forensics research landscape rather than a single classifier-design problem. Its linked technical strands are:

- **Video detection:** methods examine spatial artifacts within frames and temporal irregularities across frames, including the transition from GAN-based to diffusion-model-driven deepfakes and pixel-wise temporal-frequency analysis. [22][9][10]
- **Audio detection:** systems distinguish bona fide speech from text-to-speech, voice-converted, vocoded, and audio-language-model speech using learned spectral, supervised, and self-supervised representations. [19][21][16]
- **Cross-dataset generalization:** this is the ability to retain performance when test media come from a different dataset, generator, language, manipulation pipeline, or recording condition; feature disentanglement and broader pretrained representations are principal responses. [24][25]
- **Transformer-based architectures:** transformers use self-attention to relate distant image patches or video elements, supporting patch-based video representations and separate spatial-temporal analysis. [11][12]
- **Multimodal audio-visual analysis:** these methods test agreement between what is heard and what is seen, and increasingly detect and localize audio-only, visual-only, and combined manipulations embedded in otherwise authentic video. [27][39]
- **Foundation-model integration:** pretrained models such as CLIP and speech foundation models are adapted to detection so that broad visual or speech representations can support transfer to unfamiliar forgeries and languages. [13][25]
- **Privacy-preserving detection:** federated learning trains a shared detector across decentralized devices without centralizing their training data; FedForgery combines this approach with variational-autoencoder residual features for generalized face-forgery detection. [40]
- **Evaluation infrastructure:** standardized benchmarks seek comparable preprocessing and metrics, while in-the-wild collections test contemporary media drawn from social media and detection-platform users. [28][41]

Provenance infrastructure complements forensic classification. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) Content Credentials are designed to attach cryptographically verifiable records of a work’s capture or generation and subsequent edits, although they cannot authenticate media with absent or stripped credentials.

Secure aggregation, differential privacy, encrypted or split computation, and on-device inference can reduce exposure of face, voice, and communications data, but model updates and embeddings may themselves disclose information unless protected.

### What developments in synthetic video and speech

The media to be detected has changed in kind as well as quality. A 2024 survey traces facial deepfake generation from GAN-driven manipulation to diffusion-model-driven content and identifies the newer systems’ realism and resistance to detection as new challenges.[22] The Interpol review further distinguishes traditional manipulations of existing media—face swaps, reenactment, attribute editing, and lip-syncing—from media in which an image or video modality is wholly synthesized; it stresses that these categories are generated by different architectures and leave different traces.[7] A detector optimized for altered faces is therefore not automatically a detector of modern text-to-video output.

Fully synthetic video presents a particular gap. Existing staples such as FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF, and DFDC were designed primarily for manipulation detection, and SynthForensics argues that their face-centric focus on alteration artifacts is inadequate for pure text-to-video generation.[35] SynthForensics responds with 6,815 human-centric videos generated by five open-source text-to-video systems.[35] This shift enlarges the target from “was this face altered?” to “was this audiovisual scene generated?”, requiring methods that can address people, objects, physics, motion, backgrounds, and narrative context.

Synthetic video still displays potential forensic clues. The Interpol review identifies unresolved spatiotemporal consistency problems such as texture sticking, unnatural merges, inaccurate physics, and objects appearing or disappearing across frames.[7] However, these are not guaranteed signatures: a generator can improve, a video can be edited or compressed, and an authentic video can contain unusual motion or visual defects.

The technical aim is consequently not to find one permanent defect but to combine visual, temporal, semantic, and provenance evidence, as discussed in the subsequent video and multimodal sections.

Speech generation has become more realistic, more expressive, and more transferable. VALL-E demonstrated zero-shot synthesis that can preserve a prompt’s reverberant acoustic environment and emotion, while VALL-E 2 is presented by Microsoft Research as reaching human parity in zero-shot TTS. These developments matter for detection because voice cloning can reproduce not only linguistic content but also speaker identity, emotional style, and environmental cues that listeners might treat as authenticity signals.[42][43]

The broadening of synthesis is reflected in audio datasets. ASVspoof 2021 contains 20,637 bona fide and 572,616 spoofed samples produced using more than 100 attack algorithms.[6] ADD 2023 contains 243,194 real and 273,874 fake utterances and supports detection, manipulation-region localization, and algorithm recognition.[6] VoiceWukong includes speech produced with 19 commercial and 15 open-source tools, covering 29 TTS and five VC methods; it contains 265,200 English and 148,200 Chinese fake voices, with an equal number of real voices in each language.[44] These resources expand the variety of known attacks, but the rapid arrival of commercial tools and codec-based models keeps the unknown-generator problem central.

ASVspoof 5 Track 1 evaluates utterance-level bona fide-versus-spoof classification, using minimum detection cost function (minDCF) as its primary metric and EER and log-likelihood-ratio cost as secondary metrics.[141]

Large multimodal datasets also expose a limitation of conventional binary labels. AV-Deepfake1M contains fake audio over real video, real audio over fake video, and both fake audio and fake visuals. Its fake speech was generated with VITS and its lip-synchronized visual frames with TalkLip.[6] This design permits a detector to ask which modality was manipulated and when, rather than treating audio and visual evidence as interchangeable. It supports the emerging premise that cross-modal agreement can provide evidence even when neither modality alone has an obvious artifact.

The benchmark-to-deployment gap has become concrete. Deepfake-Eval-2024 contains 1,072 real and 964 fake video files, totaling 45.1 video hours, plus 1,167 real and 814 fake audio files totaling 56.5 audio hours in one comparison table; its combined audio-video collection spans 52 languages, although 78.7% is English.[2] The paper reports that off-the-shelf open-source systems had average relative AUC declines of 50% for video, 48% for audio, and 45% for images compared with the datasets used in their original publications; the highest AUC reported across open-source models and modalities was 0.58.[2] The later deployment section considers these results in detail, but their landscape implication is immediate: an academic detector score and a real-world operating score are different quantities.

Fine-tuning on representative recent data can materially improve this result without solving it. The Deepfake-Eval-2024 authors fine-tuned models on 60% of their data and tested on the remaining 40%, reporting average AUC improvements of 57.6% for video and 80.6% for audio; peak accuracy reached 0.75 for video and 0.86 for audio.[2] This supports continual adaptation as an operational requirement, not a one-off model release. It also indicates that representative data can help more than architectural novelty alone when the incoming media distribution changes.

Audio evaluations show the same pattern. VoiceWukong found that AASIST2, the best of 12 tested detectors, had EER of 13.50% on English and 13.54% on Chinese synthetic voices, compared with 0.82% EER on its original evaluation dataset. All other evaluated systems had EER above 20% on both language sets.[44] EER is an error metric, so lower values are better; this comparison shows that the real difficulty is not only discriminating real and fake speech but retaining that discrimination across new generators, languages, and recording conditions.

Governance commitments have developed alongside this technical shift. Their common direction is toward transparency, provenance, platform response, rights against harmful impersonation, and protection against sexual or election-related abuse. The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, and its Article 50 transparency obligations applied from 2 August 2026, with a transition until 2 December 2026 for Article 50(2) marking.[3] The Act’s relevance to detection research is practical: disclosure and machine-readable marking create an incentive for interoperable provenance checks, while detector limitations mean that policy cannot presume an automated classifier can make definitive authenticity decisions.

The EU also enacted Directive (EU) 2024/1385 on combating violence against women and domestic violence on 14 May 2024; it is in force, and Member States must transpose it by 14 June 2027.[4] Its recitals call for online or other accessible and secure information-and-communication-technology channels to report specified cybercrimes, including non-consensual sharing of intimate or manipulated material.[4] The United States has followed a more fragmented federal-and-state path: federally, the TAKE IT DOWN Act was signed on 19 May 2025, with its criminal prohibition effective immediately and covered platforms required to establish their notice-and-removal process by 19 May 2026.[5] State measures increasingly combine political-advertising disclosures, remedies for non-consensual intimate deepfakes, and rules on digital replicas; these are summarized in “United States regulatory frameworks.”

International governance is less deepfake-specific but increasingly relevant to the surrounding risk environment. G7 Digital and Tech Ministers endorsed the Hiroshima AI Process Comprehensive Policy Framework on 1 December 2023 as guiding principles and a code of conduct for advanced AI systems.[48] UN Member States adopted the Pact for the Future and its Global Digital Compact on 22 September 2024, committing to an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and a Global Dialogue on AI Governance; the General Assembly established the Dialogue by consensus on 26 August 2025.[49][50]

These commitments do not create a universal global deepfake-detection rule. They instead establish a governance environment in which technical systems must support transparency, risk assessment, accountability, and human oversight across jurisdictions. The “EU regulatory frameworks,” “United States regulatory frameworks,” and “International regulatory frameworks” sections distinguish binding provisions from proposals and voluntary commitments.

**The developments since 2022 therefore make provenance-aware, continuously evaluated, multimodal detection more important, while also making any claim of a single definitive detector less credible.** Synthetic media is increasingly varied; benchmark design is increasingly realistic and standardized; and governance is increasingly focused on disclosure, harmful use, and response mechanisms.[22][41][29][3][5][49]

Audio threats also include replayed or re-recorded speech and diffusion-based synthesis, alongside text-to-speech, voice conversion, and neural-codec systems. ASVspoof 2021 made transmission-channel and compression effects explicit in controlled speech-spoofing evaluation, helping show that a detector’s performance depends on the route by which audio reaches it.

Voluntary provenance and response commitments have also emerged alongside binding law. The 2024 Tech Accord to Combat Deceptive Use of AI in 2024 Elections committed participating technology companies to develop and deploy practices including detection, provenance, transparency, and responses to deceptive election-related AI content; it is an industry commitment rather than legislation.

New multimodal benchmark infrastructure broadens the manipulation configurations under test. DeepfakeBench-MM (2025) lists 11 multimodal methods and five datasets and supports cross-multimodal-dataset evaluation, cross-pipeline evaluation, official train/validation/test splits, and code access.[51] Its Mega-MMDF dataset contains 1.2 million audio-visual samples—100,000 real and 1.1 million fake—totalling 1,723 hours, with mean clip duration of 5.17 seconds and 930 GB of MP4 data.[51] Mega-MMDF uses 22 pipelines—one real and 21 forgery pipelines—and covers real audio/real video (RARV), real audio/fake video (RAFV), fake audio/real video (FARV), and fake audio/fake video (FAFV).[51]

### Synthesis

Since 2022, deepfake detection has developed from a largely image- and face-artifact-centered task into a broader media-forensics discipline spanning video dynamics, synthetic speech, audio-visual consistency, localization, source attribution, pretrained models, and privacy-conscious collaboration. Standardized benchmarks such as DeepfakeBench have improved comparability, while large audio-visual, multilingual, and in-the-wild datasets have made distribution shift visible rather than theoretical.[28][27][41][44]

The strongest pattern is not uniformly rising detector reliability but a race between expanding synthesis capability and attempts to generalize beyond known data. Controlled cross-dataset results demonstrate real progress, yet Deepfake-Eval-2024 and VoiceWukong show that systems can lose substantial accuracy or incur much higher error on contemporary media.[1][2][44] Policy developments accordingly favor disclosure, provenance, reporting, removal, and remedies alongside detection, because detection remains a necessary but incomplete component of trustworthy media governance.[3][4][5][38]

## Video detection methods

Deepfake video detection asks whether a moving image has been manipulated or wholly generated. Since 2022, the field has moved beyond treating video as a set of independent face images. Modern systems seek evidence within frames, across adjacent and distant frames, in frequency representations, and in facial behaviour. This matters because a detector that learns only one generator’s visual artefacts can fail when the generator, compression path, or manipulation type changes.

### What recent technical methods detect deepfake video?

The starting taxonomy remains useful. **Naïve detectors** use a convolutional neural network (CNN)—a model that learns visual patterns through local image filters—to classify real versus fake directly. **Spatial detectors** add mechanisms for localisation, reconstruction, blending-boundary analysis, capsule representations, or feature disentanglement. **Frequency detectors** transform visual input into a frequency representation, seeking statistical traces such as phase irregularities or high-frequency noise. DeepfakeBench’s 2023 unified benchmark includes 15 detector methods—MesoNet, MesoInception, CNN-Aug, EfficientNet-B4, Xception, Capsule, DSP-FWA, Face X-ray, FFD, CORE, RECCE, UCF, F3Net, SPSL, and SRM—and nine datasets: FaceForensics++ (FF++), CelebDF-v1, CelebDF-v2, DeepFakeDetection (DFD), DeepFake Detection Challenge Preview (DFDC-P), DeepFake Detection Challenge (DFDC), UADFV, FaceShifter, and DeeperForensics-1.0.[31][29]

Xception uses depthwise separable convolutions; FaceForensics++ includes raw videos and H.264-compressed c23 and c40 variants, with c23 retaining higher quality than c40.[95][29]

- **Spatial and blending-artifact learning:** Shiohara and Yamasaki’s CVPR 2022 Self-Blended Images (SBI) method creates pseudo-fakes by transforming one real face into pseudo-source and pseudo-target versions, then blending them with a facial mask. The aim is to teach a classifier blending boundaries and statistical inconsistencies that are shared across manipulations rather than generator-specific texture patterns. SBI trains an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B4 without using manipulated faces even for validation. [52] Shiohara and Yamasaki report two distinct FaceForensics++ cross-manipulation evaluations. For their primary EfficientNet-B4 SBI detector, evaluated by training on three FaceForensics++ manipulation subsets and testing on the held-out subset, AUC was 99.99% for DeepFakes, 99.88% for Face2Face, 99.91% for FaceSwap, 98.79% for NeuralTextures, and 99.64% for the whole set. [52] In a separate architecture-comparison table using the SBI training-data method, Xception + SBIs achieved AUCs of 99.99%, 99.90%, 98.79%, 98.20%, and 99.22%, respectively, for DeepFakes, Face2Face, FaceSwap, NeuralTextures, and the whole FaceForensics++ set. [52]

- **Frequency-aware visual analysis:** M2TR, published by Wang and colleagues at ICMR 2022, combines an RGB stream with a frequency stream. Multi-scale transformers inspect patches at different spatial scales, learnable filters extract frequency-domain evidence, and a cross-modality fusion block combines the two streams. Here, “multi-modal” means RGB plus frequency-domain information, not audio plus video. M2TR also predicts a manipulated-region mask alongside the real/fake label, using localisation supervision to reduce overfitting. [53] DeepfakeBench similarly describes SPSL as combining the image with its phase spectrum to capture up-sampling artefacts, SRM as combining RGB and high-frequency noise, and F3Net as using learnable frequency bands and local frequency statistics. [29]

Frequency methods can improve transfer when their cue survives processing. In DeepfakeBench’s frame-level cross-data experiment, an improved Face-Warping Artefacts (iFWA) model that adds spectrum features achieved an average AUC of 0.8035 across FF++ c23, FF++ c40, Celeb-DF-v2, DFDCP, and DFD, compared with 0.7316 for FWA without the spectrum feature.[54] Yet frequency traces are not universal: DeepfakeBench found no obvious average-spectrum artefacts in its processed deepfake images and attributed this to resizing, blending, and MPEG compression; diffusion-generated imagery also exhibited fewer visible frequency artefacts than GAN-generated imagery in its visualisation.[29] **Frequency evidence is therefore a valuable input channel, not a stand-alone signature of all synthetic video.**

A second group of methods models **temporal inconsistency**: unnatural changes across frames that may not be visible in a still image. Gu, Chen, Yao, and Ding’s AAAI 2022 Dynamic Inconsistency Learning uses a *snippet*—a short run of successive frames—as its basic unit. Its Intra-SIM module uses bidirectional temporal differences to mine short-term motion inside each snippet, while its complementary module links information across snippets; both can be added to a two-dimensional CNN. This design targets an important failure mode of sparse frame sampling: it can miss local motion that carries manipulation evidence.[9]

Other temporal detectors use different signals:

- **Long-distance attention:** Lu and colleagues’ 2023 IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems model treats forgery detection as fine-grained classification. It has separate spatial and temporal components, each using long-distance attention to capture respectively within-frame generative defects and inconsistencies across consecutive frames. [55]

- **Motion and expression features:** A 2024 expression-key-sequence system first identifies dynamic facial-expression sequences through optical-flow differences, then uses spatial and temporal branches to analyse expression-relevant regions and inconsistency between adjacent frames. [12] DFGaze, published by Peng and colleagues in IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security in 2024, similarly analyses spatial-temporal gaze inconsistency, supplemented by texture and facial-attribute modules. [56]

A 2024 temporal-coherence framework examined facial-region colour patterns through global and local-successive temporal views, using global temporal self-attention and high- and low-resolution features. It was evaluated on FaceForensics++, DeepFakeDetection, DFDC, Celeb-DF v2 and DeeperForensics, with reported effectiveness on unseen fake types.[142]

MSVT, the 2023 Multiple Spatiotemporal Views Transformer, uses relational modelling across space and time.[142]

- **Physiological and behavioural cues:** Blinking, head swings, heart-rate signals, and lip motion are recurrent candidates for detecting behaviour that looks implausible over time. **Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG)** estimates a pulse from subtle variations in skin colour over time and is therefore one way to derive the heart-rate signal used in this line of work. [56] These methods seek higher-level signs of natural human movement rather than only synthesis artefacts.

UMCL combines remote-photoplethysmography signals, facial-landmark dynamics and text-prompt embeddings, with cross-quality similarity learning connecting high- and low-quality representations.[143]

With 50% of sampled frames retained, UMCL reported AUCs of 92.30% on raw video, 92.81% on c23-compressed video and 80.04% on c40-compressed video—respectively 23.91, 26.53 and 23.71 percentage points above CPML.[143]

In cross-manipulation testing, UMCL improved by 3.91 percentage points when trained on DeepFakes and achieved 90.41% AUC on NeuralTextures, versus 93.85% for Face X-ray.[143]

With opposite text prompts, UMCL retained AUC above 88% across raw, c23 and c40 video, while CPML fell to 10%; with unrelated prompts, UMCL achieved 87–93% AUC versus CPML’s 42–48%.[143]

- **Temporal-frequency analysis:** Wang and colleagues’ 2023 FCAN-DCT analyses both spatial and temporal frequency domains. It uses a discrete cosine transform-based Forgery Clue Augmentation Network with a Compact Feature Extraction module and a Frequency Temporal Attention module. [57] Kim and colleagues’ ICCV 2025 approach goes further by applying a one-dimensional Fourier transform over time at each pixel, making pixel-wise temporal-frequency features sensitive to unnatural movement. Its Attention Proposal Module selects regions likely to contain temporal artefacts; a joint transformer combines those features with spatial-temporal context. [10]

The central engineering problem is that a nominally spatiotemporal model can still shortcut on only one cue type. Wang and colleagues’ CVPR 2023 **AltFreezing** divides a 3D convolutional network’s parameters into spatial-related and temporal-related groups, alternately freezing one group during training so the other must learn. It also generates video-level augmentations: temporal dropout, temporal repetition, and clip-level blending.[58] In its FaceForensics++ leave-one-manipulation-out test, AltFreezing reported video-level AUCs of 99.8% for DeepFakes, 99.7% for FaceSwap, 98.6% for Face2Face, and 96.2% for NeuralTextures, averaging 98.6%.[58] When trained on FaceForensics++ and tested on Celeb-DF-v2, DFD, FaceShifter, and DeeperForensics, it reported AUCs of 89.5%, 98.5%, 99.4%, and 99.3%, respectively, averaging 96.7%.[58]

Transformer architectures have become a major route for connecting dispersed visual and temporal evidence. A **vision transformer (ViT)** represents a frame as patches and uses self-attention—learned weighting of relationships among patches—to aggregate information over broad image regions. Video transformers extend that logic over frames and patches.

- **TALL:** Xu and colleagues’ ICCV 2023 Thumbnail Layout for Deepfake Video Detection transforms consecutive frames into a fixed thumbnail layout. It masks the same position in each frame, resizes the frames into sub-images, and arranges them in a structured composite. TALL-Swin uses that representation with a Swin Transformer, allowing an image-oriented transformer to retain clip order and spatial-temporal dependencies. [1]

- **Spatiotemporal Dropout Transformer:** Zhang and colleagues’ ACM MM 2022 approach reorganises a video into a bag of patches for a vision transformer and applies spatiotemporal dropout as training augmentation. The dropout operation is intended to force learning from patch-level spatial-temporal cues rather than a narrow subset of artefacts. [11]

- **M2TR video extension:** Spatial-temporal M2TR samples 16 frames and feeds their M2TR frame features into a temporal transformer with four encoder layers and eight attention heads per layer to output a video-level prediction. [53]

- **TimeSformer:** TimeSformer applies divided space-time attention, modelling image structure and temporal evolution jointly. In a 2026 cross-dataset study, it reached 68.1% accuracy, 0.679 AUC, and 66.4% F1 with 32-frame clips without fine-tuning; 96-frame clips raised these to 71.2%, 0.722, and 69.6%. With 30% target-domain fine-tuning and 96 frames, the study reported 78.4% accuracy, 0.801 AUC, and 70.0% F1. [59] The same study explains the difference from frame-wise ViT: TimeSformer enables direct token interactions across space and time, whereas a conventional ViT processes frames spatially and aggregates them later. [59]

Transformers do not remove the need for low-level forensic design. A Detail-Aware Transformer (DAT) uses multi-head self-attention to adapt to different regional blending traces, then adds patch-based self-supervised pre-training and region-level contrastive learning. Its premise is that broad receptive fields help, but an unmodified transformer can miss subtle low-level blending evidence.[60] **Transformers expand the context a detector can inspect; task-specific cues, supervision, and training objectives determine whether that context captures forensic evidence rather than irrelevant identity or dataset correlations.**

Several recent methods instead seek cues thought to be less tied to a particular known forgery pipeline. Choi and colleagues’ CVPR 2024 method models abnormal changes in StyleGAN style-latent vectors with a contrastively trained StyleGRU and style-attention fusion with content features.[61] FakeRadar, published at ICCV 2025, synthesises boundary-near “forgery outliers” through dynamic subcluster modelling, then trains a real/fake/outlier discriminator with outlier-driven contrastive learning and outlier-conditioned cross-entropy.[62] Yan and colleagues’ CVPR 2025 method identifies **Facial Feature Drift**—subtle drifting or flickering of facial features—and constructs Video-level Blending pseudo-fakes to simulate it; its Spatiotemporal Adapter adds separate spatial and temporal 3D-convolution streams linked by cross-attention to a pretrained image model.[63]

A related route learns normal facial motion before detecting abnormal video. LipForensics transfers lip-reading representations: it freezes a ResNet-18-based visual extractor trained on 25 mouth crops and fine-tunes a multi-scale temporal convolutional network to identify unnatural mouth motion.[64] RealForensics learns dense video representations from audio-visual correspondence in real talking-face videos, then discards audio at detection time and trains a visual detector to classify videos while predicting the learned video targets.[65] The latter reported FaceForensics++-trained cross-dataset AUCs of 86.9% on Celeb-DF-v2, 75.9% on DFDC, 99.7% on FaceShifter, and 99.3% on DeeperForensics, averaging 90.5%.[64]

These methods show why visual-video detection overlaps with, but is not identical to, audio-visual detection. RealForensics uses audio only as a self-supervised training signal for a visual detector; methods that jointly assess synchronisation at inference belong in **Multimodal audio-visual analysis**. Likewise, CLIP-based adapters and foundation-model video detectors belong in **Foundation model integration**.

**Taken together, post-2022 video detection has shifted from single-frame artefact classification toward complementary spatial, temporal, spectral, and behavioural evidence.** SBI and frequency methods improve access to local visual traces; temporal convolution, optical flow, and temporal-frequency methods target motion irregularities; transformers integrate longer-range context; and synthetic pseudo-fakes or real-video pre-training aim to avoid dependence on a known generator’s artefacts. AltFreezing’s explicit balancing of spatial and temporal learning captures the period’s main lesson: neither spatial-only nor temporal-only evidence is reliably sufficient when manipulation methods and post-processing vary.[52][58][10][53][65]

- **Reconstruction, localisation, and residual learning:** Cao and colleagues’ 2022 Reconstruction-Classification Network (RECCE) first reconstructs an expected authentic representation and uses the discrepancy, or residual, to classify a frame as real or manipulated. Predicting altered regions can make a result more auditable, but pixel-level localisation labels are costly to obtain and a detector can localise a dataset-specific artefact rather than the edit itself.

Physiological and semantic cues also have clear deployment limits: lighting, makeup, camera processing, occlusion, demographic variation, low frame rates, and compression can obscure pulse, eye, head-pose, or expression signals. The absence of a recoverable physiological signal is therefore not itself evidence that a depicted person or video is synthetic.

Authentic blur, dropped frames, variable frame rates, autofocus and conferencing artefacts can resemble temporal evidence of manipulation.[95][142]

DeepfakeBench’s 2023 implementation catalogue also illustrates several complementary spatial generalization strategies. Face X-ray trains an HRNet with constructed blended images and fake samples from FaceForensics++ to use blending artefacts for detection of unseen forgeries.[54] FFD applies attention to detect and localise manipulation regions, using manipulation-appearance and direct-regression attention layers and three loss functions to guide learning.[54] DSP-FWA uses ResNet-50 to expose face-warping artefacts introduced by resizing and interpolation in the basic DeepFake-generation algorithm.[54] RECCE constructs a multi-scale graph over encoder and decoder features, treats reconstruction differences as forgery traces, and jointly optimises reconstruction and classification end to end.[54] CORE regularises the cosine distance between representations obtained under different augmentations to enforce representational consistency.[54] UCF uses multi-task disentanglement to counter overfitting to irrelevant features and method-specific textures by uncovering common features.[54]

Transformer-based variants include UIA-ViT, an ECCV 2022 Unsupervised Inconsistency-Aware Method based on a Vision Transformer for Face Forgery Detection.[47] Other reported architectures are Video Swin Transformer; GenConViT, which combines convolutional backbones and Transformer modules for local textures and global semantics; DeepFake-Adapter, which adds dual-level adapters to pretrained ViTs; and MSIDSnet and Swin-Fake, which respectively use dual-stream or consistency-learning designs to fuse spatial, frequency, and temporal features.[59] Transformer methods model long-range dependencies and spatiotemporal relationships, and require substantial computational resources and large-scale pretraining for optimal performance.[59] CNN-LSTM hybrids provide a non-Transformer temporal alternative: CNNs extract spatial features and Long Short-Term Memory networks model temporal dynamics across frames to identify inter-frame inconsistencies.[66]

## Audio detection methods

### What recent technical methods detect deepfake audio?

Judgment — Audio deepfake detection asks whether a recording is *bona fide*—made by a real speaker—or spoofed through text-to-speech (TTS), voice conversion (VC), editing, or cloning. It matters because synthetic voices can impersonate people, defeat voice-authentication systems, and make fraud or misinformation more credible.

Since 2022, detectors have generally followed either a *pipeline* design—an audio-feature front end followed by a classifier—or an *end-to-end* design that learns directly from the waveform. Pipeline front ends include Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), linear-frequency cepstral coefficients (LFCCs), Constant-Q Transform (CQT) spectra, log-power spectra, supervised embeddings, and self-supervised Wav2vec- and XLS-R-based embeddings; back ends include Gaussian-mixture-model (GMM), ResNet, Res2Net and differentiable architecture search (DARTS) classifiers. End-to-end systems include RawNet2, RawGAT-ST and AASIST. (Babu et al., 2022)[15][16][44][67]

**Acoustic and vocoder cues —** The 2025 *Audio Deepfake Detection: What Has Been Achieved and What Lies Ahead* survey identifies time–frequency, spectral-phase and cepstral features; the 2023 *Audio Deepfake Detection: A Survey* identifies speech-production cues including pitch, duration and energy. ASVspoof 5 (2026) found that neural codecs and speech enhancement can give bona fide speech artefacts resembling TTS- and VC-vocoder artefacts.[15][16][46][68]

Feature choice remains consequential. Müller et al. (2022, Interspeech, *Does Audio Deepfake Detection Generalize?*) reimplemented 12 architectures under common settings: LSTM, LCNN, LCNN-Attention, LCNN-LSTM, MesoNet, MesoInception, ResNet18, Transformer, CRNNSpoof, RawNet2, RawPC and RawGAT-ST. They found that CQT or log-spectrogram inputs consistently beat mel-spectrogram inputs; replacing mel spectra with CQT improved equal error rate (EER) by 37% on average with other factors fixed. EER is the operating point at which false-positive and false-negative rates are equal, so lower values indicate better separation.[69]

- **Convolutional and cepstral systems:** LCNNs, ResNets, VGG-style networks and capsule networks classify spectrogram or cepstral inputs. A 2022 attack-agnostic evaluation found no universally best input front end: LCNN EERs for LFCC-plus-spectrogram input were 9.137%, 9.158% and 3.125% across three folds, versus 9.526%, 9.523% and 2.370% for LFCC alone. [70]

- **Raw-waveform and graph-attention systems:** RawNet2 processes the waveform directly. RawGAT-ST models links between time segments and frequency sub-bands; AASIST extends this idea with a heterogeneous graph-attention layer that combines spectral and temporal nodes, allowing one model to seek artefacts at different times and frequencies. The 2022 AASIST paper reported a 20% relative improvement over the then state of the art and an 85,000-parameter AASIST-L variant that outperformed competing systems. [44][71]

- **Efficient systems:** SpecRNet is a neural architecture designed for lower computation. Its 2022 benchmark reported processing an audio sample in up to about 40% less time than its comparison architecture while achieving performance comparable to LCNN, making speed and resource use an explicit design objective rather than an afterthought. [72]

RawNetLite (2025) processes a three-second, 16-kHz waveform with a one-dimensional convolution, three residual blocks, adaptive average pooling, a bidirectional GRU with 128 units per direction, and a classifier. Its training uses waveform augmentation, focal loss, and domain mixing.[144]

The field has also expanded from whole-recording binary classification to *partial fake detection*, which must locate the time boundaries of manipulated clips, and *algorithm recognition*, which seeks to identify the generator used. ADD 2023 formalised both as Manipulation Region Location and Deepfake Algorithm Recognition tasks alongside ordinary real-versus-fake detection.[19][45]

Transformer-based systems increasingly supply learned speech representations rather than relying only on fixed acoustic descriptors. Martín-Doñas and Álvarez (2022, ICASSP) used a pretrained wav2vec 2.0 feature extractor and a downstream classifier, combining contextual representations from different Transformer layers and applying data augmentation. WavLM-based systems similarly use multiple hidden layers and time steps: Guo et al. (2024, ICASSP) combined self-supervised WavLM features with a Multi-Fusion Attentive classifier that fuses information across both dimensions.[73][74]

Transformers also operate directly on spectrogram patches. PS3DT converts speech into a mel-spectrogram, divides it into 16-by-16 patches, processes these through a 12-layer, 12-head Transformer encoder, then groups patches by temporal position before mean-pooling for classification. On the within-dataset ASVspoof 2019 evaluation, PS3DT reported 4.54% EER, compared with 5.26% for PaSST and 6.23% for LCNN+LSTM.[75]

These figures show strong benchmark discrimination but not a settled universal architecture. On ASVspoof 5, open-condition systems commonly used large self-supervised speech foundation models—especially wav2vec 2.0 and WavLM—as front ends fine-tuned with relatively lightweight MLP or LCNN back ends; the challenge reported lower EER and minimum detection cost function (minDCF) in this setting than under closed-data rules. MinDCF incorporates application-specific costs and thresholds, unlike EER.[46][68]

In RawNetLite’s 2025 AVSpoof2021 evaluation, adding AVSpoof2021 training data increased fake-class F1 by 48.03 points and fake recall by 55.48 points relative to FakeOrReal-only training.[144]

On ASVspoof 5, SEMAA reported minDCF of 0.2994 on development, 0.3604 on Eval_prog, and 0.581 on closed evaluation; it reported 29.75% improvement over its vanilla model and 18.28% over baseline B02 on evaluation-set minDCF.[68][145]

Audio language models (ALMs), which generate audio using neural-codec-style token representations, have created a further target beyond traditional vocoder-based TTS and VC. The 2025 Codecfake study identified ALM audio as a challenge for ADD models trained solely on vocoded data, introduced an open dataset of more than one million English and Chinese samples, and proposed CSAM, a domain-balanced adaptation of sharpness-aware minimization intended to learn a more generalizable solution across domains. Its experiments reported that training an ADD model with Codecfake effectively detected ALM-based audio.[21] A separate 2024 evaluation collected 12 types of ALM-based deepfake audio and tested the latest countermeasures; its codec-trained countermeasure achieved 0% EER under most ALM test conditions.[20]

The ALM findings report separate training and test conditions: Codecfake addresses the difficulty posed by ALM audio to models trained solely on vocoded data and evaluates ADD-model training with its English-and-Chinese dataset, whereas the 2024 study evaluates a codec-trained countermeasure on 12 ALM-generated audio types and reports 0% EER in most of those conditions.[21][20] This is why current work combines expanded datasets, augmentation, pretrained speech encoders and adaptation rather than treating a single synthetic-speech artefact as durable evidence of fakery.[21][20][79][46]

Methods aimed at generalization take several forms. Attack-augmented training exposes models to perturbations such as low-pass filtering, MP3 compression, neural-codec processing and noise reduction; in one cross-model TED evaluation, Wav2Vec2-base EER fell from 10.1% without augmentation to 8.9% with low-pass filtering, 8.3% with MP3 compression and 5.5% with SepFormer noise reduction. Few-shot adaptation then fine-tunes a detector using one minute of target-domain speech, although Encodec compression reduced the benefit.[79]

Other approaches model what genuine speech should look like rather than memorising known fake generators. SLIM first learns the relationship between speaking style and linguistic content from real speech only, then combines its mismatch signal with pretrained acoustic features such as wav2vec; its authors report stronger out-of-domain performance with frozen encoders and use the style–linguistic mismatch to explain decisions. One-class systems likewise cluster bona fide speech in an embedding space and push spoofs away; speaker-attractor multi-centre one-class learning accommodates multiple real-speaker clusters rather than assuming all genuine speech has one distribution.[80][16]

Continual learning addresses generators that arrive after deployment. Radian Weight Modification classifies genuine audio as a compact cross-task feature distribution and fake types as more dispersed distributions, then changes gradients using within-class cosine distance; its AAAI 2024 evaluation reported better acquisition of new knowledge and less forgetting than mainstream continual-learning methods. Cross-language adaptation is another route: adversarial domain adaptation trains a detector to retain real/fake information while reducing language dependence, and experiments over 137 hours supported detection of previously unseen algorithms in a new language domain.[81][82]

A source-level conflict remains important. Pianese et al. (2022, WIFS) state that training a speaker-verification-based detector only on real speech automatically ensures generalization. Later evaluations instead find that systems trained on one set of deepfakes often perform poorly on another, including self-supervised systems, and identify unknown attacks, languages and domains as continuing problems. The weight of subsequent cross-dataset evidence therefore favours real-only training as one useful strategy, not a guarantee of generalization.[83][84][85][86][19]

The practical implication is that an audio detector should combine complementary acoustic evidence, pretrained representations, domain-relevant augmentation and explicit testing against new generators, languages and processing paths; low benchmark EER alone does not select a deployment-ready method.[69][79][46]

Acoustic-channel shift reduces reliability — The 2025 *Audio Deepfake Detection: What Has Been Achieved and What Lies Ahead* survey identifies lossy codecs in real-world communication; the 2024 Cross-Domain Audio Deepfake Detection evaluation found reverberation and Encodec compression difficult; and ASVspoof 5 (2026) identifies replay, re-recording and room reverberation as particularly challenging. Telephone channels add compression, packet loss, bandwidth, transmission-infrastructure and data-rate distortions.[15][79][75][68]

Other systems examine source–filter and phase cues, prosodic or linguistic consistency, and agreement between acoustic evidence and a speaker embedding. HuBERT, alongside wav2vec 2.0 and WavLM, is also used as a self-supervised speech encoder whose representations can be frozen, fused with acoustic features, or fine-tuned for spoof classification.

- **Learnable and artefact-specific front ends:** A 2022 Odyssey method learns an optimal weighted combination of multiple time–frequency resolutions in an end-to-end anti-spoofing detector, concatenates the selected representations, and prunes resolutions with low importance to reduce model complexity and size; it was evaluated on ASVspoof 2019 and outperformed similar baselines. A 2023 spectra-temporal fusion method instead combines a local spectral deviation coefficient (SDC) for frame-level inconsistencies with a bidirectional LSTM network for sequential temporal coefficients (STC) that capture utterance-level artefacts. AReLU, an attention rectified linear unit, boosts relevant input features and suppresses irrelevant ones in an end-to-end countermeasure; it outperformed systems using standard nonlearnable activation functions on the ASVspoof 2019 logical-access task. [87]

Recent self-supervised-learning integrations also combine pretrained encoders with task-specific detectors. The XMUspeech ASVspoof 5 system trained a base-sized HuBERT feature extractor on ASVspoof 5 bona fide and spoofed speech, used AASIST or HM-Conformer as its classifier, and applied adaptive multi-scale feature fusion (AMFF) to integrate multiple Transformer layers with hand-crafted features. HM-Conformer combines CNN local-feature extraction with Transformer self-attention for global dependency, then uses hierarchical pooling and multi-level classification-token aggregation to compact features and aggregate task-related information across encoder blocks. AASIST3 combines KAN enhancement, self-supervised-learning features, and additional regularization.[88][89]

SEMAA uses an AASIST-derived backbone, higher-order two-dimensional attentive-statistics pooling, and channel-dependent self-attention graph aggregation.[68][145]

## Cross-dataset generalization

### How do approaches for cross-dataset generalization improve deepfake detection?

Cross-dataset generalization tests whether a detector trained on one distribution still works when the test material differs in generator, manipulation type, identity, language, recording pipeline, compression, or scene. It matters because a detector can learn shortcuts—features that separate real and fake material in its training set but do not represent forgery itself. Video models may depend on particular spatial traces such as blending edges or on temporal flicker; audio models may depend on the synthesis artifacts, language, codec, or speakers represented in one benchmark.[90][84]

**The central finding is that transfer improves when training shifts from memorizing known generator artifacts toward modeling evidence that is shared across forgery types, or toward modeling the structure of authentic media.** The most successful approaches combine data construction, representation learning, perturbation robustness, and evaluation across genuinely different test distributions. Their benchmark gains are material, but none removes the domain-shift problem.[33][90][54][16]

#### The generalization target: common signals, not dataset shortcuts

Video research distinguishes *within-domain* evaluation—training and testing within the same dataset—from *cross-domain* evaluation, in which a detector is trained on one dataset and tested on others. DeepfakeBench, for example, trains on FaceForensics++ at c23 compression and evaluates 14 test datasets, including eight cross-domain datasets. Its purpose is to measure transfer rather than fit to the source dataset.[31][29]

A useful way to interpret this setting is that a detector must separate two questions: “does this look unlike source-domain real video?” and “does this contain evidence of manipulation?” The first can produce strong benchmark results while failing on new cameras, social-media processing, or generators. In DeepfakeBench, models trained on one forgery type often struggled on unseen forgery types; after training on FF++ c23, the reported cross-domain average AUC across eight datasets was 0.7875 for SPSL, 0.7801 for UCF, and 0.7718 for Xception, with SPSL the highest value shown.[54][91]

Several methods explicitly address this shortcut-learning problem:

- **Synthetic pseudo-forgeries:** Self-Blended Images (SBI), introduced at CVPR 2022, constructs synthetic fakes by blending a transformed copy of a single pristine face back into the original. The training examples contain blending boundaries and local statistical inconsistencies without reproducing a particular real deepfake generator. The intended result is that the classifier learns generic traces rather than generator-specific artifacts. [33][52]

- **Feature disentanglement:** UCF, or *Uncovering Common Features*, decomposes image information into forgery-irrelevant, method-specific forgery, and common forgery features. It uses multi-task learning to predict both the forgery-method category and the binary real/fake label, plus contrastive regularization to keep the common and method-specific representations distinct. [24]

- **Real-only or paired-data learning:** RealForensics learns audio-visual representations from large collections of authentic talking-face video before detector training; the aim is to recognize deviations from natural facial behavior rather than signatures of a particular manipulation pipeline. A related paired-data strategy matches fake videos with real videos from the same source, reducing superficial differences in background, identity, and capture conditions. [65][14]

- **Spatial–temporal balancing:** AltFreezing divides a spatiotemporal network’s weights into spatial-related and temporal-related groups and alternately freezes each group during training. This is intended to prevent a video detector from relying only on spatial artifacts or only on temporal inconsistency. [90][58]

- **Outlier and latent-space methods:** FakeRadar uses large pretrained models such as CLIP to identify distributional gaps among real video, known fakes, and unseen manipulations. It generates feature-space outliers near estimated cluster boundaries and trains a three-way real/fake/outlier classifier. Latent Space Augmentation for Deepfake Detection (LSDA) likewise seeks representations less tied to one forgery type. [62][92]

These methods make possible a more demanding form of forensic detection: recognizing manipulation evidence under a changed data distribution rather than merely reproducing high scores on a familiar benchmark.

#### Video: training for transferable spatial and temporal evidence

SBI provides a clear controlled example of synthetic-data generalization. In its cross-dataset protocol, the CVPR 2022 study trained EfficientNet-B4 with SBI samples on FaceForensics++ and reported video-level AUCs of 93.18% on Celeb-DF, 97.56% on DeepFakeDetection, 72.42% on DFDC, 86.15% on DFDC Preview, and 84.83% on FFIW. It reported improvements of 4.90 percentage points over an unsupervised baseline on DFDC and 11.78 points on DFDC Preview.[52][93] The variation—from 97.56% on DFD to 72.42% on DFDC—shows that synthetic blending helps transfer but does not make test domains interchangeable.[52]

For its 2022 cross-dataset evaluations, SBI reported a mean AUC of 87.33%, versus 82.75% for the compared frame-level methods, a 4.58-percentage-point difference.[52]

Temporal modeling is another route to transfer because a realistic single frame can conceal inconsistencies that appear across frames. TALL, published at ICCV 2023, converts a sequence of frames into a fixed “thumbnail layout”: consecutive frames are masked at fixed positions, resized, and arranged together so a visual model can access spatial and temporal dependencies. TALL-Swin achieved 90.79% AUC when trained on FaceForensics++ and tested on Celeb-DF.[1]

AltFreezing combines temporal dropout, temporal repetition, and clip-level blending with its alternating-freezing training schedule. In a FaceForensics++-to-unseen-dataset evaluation, it reported video-level AUCs of 89.5% on Celeb-DF-v2, 98.5% on DeepFakeDetection, 99.4% on FaceShifter, and 99.3% on DeeperForensics, averaging 96.7%. In leave-one-manipulation-out FaceForensics++ testing, it reported a 98.6% average AUC across Deepfakes, FaceSwap, Face2Face, and NeuralTextures.[58] Those high scores show that jointly trained spatial and temporal cues can transfer across the selected benchmark shifts; they do not mean all video-generation pipelines leave the same temporal evidence.

A more recent temporal-frequency approach performs a one-dimensional Fourier transform along the time axis at each pixel, seeking local motion irregularities that ordinary spatial-frequency methods miss. Trained on FaceForensics++, it reported video-level AUCs of 89.7% on Celeb-DF, 75.2% on DFDC, 99.3% on FaceShifter, 99.4% on DeeperForensics, and 97.3% on DFD, averaging 92.2%.[10] Again, DFDC remains the harder transfer target, which is consistent with it containing compression, downsampling, noise, and different collection conditions.[52]

Training choices can matter as much as architecture. In DeepfakeBench, adding a spectrum feature to FWA raised average frame-level AUC from 0.7316 to 0.8035 across FF++ c23, FF++ c40, Celeb-DF-v2, DFDC Preview, and DFD. The authors reported gains on every listed dataset, from 1.39% on FF++ c40 to 10.05% on DFD. Similarly, Xception with the benchmark’s data-augmentation and pre-training settings rose from an average AUC of 0.6250 to 0.8160 across the same five tests, although its FF++ c23 score fell from 0.9893 to 0.9637.[31]

That trade-off matters: optimising for source-domain performance can reduce cross-domain transfer. DeepfakeBench found that removing augmentation improved within-domain FF++ c23 performance by about 2% for Xception and SPSL, while Gaussian blur helped on compressed FF++ c40 but blur and compression could harm performance on external datasets by obscuring high-frequency evidence.[91][95] **Video generalization therefore requires targeted diversity and validation on held-out domains, not the blanket addition of more augmentation, frames, or data.**[91][31]

#### Audio: broadening generators, channels, languages, and spoof representations

Audio deepfake detection faces the same problem in a different form. Speech detectors trained on one set of text-to-speech systems can perform poorly on another, and performance is affected by language, speaker population, compression, noise reduction, and neural audio codecs. In a 2024 analysis, Müller and colleagues decomposed transfer failures between ASVspoof 2019 training and new test data into *hardness* and *difference*. On the In-the-Wild dataset, the EER performance gaps were 78.2 points for LCNN, 40.6 for RawNet2, 30.3 for self-supervised Wav2Vec2, and 31.4 for WhisperDF; nearly all of each gap was attributed to difference rather than intrinsic hardness.[96]

This result supports a practical conclusion: newer or realistic deepfakes are not simply harder examples of the same task. They can be different enough in synthesis process or recording distribution that the detector’s learned decision boundary is misplaced. Neural codec compressors are especially consequential; the Cross-Domain Audio Deepfake Detection study reports that they greatly affect detection accuracy.[79]

The major technical responses are complementary:

- **Diverse cross-domain data and attack augmentation:** The 2024 CD-ADD dataset contains more than 300 hours of speech generated by five zero-shot text-to-speech systems, with prompts from different datasets and nine attacks, including neural codecs and noise reduction. With attack-augmented training, Wav2Vec2-large achieved 4.1% EER and Whisper-medium 6.5% EER in the paper’s experiments. [79][78]

- **Signal and codec augmentation:** Attack augmentation exposes models to low-pass filtering, MP3 compression, noise reduction, and related transformations. For Wav2Vec2-base on CD-ADD’s TED subset, reported cross-model EER fell from 10.1% without the listed augmentation to 8.9% with low-pass filtering, 8.3% with MP3 compression, and 5.5% with SepFormer noise reduction. [79] RawBoost also improved AASIST2’s VoiceWukong EER from 31.40% to 13.50% in English and from 32.67% to 13.54% in Chinese. [44]

- **Latent-space and one-class methods:** Latent Space Refinement uses multiple learnable spoof prototypes to represent variation within fake speech, while Latent Space Augmentation diversifies spoof representations directly in the embedding space. One-class approaches instead compact genuine speech into one or more embedding clusters and push spoofed speech away; speaker-attractor multi-center one-class learning accounts for genuine-speaker variation through multiple attractors. [97][16]

- **Language-invariant adaptation:** Cross-lingual domain adaptation trains a model to retain real/fake information while reducing language dependence. Its authors report that English data enriched with diverse deepfake algorithms can transfer spoof-artifact knowledge across languages, and their evaluation covered 137 hours of clips. [82]

- **Pretrained speech representations:** Self-supervised speech models such as Wav2Vec2, WavLM, XLS-R, HuBERT, and Whisper are pretrained on broad speech data before task-specific fine-tuning. In SONAR experiments, speech foundation models generalized more strongly than existing detectors and retained strong cross-lingual performance despite English-only fine-tuning. [25] However, larger pretrained models still need task-specific exposure to changed generator and channel conditions. [79][18]

MLAADv3 contains more than 76,000 utterances spanning 23 languages, 54 synthesis systems and more than 160 hours of audio.[146]

MLAADv3’s generator-separation protocol fine-tuned on VITS and Griffin–Lim outputs and tested on XTTS v1.1 and XTTS v2, then reversed the direction; reported language results averaged both directions and 10 repetitions using 90% of test data.[146]

Compression-aware representations are particularly relevant for deployment-like audio. Real social-media and voice-communication audio commonly uses MP3, AAC, or Opus, unlike the lossless FLAC conditions associated with ASVspoof 2019; these codecs remove time- and frequency-domain information on which detectors may depend. Codec augmentation, Frequency–Time Domain Knowledge Distillation, and direct MP3-domain Efficient Spectrograms are intended to preserve discrimination under these transformations.[19]

The strongest evidence against simple “more data” prescriptions is mixed. Combining CD-ADD with ASVspoof improved Wav2Vec2-base EER on the Libri cross-model subset from 12.14% to 7.85%, but changed the TED result from 20.34% to 21.40%.[79] Likewise, multi-domain training worsened AASIST2 VoiceWukong EER to 48.18% in English and 49.89% in Chinese, versus 31.40% and 32.67% without it.[44] Audio training data must therefore be selected and augmented for the expected deployment distribution rather than simply pooled.

1. Train and validate video detectors on multiple held-out generators, compression pipelines, identities, and scenes; retain separate cross-manipulation and cross-dataset tests.
2. Use synthetic pseudo-forgeries, paired real/fake examples, and spatial–temporal objectives to suppress generator-specific visual shortcuts.
3. For audio, include codec, noise-reduction, language, and zero-shot-TTS shifts in the evaluation plan; report EER separately for each target condition.
4. Treat foundation-model features and wider training data as enabling components, not substitutes for out-of-domain testing and targeted adaptation.

Cross-dataset evaluation is an intermediate test between closed-benchmark evaluation and deployment: it tests a specified distribution shift, rather than the random train/test split of one corpus. A meaningful evaluation matrix varies the training and test datasets, attack families, codecs, and operating points rather than relying on one held-out split.

Other video domain-generalization methods train on multiple source datasets with objectives that make representations invariant to source-domain identity, alongside augmentations such as resizing, cropping, colour changes, noise, blur, and compression. Reconstruction-and-classification approaches such as RECCE seek to emphasize manipulation residuals over identity information.

Audio systems also use domain-adversarial training to reduce corpus and channel information in the learned representation, and episodic or meta-learning schemes that simulate an unseen spoofing attack during training. Evaluation splits must separate speakers, attacks, and recording conditions; otherwise speaker or corpus leakage can inflate apparent generalization.

XMAD-Bench is a proposed 668.8-hour multilingual audio benchmark whose splits separate speakers, generators, and genuine-audio sources to test transfer across languages, speakers, generators, and sources. Its reported in-domain results can reach 100%, while cross-domain performance can approach chance.[147]

Target-domain or test-time adaptation tests how quickly a detector can adjust to a new distribution, rather than measuring zero-shot transfer; evaluation data must remain separate from the material used for adaptation.[52][95][143][144][79][148][149]

Cross-dataset approaches also include calibrated abstention, in which a detector defers an uncertain authenticity decision rather than forcing every case into a real-or-fake label.[52][95][143][144][79][148][149]

Cross-dataset reporting should therefore include threshold-specific error rates as well as threshold-independent ranking metrics.

When trained on FaceForensics++, RealForensics reported cross-dataset AUCs of 86.9% on CelebDF-v2, 75.9% on DFDC, 99.7% on FaceShifter, and 99.3% on DeeperForensics, averaging 90.5%.[64] In the same FaceForensics++-training protocol, LipForensics reported video-level AUCs of 82.4%, 73.5%, 97.1%, and 97.6%, respectively, averaging 87.7%.[64]

LipForensics targets semantic irregularities in mouth movement rather than low-level generator artifacts. It pre-trains a CNN comprising a spatiotemporal feature extractor and temporal convolutional network on lipreading, freezes the feature extractor, and fine-tunes only the temporal network on forgery data so that the model does not revert to unwanted artifacts. Its input is 25 aligned grayscale mouth crops; a ResNet-18 with an initial 3D convolutional layer produces mouth-motion-sensitive embeddings, and a multi-scale temporal convolutional network (MS-TCN) detects high-level irregularities. The cues include failure to close the mouth for particular phonemes, unnatural changes in movement speed, and frame-to-frame alterations in mouth shape or interior, including teeth, even without speech.[64]

In the authors’ cross-dataset ablation, lipreading pre-training improved performance by 8.4 percentage points on average and freezing the feature extractor added 4.9 percentage points on average, supporting the use of high-level mouth-motion features for transfer.[64]

LipForensics cannot operate on isolated images; it is unlikely to detect a video in which the mouth is occluded or unaltered, can lose performance when mouth motion is limited, and requires a large labelled pre-training dataset.[64]

A cross-manipulation protocol can isolate unseen-forgery transfer more directly: the updated FaceForensics++ and DeeperForensics material permits training on a subset of six manipulation methods and testing on the remaining forgeries without confounds from filming conditions or video-quality level.[64]

## Transformer-based architectures

### How are transformer-based architectures used for deepfake detection?

Judgment — Transformers use *self-attention*: a mechanism that lets a model weigh relationships among many input elements rather than inspect each small neighbourhood in isolation. For deepfake detection, this matters because forgery evidence may be distributed across distant facial regions, across frames in a video, or across time–frequency regions of speech.

In video, Vision Transformers (ViTs) divide an image into patches and relate them globally; video transformers additionally relate patches or frame features over time. M2TR is a representative 2022 design: it applies multi-scale transformers to differently sized RGB patches, uses a frequency stream to expose compression- and synthesis-related patterns, and fuses the two streams through cross-modality fusion. Here “multi-modal” means RGB and frequency-domain visual data, not audio and video. Its ST-M2TR extension samples 16 frames and feeds the frame features to a temporal transformer with four encoder layers and eight attention heads for a video-level decision.[53] This architecture makes spatially dispersed and temporally evolving artifacts available to one classifier rather than treating frames as independent images.

Temporal attention can improve learned representations. In RealForensics’ self-supervised ablation, replacing an MLP predictor with a shallow transformer raised reported accuracy after FaceForensics++ training from 91.8% to 97.1% on FaceShifter and from 92.9% to 97.1% on DeeperForensics.[26] Yet attention is not intrinsically sufficient: Detail-Aware Transformer researchers find that a vanilla transformer is weak at subtle blending traces, so they add region-sensitive attention, patch-augmentation pretraining, and region-level contrastive learning.[60]

Audio transformers follow two principal designs:

The Transformer evaluated by Müller and colleagues used four layers, 256 hidden dimensions, skip connections, and positional encoding.[69]

- **Self-supervised speech encoders:** Wav2Vec 2.0 maps raw waveforms to contextual transformer representations, then a downstream classifier identifies bona fide versus spoofed speech. The 2022 Vicomtech ADD system drew representations from multiple transformer layers rather than one final layer. [73][15]

- **Spectrogram transformers:** Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) converts audio into Mel-spectrogram patches and applies a ViT; PS3DT similarly uses 16×16 Mel patches and a 12-layer, 12-head transformer before temporal pooling. [45][75]

Published controlled results are strong but dataset-specific. A CQT-spectrogram ViT reported 0.19% equal-error rate (EER) and 0.1102 tandem detection cost function (t-DCF) on ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access. On FakeAVCeleb’s audio subset, AST achieved 0.979 accuracy and 0.985 ROC-AUC, while Wav2Vec achieved 0.991 accuracy and 0.990 ROC-AUC. These figures demonstrate discriminative capacity under stated benchmark conditions, not universal deployment performance.[19][45]

Transformer generalization remains conditional. A 2023 comparison found ViT benefited particularly from varied training data and characterized Swin Transformer as performing very well in cross-dataset scenarios, whereas FA-ViT researchers reported that fully fine-tuned pretrained ViTs still generalized unsatisfactorily.[98][99] The direction of travel is therefore toward task-specific adapters, self-supervised or contrastive training, frequency cues, and temporal modeling—not a transformer-only replacement for all other detector components.

Temporal and multimodal attention also impose computational costs: one synchronization study’s sparse convolution-attention encoder matched a transformer-decoder variant while reducing training time from eight to two hours per epoch on its specified A100 setup.[100]

1. Use transformers where the threat requires cross-region or cross-frame context, rather than assuming frame-level classification is enough.
2. Evaluate adapted transformer systems across generators, datasets, codecs, and speech conditions; benchmark scores alone do not settle generalization.
3. Prefer pretrained speech or vision representations with task-specific detection heads and robust augmentation when compute or labelled forensic data are constrained. [46][101]

TALL-Swin (2023) combines Thumbnail Layout (TALL) with a Swin Transformer to encode video-frame information in a transformer architecture. On the cross-dataset FaceForensics++ → CelebDF test, it achieved 90.79% AUC.[1]

Petmezas (2026, 'Cross-dataset video deepfake detection using Transformer and CNN architectures') compared TimeSformer with ViT, ResNet50, ResNet101, CNN–LSTM and CNN–Attention architectures across video-deepfake datasets.[59] TimeSformer uses divided space–time attention, jointly modelling spatial structure and temporal evolution across multiple frames.

MSVT uses multiple spatiotemporal views, while a 2024 temporal-coherence model uses global temporal self-attention to capture longer-range facial dynamics.[142]

With no fine-tuning, it achieved 68.1% accuracy, 0.679 AUC and 66.4% F1-score using 32-frame clips, rising to 71.2% accuracy, 0.722 AUC and 69.6% F1-score with 96-frame clips; these results were averaged over three runs. With 30% target-domain fine-tuning and 96-frame clips, TimeSformer achieved 78.4% accuracy, 0.801 AUC and 70.0% F1-score.[59]

- **Hybrid raw-audio models:** Rawformer combines convolution with a Transformer to model local and global audio artifacts and their relationships directly from raw audio. In cross-dataset evaluation, Rawformer generalized better than AASIST, and SE-Rawformer performed better than Rawformer. [16]

- **Conformer-style hybrids:** HM-Conformer combines convolutional neural networks for local features with Transformer self-attention for global dependencies. Its hierarchical pooling and multi-level classification-token aggregation compact features and aggregate task-relevant information across encoder blocks. [88]

Whispeak’s Wav2Vec-Conformer and Wav2Vec-RawGAT-ST combine pretrained speech representations with Conformer and graph-attention back ends, respectively.[148]

## Multimodal audio-visual analysis

### How does multimodal audio-visual analysis detect deepfakes?

Judgment — Multimodal audio-visual detection asks whether what is heard and what is seen plausibly belong together. Rather than searching only for image artifacts or synthetic-speech artifacts, it tests correspondence between speech timing, phonetic content, lip and face motion, and—where available—identity or behavioural signals.

Audio-Visual Feature Fusion (AVFF, CVPR 2024) first learns normal speech–facial-motion correspondence from real videos using self-supervised contrastive learning, cross-modal reconstruction, complementary masking, and feature fusion; it then fine-tunes those representations to classify real and fake videos. This design addresses the risk that supervised fusion learns dataset-specific audio-video cues rather than relationships useful for previously unseen fakes.[102] AVAD similarly learns real speech–mouth synchronization, models alignment features such as audio–lip-motion delay distributions with an autoregressive Transformer, and flags low-likelihood sequences as anomalous.[51]

AVoiD-DF used joint audio-visual learning in 2023; a separate 2023 method combined embedding fusion with multidimensional contrastive loss for temporal audiovisual forgery detection.[142]

For lip-sync fakes, LipFD (NeurIPS 2024) uses a Transformer to model long-range relationships between audio spectra and lip motion, combines this with multi-scale facial-region evidence, and also uses links between lip and head movement.[103] A more interpretable CVPR Workshop 2024 approach independently transcribes audio with automatic speech recognition and visible speech with lip-reading, then classifies clips using the normalized Levenshtein distance—word-edit disagreement—between the transcripts. It therefore tests semantic disagreement, not merely generator artifacts.[39]

UMCL’s rPPG, facial-landmark and text representations all derive from one visual input: it avoids audio-video synchronization problems but cannot test face–voice or lip–phoneme consistency.[143]

Published results show both value and limits of fusion:

| Evaluation setting and metric | Published result |
|---|---|
| Mega-MMDF AUC, FRADE: video / audio / multimodal input | 0.995 / 0.897 / 0.994. [51] |
| Mega-MMDF AUC, MRDF: video / audio / multimodal input | 0.928 / 0.700 / 0.970. [51] |
| AV-Deepfake1M localization, UMMAFormer AP@0.5 / AP@0.95 | 51.64 / 1.58. [27] |
| LAV-DF localization, UMMAFormer AP@0.5 / AP@0.95 | 98.83 / 37.61. [27] |

**The central forensic signal is cross-modal consistency, but fusion is conditional rather than automatically beneficial.** MRDF gained from both modalities, whereas FRADE’s video-only score slightly exceeded its fused score; the benchmark identifies a general bias toward visual evidence.[51] Deliberately masking video during training improved AVTS and MRDF’s reported within-dataset and cross-dataset results, a practical response to visual-modality dominance.[51] Fine-grained localization remains much harder than coarse detection, especially when only short segments of otherwise genuine audio-visual material are altered.[27]

Dubbing, translation, voice-over, music, accessibility edits, delay and rerecording can create legitimate audio-visual discrepancies, so a synchronization mismatch alone does not establish manipulation.[143][142]

## Foundation model integration

### How are foundation models integrated into deepfake detection?

Judgment — A foundation model is a large model pretrained on broad data before being adapted to a narrower task. In deepfake detection, this replaces training every representation from scratch: the pretrained encoder supplies general visual, speech, or audio-visual features, while a smaller detection component learns authenticity-specific cues.

For video, 2025 CLIP-based systems preserve the pretrained vision-language encoder and add spatial–temporal adapters or side-network decoders. The facial-component-guided method updates lightweight modules rather than the CLIP encoder; it reported cross-dataset video-level mean AUC of 92.3% while tuning 250,000 parameters, compared with 85.1% for 303.0-million-parameter full fine-tuning. Forensics Adapter instead runs a small Vision Transformer alongside pretrained CLIP and exchanges CLIP features and adapter-derived attention bias; it reported video-level AUCs of 0.957 on CDF-v2, 0.872 on DFDC, and 0.972 on DFD.[13][94]

For generated-face images, the same CLIP adaptation achieved 81.0% mean average precision (AP) in a zero-shot DiffusionForensics test, versus 65.2% for SBI; this is cross-generator image evidence, not unconstrained video deployment performance.[13]

Visual foundation-model integration also uses DINO for self-supervised visual features and face-recognition encoders for identity-related representations.[68][79][148][147]

For audio, self-supervised speech foundation models such as wav2vec 2.0 and WavLM serve as frozen or jointly fine-tuned acoustic front ends, followed by lighter classifiers such as multilayer perceptrons, LCNNs, AASIST, or GAT/Res2Net/LSTM back ends. In ASVspoof 5 Track 2’s open condition, T45 combined wav2vec2 Large with GAT, MFA-Res2Net and LSTM, reaching minimum a-DCF of 0.07.[104][68]

Other pretrained speech encoders used in detection include UniSpeech-SAT and data2vec.[68][141]

On ASVspoof 2021 DeepFake, XLS-R+SLS reported 1.92% equal error rate, WavLM with multi-fusion attention 2.56%, and XLS-R+RawNet2+AASIST 2.85%.[144]

Early and intermediate Transformer layers can preserve acoustic texture and phonetic detail better than final semantic layers; a 2024 cross-domain model learned weights for combining Wav2Vec2 and Whisper layers rather than using only their final layers.[141][79]

Foundation-model pretraining improves reported transfer: SONAR found stronger generalization and cross-lingual performance after English-only fine-tuning. But direct prompting is not enough: Qwen2-Audio scored F1 0% on VoiceWukong English and 33.16% on Chinese; Qwen2.5-Omni obtained AUC 0.568 on Mega-MMDF zero-shot.[25][44][51]

1. Use adapter-based specialization, not zero-shot foundation-model classification alone.
2. Report pretraining data and overlap controls: ASVspoof 5 warns that overlap with data used to create evaluation spoofs can make comparisons unfair. [46]

On the ASVspoof 5 progress set in the open condition, AASIST with a Wav2Vec2.0 Large 960 front end, trained on ASVspoof 5 and ASVspoof 2019 LA with Gaussian-noise and MP3-compression augmentation, achieved minimum detection cost function (min DCF) 0.063, actual DCF 0.113, Cllr 0.164, and equal error rate (EER) 2.27%.[105]

ASVspoof 5 distinguishes closed-condition systems, restricted to corpus-internal data and supplied models, from open-condition systems that may use external data or foundation models subject to speaker-overlap limits.[68][141]

In ASVspoof 5 Track 2, the strongest open-condition system reported a minimum architecture-agnostic detection cost of 0.07; the next four systems reported 0.11–0.14.[68][141]

## Privacy-preserving techniques

### What privacy-preserving techniques are used for deepfake detection?

Privacy-preserving detection limits exposure of biometric media and spoken content while retaining forgery cues. It matters where video, voice, or identity data cannot lawfully or safely be pooled.

**Training-data flow: local media, shared updates.** Federated learning keeps raw training data at participating clients: each client trains locally and a server aggregates uploaded model parameters or updates. The 2022 FedForgery paper applies this arrangement to face-forgery detection: separate data centres retain non-exchangeable datasets, train local detectors, and send parameters for weighted global aggregation. Its detector combines federated learning with a variational autoencoder that learns residual feature maps—reconstruction differences intended to expose face-forgery artefacts, including unknown types.[40]

The 2026 FMM-MMF Video-KYC framework likewise processes facial video, audio streams, and behavioural metadata locally, and shares encrypted model updates and encrypted feature embeddings for Federated Averaging with adaptive client weighting. Its utility measurements are a 4.7 GB average local-training GPU-memory footprint, 0.42 seconds of aggregation overhead per communication round, and less than 0.6% accuracy deviation from centralized training.[106]

Inference-input flow: transformed utterance, concealed words. SafeEar is a content-privacy-preserving audio deepfake detector: HuBERT-equipped multilayer residual vector quantisers separate semantic features—the linguistic content—from acoustic features, and the detector receives only the acoustic features. It then randomly shuffles acoustic tokens in time and reduces their dimensionality to obstruct reconstruction of the words in an individual inference utterance. On ASVspoof 2019 LA it achieved accuracy comparable to mainstream methods and on ASVspoof 2021 LA outperformed most baselines; in content-recovery attacks in which adversaries had ASR models, knowledge of the algorithm, or the shuffling order, ASR word-error rate exceeded 90%.[19]

Conclusion: Federated training protects raw training media and biometric data from central pooling, while SafeEar protects the spoken content of an individual inference input. Federated learning requires local compute and can expose participants to parameter-based de-anonymization and federated-poisoning attacks; anomaly detection of client updates, robust aggregation, and differential privacy are additional protections.[107][106]

For remote inference, 2023 security protocols allow an untrusted server to detect deepfake video without learning its content. Trusted execution environments (TEEs) protect data and code during execution through hardware-based encryption, while fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) permits computation on encrypted data and models without decryption; FHE carries substantial speed and scalability costs. Synthetic-data generation, differential privacy, and split learning are further privacy-enhancing technologies: synthetic data replaces human-generated training data with statistically similar generated data; differential privacy adds statistical noise to obscure sensitive details; and split learning divides a model between clients and a server so raw client data is not shared.[108][107]

FMM-MMF reported 98.72% accuracy (95% confidence interval [97.94%, 99.18%]) in stratified session-level cross-validation in which user, device, and KYC-session instances were not shared between training and test splits.[106]

Its sensitive operational and behavioural Video-KYC dataset cannot be fully released in raw form, which limits independent replication and external benchmarking.[106]

SafeEar’s Transformer detector has high computational cost, and its generalisation is reduced across languages and under unknown codec conditions.[15]

SafeEar’s protection against recovering spoken words does not necessarily prevent its acoustic representation from revealing a speaker’s voice, health, accent, demographic information or environment.[19]

## Benchmark performance versus real-world deployment

### What specific benchmark performance metrics have been published for video

Judgment — Video detectors are normally evaluated either per frame or per video. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC/AUROC) measures how well a system ranks real above fake material across all decision thresholds; 0.5 is chance ranking and 1.0 is perfect ranking. Frame-level and video-level AUC are not interchangeable: the latter aggregates evidence across frames and usually reflects a different, often easier, decision task.

The 2023 NeurIPS benchmark *DeepfakeBench* standardized comparisons across 15 detectors and nine datasets, using accuracy, AUC, average precision (AP), and equal error rate (EER), but its principal results are frame-level. It trains on FaceForensics++ at c23 compression and separates six within-domain tests from eight cross-domain tests. That protocol is valuable because it holds the training source constant while varying test datasets, although it remains a controlled benchmark rather than a live deployment trial.[29][31]

The results make DFDC a recurrently difficult transfer target: SBI reached 72.42% video-level AUC there and LSDA reached 0.736 frame-level AUC, despite much higher scores on several other held-out datasets. ### What specific benchmark performance metrics have been published for audio

Judgment — Audio deepfake detection commonly uses equal error rate (EER): the error rate at the threshold where the false-acceptance rate—fake speech accepted as genuine—and false-rejection rate—genuine speech rejected as fake—are equal. Lower EER is better. Audio spoofing research also uses tandem detection cost function (t-DCF), which measures the cost of a spoofing detector when coupled to an automatic speaker-verification system rather than as a standalone classifier.

Published benchmark results on tightly specified challenge data can be exceptionally strong. A 2024 Mel-spectrogram, VGG18 and attention-based capsule-network system reported 0.06% EER on ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access (LA); a 2022 Wav2Vec 2.0 plus variational-information-bottleneck system reported 0.40% EER and 0.0107 t-DCF on the same benchmark.[19] The 2023 AASIST2 study reported EERs of 0.15%, 1.61% and 2.77% for wav2vec plus AASIST2 on ASVspoof 2019 LA, ASVspoof 2021 LA and ASVspoof 2021 DeepFake (DF), respectively.[76]

### How does video detection performance differ between controlled benchmark environments

DeepfakeBench quantifies the benchmark generalization gap directly. With FaceForensics++ c23 training, Xception’s mean frame-level AUC was 0.9450 within domain and 0.7718 across eight other datasets; the derived difference is 0.1732 AUC points. UCF similarly declined from 0.9527 to 0.7801, a 0.1726-point difference. SPSL recorded 0.9408 within-domain and 0.7875 cross-domain.[31][32]

In that controlled 2023 comparison, individual cross-domain AUC cells ranged from 0.55 to 0.95.[95][29][150]

These are not merely differences among architectures. Training choices can reshape apparent generalization: DeepfakeBench’s Xception baseline averaged 0.6250 frame-level AUC across FF++ c23, CDF-v2, DFD, DFDCP and FF++ c40 before its stated augmentation and pre-training settings, then 0.8160 after them. Its FF++ c23 score fell slightly, from 0.9893 to 0.9637, while external-dataset scores rose.[31] Spectrum features likewise increased FWA’s cross-data average from 0.7316 to 0.8035 in that benchmark.[31]

In the original Xception FaceForensics++ evaluation, accuracy declined from 99.26% on raw video to 95.73% on c23-compressed video and 81.00% on c40-compressed video.[29][139][150]

In DeepfakeBench’s 2023 within-domain tests, FaceForensics++ c23-trained Xception scored 0.9637 frame-level AUC on c23 and 0.8261 on c40; UCF scored 0.8399 on c40.[29][139][150]

FaceForensics++ c40 was the lowest-scoring within-domain condition for 12 of DeepfakeBench’s 15 detectors, and NeuralTextures was the lowest-scoring manipulation subset in every detector row.[29][139][150]

Controlled corruption tests identify particular vulnerabilities without reproducing the full field environment. RealForensics reported clean-video AUC of 99.8% and a mean 95.6% across saturation, contrast, occlusion, noise, blur, pixelation and compression tests; noise was the weakest condition at 79.7%.[26] Yet benchmark media are often high quality and limited in resolution, format, lighting, angles and processing history relative to forensic casework, while resizing and compression can remove frequency-domain cues on which some detectors depend.[7]

More deployment-like testing confirms a larger gap. Deepfake-Eval-2024 consists of 45.1 hours of video collected from social media and users of detection platforms in 2024. Its authors found that open-source video detectors had an average relative AUC decrease of 50% versus the academic datasets used in their original publications; the maximum off-the-shelf AUC across modalities and models was 0.58.[2] For the three named video systems, GenConViT fell from 0.96 AUC on its original test data to 0.63, FTCN from 0.87 to 0.50, and StyleFlow from 0.95 to 0.51.[2]

On Deepfake-Eval-2024’s circulating audio, AASIST scored 0.43 AUC against 1.00 on its original evaluation, RawNet2 scored 0.53 against 0.99, and P3 scored 0.58 against 1.00.[2]

Li, Azzali and Farid’s 2026 evaluation reported mean accuracy of 94.7% across five video detectors on clean media and 67.8% under JPEG processing, noise, low light, blur and encoding; XceptionNet scored 96.8% clean and 85.3% under blur, while MesoNet scored 88.1% clean and 65.1% under the altered conditions.[151]

Network delivery can add another shift for systems dependent on temporal or audio-visual synchronization. In LipFD’s real-world scenario table, WeChat-call accuracy was 72.53%, 81.67%, 71.34% and 52.89% in the table’s reported latency-and-language conditions; streaming-media accuracy was 74.41%, 90.18%, 82.24% and 60.98%.[103] These accuracy values cannot be directly compared with AUC, but they show that an operational channel can materially change performance.

The 2025 VCF conferencing benchmark used target videos from VCD and source faces from LaPa matched on gender, ethnicity, age and facial hair; all 14 evaluated detection methods degraded substantially as resolution, H.264 rate and background varied.[152]

**The video evidence supports a clear ordering: within-domain scores are highest, cross-dataset scores are lower, and social-media or platform-user media can be lower again because it combines unfamiliar generators with capture, editing, transmission and scene variation.**[31][52][2]

### How does audio detection performance differ between controlled benchmark environments

Müller et al.’s 2022 Interspeech study offers the clearest same-system comparison. RawGAT-ST with full-length raw audio achieved 1.229 ± 0.43% EER on ASVspoof 2019 LA evaluation but 37.154 ± 1.95% EER on the authors’ In-the-Wild dataset. RawNet2 with four-second raw-audio inputs reached 33.943 ± 2.59% EER on that in-the-wild set.[69] The In-the-Wild collection comprises 19,963 genuine and 11,816 fake clips from 58 celebrities and politicians collected from social networks and video-sharing platforms.[69][6]

Müller et al. reported 38.767 ± 1.28% EER for four-second RawGAT-ST inputs and 37.819 ± 2.23% EER for full-length RawNet2 inputs on their 2022 in-the-wild audio set.[69]

When Müller et al. trained RawNet2 on all ASVspoof 2019 training, development and evaluation data, its EER on their 2022 in-the-wild set was 33.1 ± 0.2%.[69]

Cross-model experiments show that this is not simply a web-audio problem. In the 2024 EMNLP cross-domain study, Wav2Vec2-base achieved EER below 1% when trained and tested on samples from the same text-to-speech model. Training on YourTTS and testing on Seamless Expressive instead yielded 29.71% EER on Libri prompts and 44.00% on TED prompts.[79] Attack augmentation helped in a narrower test: on TED, the baseline EER of 10.1% became 8.9% under low-pass filtering, 8.3% under MP3 compression and 5.5% under SepFormer noise reduction.[79]

In the 2024 CD-ADD study, Wav2Vec2-base trained without additional ASVspoof data yielded in-model EERs of 0.11% on Libri prompts and 0.35% on TED prompts, versus withheld-TTS cross-model EERs of 12.14% and 20.34%, respectively.[79]

Adding ASVspoof data in the 2024 CD-ADD study changed Wav2Vec2-base in-model EER to 0.07% on Libri prompts and 0.12% on TED prompts; its corresponding withheld-TTS cross-model EERs were 7.85% and 21.40%.[79]

The audio gap stems from changing distributions, including generator family, speaker, language, codec, bitrate, replay, rerecording and acoustic environment. The ASVspoof 2019 corpus is described as clean studio speech and directly generated fakes, whereas social-media content may be repeatedly compressed or replayed and rerecorded.[45] A 2024 analysis decomposed performance gaps from ASVspoof 2019 to ASVspoof 2021 DF and to In-the-Wild, attributing the reported gaps overwhelmingly to distributional difference rather than an intrinsic hardness component; for example, the In-the-Wild EER gap was 40.6 points for RawNet2 and 30.3 points for SSL W2V2.[96]

Compression is not a universal penalty with a fixed size. PS3DT achieved at least 97% accuracy in each of nine ASVspoof 2021 DF compression conditions, but the same study found worse results at lower bitrates and after transcoding, with AAC more robust than MP3.[75] VoiceWukong further found serious degradation for most systems after resampling, replay and time stretching; AASIST2’s English false-rejection rate reached 79.51% at a 1.1 time-stretch factor.[75][44]

In the 2024 CD-ADD TED cross-model corruption tests, Wav2Vec2-base EERs without attack augmentation were 45.0% under white noise, 33.1% under reverberation, 35.2% under a 6-kbps neural codec and 27.7% under noise gating.[79]

With attack augmentation, Wav2Vec2-base EERs in those 2024 CD-ADD TED cross-model conditions were 10.3% under white noise, 23.7% under reverberation, 28.9% under a 6-kbps neural codec and 14.5% under noise gating.[79]

**The consistent result is a distribution-shift penalty, not a single universal real-world error rate: controlled audio scores can be near-perfect, but errors rise sharply when synthesis model, language, codec, replay path or collection channel changes.**[69][79][44]

### Synthesis

Benchmark performance is useful for comparing systems only when the dataset, training data, decision unit and metric match. Video studies report near-saturated AUC on familiar FaceForensics++ manipulations but substantially lower cross-domain and in-the-wild results; audio studies report sub-1% EER on some ASVspoof conditions but double-digit or far higher EER under unseen synthesis models and web-collected speech. The direction is consistent across modalities, though the size of the penalty depends on the target generator, language, channel and scoring protocol.[52][31][2][69][79][44]

1. Report video results at both frame and video level, and identify the exact dataset, compression condition, split and aggregation rule.
2. Test audio and video detectors on target-platform material, including codecs, replay/rerecording, languages, network delivery and current generators.
3. Set operational thresholds using false-acceptance costs and score calibration, not AUC or EER alone. [19] [46]

## Ethical concerns

### What primary ethical concerns have researchers identified regarding deepfake technology?

Judgment — Deepfakes are not ethically problematic simply because they are synthetic. The central issue is whether a realistic imitation transfers a person’s apparent voice, face, actions, or authority to someone else without consent, thereby enabling deception or injury at interpersonal and institutional scale.

Researchers consistently identify misinformation, fraud, identity abuse and declining trust in media as the principal cross-modal risks. Realistic text-to-speech (TTS)—systems that generate speech from text—and voice conversion (VC)—systems that alter one speaker to resemble another—enable impersonation, scams, false statements and political manipulation; facial and video synthesis similarly enable fabricated visual evidence and deceptive public communications. These risks extend to attacks on automatic speaker verification (ASV), meaning systems that use a voice as a biometric identity check.[25][15][79][7] The resulting ethical problem is that an attacker can borrow the perceived credibility of an individual or institution, not merely create a false file.

Political and civic harms are a prominent category. Synthetic speech and video can fabricate messages by political figures, interfere with elections, reinforce partisan divisions, influence investment judgments and degrade the information environment on which democratic deliberation depends. Researchers document a 2024 fake-audio incident that urged voters to skip the New Hampshire primary, while a study of deepfake-enhanced financial news found that it shifted simulated investment choices among 536 participants, especially where the material appeared realistic and judgments were intuitive.[19][110][111] Yet the evidence does not show that deepfakes are uniquely more effective than other manipulative media: two false-memory studies found no advantage for deepfake video over photographic or text-only misinformation, and a 2025 scoping review warns that many studies lack suitable non-deepfake controls.[111] Thus, the stronger conclusion is that deepfakes lower the cost and increase the apparent realism of manipulation, rather than that every deepfake is uniquely persuasive.

Non-consensual synthetic intimate imagery (NSII), also called AI-generated image-based sexual abuse (AI-IBSA), is a distinct and severe harm. It involves a fake depiction of a real person’s face, body or voice in intimate material without consent; it violates sexual autonomy and what researchers call *representational privacy*: the privacy interest in a technologically produced representation of oneself.[112][113] Victim-survivors report shame, anxiety, depression, avoidance, social isolation, disrupted work and intrusive memories; women are predominantly, though not exclusively, affected.[112][111] A 2023 survey of 16,693 adults in ten countries reported 2.2% self-reported victimization and 1.8% self-reported perpetration of deepfake pornography, while cautioning that victimization may be undercounted because people may not know material exists or recognize it as synthetic.[113] This makes consent, control over likeness and rapid access to removal or remedy central ethical requirements, not secondary content-policy questions.

Deepfake misuse also creates security and evidentiary risks. Reported and attempted cases include CEO voice impersonation intended to authorize fraudulent transactions, while fabricated media can be introduced as evidence and genuine recordings can be dismissed through the “deepfake defense”—claiming an authentic recording is fabricated.[15][114] In court, either accepting a fabricated record or rejecting an authentic one can contribute to a miscarriage of justice.[7] The implication is that authenticity disputes affect due process, access to justice and institutional legitimacy as well as individual reputation.

Deepfake technology remains dual-use. The same capabilities support creative arts, entertainment, accessibility, language learning and legitimate voice applications; ethical assessment should therefore distinguish consensual, contextualized creation from deception, exploitation and foreseeable harm.[15][6][112] This distinction rules out treating synthetic generation itself as the ethical violation.

The *liar’s dividend* is the opportunity for a powerful person to dismiss genuine footage as a deepfake, shifting the burden onto journalists, victims and investigators to prove that authentic material is genuine.[115]

AI-generated child sexual abuse material is a child-protection harm because it can exploit and harm children and can reuse existing abuse imagery, causing further trauma to already identified victims.[116]

### What primary ethical concerns have researchers identified regarding deepfake detection?

Detection systems introduce a separate set of ethical problems: reliability, unequal error, privacy, transparency and misuse of defensive knowledge. A *false positive* labels authentic media as fake; a *false negative* accepts a manipulation as genuine. Both matter: false negatives allow fraud or misinformation to circulate, whereas false positives can cause censorship, suspicion, reputational harm or legal consequences.[64][66][38] Detector confidence is not necessarily reliable: one video-forgery study found that incorrect predictions were often made with high confidence.[26] Therefore, a detector score is forensic evidence to be interpreted, not an automatic authenticity verdict.

Platforms or investigators—not the classifier—make consequential decisions to label, remove or rely on media; those decisions require contextual review and a route to contest a false alert. False positives can mislabel authentic and legitimate expression as manipulated, causing unwarranted censorship as well as suspicion, reputational injury or legal consequences.[64][66][38][7]

Fairness is a deployment concern because aggregate benchmark accuracy can conceal unequal treatment of people and speech communities. In an evaluation of six synthetic-speech detectors, bona fide speech from male speakers, adolescents, older people, South Asian- and Australian-English-accented speakers, and speech-impaired speakers had higher false-positive rates.[15] Video research likewise found that benchmark datasets were demographically imbalanced and that, for EfficientNetB0 on Celeb-DF, attributes including Black identity, male identity, a goatee, a big nose and chubbiness were associated with roughly double the probability of an incorrect decision.[36] These results concern the studied models and datasets, but they establish why systems used in journalism, border control, employment, financial onboarding or criminal justice require subgroup audits rather than a single overall score.

Privacy arises because detectors often process sensitive voice, face and biometric data. Centralized training can expose personal data to leakage, motivating federated learning, in which organizations train locally and share model updates rather than raw records.[40] Privacy-preserving designs also operate at the feature level: SafeEar separates semantic features—information about what was said—from acoustic features used for spoof detection, then shuffles and reduces acoustic tokens to impede reconstruction of speech content.

In its content-recovery tests, automatic speech-recognition systems exceeded 90% word-error rate under several attacker-knowledge conditions.[19] Federated systems are not inherently safe: poisoned client updates can degrade or bias the shared model, so robust aggregation, anomaly detection, consent, secure parameter storage and audit logging remain necessary.[106] Privacy-preserving detection therefore reduces exposure but does not eliminate security or governance obligations.

Explainability and accountability are also necessary where a decision affects content removal, a person’s credibility or legal evidence. Deep neural detectors are often “black boxes”; attention maps or saliency methods can show influential regions without explaining why they matter, and explanations can themselves bias human users toward selected cues.[15][7] Detector labels also omit contextual facts relevant to authenticity, and their uncertainty can be difficult to interpret; forensic researchers therefore advocate likelihood-ratio reporting, which compares how probable evidence is under competing propositions.[7] In high-stakes settings, accountable use requires explanation, calibrated uncertainty and an opportunity for human review or appeal.

Finally, detection research has a dual-use problem. Publishing specific artifacts, open benchmarks and weaknesses can improve defensive testing, but it can also tell generators which traces to remove or which transformations evade a detector. DeepfakeBench explicitly describes this as a risk and considers controlled access and continuing updates; separately, researchers warn that identifying flaws in face forgeries may facilitate improved fakes.[29][26] Transparency should therefore be coupled with responsible disclosure and access controls, rather than abandoned.

### Synthesis

Taken together, the research shows two linked ethical duties. First, governance of generation must focus on consent, deception, personal safety and institutional information integrity, particularly for intimate abuse, fraud and political manipulation. Second, governance of detection must not treat a classifier as a neutral truth machine: false positives, false negatives, demographic disparities, privacy invasion and opaque reasoning can independently harm people.[112][7][15][40]

Detection and awareness interventions also have human costs. In one reported study, warning people about deepfakes did not improve real/fake discernment but reduced general news credibility; feedback or later revelation that participants had been deceived was associated with distress, greater AI anxiety and lower self-efficacy.[111] The practical direction is calibrated, privacy-conscious and fairness-audited detection embedded in human verification and redress processes—not automated labeling, mass surveillance or public skepticism toward all media.

## EU regulatory frameworks

### What major EU regulatory frameworks relevant to deepfakes have been

The EU governs deepfakes through a layered regime rather than one dedicated deepfake statute. **Enacted measures** regulate synthetic-media generation and disclosure, platform distribution, and specified criminal harms; **proposals** address voluntary transparency implementation and child-sexual-abuse material (CSAM).[117][118][4][119]

**The AI Act—Regulation (EU) 2024/1689—entered into force on 1 August 2024; Article 50 transparency duties applied from 2 August 2026.** Providers of systems generating synthetic audio, images, video or text must machine-readably mark outputs as artificial or manipulated and make them detectable, subject to technical feasibility, interoperability, robustness and reliability requirements. Professional deployers of image, audio or video deepfakes must disclose manipulation; creative, satirical and fictional works receive a less intrusive disclosure rule, and authorised criminal-investigation uses are exempt.[118][3]

The AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026. It added a prohibition on AI systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit or intimate material or child sexual abuse material (CSAM), including nudification applications; this ninth prohibited practice applies from December 2026.[120]

Directive (EU) 2024/1385, adopted on 14 May 2024 and requiring national transposition by 14 June 2027, requires criminalisation of intentional production or manipulation and subsequent public ICT sharing that makes a person appear engaged in sexually explicit activity without consent and is likely to cause serious harm. It requires maximum available imprisonment of at least one year, prompt removal or access disabling, electronic-evidence capability and victim support.[4] The Digital Services Act (DSA) adds notice-and-action and trusted-flagger mechanisms for illegal content.

Very large online platforms and search engines must assess and mitigate systemic risks, including risks to civic discourse and electoral processes, alongside duties concerning the protection of minors.[119]

The European Media Freedom Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1083, requires relevant very large platforms to let media-service providers declare compliance with applicable professional or regulatory standards. Its media-service scope includes broadcasters, on-demand audiovisual services, podcasts and the press, but generally excludes ordinary user uploads.[153]

Proposals and voluntary measures. On 7 January 2026, the European Commission was developing a voluntary, non-binding Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content; the January 2026 first draft described here proposed machine-readable marking through watermarking and metadata, content-detection and interoperability guidance, automated detection combined with human oversight, and modality-specific disclosure for lawful deepfakes.[117]

The Commission’s proposed recast Child Sexual Abuse Directive, COM(2024) 60 final, published in February 2024, would extend CSAM definitions to reproductions and representations intended to cover deepfakes, avatars, synthetic bodies, sensory feedback and immersive environments.[119] A June 2026 provisional Parliament–Council agreement on the proposed recast Directive would also criminalise acquiring, possessing or disseminating AI systems specifically designed or adapted to produce CSAM.[119]

The proposed Child Sexual Abuse Regulation, COM(2022) 209 final, would require service-level risk assessment and mitigation, permit detection orders issued by judicial or independent administrative authorities for known and new—including AI-generated synthetic—CSAM and grooming-related content, and establish an EU Centre on child sexual abuse. Parliament’s November 2023 mandate excluded grooming and text-based communications from detection orders and required judicial authorisation as a last resort; the Council’s 26 November 2025 General Approach removed mandatory detection orders, retaining DSA-aligned risk assessment and voluntary scanning.[119]

### Synthesis

EU law distinguishes lawful synthetic media, where provenance marking and disclosure are central, from illegal abuse, where criminalisation and removal apply. The 2026 prohibition moves beyond labelling for sexual deepfake and CSAM-generating systems; unresolved CSAM legislation concerns the permissible scope of platform detection.[118][4][120][119]

## United States regulatory frameworks

### What major United States regulatory frameworks relevant to deepfakes have

Judgment — United States deepfake governance is a layered system, not a single general deepfake statute. The frameworks are grouped below by jurisdiction and legal status: federal enacted, state enacted, federal proposed, and state proposed.[5][121][122][123] Federal law now concentrates on non-consensual intimate imagery and AI voice calls, while states have supplied most rules on election deception, sexual deepfakes, child sexual abuse material (CSAM), and unauthorized voice or likeness replication.

At federal level, the 2022 Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) reauthorization created a civil cause of action for disclosure of non-consensual intimate imagery: a depicted individual may seek damages or injunctive relief. Its original text did not expressly mention digitally created or altered images, so whether this remedy reaches intimate deepfakes remains unsettled.[5] The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) then adopted its Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) Declaratory Ruling on February 2, 2024, effective February 8, confirming that AI technologies generating human voices fall within restrictions on artificial or prerecorded voices.

Such calls generally require the called party’s prior express consent; telemarketing or advertising calls require prior express written consent, and prerecorded messages must identify the responsible entity and, for advertising or telemarketing, provide an opt-out method.[124] This makes voice-cloning robocalls actionable under an existing consumer-protection regime rather than creating a general rule for synthetic audio.

The principal federal deepfake statute is the TAKE IT DOWN Act, Public Law 119–12, approved May 19, 2025. It criminalizes specified knowing publication and threats involving non-consensual intimate visual depictions, including a “digital forgery”: an AI- or technologically created or altered intimate depiction of an identifiable person that a reasonable person would find indistinguishable from an authentic depiction. Adult-offence liability requires non-consent, non-public/non-commercial exposure, absence of public concern, and intended or actual psychological, financial, or reputational harm; minor offences use different intent elements. Maximum imprisonment is two years for relevant adult offences and three years for minor offences.[125]

The Act also imposes a notice-and-removal regime on covered public-facing platforms that primarily host user-generated content or regularly host non-consensual intimate depictions. After a valid request, platforms must remove the reported depiction within 48 hours and make reasonable efforts to remove known identical copies; the Federal Trade Commission enforces unreasonable non-compliance as an unfair or deceptive act or practice. The criminal provisions took effect immediately, while platforms had until May 19, 2026 to establish the removal process.[5][125] **This is a notice-triggered removal duty, not a statutory requirement to proactively detect every deepfake.** Whether its removal provisions cover “digital forgeries” is legally unsettled because those provisions refer to intimate visual depictions, although the statute defines a digital forgery as a type of intimate visual depiction.[5]

State enactments have expanded more rapidly and fall into four practical categories:

- **Non-consensual intimate imagery and CSAM:** Georgia expanded its covered non-consensual nude or sexually explicit transmissions to falsely created video or still images on May 2, 2022. Louisiana’s Deepfake Law, effective August 1, 2023, criminalized knowing distribution of non-consensual sexual deepfakes and knowing creation or possession of sexual deepfakes depicting minors, with stated penalties of five to 30 years’ imprisonment and up to $50,000 in fines. Texas made distribution of specified non-consensual sexually explicit deepfakes a Class A misdemeanor from September 1, 2023. [121]

- **Identity, voice, and performer rights:** California Assembly Bill 2602, approved September 17, 2024, makes certain employment-service contract clauses unenforceable where they authorize a digital replica to substitute for an individual’s new performance, fail to specify intended uses, and were agreed without legal or union representation. [126] Illinois’s 2024 Right of Publicity Act amendment prohibits knowing public distribution of unauthorized digital replicas in sound recordings or audiovisual works. Tennessee’s ELVIS Act gives every individual a property right in use of their name, photograph, voice, or likeness and supplies civil actions for defined unauthorized uses. [121]

- **Election deception and disclosure:** The National Conference of State Legislatures reports that 31 states have enacted laws regulating deepfakes in political messaging. Their dominant pattern is disclosure, rather than a categorical ban; state statutes use differing terms such as deepfake, synthetic media, and deceptive media, which can affect legal coverage. [123] Alabama’s law, effective October 1, 2024, criminalizes distribution of materially deceptive AI-generated election media, with a disclaimer safe harbor. Arizona’s Political Deepfake Law, effective June 4, 2024, requires clear and conspicuous AI disclosure for knowing deceptive candidate deepfakes distributed within 90 days before an election; its General Deepfake Law also gives citizens and qualifying candidates civil remedies for certain harmful, undisclosed, non-consensual digital impersonations. [121]

- **Provenance and transparency:** Utah’s 2024 election law requires audible disclosures at the beginning and end of covered synthetic-audio communications and visible disclosures during synthetic visual content intended to influence voting. California’s AI Transparency Act, effective August 2, 2026, requires covered generative-AI providers to embed detectable latent disclosures in generated image, audio, and video and provide detection tools; from January 1, 2027, certain large platforms must expose compliant provenance data to users. [127][121] Provenance means technical information associated with content that indicates where it came from or whether a generative system materially altered it. These laws make attribution and inspection more feasible, but do not guarantee that every manipulated file carries intact provenance.

California illustrates the constitutional constraint on election regulation. AB 2839 restricted malicious distribution of materially deceptive election communications, while AB 2655 required large platforms to identify and remove or label political deepfakes and accept reports. Both were enacted, but AB 2839 was narrowly enjoined and AB 2655 broadly enjoined in *Kohls v. Bonta*; NCSL reports that a federal district court found California’s law unconstitutional in September 2025.[121][123] Enactment, therefore, does not by itself establish present enforceability.

Federal proposals remain significant but have not created a comprehensive federal rule on political deepfakes. In February 2024, the FCC described bipartisan Senate legislation to prohibit AI-generated content falsely depicting federal candidates to influence an election, and separate proposed robocall legislation that would double the statutory penalty for AI impersonation intended to defraud. The Federal Election Commission had also sought comment on prohibiting deceptive AI use in campaign advertising.[124][128] These proposals target harmful use—electoral deception and fraud—rather than requiring platforms to deploy a particular detector.

The DEFIANCE Act of 2025, S.1837, passed the Senate by unanimous consent on January 13, 2026 and was then held at the House desk. It would expand federal civil remedies for non-consensual intimate digital forgeries: authentic-looking intimate deepfakes of identifiable people created through AI or other technology. A subject could sue for knowing production, possession with intent to disclose, disclosure, or solicitation and receipt under stated non-consent and knowledge conditions; proposed remedies include liquidated damages of $150,000, rising to $250,000 in specified sexual-assault, stalking, or harassment circumstances, plus injunctions and privacy protections in litigation.[129][130]

The NO FAKES Act of 2026, S.4591, was introduced May 20, 2026 and placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar on June 24. It would create a federal digital-replication right over a person’s voice and visual likeness, authorize civil actions for unauthorized public replicas and certain replica-generating services, and establish notice-and-removal safe harbors for online services. It includes exceptions for specified news, documentary, historical, commentary, criticism, scholarship, satire, parody, and fleeting uses, except for sexually explicit replicas.[122] The proposal would not impose a general platform duty to monitor for replicas.[122]

At state level, proposals range from transparency obligations to criminal prohibitions. Illinois H 1860 would prohibit specified knowing distribution of materially deceptive media during the 90 days before an election when accompanied by stated harmful electoral intent. New York’s pending Political Artificial Intelligence Disclaimer proposals would require disclosure of synthetic media in political communications and campaign-committee recordkeeping. Rhode Island S.2456 would prohibit use of defined synthetic media within 90 days of an election.[123][127] Other pending measures address non-electoral harms: New Jersey proposals would prohibit deepfake pornography with criminal and civil consequences, while Pennsylvania H.1598 would clarify that AI generation is not a defence to CSAM offences.[127]

- **Deceased performers:** California Assembly Bill 1836, enacted in 2024, imposes liability for producing, distributing, or making available without appropriate consent a deceased personality’s digital voice or likeness replica in an expressive audiovisual work or sound recording, subject to specified exceptions. [127]

### Synthesis

**The direction since 2022 is toward targeted accountability for harmful deepfake uses, with disclosure and consent as the dominant regulatory tools rather than a universal prohibition on synthetic media.** Federal law has established criminal and removal mechanisms for intimate-image abuse and consent rules for AI voice calls; state law has filled most gaps in election integrity, CSAM, impersonation, and publicity rights.[5][124][123][121] Disclosure-based election laws remain more common than bans, but constitutional litigation has limited some enacted restrictions, notably California’s.[123][121] Proposed federal measures would further protect intimate-deepfake victims and voice-and-likeness rights, but as of the recorded legislative actions they remain proposals rather than binding nationwide law.[122][129]

## International regulatory frameworks

### What major international regulatory frameworks relevant to deepfakes have been

Judgment — International action addresses deepfake harms chiefly through AI-wide rules on rights, risk, transparency and content provenance, rather than a single global deepfake law. The binding and voluntary instruments therefore matter for synthetic media even when they do not name deepfakes.

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225) opened for signature on 5 September 2024 and was not in force as of 3 November 2026: entry into force requires five ratifications, including at least three by Council of Europe member States, and two ratifications or accessions were recorded.[131][132] Upon its entry into force, Parties must ensure that AI activities respect human rights and do not undermine democratic institutions, processes, public debate, or people’s ability to form opinions freely; establish proportionate lifecycle risk-and-impact management to identify, assess and mitigate risks; assess whether moratoria, bans or other measures are needed; provide accessible and effective remedies, information, avenues to contest AI-informed decisions and lodge complaints, and notice where appropriate of AI interaction; account for children and persons with disabilities, ensure public consultation and digital literacy, and designate independent and impartial oversight mechanisms.[133] The European Union signed the Convention on 5 September 2024 and ratified it on 15 May 2026.[132][131][133]

The following adopted international instruments are nonbinding commitments rather than enforceable deepfake-specific rules. On 1 December 2023, the G7 endorsed the Hiroshima AI Process Comprehensive Policy Framework, comprising the OECD report Towards a G7 Common Understanding on Generative AI, International Guiding Principles, an International Code of Conduct and project-based cooperation on AI. Its voluntary, risk-based Code of Conduct calls on organizations developing advanced AI systems to identify, evaluate and mitigate lifecycle risks; use internal and independent external testing, including red-teaming; enable development traceability; publish transparency reports on significant releases, including evaluations, capabilities, limitations, risks and red-team results; and, where technically feasible, deploy reliable content-authentication and provenance mechanisms such as watermarking.[48] Provenance data should identify the generating service or model, but need not identify the user.[134][48]

UN Member States adopted the Global Digital Compact on 22 September 2024 and, on 26 August 2025, established an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and Global Dialogue on AI Governance: assessment and coordination bodies, not deepfake-specific regulators.[49][135][50]

**International proposals —** Before the United Nations adopted and established the Scientific Panel and Dialogue, the UN High-level Advisory Body on AI’s September 2024 report, 《Governing AI for Humanity》, proposed a globally networked AI-governance architecture comprising an International Scientific Panel on AI, a policy dialogue on AI governance, an AI standards exchange, a capacity-development network, a global fund for AI, a global AI data framework and an AI office within the UN Secretariat.[136][137] The Global Digital Compact subsequently committed to establishing the Panel and initiating the Dialogue, with an intergovernmental process to determine their terms of reference and operating modalities before General Assembly adoption.[49][138] The General Assembly then established both bodies on 26 August 2025; the Panel produces evidence-based assessments and early warning on threats including disinformation and algorithmic manipulation, while the Dialogue convenes governments, the private sector, civil society, academia and the technical community annually to exchange practices and foster common approaches.[135][49][50]

### Synthesis

International governance is layered: the Convention supplies the only binding treaty architecture, while G7 and UN instruments promote provenance, transparency and cooperation without a globally enforceable deepfake-detection or labelling rule.[131][132][48][138]

### Open questions

Open questions remain over whether detectors can retain calibrated accuracy across genuinely novel generators, languages, codecs, edits, and adversarial evasion; whether multimodal and foundation-model systems improve this robustness rather than merely benchmark scores; and what false-positive and false-negative rates are acceptable in platform moderation, elections, fraud response, and legal evidence. These questions would be settled by independently evaluated, longitudinal deployment tests with representative media, demographic reporting, fixed operating thresholds, and documented human-review outcomes.
