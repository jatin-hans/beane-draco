# Low-Connectivity Telehealth UX and Clinical Outcomes in East Africa

## Executive summary

For rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, make asynchronous, offline-first, voice/SMS-capable consultation the default and treat live video and large image transfer as escalation paths. Structured capture, local save-and-resume, visible sync status, and explicit referral rules are more dependable than a connection-dependent visit, because rural networks can support mobile contact while still failing data sessions, uploads, or sustained calls.

The clearest comparable-quality result is Kenya’s Mashavu workflow, where CHWs sent structured history and vital signs to a remote nurse and the advice matched the in-person consultation in 89.2% of 91 cases.[1] Tanzania’s electronic childhood-illness form improved documented danger-sign checks from 41% with paper to 74%, but its 53% expert-matching classifications show that structured digital assessment improves completeness rather than establishing diagnostic equivalence.[2] Use Babylon Rwanda’s basic-phone USSD, call-centre, SMS, prescription and follow-up model as the strongest named-service pattern for low-connectivity access, while requiring case-specific validation before attributing equivalent offline, diagnostic-image, or medication-reconciliation behaviour to mPharma or Zipline.[3][4][5]

## Low-connectivity primary care landscape

### What low-connectivity conditions shape remote consultations for rural primary care

Judgment — Rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania needs a different baseline from urban telehealth. The working assumption should be that voice may work when data does not, data may work too slowly or unreliably for a live interaction, power and charging may fail, and a clinician may need to resume a case after the network returns rather than repeat it from memory.

A useful distinction is the **coverage-to-consultation gap**: population coverage, subscription counts, and even measured download speed describe network reach or average performance; they do not tell a provider whether a particular call, clinical form, image upload, referral, or prescription exchange will finish at a rural facility. Across the three countries, low-connectivity design must therefore address connection establishment, session interruption, affordability, device capability, local digital skills, and continuity of the clinical record.

#### Uganda

Uganda is predominantly mobile-accessed rather than fixed-line. The Uganda Communications Commission reported fixed-line density of approximately 0.6 subscriptions per 100 inhabitants in 2025 and described mobile networks as the dominant access modality. Mobile-data subscriptions rose from 15.7 million in 2024 to 18.5 million in 2025, but represented only approximately 39% of active mobile subscriptions; the Commission said a large portion of active subscribers remained on voice-only or basic-data services.[6]

Rural network conditions are uneven. A 2017 literature review reported that 3G and 4G services were unavailable in most rural areas because low incomes made provision commercially unattractive. In 2025, UCC monitoring in eight border towns found that data speeds could be acceptable while session reliability remained weak, with low session-success rates, high latency, and packet loss; no operator consistently complied across every monitored border area.[7][6] UCC also cautioned that service quality varies by environment and operator, concluding that reliability matters as much as speed and must be assessed in consumers’ actual environments.[6]

Uganda’s infrastructure is expanding, but national expansion does not remove the rural design constraint. Fiber infrastructure reached 62,941 km in 2025, up from 47,771 km in 2024, while the Central Region accounted for 63% of deployed fiber, or 39,716 km. Fixed-internet subscriptions rose from 106,797 to 228,718 over the same period, with most deployment concentrated in the Central Region and major urban centres.[6] At the last mile, 53 of 64 subsidized tower sites had been commissioned by December 2025, extending broadband coverage to previously unserved sub-counties.[6]

Uganda’s April 2013 National eHealth Strategic Plan reported that data connectivity and networking covered almost 100% of the country, using fibre in major towns and wireless mobile connectivity in district, rural, and remote areas; it reported mobile-phone penetration above 40% and internet penetration of 3.2 users per 100 inhabitants.[8] That plan also stated that internet costs remained high and power was unreliable or unavailable, especially in lower health units and rural communities.[8] These statements are compatible when read as different measures: nominal wireless reach is not the same as affordable, powered, reliable higher-generation data service during a consultation.

The most concrete rural telehealth example is a CHW-to-clinician call-centre model rather than a direct patient-to-doctor app. A Kampala call centre staffed by four nurses and two clinical officers supported approximately 3,500 CHWs in 23 rural districts; the communities served were at least 7 km from a health facility and were estimated to contain 2.45 million people.[9] CHWs had mobile phones and tablets, met monthly for training and patient discussion, and received ordered products at those cluster meetings.[9]

The workflow combined several low-bandwidth communication modes. First, the call centre called CHWs and sent messages directing them to prerecorded COVID-19 information in several local languages. Second, when CHWs called the toll-free line, they heard a standard message and were redirected to a clinical expert for triage. Third, staff used the Ministry of Health COVID-19 risk-profile protocol to identify low-, medium-, and high-risk cases from symptoms and exposure or travel history. Fourth, call-centre health professionals used the Ugandan Ministry of Health COVID-19 risk-profile protocol to classify low-, medium-, and high-risk cases; it covered COVID-like illness symptoms, exposure to someone with COVID-19, or travel to a high-risk area, and defined symptoms as fever of 100.0°F, difficulty in breathing, and cough. Medium- and high-risk cases were referred to district surveillance teams, while the centre also supported malaria, pneumonia, diarrhoea, sexual and reproductive health, diabetes, hypertension, product ordering, and routine referral questions.[9]

Use of this service rose from 850 teleconsultations in April 2020 to more than 5,200 in June 2021; CHWs made 35,553 calls between 23 March 2020 and the end of June 2021. During the first 16 months, 82% of CHWs used the call centre, 71% used it more than once, and repeat callers averaged 14 calls.[9] Among CHWs who had used the service, 93.2% reported receiving the help needed to treat their patient, while 77.3% said support improved the quality of their work.[9]

The model also joined clinical contact to supply operations. CHWs could order oral rehydration salts, malaria tests, modern contraceptives, and other products through the call centre; during the first four months of lockdown, reported orders rose 26%, and 75% of surveyed CHWs said the service improved access to health products.[9] The same call-centre technology, skills, and network supported COVID-19 education, CHW diagnosis, referral and treatment support, cluster-meeting coordination, and delivery of medicines and other products; using one call centre for medical support and ordering medications and supplies required triage of inbound calls.[9]

Adoption was not universal. After one year, 18% of CHWs had not used the call centre. Interviewees associated non-use with lack of face-to-face trust-building and scepticism about telephone services in a context where people had experienced scams and fraud through calls.[9] This makes trust a clinical-workflow issue, not merely an onboarding issue: an apparently available line is less useful when the provider or patient doubts who is answering, whether advice is legitimate, or whether follow-up will occur.

Uganda’s policy documents make the required clinical workflow broader than a telephone call. The National eHealth Strategic Plan identified community follow-up, sending information to lower-level workers, distant consultation on patient management, and referral between health workers as needs. It proposed mobile-phone specialist advice for remote professionals and Village Health Teams, electronic sharing of laboratory results to support diagnosis at HCIV, HCIII, HCII, and isolated practices, and text messaging for referral and operational information.[8] The same plan proposed individual electronic health records for continuous capture and retrieval of personal information.[8]

Continuity is especially important in this setting because Ugandan primary-care research distinguishes **informational continuity**—consistent availability of the medical record—from **relational continuity**—an ongoing relationship with a particular provider.[10] Uganda’s earlier eHealth landscape was described as fragmented: applications were often stand-alone, donor-funded, non-interoperable, and unable to share information; paper records could be misplaced or unavailable when a patient visited another facility.[8] The COVID-19 response similarly found that multiple reporting systems not integrated with DHIS2 produced heavy workload, incomplete and duplicate information, and delayed communication and decision-making.[11]

Stakeholders at underserved Nairobi facilities reported unreliable power, internet, digital infrastructure, interoperability, and skilled ICT capacity as barriers to exchanging health information. They said fragmented systems could prevent providers from retrieving pertinent information when a patient had visited more than one facility, creating conditions for medical mistakes.[108]

Rural provider capability is another operating condition. Uganda’s national plan reported that computers in rural and remote health facilities were few, poorly maintained, and underused; IT professionals to manage and maintain equipment and support health workers were unavailable especially in lower-level facilities from HCIV to HCII and in communities.[8] The same plan said most rural health workers and consumers were not computer literate, and that nurses and doctors could experience ICT as an additional burden alongside routine workloads.[8] A rural primary-care intervention in Tororo likewise noted that new work creates indirect opportunity costs in provider time and effort amid low staffing and high patient volumes.[10]

**For Uganda, the design decision is to treat a live connection as optional rather than guaranteed: voice, SMS, locally retained case data, referral handoff, and later synchronization should support the core consultation, while video and large diagnostic transfers should be deliberate escalation paths.** This follows from the mobile-first access pattern, unreliable data-session conditions, rural power and skill constraints, and the demonstrated utility of a telephone-and-SMS clinician-support model.[6][8][9]

#### Kenya

Kenya has substantial national mobile adoption, but rural consultation conditions vary sharply by place and operator. The Communications Authority reported 84.1 million active mobile subscriptions in the third quarter of FY2025/2026, with smartphones representing 63.7% of connected mobile phones and 84.4% of connected users using mobile broadband.[12] These national figures coexist with rural quality differences: in FY2022–2023, Airtel and Telkom performed well in urbanized areas but had below-average coverage in rural and far-flung areas.[13]

The regulator’s results show why a platform should not infer consultation viability from a coverage icon. Safaricom’s end-to-end quality score was 87.60%, Airtel’s was 75.07%, and Telkom’s was 54.75%, against an industry average of 72.47%.[13] Airtel and Telkom failed critical unsuccessful-call and data-internet KPIs, measures the regulator linked to coverage and internet availability or accessibility.[13] Telkom’s coverage was reported to diminish rapidly about 5 km from town centres and serving cells, and the regulator found Telkom rural-county coverage very poor and absent in some locations.[13]

County-level variation was also pronounced. Average KPI compliance was 93.94% in Nairobi City, 57.57% in Baringo, and 54.54% in Laikipia. Consumer-experience results reported coverage challenges for 27.43% and connectivity challenges for 14.97% of respondents; industry coverage satisfaction was 80.83% and voice-quality satisfaction was 87.13%.[13] These figures describe different dimensions: satisfaction and site uptime can coexist with locations where calls fail, data does not connect, or a session cannot be sustained.

Kenya’s rural infrastructure has had localized gaps. In rural Kitui, areas that had lacked mobile broadband and network coverage since independence in 1963 received a broadband extension in November 2020; residents had previously walked many kilometres to seek connectivity.[14] In three locations after coverage changes, successful calls rose from 69% to 100% in Nguuku, 47% to 89% in Kasiluni, and 83% to 98.9% in Nthagani.[14] This is an infrastructure result, not a clinical-completion measure, but it demonstrates that call-based care may be impossible before local coverage changes and viable afterward.

The practical rural constraints include electricity, affordability, and digital familiarity. A Kenya-focused report identified frequent power outages and poor-quality infrastructure as challenges for rural network expansion.[14] Another source reported that 1 GB of mobile broadband cost over 4% of GNI, approximately US$65, and that only 21% of rural Kenyans had electricity services.[15] A Kenya review identifies connectivity, affordability, and digital-literacy barriers in rural communities, while costs of internet, mobile data, and devices affect low-income patients and providers.[16]

In a mobile-data-collection implementation spanning five East and Southern African countries, including Kenya, surveyors at some locations could not transmit completed questionnaires until they reached better coverage, and some phones lost power before the day ended.[109]

Kenyan practice has included both task-shifted telemedicine and hybrid service design. The Mashavu: Networked Health Solutions system was field-tested in rural Kenya to increase access to preprimary health care. Community health workers collected a patient’s chief complaint, medical history, and vital statistics in remote locations and sent those data by internet to a consulting nurse.[1] The nurse’s advice about patient action—such as referral or no further care—was the same in telemedicine and in-person consultations in 89.2% of 91 cases, while overall medical-feedback agreement was 78.4% across 102 cases.[1] The study’s authors concluded that the service could provide approximately the same quality of care and advice as an in-person nurse clinic visit.[1]

A later Kenya example illustrates a different service architecture. In Teso North Sub-County, Busia County, a maternal, newborn, and child-health pilot combined virtual doctor consultations with CHW triage, household follow-up, SMS engagement, referral, and facility care.[17] The channel stack was deliberately mixed:

- **IVR:** mothers and caregivers could complete danger-sign self-screening for themselves or infants. [17]
- **USSD:** clients could initiate registration and complete basic interactions. [17]
- **SMS:** one-way and two-way messages provided health education, referral notifications, and antenatal or postnatal alerts. [17]
- **Toll-free hotline:** users could obtain real-time consultation with doctors for maternal or infant concerns. [17]
- **CHW visits:** community workers provided household-level follow-up, support, and continuity of care. [17]

The hotline was the preferred channel, used by approximately 88% of telehealth-platform users; 50% of registered clients engaged in consultations with Health X doctors by hotline.[17] From June 2023 through September 2024, the service recorded 1,108 hotline calls, including 677 clinical encounters.

Of those clinical encounters, 435, or 64%, were managed by Health X without referral and 79, or 12%, required onward referral.[17] Twenty-three percent of clinical calls occurred outside the standard 8 AM–5 PM CHW workday.[17]

The low-tech self-service routes did not displace human contact. The IVR and USSD danger-sign assessment and “Hail your CHW” functions had very limited uptake; early explanations included lower perceived value, limited promotion, and a preference for the more direct and reassuring doctor hotline.[17] Clients and CHWs reported low literacy, trouble using the platform independently, shared or limited phone access, inactive SIM cards, long callback waits, and missed callbacks.[17] Strong supervision, CHW technical proficiency, troubleshooting ability, and clear roles improved onboarding.[17]

Kenya’s formal environment remains operationally incomplete for remote consulting in government-funded primary care. A 2023 readiness assessment found that Kenya had national digital-health legislation or policy, a digital-health body, a telemedicine or telehealth strategy or policy, and digital-health tools in use. The same assessment marked remote consulting as absent from health-worker scope of practice, and found no national affordable-airtime agreements for government primary care, remote-consulting service-delivery guidelines, or payment and reimbursement mechanism.[18] Kenya did include remote consulting in its national self-care guideline for reproductive health approved in June 2023.[18]

For Kenya, the strongest operational pattern is hybrid rather than digital-only: a low-cost voice/SMS route handles first contact and continuity, CHWs help people complete the pathway, and clear escalation carries patients to a facility when a remote exchange is not enough. Rural operator variation and limited uptake of self-service USSD/IVR make a video-first or app-only model a poor default.[13][17]

Kenyan provider-to-provider telemedicine was used during COVID-19, and a two-way SMS system supported automated monitoring and communication; network problems and additional health-worker workload reduced the SMS system’s efficiency. Kenyan stakeholders also identified unreliable power, connectivity, interoperability, staffing, and fragmented records as barriers to exchanging information about care delivered elsewhere.[110][16][108]

#### Tanzania

Tanzania has extensive reported mobile coverage and extensive use of older mobile technologies, yet rural consultation design must still account for local instability, feature-phone access, affordability, and intermittent electricity. In March 2025, TCRA reported population coverage of 98.4% for 2G, 92.2% for 3G, 90.7% for 4G, and 23% for 5G; geographical coverage was 76.2%, 74.1%, 73.6%, and 3.6%, respectively.[19] The gap between population and geographical coverage matters for rural services because a person or facility may be located outside the covered areas despite high national population coverage.

Tanzania also retains substantial 2G and 3G internet use. In March 2025, TCRA counted 22,037,228 internet subscriptions using 2G technology and 4,465,297 using 3G; mobile wireless represented 99.6% of all internet subscriptions.[19] By June 2026, TCRA listed 24,628,003 2G internet subscriptions and 37,575,797 mobile-broadband subscriptions.[20] The June report gave 2G geographical and population coverage as 98.66% and 79.86%, and 3G geographical and population coverage as 93.95% and 76.41%, respectively.[20] The ordering and values differ from the March 2025 report, so these national series should be used as reported period-specific indicators rather than treated as a single directly comparable coverage trend.

Feature phones remain more common than smartphones. In March 2025, smartphone penetration was 35.29% and feature-phone penetration was 82.64%; TCRA stated that a large percentage of the population still owned feature phones compared with smartphones.[19] In June 2026, smartphone penetration was 44.74% and feature-phone penetration 83.38%.[20] Tanzania also recorded 55.05 billion local SMS messages and 48.82 billion local voice minutes in the quarter ending June 2026.[20] These patterns make voice and SMS clinically relevant channels rather than merely fallback channels.

Regulatory quality measurements show that aggregate network performance is not uniform. For April–June 2026, all operators passed TCRA’s network-availability threshold of greater than 99% in 22 measured service areas.[20] For mobile data speed, measured against an average threshold of at least 10,000 kbps, TTCL passed in all 22 areas, Vodacom in 21 of 22, Airtel in 17 of 21, Yas in 16 of 20, and Halotel in 15 of 22.[20] For call-connection success, measured against a 98% threshold, Yas passed all 22 areas, Vodacom and Halotel passed 20 each, TTCL passed 18, and Airtel passed 17.[20] These tests support multi-channel design: a voice call may connect in a setting where a form upload, image transfer, or synchronous video consultation is poor.

Rural Tanzanian practice already includes remote-support and digital-training activity. In Ulanga District, a rural district with a population of 265,203, one hospital, two health centres, and 23 dispensaries, the REaCH programme was developed and pretested in July 2020 and delivered through Moodle and WhatsApp to 12 tier-one trainees, who cascaded it to 63 tier-two trainees from August to September 2020.[21] Sixty-one of 63 tier-two trainees, or 97%, completed the training.[21] This is training completion, but it shows that remote delivery can be incorporated into a rural primary-care support structure.

A Tanzania-centred asynchronous movement-disorders referral programme reported one practising neurologist per 10 million people in Tanzania and assessed 19 patients, mostly Tanzanian, with additional referrals from Rwanda, Malawi, and Zanzibar. From 2015, referring clinicians sent encrypted, anonymised videos, text documents, and images to a Canadian specialist server via broadband or WhatsApp; specialist reports returned to referring clinicians and were also used for teaching.[111]

Tanzania’s remote-consultation constraints extend beyond signal. In qualitative work, network instability was specifically reported in remote rural villages and during heavy rain.[18] Health workers identified airtime as essential, while some patients could not afford airtime and some elderly people lacked regular or easy access to a mobile device.[18] Facilities participating in remote-consulting work maintained disease registers and recorded phone numbers; for patients without phones, some recorded a close relative’s or friend’s contact details.[18]

Tanzania was reported to have one practicing neurologist per 10 million people, illustrating a specialist-access constraint for peripheral providers seeking remote advice.[111]

A basic phone can limit the available diagnostic workflow. Tanzanian patients and health workers reported that basic push phones restricted the range of remote services, especially sending photographs through WhatsApp.[18] This is a direct reason to distinguish between a workflow that requires clinical images and one that can proceed by voice, structured symptom capture, referral, or in-person assessment.

From 2015 onward, an East and Central African movement-disorders service accepted encrypted, anonymised videos, text documents, and images for later review by specialists in Canada. One series reported 19 patients, most from Tanzania, with additional referrals from Rwanda, Malawi, and Zanzibar.[111]

Clinical decision support has shown benefits for structured assessment, but not equivalence with expert clinical classification. In a Tanzanian cluster randomized trial involving nine primary-health-care facilities and children aged 2–59 months, CHWs using an electronic childhood-illness algorithm checked 74% of children for danger signs compared with 41% using a paper algorithm. Symptom checks were recorded for 99% in the electronic arm, compared with 75% in the paper arm and 77% in the control arm.[2] Expert-matching disease classifications were 53% in the electronic arm, 39% with paper, and 34% in the control arm.[2] Antibiotic prescription rates were 25% in the electronic arm, 26% in the paper arm, and 70% in the control arm.[2][22] (Rambaud-Althaus et al., 2017)[22]

The implication is that digital forms can make danger-sign and symptom assessment more complete while still requiring clinical escalation rules for uncertain cases. A structured form is valuable partly because it reduces skipped questions, creates a transferable record, and makes it visible when a clinician lacks information needed to decide. It does not eliminate the need for physical examination, laboratory testing, or referral.

Tanzania’s system readiness also shapes interface expectations. It also found no remote-consulting scope of practice, national affordable-airtime agreement for government-funded primary care, service-delivery guidelines, or payment and reimbursement mechanism.[18] Stakeholders warned that informal remote consulting could burden health workers and compromise care quality when they lacked patient histories, records, referral information, or details of available services.[18]

For Tanzania, a consultation should have a voice/SMS-capable minimum pathway, an optional smartphone pathway for richer data, and a clear route to referral when images, examinations, records, or test results are missing. High reported population coverage supports mobile delivery, while feature-phone prevalence, village-level instability, weather-related disruption, and varying operator results rule out a broadband-only assumption.[19][18][20]

#### Development context and relevance of the named services

Babylon Health’s Babyl deployment in Rwanda is relevant because it operated at national scale through channels that do not require a personal smartphone. Babyl launched in Rwanda in 2016, became the country’s first nationwide telemedicine service, and operated from 2019 through September 2023 in the analysed service period. It combined nurse-led triage, physician oversight, e-prescriptions, national health insurance, central call centres, USSD enrolment, telephone consultations, SMS prescriptions, laboratory ordering through the integrated Health Management Information System, and follow-up consultations as needed.[3]

Babyl’s patient-access design addressed shared-device constraints. Since 2016, users had registered with a national ID through a mobile phone, but that route was limited to people with a registered mobile. In April 2021, Babyl enabled registration and access from any phone using a national ID, without requiring a phone registered in the patient’s name. The company reported a 64% increase in female registrations and a 55% increase in consultations after the change.[4][5] USSD menus used number keys, enabling basic-phone access.[5]

Babyl’s deployment also illustrates the relationship between clinical workflow and service continuity. It integrated e-prescriptions, laboratory referrals, and sick-note issuance with Rwanda’s national Health Management Information System (HMIS), enabling continuity between virtual and facility-based care.[3] Across 3,899,788 recorded consultations from 2019–2023, 3,676,385 were completed, a 94.3% completion rate, while 223,403 were recorded as no-shows.[3] Integration with insurance, laboratory, and pharmacy systems was associated with 12–15% increases in consultation completion for each additional integration; the reported association does not specify whether the increase was relative or percentage-point.[3]

The Rwanda experience also shows that basic-access channels do not remove digital-literacy barriers. In a user-experience study, 78.3% of rural participants reported poor or very poor connectivity, compared with 23.5% of urban participants; smartphone ownership was 34.8% among rural participants and 76.5% among urban participants.[23] None of the participants older than 50 registered independently, compared with 89.5% of those aged 18–30, and rural participants averaged 2.8 registration attempts compared with 1.3 among urban participants.[23] Babyl halted in September 2023 for system redesign after reaching 450 of Rwanda’s 510 primary health facilities and enrolling 2 million patients by August 2020.[23]

mPharma is relevant to Uganda and Kenya primarily as a pharmacy-linked care and medicines network. Its 2021 impact report identified Kenya and Uganda among its operating markets, and the company said in July 2023 that it had expanded into eight additional African countries including Kenya and Uganda, with more than 400 pharmacies in key markets serving more than 100,000 patients each month.[24][25] Mutti Doctor’s 2021 impact report listed 41,535 community-health screening consultations, 5,806 consultations for children, 560 primary-care nurses, and 250 doctors as of February 2022.[24]

mPharma’s Mutti+ model in Nigeria links non-urgent primary care at a mutti pharmacy with free primary-care consultations, free laboratory tests, and access to 150 medicines for less than ₦1000 monthly.[26] Its reported analysis of more than 500,000 mutti-pharmacy visits identified 14 high-spending conditions, including malaria, diarrhoea or gastroenteritis, peptic ulcer, pneumonia, diabetes, and hypertension.[26] This pharmacy-linked model is relevant to medication availability and dispensing continuity. The later named-service comparison addresses mPharma’s documented interface behaviour for interruption handling, store-and-forward diagnostics, and medication reconciliation.

Zipline is relevant to rural primary care as an ordering and fulfilment service, not as an established clinical diagnostic-decision interface or patient-level medication-reconciliation tool. In Rwanda, clinic and hospital staff submit medical-product orders through SMS, WhatsApp, or telephone calls; staff receive launch confirmation and an SMS notification when delivery arrives.[27] Zipline also developed a Rwanda electronic database, accessible to government, to track blood demand, supply, and usage at SKU level in real time without dependence on hospitals’ existing connectivity.[28] In Rwanda, rural clinics were described as facing both stockouts and wastage from overstocking; in 2018, Rwanda was reported to lose 6% of blood donations through overstocking.[27] At the beginning of 2020, Zipline delivered more than 75% of Rwanda’s blood supply outside Kigali.[27] In 2019, its Rwanda centres made more than 11,000 deliveries of over 20,000 blood units, 30% of which were emergency deliveries.[27]

Zipline’s role can affect a remote clinician’s ability to act on a decision. In Ghana, an IDinsight evaluation found that Zipline facilities stocked one more product on average than control facilities, whose baseline was 10.24 of 18 products; this was a 9.4% increase.[29] Across all 18 products, Zipline reduced average days without a product by five days compared with 45 days in control facilities, an 11% drop; for products facilities chose to stock, it reduced days without a product by three days compared with 14.4 days, a 20% reduction.[29] It also reduced the probability of stockout at treatment time for severe-malaria treatment by 26 percentage points from a 41% control probability, and for anti-snake serum by 42 percentage points from a 73% control probability.[29]

Zipline’s clinical relevance is therefore downstream of assessment and prescribing: a clinician can choose a treatment only if the supply chain can make that treatment available promptly. Its documented Rwanda and Ghana work concerns blood, vaccines, medicines, supplies, and delivery ordering, while the named-service comparison should separate inventory availability from patient-specific medication reconciliation.

The named services matter to a low-connectivity platform in different ways: Babyl demonstrates USSD, telephone, SMS, insurance, prescription, laboratory, and facility integration at national scale; mPharma demonstrates pharmacy-linked care and medicine access in Kenya and Uganda; Zipline demonstrates how supply availability changes whether a clinical plan can be fulfilled. These are complementary service roles rather than interchangeable clinical interfaces.[3][25][26][29]

The three-country landscape supports a common design position: mobile access is widespread, but reliable clinical exchange is local, variable, and shaped by cost, electricity, skills, device type, and referral capacity rather than network generation alone. Uganda most clearly demonstrates CHW-mediated telephone and SMS clinical support; Kenya most clearly demonstrates a hybrid model joining hotline care to CHW and facility follow-up; Tanzania shows both extensive legacy-network use and a need for voice/SMS alternatives to image- and app-dependent care.[9][17][19]

For rural providers, the platform should preserve the consultation record locally, permit delayed completion of non-urgent exchanges, use voice and SMS for the minimum viable pathway, and attach referral, laboratory, prescription, and supply information to the same case record. Babyl’s Rwanda model shows the service-continuity value of integrating those systems, while mPharma and Zipline show that dispensing and stock availability remain separate operational dependencies.[3][26][29]

The 2025 fibre distribution quantifies the urban-concentration constraint: 23,225 km of Uganda’s deployed fibre lay outside the Central Region, and the Central Region held 1.71 times as much fibre as the rest of the country combined.[6]

The FY2022–2023 county spread between Nairobi City’s 93.94% average KPI compliance and Laikipia’s 54.54% was 39.40 percentage points, quantifying why a national mobile-adoption figure is not a consultation-viability measure for rural Kenya.[13]

In June 2026, feature-phone penetration exceeded smartphone penetration by 38.64 percentage points and was 1.86 times smartphone penetration, reinforcing the need for a feature-phone-capable minimum pathway.[20]

In the Rwanda user-experience study, rural participants were 3.33 times as likely as urban participants to report poor or very poor connectivity (78.3% versus 23.5%).[23]

Scope and terms — This section concerns rural primary-care providers in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania who conduct remote consultations over often-intermittent 2G/3G mobile connections. Evidence is current to 20 November 2026; publication dates, deployment periods, and measurement periods are stated separately where the evidence provides them. Uganda’s April 2013 National eHealth Strategic Plan defines telemedicine as healthcare delivery at a distance and describes **store-and-forward** as acquiring medical data, including images, and transmitting it to a health professional for offline assessment and treatment recommendation.[8]

In this section, **asynchronous exchange** means that case information, instructions, or results are sent and reviewed without both parties being connected at the same time; the Ugandan TB workflow, for example, returned sputum-test results and follow-up instructions by automated SMS after CHWs entered information into an application.[2] **Offline-first operation** means retaining newly created patient data in the phone cache, continuing the user’s work, and later replicating data to and from the server when connectivity is available.[30] A **completed consultation** is Babyl’s recorded category of completed consultation, distinct from a recorded no-show.[3] **Diagnostic accuracy** here means agreement between the remote primary diagnosis and the diagnosis reached by face-to-face examination.[31] **Medication reconciliation** means establishing and reconciling a patient-level medication record across the clinical and dispensing handoff; this is distinct from ordering stock or documenting prescriptions.

A reported offline-first EHR handoff shows why: a doctor could see a medicine order as synchronized while the pharmacy device had not received it, leading staff to request a paper prescription.[32]

Peripheral-provider-to-remote-clinician consultation: Touch Health’s SimuConsult in Tanzania is a physician-led visit facilitated by a health-post worker or community entrepreneur in the patient’s community. A Tanzanian health worker described consulting an on-duty doctor, referring an emergency patient to hospital, and speaking with both doctor and patient about progress about an hour later.[33][18]

Treat a remote consultation as a chain of identification and consent; triage; capture of history, observations, tests, and media; transmission; remote review; communication of the decision; referral or prescription; dispensing; and follow-up. The interface should show distinct saved locally, queued, sent, received, reviewed, and actioned states at each hand-off, because an attachment, prescription, or referral can fail independently of the clinical form.

Run urgent red-flag checks locally and provide a voice or in-person escalation route that does not wait for synchronization. A queued referral, prescription, or image must never be displayed as received or reviewed by its destination until the platform has an acknowledgement.

For store-and-forward assessment, permit compressed clinically adequate still images and short structured records to be captured offline, retained with the encounter, and transmitted resumably when service returns. This is more robust on often-2G/3G connections than requiring a sustained video session or repeated high-resolution image upload.

A 2025 Central Uganda formative evaluation found that the vast majority of the population had access to at least 2G networks. The same evaluation found that telemedicine integration at health centre IV facilities was minimal despite widespread phone and internet access, whereas referral hospitals had used technology for referrals, consultations, and knowledge sharing.[34]

The 2016 Uganda malaria m-Health/GIS study specified a 2G-first diagnostic pattern: in areas with only 2G, a smartphone with automated cell-count software could run mobile microscopy offline at the point of care, make the diagnosis locally, retain the patient record, and transmit that record to a larger health centre when 3G connectivity returned.[35] The study also specified that transferred data should be readable and integrable with widely used electronic health records.[35]

A 2022 survey at four Central Region health facilities found that 78.7% of 61 providers had previously used telemedicine, 96.7% said it could help them monitor patients more rapidly, 98.4% said they could easily learn it, and 49.2% considered their health centre to have the infrastructure needed to support it. Providers reported that patient technology challenges accounted for 34 of 132 barriers, low patient engagement for 25, lack of implementation support for 24, provider or practice technology challenges for 19, and lack of reimbursement for 19.[34]

A 2024–2025 Uganda stakeholder study identified poor internet connectivity, unreliable electricity, weak network coverage, limited internet access, lack of smartphones, and language barriers as telemedicine barriers, particularly in rural areas. Participants also identified absent physical examination as a diagnostic-accuracy and trust concern, including difficulty describing a child’s possible measles skin findings remotely.[36]

## Named-service UX comparison

Judgment — For rural primary-care providers working through intermittent 2G/3G, these cases solve different parts of the care pathway. Babyl lowers the communications threshold for a consultation; Mutti Doctor enriches a live remote examination; Zipline provides medical-product logistics rather than medical advice, diagnosis, prescriptions, or therapeutic recommendations. None substitutes for a provider-facing offline consultation record with resumable capture and patient-level medication reconciliation.[37]

### How does Babylon Health's Rwanda deployment handle intermittent connectivity

Babyl Rwanda launched in 2016 and operated until its closure for redesign in September 2023. In March 2020, Babylon and the Government of Rwanda entered a separate 10-year partnership to develop Digital-First Integrated Care.[4] Patients used *811# USSD menus on feature phones to register and book; SMS confirmed appointments and reminded patients five minutes before them; a triage nurse called first, then escalated to a senior nurse, GP, or physical facility.[38][23] The clinical workflow also included electronic prescriptions sent by SMS to patients and pharmacies, laboratory-test ordering through an integrated HMIS platform, and follow-up consultations as needed. The model used text and voice rather than multimedia data, but required mobile-network coverage and internet-connected practitioner call centres.[23][5][39][40]

Babyl also removed the requirement for personal phone ownership in April 2021: patients could use a shared device and national ID. The service reported a 64% rise in female registrations and a 55% rise in daily consultations after this change.[5]

Its asynchronous diagnostic handoff centred on laboratory work. A clinician ordered a test; the patient received an SMS requisition code and sample-collection instructions; a partner laboratory validated the code; the ordering clinician received results in a portal, with an SMS notification and potential follow-up for the patient.[5] Babyl therefore provides a useful pattern for delayed lab-result return, rather than a documented clinical-image store-and-forward workflow.

Babyl sent SMS prescription codes containing medicine details, dose instructions, and validity dates; partner pharmacies validated and fulfilled them.[5] This is pharmacy-verifiable e-prescribing. In practice, facility providers reported that they could not access Babyl consultation records and did not know prescriptions or referral reasons, while medicine stock-outs disrupted collection.[23] **The deciding implication is that a prescription code must be paired with a facility-readable consultation summary and an explicit pharmacy acknowledgement if it is to support continuity of medicines.**[23]

In an independently observed Rwanda study, standardized patients presented malaria, upper-respiratory-infection, and diarrhoea scenarios to Babyl and conventional care across 81 initial-consultation facilities, generating 2,677 complete visits: 1,218 Babyl visits and 1,459 conventional-care visits.[112]

For malaria, correct case management did not differ between Babyl and conventional care; correct case management in conventional care was 93%, and both models appropriately ordered malaria tests.[112]

For upper respiratory infection, correct case management was 28 percentage points higher with Babyl than with conventional care, whose baseline was 29%; the difference was primarily due to fewer unnecessary medicines and laboratory tests.[112]

A report describes a store-and-forward approach in which providers relayed images or structured data when connectivity permitted for remote-specialist review without continuous bandwidth.[41]

The 2026 BMC Primary Care article reports a 94.3% Babyl consultation-completion rate and contrasts it with 15–30% dropout in Nigerian digital-health pilots. It reports that each additional integration—insurance, laboratories, or pharmacy—was associated with a 12–15% increase in consultation completion. A separate report gives 87–94.3% completion for key clinical workflows, including respiratory and malaria consultations.[40][41]

### How does mPharma's telemedicine interface handle intermittent connectivity

Mutti Doctor is a pharmacy-based remote-examination model. A licensed community-health nurse supports the patient in the pharmacy while a doctor consults remotely; the setup includes a digital stethoscope, otoscope, thermometer, illuminated examination camera for skin and throat imaging, and rapid diagnostic tests.[42] The service was announced for Kenya and Rwanda; mPharma’s 2021 company footprint also listed Uganda, while Tanzania was not listed for Mutti Doctor.[42][24]

mPharma launched Mutti Doctor as a virtual doctor-office service through Mutti pharmacies in 2021.[24][42]

The consultation pattern is synchronous: Mutti Doctor was designed to give the doctor patient diagnostic data during the live virtual consultation.[43] The supplied device set supports examination of heart, lungs, ears, throat, skin, abdomen, and temperature.[44] This provides richer clinical input than a telephone consultation, but makes the live encounter dependent on a functioning connection between pharmacy and remote doctor.

An mPharma partner reported that more than 90% of patients visiting Mutti Doctor locations received a virtual consultation within 10 minutes, citing an mPharma survey without a stated survey date or denominator. This measures waiting time, not completed consultations.[113]

The available Mutti Doctor interface descriptions do not document an offline mode, retry state, deferred upload, or low-bandwidth transport behaviour.[43][45]

mPharma requires electronic records of prescriptions, diagnoses, and consultation highlights, and patients can fulfil prescriptions through Mutti pharmacies or delivery-only Mutti pharmacies.[45] mPharma’s electronic prescription network lets doctors know the real-time location and availability of medicines; its communication layer lets a network pharmacy chat with the prescribing doctor when the pharmacist has a question.[24] Doctors receive confirmation when patients pick up medication, and the system follows prescriptions from prescribing through dispensation and consumption.

Its digitized prescription data identify medicines fully, partly, or not dispensed and track substitutions and their reasons.[46] In mPharma’s October 4, 2021 company case, a 44-year-old woman with diagnosed hypertension and poor medication compliance had a blood pressure of 202/124 mmHg, received an initial antihypertensive dose and counselling after diagnosis of hypertensive emergency, was referred for emergency hospital care, and subsequently returned for discussion and follow-up; the account reports improved medication compliance.[46][42] **Mutti Doctor is consequently most useful as a nurse-supported synchronous tele-examination model, not as a demonstrated low-bandwidth asynchronous diagnostic system or medication-reconciliation workflow.**

mPharma’s model assigns community-health nurses to operate devices and facilitate data entry, reducing the cognitive and technical complexity faced by frontline providers or patients.[41]

In mPharma’s October 4, 2021 company case, a 12-year-old uninsured patient with five days of cough, rhinorrhea, headache, and left-ear pain received a digital consultation. The remote examination recorded fever, tachycardia, a clinically clear chest, and an intact inflamed left tympanic membrane with scanty whitish discharge; when the patient could not afford urgent FBC and an ear-discharge swab, she received analgesia and discounted affordable empirical antibiotics, and the account reports recovery and prevention of mastoiditis and meningitis.[42]

### How do Zipline's clinical decision tools handle intermittent connectivity

Zipline’s documented Rwanda role is medical-supply ordering and delivery, rather than remote consultation or clinical decision support. Facility personnel can submit orders through SMS, WhatsApp, phone calls, Google Forms, text, mobile apps, or web apps; staff receive launch and arrival notifications by text.[27][28][29]

Zipline’s Rwanda supply database tracked SKU-level blood demand, supply, and usage for government users in real time, reducing reliance on hospitals’ existing connectivity for that tracking function.[28] Its Ghana operations nevertheless relied on electricity and mobile networks that were frequently disrupted, with grid power, generators, UPS backup, and monitoring used to improve operational uptime. A Zipline senior employee stated a system goal of 100 percent uptime across electricity, telecom, staff, and equipment.[47]

For diagnostics, Zipline transports samples, sensitive equipment, and diagnostic tests as physical items.[48] For medicines, it supports central inventory, on-demand orders, delivery tracking, and stock monitoring.[29] Ghana facilities served by Zipline stocked one additional product on average among 18 products in scope and had five fewer days without products over the previous three months than controls.[29] **Zipline should therefore be treated as a logistics dependency in the clinical workflow—helping clinicians obtain treatment and tests—not as the interface that captures diagnostic reasoning or reconciles a patient’s medicines.**[29]

For Ghana facilities, Zipline’s 26-percentage-point reduction in severe-malaria-treatment stockout from a 41% control probability equals a 63.4% relative reduction; its 42-point reduction in anti-snake-serum stockout from 73% equals a 57.5% relative reduction.[29]

### Synthesis

Judgment — Rank Babyl first for low-capability access because it deliberately combines USSD, SMS, voice, basic-phone navigation, and shared-device identity verification. Rank Mutti Doctor second for connectivity-constrained consultation UX because its nurse-and-device model adds clinically valuable examination input but depends on synchronous exchange. Treat Zipline separately: it strengthens the fulfilment side of the workflow through multi-channel ordering and rapid supply delivery, not the consultation interface.[5][43][42][27]

For Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, adopt Babyl’s access pattern, Mutti Doctor’s assisted examination pattern where a staffed pharmacy or clinic is available, and Zipline-like supply-status confirmation before prescribing. A different ranking would require demonstrated offline queueing, resumption after disconnection, and cross-facility medication-list reconciliation within one of these products.

## Effects on consultation quality and patient outcomes

Asynchronous communication, offline-first architecture, and diagnostic workflow design solve different parts of the same rural-care problem. Asynchronous communication removes the need for patient, frontline provider, and remote clinician to be connected at the same time. Offline-first architecture prevents the clinical task from stopping when a connection drops. Diagnostic workflow design determines whether the information captured remotely is sufficient for a safe decision, escalation, or referral.

For rural primary-care providers in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania working over intermittent 2G/3G connections, the practical goal is not to make every consultation remote. It is to preserve continuity for cases that can safely be handled remotely, capture the information needed for later review, and make the in-person escalation route explicit when remote evidence is inadequate.

### How do asynchronous communication patterns affect consultation quality and patient

Asynchronous communication means that one party captures and sends information now, while the clinician reviews and responds later. In clinical practice this includes SMS follow-up, structured questionnaires, store-and-forward photographs or test results, and provider-to-provider case messages. Store-and-forward teledermatology, for example, sends images and clinical information for later review, unlike live video, which requires both people to be present at the same time.[51][52]

This timing difference matters on unstable networks. The Turkana County asynchronous provider-to-provider teleconsultation project describes asynchronous teleconsultations as more practical in low- and middle-income settings with limited, low-bandwidth internet and unreliable electricity. It connected non-physician clinicians in 20 remote primary-health-care clinics and two hospitals to remote doctors and specialists, and its platform was designed for commonly used mobile devices with minimal technology training.[53] Between January 2017 and June 2018, the service facilitated 104 provider-to-provider asynchronous teleconsultations initiated from 17 of 22 intervention facilities: 73.5% were between Turkana non-physician clinicians (nurses and clinical officers) and remote medical officers, 12.5% were initiated by Turkana medical officers, and 14.1% were between non-physician clinicians and remote specialists.[53]

The Turkana workflow used asynchronous consultation for non-emergency cases that local clinicians would otherwise attempt to refer. Remote physicians either supported local management or advised referral, and the platform also supported coordination with the referral hospital when a patient needed to travel. Providers and county health managers reported better local management, fewer unnecessary referrals, stronger communication, and improved coordination for necessary referrals. The study did not measure patient-level outcomes, so these are service and provider effects rather than measured health effects.[53] For a rural platform, this supports an interaction pattern of structured case submission, remote review, and documented referral coordination—not unstructured messaging alone.

Uganda’s National eHealth Strategic Plan similarly distinguishes interactive telemedicine from store-and-forward care. It defines interactive services as real-time communication by telephone, web conference, video conference, or similar means, while store-and-forward acquires medical data such as images and transmits it for offline assessment and treatment recommendations. The plan proposed voice, text messaging, and email for teleconsultation and referral between higher- and lower-level facilities.[8] A 2017 Uganda review reported that Siedner et al. (2012) found that adding SMS to standard GPRS cellular-network connectivity significantly reduced connection failures for mobile-health applications in remote areas of sub-Saharan Africa.[7]

**Asynchronous exchange improves resilience because it decouples information capture and transmission from clinician review; its quality benefit comes from the completeness and usability of the submitted case, not from messaging alone.** This follows from the Turkana workflow, the Uganda store-and-forward definition, and the reported reduction in connection failures when SMS supplemented GPRS.[53][8][7]

The clinical performance of asynchronous care is strongest where a condition can be represented adequately in structured history, examination findings, images, or test results. In dermatology, store-and-forward permits a clinician to review images and text later, avoiding the bandwidth and scheduling demands of video. A review states that store-and-forward may be preferable where stable, fast internet is unavailable, and that it offers greater flexibility for patients and clinicians.[52] In a study of pigmented lesions removed for histology, the authors reported that stored video-image assessment could diagnose pigmented lesions as accurately as conventional clinical examination.[54] This does not mean that image-based consultation can replace examination for all skin conditions: the same authors emphasized that stored images cannot examine the whole patient or palpate a lesion.[54]

Store-and-forward can also outperform a poor live connection. In one high-definition teledermatology comparison, store-and-forward and uncompressed video produced about the same diagnostic concordance and confidence, and both performed significantly better than compressed video.[55] That distinction is important on 2G/3G networks: a live video consultation that technically connects but delivers compression artifacts, frozen images, or broken audio can be clinically worse than a well-captured image set reviewed later.

The benefits are not confined to image specialties. In a rural Indian provider-to-provider primary-care study, a digital assistant guided community health officers through adaptive history-taking questionnaires. The remote doctor could either call the patient or officer or, if the information was sufficient, make a diagnosis and triage decision asynchronously. Doctors considered the submitted information sufficient to diagnose without speaking to the patient in 84% of 104 cases, and community health officers reported that they were less likely to skip assessment steps.[56][57] However, diagnostic concordance was 71.4% in asynchronous cases and 81.2% in synchronous cases; the observed difference was not statistically significant.[57] This supports guided asynchronous history collection as a feasible workflow, rather than evidence that it is interchangeable with live assessment in all presentations.

Asynchronous communication can also support continuity after the initial encounter. In Tanzania’s REaCH remote-consulting work, facilities used calls for scheduled patient follow-up, advice, treatment questions, prescriptions, test results, referrals, and urgent help. One facility followed up biweekly; another advertised an inquiry number from 06:00 to 19:00.[18] A Tanzanian patient described telephone follow-up on medication timing and a return for laboratory tests, after which the patient reported that blood sugar had moved toward normal.[18] Another patient who mixed up several medicine bottles was asked to describe tablet colours and sizes; when that was insufficient, the doctor directed the patient to a nearby pharmacy so the pharmacist could clarify the medicines during a call.[18]

This is a useful distinction for medication workflows: remote communication can support medication clarification, adherence counselling, refill decisions, and escalation, but verbal description alone may be insufficient for medication reconciliation. Medication reconciliation means building and verifying the accurate, current list of all medicines, doses, routes, and schedules. The Tanzania example shows that a safe workflow needs a fallback to pharmacy review or physical inspection when identity cannot be confirmed remotely.[18]

Patient outcomes are mixed and condition-specific. A systematic review of asynchronous general-practice consultations reported patient-reported query resolution in 33% to 66% of cases; one included study reported complete resolution more often after e-consultations than face-to-face consultations, 55% versus 33%.[58] The same review found no difference in hospital admissions or emergency-care seeking by consultation type, but also stated that safety outcomes were not widely reported.[58] In a sinusitis cohort, text-based virtual visits were associated with more unplanned revisits than in-person office visits: 8.0% versus 1.7% within 24 hours and 14.9% versus 7.4% within 30 days.[59] The direction is clear: asynchronous care can resolve selected problems, but insufficient assessment can shift work into repeat contacts.

Kenya’s hybrid maternal and newborn programme illustrates the difference between engagement and timely completion of a care pathway. The programme combined USSD registration, IVR danger-sign screening, SMS reminders and alerts, a toll-free clinical hotline, and community health-worker visits.[17] The hotline was the dominant channel, used by approximately 88% of telehealth-platform users, while IVR/USSD risk assessment and the “Hail your CHW” feature had limited uptake.[17] The intervention site recorded an average of 4.99 postnatal-care CHW-client touchpoints within six weeks, versus 3.96 in control community units (p=0.003).

Within six weeks, 334 of 625 women and infants (53%) in intervention sites received all three postnatal touchpoints on time, compared with 965 of 1,589 (61%) in control sites.[17]

This result matters for product metrics. A platform can increase contact frequency, reach users outside working hours, and improve follow-up visibility without increasing completion of the full recommended clinical pathway. The relevant measurement is therefore not only messages sent, calls answered, or contacts completed; it is whether the patient received the required clinical action on time.

For Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, use asynchronous communication as a recoverable follow-up, documentation, and escalation layer; reserve live calls or in-person review for time-critical decisions, insufficient histories, uncertain medication identity, and problems requiring examination. The evidence supports its resilience and access value, while showing that communication alone does not make remote assessment clinically complete.[53][60][18]

In Turkana County, 17 of 22 intervention facilities initiated asynchronous teleconsultations between January 2017 and June 2018, an uptake of 77.3% across the enrolled facilities.[53]

Consequential asynchronous handoffs need an identified actor and acknowledgement rather than an assumption that a message has been acted on. The remote reviewer should record a response to each queued case; the local provider should acknowledge urgent findings and record the action or referral; and the pharmacy should confirm receipt of a prescription or identify a supply or receipt problem. This reflects the delayed-feedback characteristic of asynchronous teleconsultation, the Tanzanian emergency pathway in which a health worker consulted an on-duty doctor, referred the patient, and later checked progress, and the medication-clarification pathway in which the doctor directed the patient and pharmacist to resolve uncertain medicine identity.[53][18]

The Turkana nREM interface let non-physician clinicians enter narrative case information, enter structured data including HIV/VDRL status and ambulance use, capture and redact camera images, and upload DICOM data in a browser when available. Remote physicians received an SMS notification with a case hyperlink and used a webpage with chat, scheduling, reporting, and DICOM-image viewing functions.[53]

An African movement-disorders asynchronous consultation programme required local physicians to record videos using a standard neurological-examination protocol and submit case reports through a referral template. A project manager uploaded the materials to a secure electronic medical record and assigned the case to a specialist, who returned a diagnostic evaluation and treatment recommendations.[114]

No generic asynchronous interaction pattern has a documented consultation-completion rate above 85%, and no generic pattern meets both that threshold and diagnostic accuracy comparable with an in-person reference. Define completion as the proportion of initiated eligible encounters that reach a documented clinician disposition, rather than the proportion of messages sent successfully.

### How does offline-first architecture affect consultation quality and patient outcomes

Offline-first architecture means that the application can show the necessary forms, patient data, reference content, and locally entered records without a live server connection. The device saves work locally first, then synchronizes changes when connectivity returns. This differs from an online-first system that blocks an assessment, prescription handoff, or referral because an immediate request to a remote server has failed.

ODK Collect can securely retain completed questionnaires until signal returns and then upload them; OpenSRP documents offline registration and service recording with automatic or user-triggered synchronization when connectivity returns.[109][115]

The Community Health Toolkit describes this pattern directly: when a user registers a family or completes a task, the application stores new patient data in the phone’s cache and attempts to submit it to the server. It periodically checks for relevant server updates, refreshes the local cache, and performs this replication without interrupting the user’s work.[30] This is the key operational benefit for a rural clinician: a consultation can be completed at the bedside even when data transmission must wait.

An offline-first electronic-health-record feasibility study found that synchronizing only new or edited events, rather than the entire database on every sync, made synchronization more efficient and reliable when a clinic needed repeated synchronization during a patient visit.[32] In a different deployment, providers returned from refugee camps without internet access and synchronized once or twice daily; because they did not need multiple in-encounter syncs, they did not experience the same synchronization difficulties.[32] The implication is not that daily synchronization is universally sufficient.

Offline records can also travel between devices when the originating device never connects to the network: ODK-Liberia used bidirectional Bluetooth transfer between Android devices and archived source files before copying them, while OpenSRP supports Wi‑Fi Direct transfer through a chain of devices until one can synchronize with the cloud.[116][117][115]

It is that synchronization frequency must match the clinical dependency: a consultation note may safely queue, while an urgent prescription, referral, or stock decision may require an immediate confirmation path.

In the ODK-Liberia deployment, median time from form completion to arrival in the central database was 24 days for routine visits, 27 days for integrated community case-management forms, and 17 days for supervision forms. These transfer times distinguish preserved data capture from a clinically timely handoff.[116]

Medication handoffs demonstrate the risk of treating synchronization as invisible infrastructure. In the same feasibility study, a clinician believed a medication order had reached the pharmacy device, but the pharmacy had not received it and asked for a paper prescription.[32] A medication-reconciliation or prescribing workflow therefore needs named ownership and acknowledgement at each handoff: the clinician records the order and sees whether it is saved locally or queued; the pharmacy explicitly confirms receipt or reports that the order has not arrived; and the clinician resolves the discrepancy before relying on the handoff. The reported pharmacy-order failure shows why an apparent synchronization on the clinician’s device is not confirmation that the pharmacy has received the order.[32]

**Offline-first architecture protects consultation quality operationally by allowing local capture and clinical work to continue through an interruption; it does not by itself ensure that other care roles have received the latest information.** Local persistence must be paired with synchronization status, conflict handling, and role-specific confirmation.[30][32]

This is particularly relevant in Uganda. A Uganda literature review reported that 3G and 4G services were not available in most rural areas because provision was not economically viable, and identified power blackouts, loss of internet connectivity, and an unskilled health workforce as barriers to eHealth uptake.[7] Uganda’s eHealth strategy also described fragmented electronic data across donor-funded, public, and private projects, with data that could differ by source, and called for individual electronic health records to support continuous capture and retrieval of personal information.[8]

Rwanda’s Babyl experience demonstrates the continuity problem from the service side. Rural participants reported poor or very poor connectivity more often than urban participants: 78.3% versus 23.5%.[23] Providers reported that they could not access Babyl consultation records, that incomplete information reached facilities, and that this fragmented care when patients later arrived for medication collection or follow-up.[23] This is not evidence that Babyl had an offline-first architecture; it shows why local availability of a synchronized clinical summary matters.

Judgment — For an offline-first rural primary-care platform, each encounter should create a durable local clinical packet: patient identity and contact route; presenting complaint; red flags; structured history; recorded observations; images or test attachments; assessment; treatment; referral plan; medication list; and follow-up deadline. The packet should be usable before upload, encrypted on the device, and reconciled with the server when a connection returns. This is the minimum unit that lets care continue when synchronization is delayed.

Offline-first architecture has an indirect patient-outcome pathway. It can prevent an interrupted connection from becoming an abandoned assessment, preserve documentation for follow-up, and reduce the need for duplicate data entry. Yet the supplied findings report architecture performance and workflow experience, not a measured improvement in diagnostic accuracy, consultation completion, or patient health outcomes attributable solely to offline-first design. The design conclusion is therefore operational: offline-first is a continuity safeguard for low-connectivity clinical work, not a substitute for clinical validation, supervision, or medication availability.[30][32][61]

For clinically material fields such as patient identity, allergies, the medicine list, red flags, assessment, plan, and referral destination, the system needs explicit rules for resolving conflicting offline edits. Cached decision support and medication information should display their version date and restrict recommendations when current information is unavailable.

### How does diagnostic workflow design affect consultation quality and patient

Diagnostic workflow design is the sequence that converts a complaint into an actionable clinical decision: collect history and observations, identify red flags, capture diagnostic media or tests, assess information adequacy, obtain remote advice where needed, prescribe or refer, and close the loop. In low-connectivity settings, the workflow must make information completeness visible before a case is transmitted, because later clarification costs time, airtime, and repeat contact.

Incomplete records delay specialist decisions. In a Brazilian store-and-forward teleneurology service, neurologists needed more information before making a full decision for 427 of 1,035 requests, or 41.26%. The authors stated that incomplete requests could require repeat review and delay decisions.[62] In the rural India provider-to-provider study, at least 12 of 27 discordant cases, or 44%, involved missing, incorrect, or incomplete information provided by the community health officer.[57] The digital assistant’s adaptive prompts were intended to reduce such omissions; for a child with diarrhoea, Ayu prompted the nurse to capture blood or mucus in stool, fever, and a depressed skull fontanel.

The cited Delhi study found that 25% of providers asked about blood or mucus in stool, 49% asked about fever, and 7% checked for depression in the skull fontanel.[57]

Guided algorithms can improve completion of clinical checks without guaranteeing expert-level classification. In Tanzania, a cluster randomized comparison found that community health workers using an electronic childhood-illness tool checked children for danger signs more often than those using paper: 74% versus 41%. The electronic arm also checked main symptoms for 99% of children, compared with 75% using paper, but disease classifications matched experts for 53% of electronic-tool cases, 39% of paper cases, and 34% of control cases.[2] The electronic tool also had lower antibiotic prescribing than control: 25% in the electronic arm, 26% with paper, and 70% in control.[2][22] A form can improve protocol adherence and reduce omissions, while diagnostic accuracy still depends on correct observations, clinical judgment, and escalation.[2]

In that Rwanda standardized-patient evaluation, correct malaria case management did not differ between Babyl and conventional care, whose correct-management rate was 93%. For upper-respiratory infection, Babyl’s correct-management rate was 28 percentage points higher than the 29% conventional-care baseline, largely because clinicians ordered fewer unnecessary medicines and laboratory tests.[112]

Image acquisition is equally safety-critical. In a teledermatology referral cohort, 20% of referrals were rejected, mostly because imaging was inappropriate.[63] In a pediatric teledermatology study, live consultation was needed in 10% of cases because clinical information was poor or pictures were of insufficient quality.[60] The workflow implication is direct: do not let a user submit a diagnostic image as though it were clinically adequate merely because a file was uploaded.

In a rural German primary-care dermatology workflow, GPs obtained written consent, captured skin images, attached a specialised documentation sheet, and sent the case through compliant messaging; the dermatologist agreed to respond within 48 hours. Early feedback prompted better lighting and additional image angles to help assess lesion size.[118]

Several capture patterns address this problem:

- **Structured image set:** A teledermatology study used one to six smartphone images per case, with neutral background, perpendicular camera positioning, and diffuse indirect lighting. Of 982 images, 24 were out of focus or overexposed; other images in the set could usually still support assessment. [31]

- **Image guidance and redundancy:** Skin-imaging applications commonly use imaging tips, multiple-image requirements, pose matching, image-area matching, and automatic rejection or retry. A multiple-image requirement can increase the chance of receiving a clinically sufficient image, but image quantity does not compensate for poor focus, lighting, or framing. [64]

- **Training at capture:** In 20 studies with 37 unique comparisons that reported image-acquisition training, the teledermatology meta-analysis found mean agreement between teledermatology and face-to-face (F2F) physicians of 75.9% (CI 74.4% to 77.27%), versus 62.1% (CI 60.5% to 63.7%) where no training was provided. Mean kappa concordance was 0.77 (CI 0.66 to 0.84) with training and 0.60 (CI 0.49 to 0.69) without training. [65]

- **Adequacy gate and escalation:** The workflow should label a case as ready for remote review only after required history, red flags, and minimum image views are present. If the history or images remain inadequate, route to a live call or in-person examination rather than repeatedly asking for unstructured additions. This reflects the observed 10% live-escalation rate for inadequate pediatric dermatology submissions. [60]

**Diagnostic workflow quality is decided at the collection stage: structured prompts, adequate images, and an explicit escalation route make asynchronous review safer and faster than sending an incomplete case into a remote queue.**[57][63][60][65]

Store-and-forward review can preserve specialist input when real-time transmission is poor. A tele-ophthalmology review described store-and-forward image transmission combined with real-time videoconferencing as an alternative for poor internet speeds.[66] The practical pattern is hybrid: first capture and queue the durable clinical evidence; second, use a low-bandwidth live call only for clarification, urgent advice, or guided examination. This is preferable to beginning with high-bandwidth video and losing the case when the connection fails.

However, store-and-forward should be designed as a triage and access pathway, not a universal substitute for examination. In a skin-cancer cohort, 49 biopsy-proven cancers were identified: 61.2% received an accurate first teledermatology diagnosis, 14.3% received a different diagnosis but were correctly categorized as skin cancer, and 24.5% could not be assessed remotely but were triaged and escalated on clinical suspicion.[63] In another study, two malignant tumours were diagnosed as benign remotely; the authors emphasized the importance of dermoscopic images for skin-tumour evaluation.[31]

Diagnostic agreement also varies sharply by condition. In the rural India study, telemedicine diagnostic concordance was 95.2% for hypertension and 93.3% for diabetes, but 72.2% for orthopedics, 62.5% for dermatology, 33.3% for cardiology, and 30% for miscellaneous symptoms.[57] The authors attributed lower concordance in some cases to inability to perform physical examination, including auscultation and assessment for postural hypotension.[57] This means that platform triage should route stable, protocol-led follow-up—such as hypertension review, diabetes medication follow-up, results review, or refill clarification—differently from new chest symptoms, non-specific systemic complaints, suspected severe illness, or problems requiring palpation, auscultation, or a full visual examination.

Patient outcomes from diagnostic workflows are likewise condition-specific. Store-and-forward teledermatology has shown high concordance in selected visual cases: one study reported 91.05% agreement between store-and-forward and face-to-face diagnoses in 391 patients.[31] Yet a 44-study meta-analysis reported pooled teledermatology-to-face-to-face agreement of 68.9%, with substantial variation from 13.9% to 98.0%; agreement with histopathology was 55.7% across all biopsies.[65] The correct product conclusion is not that teledermatology is unreliable. It is that diagnostic performance depends on condition, reference standard, image quality, clinician expertise, and case selection.

Uganda’s COVID-19 call-centre model shows diagnostic workflow value at a system level. From 23 March 2020 through the end of June 2021, community health workers made 35,553 calls to obtain nurses’ or doctors’ help with triage, diagnostics, referrals, and treatment questions. Among surveyed community health workers, 77.3% said the service improved work quality; among users, 93.2% felt they received the help needed to treat their patient.[9] These are provider-reported support outcomes, but they show that a low-bandwidth diagnostic workflow can extend clinical supervision and referral decision support even where a full remote examination is not possible.

In the Tanzanian childhood-illness trial, the electronic algorithm improved danger-sign checking over paper by 33 percentage points (an 80.5% relative increase) and expert-matching classifications by 14 percentage points (a 35.9% relative increase).[2]

Where teledermatology studies reported image-acquisition training, mean agreement with face-to-face clinicians was 13.8 percentage points higher than in studies without reported training—75.9% versus 62.1%, a 22.2% relative advantage.[65]

Only 24 of 982 images (2.44%) in the structured smartphone image set were out of focus or overexposed, showing the practical value of a multi-view capture protocol with reviewable redundancy.[31]

The measures in a remote diagnostic workflow are distinct. Technical image adequacy asks whether the submitted clinical information and pictures are sufficient for review; in pediatric teledermatology, poor information or insufficient picture quality triggered a live consultation in 10% of cases. Diagnostic agreement compares a remote diagnosis with an in-person or expert reference; in one store-and-forward dermatology study, agreement with face-to-face diagnoses was 91.05% in 391 patients. Treatment and referral decisions are separate outputs: in a skin-cancer cohort, 24.5% of biopsy-proven cancers could not be assessed remotely and were triaged and escalated on clinical suspicion. Patient health outcomes require a further measure, such as query resolution, hospital use, clinical control, or recovery.[60][31][63][58]

The Tanzanian childhood-illness trial illustrates why these measures must not be combined. Danger-sign checking measured completeness of assessment, rising from 41% with paper to 74% with the electronic tool; main-symptom checking rose from 75% to 99%. Expert-matching disease classifications measured agreement, at 53% with the electronic tool, 39% with paper, and 34% in the control arm.

An image-capture form should request the required views, focus, scale, and contextual history before submission, then let the reviewer return a targeted request for missing information. The workflow should confirm that treatment, referral, or follow-up actually occurred after the remote decision.

A store-and-forward diagnostic submission can identify the image or test’s capture time, body site or test type, clinical question, and capture quality alongside the patient identity and history.[118]

A 2025 prospective randomized cohort study at the American University of Beirut Medical Center compared store-and-forward teledermatology image sets with in-clinic assessments in 360 adults. Diagnosis match was 79% for unassisted patient-taken photographs, 84% for patient photographs taken after brief training, and 87% for resident-taken photographs. Treatment match was 38%, 44%, and 45%, respectively. Diagnostic match and treatment match are separate outcomes.[67]

The brief patient-photo training covered lighting, focus, framing, and lesion visibility before patients recaptured images. The resident workflow used a well-lit room, fixed 1× zoom, an approximate 30 cm distance, a camera held perpendicular to the skin, and a focus check before capture. It required two views: a distant image showing anatomical location and a close-up showing lesion details, with a ruler for scale when applicable. Residents immediately reviewed images and retook blurry photographs; images were deidentified and securely stored for store-and-forward review, with identifying information stored separately.[67]

This image-capture pattern increased diagnostic match from 79% to 84% after brief patient training and to 87% with resident capture. Teledermatologists requested additional patient history in 64% of unassisted-image assessments, 59% of assisted-image assessments, and 47% of resident-image assessments. The reviewer form recorded diagnosis and proposed treatment, time to diagnosis, whether history was needed, diagnostic confidence on a 1–10 scale, and recommended follow-up interval in days.[67]

Condition-specific results in the 2025 study were 95%, 96%, and 98% diagnostic match for acne and rosacea across unassisted, assisted, and resident image groups; 74%, 81%, and 83% for papulosquamous dermatoses; and 76%, 90%, and 86% for malignant neoplasms. Assisted patient photographs had diagnostic and treatment match rates comparable to resident photographs in the study’s regression analysis.[67]

The image workflow provides a concrete cognitive-load design: replace a free-form instruction to take a good photograph with one short capture sequence—lighting, fixed distance and zoom, perpendicular orientation, contextual view, close-up, scale where relevant, immediate review, and retake when blurry. The paired distant and close views externalize the need to remember both anatomical context and lesion detail, while immediate review prevents a later clarification cycle after transmission.[67]

A store-and-forward teledermatology implementation reported 93% teledermatologist/in-clinic concordance for diagnosis and 95% for management in 99 completed in-clinic evaluations. In its first 340 chart reviews, referring-provider and teledermatologist diagnosis was fully concordant in 36%, partially concordant in 24%, discordant in 23%, and not assessable in 17%; management was fully concordant in 15%, partially concordant in 19%, discordant in 21%, and not assessable in 44%.[68]

In that implementation, a subsequent in-person appointment after teledermatology referral had a median wait of 36 days and an 11.8% no-show rate, compared with 64 days and 18.6% for the institution’s standard dermatology referral pathway. Skin-lesion referrals were directed to in-person follow-up in 71.9% of cases, compared with 19.2% of inflammatory or rash referrals. The article identifies appropriate patient selection, high-quality photographs, dermoscopy for pigmented lesions, and effective infrastructure as factors in teledermatology effectiveness.[68]

The article also states that image comprehensiveness and quality, diagnostic confidence, and quickly obtaining additional clinical information affect teledermatology accuracy; it identifies lower diagnostic and management accuracy for malignant pigmented lesions and possible underdiagnosis of clinically significant lesions not recognized by the referring provider. Of 897 completed subsequent clinic visits, 237 patients (26.4%) underwent biopsy; the article reports comparison figures of 10.9% for patients referred directly from primary care in one passage and 11.8% for traditional referrals in another.[68]

Rwanda’s 2024 Integrated Malaria Control Guidelines provide a clinical-decision-support content model: parasitological laboratory testing is required to confirm suspected malaria before treatment, and compulsory laboratory testing applies to all malaria cases, including severe cases and all age groups. The guidelines cover uncomplicated and severe malaria, all malaria types, and public and private health professionals at every care level. They recommend ACTs for uncomplicated malaria in the first trimester of pregnancy, gradual phase-out of oral and injectable quinine in favour of artemisinin derivatives, artesunate-pyronaridine for uncomplicated malaria, and artemeter injections for severe malaria.[69]

The Rwanda guidelines report that community-level malaria diagnosis and treatment increased from 13% in 2015–2016 to 59% in 2022–2023. Over the stated period, severe malaria cases fell from 10,748 in FY2017/2018 to 1,316 in 2022/2023, and malaria case fatality fell from 17 to 6.4 deaths per 100,000 malaria cases between FY2015/2016 and FY2022/2023.[69]

In the rural India randomized crossover study, mode of teleconsultation—synchronous versus asynchronous—had no significant association with diagnostic concordance (P=.32) or treatment concordance (P=.29), while the digital assistant increased adherence to evidence-based care protocols. Diagnostic concordance was 95% (20/21; Cohen kappa=0.93) for hypertension and 93% (14/15; Cohen kappa=0.89) for diabetes, versus 33% (1/3) for cardiology and 30% (3/10) for nonspecific symptoms.[70]

For a rural East African primary-care workflow, these results support structured, protocol-led remote review for measured chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, and explicit escalation for cardiology and nonspecific symptoms. The rural India model connected frontline nurses and community health workers in primary-care clinics with remote doctors at tertiary facilities; it is a provider-to-provider pattern relevant to rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania.[70]

### Synthesis —

The combined evidence supports a layered model for low-connectivity primary care. Asynchronous communication makes case submission, follow-up, specialist support, and referral coordination possible without preserving a live connection. Offline-first architecture keeps the clinical task and local record available during an outage. Diagnostic workflow design determines whether the queued case is clinically usable: structured history, red-flag prompts, adequate image capture, visible medication and referral status, and escalation are the safety controls.[53][30][57][60]

The evidence does not support treating remote care as equivalent to in-person care for every condition. It supports remote-first handling for selected follow-up, protocol-led, visually assessable, and specialist-support cases; it supports live or in-person escalation when the problem depends on physical examination, medication identification, high-quality diagnostic imagery, or urgent assessment.[54][57][18][63]

1. Build consultations as locally saved case packets that synchronize in the background when connectivity returns.
2. Use structured asynchronous intake before any live call, with mandatory red flags, minimum clinical data, and an adequacy check for images.
3. Show explicit states for queued, sent, received, reviewed, prescribed, referred, and followed up—especially for medication and pharmacy handoffs.
4. Offer SMS or voice fallback for notification and follow-up, but do not use SMS alone for complex diagnosis or medication identification.
5. Make escalation to phone, facility, pharmacy, or referral hospital a normal workflow branch rather than a consultation failure.

## Consultation completion and diagnostic accuracy

Consultation completion and diagnostic accuracy are different outcomes. Completion asks whether a scheduled clinical encounter reaches its operational endpoint; diagnostic accuracy asks whether the remote assessment matches an appropriate clinical or pathological reference. A service can complete many consultations while still needing escalation for examination-dependent conditions.

### What specific interaction patterns have achieved consultation completion rates above

The Babyl Rwanda interrupted-time-series analysis reports 3,676,385 completed consultations out of 3,899,788 recorded consultations from 2019–2023: a 94.3% completion rate, with 5.7% recorded as no-shows. Completion exceeded 94% across the reported categories.[3] The reported operational pattern is an integrated digital service rather than a discrete interface interaction: insurance, laboratory, pharmacy and e-prescription integrations were associated with 12–15% increases in completion for each additional integration.[3]

Babyl also reports condition-specific completion of 94.3% for respiratory consultations and 87% for malaria consultations requiring laboratory confirmation. Both exceed the requested 85% threshold.[3] These figures indicate that a workflow can remain operational when malaria care requires a laboratory step, but they do not identify whether messaging, appointment reminders, triage, e-prescribing, laboratory handoff, or another interaction produced the result.[3]

Telephone and video visits were each positively associated with visit completion in a rural West Virginia primary-care study. Its outcome, however, was completion of check-in procedures rather than confirmation that the clinical consultation, diagnosis, and plan were completed.[71] This supports retaining telephone as a fallback channel under failed video connectivity, but it does not supply an above-85% completed-consultation rate.

**The documented above-85% consultation-completion results are Babyl Rwanda’s 94.3% overall rate, 94.3% respiratory-consultation rate, and 87% malaria-with-laboratory-confirmation rate; the service-level integration pattern is associated with, rather than isolated as the cause of, those results.**[3]

A separate report characterizes Babyl Rwanda’s interaction pattern as USSD access on basic feature phones; mobile and in-person agents who structure patient information and triage; mostly store-and-forward consultations and follow-ups; offline-aware form logic; and highly structured, stepwise protocols. The report links this combined pattern to consultation completion above 85% for prioritized workflows under 2G/3G and gives a range of 87–94.3% for respiratory and malaria workflows.[41]

In an African movement-disorders programme, local physicians in Tanzania, Nigeria, and South Africa submitted 43 asynchronous consultation requests by October 2017; all 43 were completed as scheduled by six specialists. Physicians submitted standardised neurological-examination videos and referral-template case reports to a project manager, who entered them into a secure electronic medical record and assigned a specialist.[114]

In the AAD asynchronous primary–specialty consultation workflow, chart review was the requested consultation mode in 87% of cases and telephone in 13%. Requested response windows were one hour for 4% of consultations, one business day for 33%, and three business days for 63%; scheduler processing averaged 3 minutes 3 seconds, specialists spent 11 minutes per consultation, and specialty turnaround averaged 6 hours 19 minutes.[72] Specialists answered within the requested timeframe in 98% of cases, used the documentation tool correctly in 98%, and routed consultations correctly in 95%; with a project manager correcting remaining process errors, the reported consultation-completion rate was 99.9%.[72]

In a Queensland asynchronous GP-to-eConsultant pilot, the GP sent a request-for-advice template containing a clinical summary and specific questions; fields were automatically populated from the GP clinical-information system. The eConsultant could answer, request more information, or request a face-to-face outpatient appointment, after which the GP arranged patient follow-up to discuss the advice.[73] The pilot generated eConsultant access for 30 patients, and all episodes were completed with a patient follow-up visit.[73]

A Kenyan community-based hybrid maternal, newborn, and child-health model reported completed referrals for 92% (3,630/3,967) of intervention-site referrals and 97% (1,873/1,938) of control-site referrals during July 2023–September 2024.[74]

In The Addis Clinic’s Kenya programme, 99% of 726 baseline specialist-referral cases were automatically flagged “answered” during February 2019–January 2020. “Answered” meant a response from one or more specialists, not confirmation that the patient received or acted on the recommendation.[119]

In Kenya’s Addis Clinic provider-to-provider programme, 99% of 726 submitted cases were automatically flagged “answered” after at least one specialist response during February 2019–January 2020. During February 2021–January 2022, 75% of 3,548 cases were flagged answered; the median specialist-response time rose from 13.1 to 18.9 hours across those respective periods.[119]

Completion measures need to distinguish registration, request submission, clinician contact, signed consultation note, diagnosis, prescription dispensing, and closed-loop follow-up. A completion rate also needs a defined population, period, denominator, and treatment of unreachable patients, duplicate attempts, and transfers to in-person care.

Two asynchronous clinician-to-clinician workflows also exceeded 85% on their own process-completion definitions. Geisinger’s Ask-a-Doc, launched after its pilot on January 1, 2014, enabled primary-care providers to contact specialists in 14 medical and five surgical specialties; chart review was requested in 87% of consultations, and its monitored workflow reported 98% on-time specialist responses, 98% correct use of the documentation tool, 95% correct routing, and 99.9% consultation completion after a project manager corrected remaining process errors.[72] In a Queensland eConsultant pilot commencing in February 2018 and extending to four rural or remote practices in November 2019, a GP sent an automatically populated clinical summary and specific question through secure messaging; the eConsultant could answer, request further information, or request face-to-face outpatient referral; and the GP arranged patient follow-up. All 30 episodes were completed with a patient follow-up visit, yielding 30/30, or 100%, episode completion.[73]

In the Asynchronous Consultation for Movement Disorders programme, six movement-disorder specialists completed all 43 scheduled requests submitted by local physicians in Tanzania, Nigeria, and South Africa by October 2017: 43 of 43 submitted requests, or 100%. The denominator excludes patients eligible for referral who were not submitted and unsuccessful attempts to upload media.[114]

For the movement-disorders referrals, local physicians sent a referral template and standardized neurological-examination video through a secure app to a project manager, who uploaded them to a secure electronic medical record and assigned a specialist. The specialist returned a diagnostic evaluation and treatment recommendations in an electronic consultant report.[114]

In the ACMD asynchronous movement-disorder referral workflow, all 43 of 43 submitted cases reached a completed scheduled referral; the programme report did not compare specialist diagnoses with in-person movement-disorder assessments.[114][56]

A small African movement-disorders specialist programme reported 100% completion of scheduled consultations, while a Kenyan provider-to-provider programme reported that 99% of cases were answered under baseline workload.[3][114][119]

### What evidence supports diagnostic accuracy comparable to in-person visits?

For general rural primary care, the closest comparative evidence is Verma et al. (2023, JMIR Formative Research, 'Diagnostic Concordance of Telemedicine as Compared With Face-to-Face Care in Primary Health Care Clinics in Rural India: Randomized Crossover Trial')’s 2023 randomized crossover trial in 10 rural Gujarat clinics.[75] In 104 patients receiving both health-worker-assisted telemedicine and in-person doctor consultations, telemedicine had 74% diagnostic concordance (77/104) and 79.8% treatment-plan concordance (83/104) against the in-person doctor’s decisions.[75][56] The study treated the in-person decision as its gold standard, and its authors describe the result as clinician concordance rather than independently verified diagnostic accuracy.[56]

A Rwanda standardized-patient evaluation sampled 81 facilities for initial consultations and analysed 2,677 complete visits: 1,218 through Babyl and 1,459 through conventional care. It assessed condition-specific correct case management against clinical guidance for malaria, upper respiratory infection (URI), and diarrhoea, rather than diagnostic agreement with an in-person examination.[112]

In that Rwanda standardized-patient evaluation, correct malaria case management did not differ between Babyl and conventional care; conventional care achieved 93%. For URI, correct management meant avoiding unnecessary medicines or tests: conventional care achieved 29%, and Babyl’s result was 28 percentage points higher.[112]

The same trial shows that performance varies sharply by presentation. Telemedicine diagnostic concordance was highest for hypertension, at 95% (20/21; Cohen κ=0.93), and diabetes, at 93% (14/15; Cohen κ=0.89), and lowest for cardiology, at 33% (1/3), and nonspecific symptoms, at 30% (3/10).[57] Missing, incorrect, or incomplete information provided by the community health officer contributed to at least 12 of the 27 discordant cases (44%).[57] Thus, structured remote assessment is best suited to protocol-led chronic-condition follow-up and is not a substitute for physical examination in cases requiring auscultation, postural measurements, or broad examination.

The Mashavu asynchronous telemedicine system in Kenya produced 78.4% agreement between the same nurse’s remote and in-person medical feedback across 102 cases. Agreement on recommended patient action—such as referral or no further care—was 89.2% across 91 cases.[1] For Mashavu, matching feedback meant the same category—immediate professional care, follow-up professional care, or no additional visits—and matching diagnoses meant the same root cause or disease, with synonymous terms accepted.[76] Patients were not explicitly instructed to present the same complaint in both encounters and saw the nurse face-to-face immediately after the store-and-forward encounter; the authors said that additional complaints discussed at the face-to-face visit could have lowered the reliability figures.[76] The stronger patient-action result means that store-and-forward primary care can support triage and referral decisions even where diagnosis-level agreement remains lower.

For tightly bounded, image-suitable diagnostic tasks, some workflows have been comparable with clinical examination. Jolliffe, Harris and Whittaker’s 2001 histology-benchmarked study found that stored video-image assessment of pigmented lesions was as accurate as conventional clinical examination; neither method classified a malignant tumour as benign.[54] In tele-ophthalmology for chronic visual loss, face-to-face assessment was the reference: diagnosis and grading agreement exceeded κ=0.8 for cataracts and age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and was κ=0.61–0.8 for glaucoma. AMD sensitivity and specificity exceeded 99%; cataract sensitivity was 87.8%, while glaucoma specificity was 76.5%.[77]

In a standardized-patient comparison of Babyl Rwanda with conventional care, correct malaria case management did not differ between services; conventional care achieved 93% correct case management. For upper respiratory infections, Babyl’s correct case management was 28 percentage points higher, largely because it ordered fewer unnecessary medicines and laboratory tests.[112]

The broader evidence is condition-dependent. A real-time tele-ophthalmology review judged six of 12 studies comparable with face-to-face consultation and one superior.[66] Conversely, a six-study teledermatology meta-analysis found in-person primary diagnoses significantly more concordant than remote diagnoses, with odds ratio 0.55 (95% CI 0.42–0.72; I²=58%).[78] A 44-study teledermatology review similarly found mean remote-to-face-to-face agreement of 68.9% and mean κ=0.67.[65]

**Comparable remote diagnostic performance is strongest for narrowly defined, image-supported tasks and protocol-led chronic care; undifferentiated primary-care complaints still require explicit in-person escalation routes.**[54][57]

Saade et al.’s 2025 prospective study at the American University of Beirut Medical Center enrolled 360 adults, used one randomized image set per patient for store-and-forward teledermatology assessment, and compared teledermatologists’ diagnoses and treatment decisions with in-clinic assessments as the reference standard.[67] Diagnostic matches were 79% for unassisted patient-taken photographs, 84% for assisted patient-taken photographs, and 87% for resident-taken photographs.[67] Compared with resident-taken photographs, assisted patient-taken photographs had no statistically significant difference in diagnostic match (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.62–1.11; P=.20) or treatment match (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.76–1.25; P=.85).[67]

Diagnostic-comparison reports need to state whether inadequate images, indeterminate cases, and safety-net referrals were excluded, because excluding them raises apparent diagnostic agreement above real-world pathway performance. Abdominal, neurologic, acute-syndrome, medication-adverse-effect, and respiratory presentations without reliable measurements require in-person assessment or additional instruments.

### Do the same interaction patterns achieve both consultation completion rates

Babyl Rwanda supplies the completion benchmark but not a paired in-person diagnostic comparison. Its 94.3% respiratory-consultation completion and 87% malaria-with-laboratory-confirmation completion show high operational continuity, while the ecological analysis lacks morbidity and mortality data and does not make individual-level clinical-outcome inference.[3]

In standardized-patient malaria visits in Rwanda, Babyl and conventional care did not differ in guideline-defined correct case management; management of upper respiratory infection was more often guideline-consistent in Babyl. Correct case management assesses the history, tests, medicines, referral, and advice against condition-specific guidance, rather than measuring diagnostic agreement.[112]

A standardized-patient assessment of Babyl in Rwanda found malaria case management comparable to conventional care and better correct case management of upper respiratory infections than conventional care.[3][112]

A Babyl standardised-patient study compared condition-specific correct case management with clinical guidelines, not remote diagnoses with an in-person reference diagnosis. It found no difference in malaria management and superior management of upper respiratory infections under its defined measure.[112]

Store-and-forward dermatology supplies high diagnostic agreement in a different workflow. In a prospective study of 391 patients with skin disorders, store-and-forward diagnosis agreed with face-to-face diagnosis in 91.05% of cases, with Cohen κ=0.906.[31] The same study classified two malignant skin tumours as benign remotely and excluded pigmented skin lesions because dermoscopic images were considered essential for their assessment.[31] It reports no consultation-completion rate.

In rural Gujarat, a guided digital assistant collected sufficient information for the doctor to diagnose without speaking to the patient in 84% of cases, enabling most consultations to proceed asynchronously.[56] Among consultations with recorded modality, asynchronous care had 71.4% diagnostic concordance and 77.3% treatment concordance, compared with 81.2% and 87.5% for synchronous care; those differences were not statistically significant.[57] The 84% measure is information sufficiency, not consultation completion.

**Babyl Rwanda has a service-level consultation-completion result of 3,676,385 completed consultations out of 3,899,788 recorded consultations (94.3%) in 2019–2023. A separate 391-patient store-and-forward dermatology study reported 91.05% agreement with face-to-face diagnoses, but two malignant tumours were labelled benign remotely. These are different workflows and cannot be combined into one completion-and-diagnostic-accuracy benchmark.**[3][31]

### Synthesis —

For rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania on intermittent 2G/3G networks, design for the two outcomes separately. First, use asynchronous structured history, laboratory handoff, e-prescribing, and pharmacy or insurance integration to protect consultation continuity; Babyl’s completion figures indicate the operational value of a connected service pathway.[3] Second, reserve store-and-forward diagnosis for image-suitable or protocol-led conditions, and route examination-dependent, nonspecific, severe, or uncertain cases to in-person care.[57][77]

1. Build a completed-consultation measure that requires documented assessment, plan, prescription or referral—not merely check-in.
2. Measure diagnostic agreement separately by condition, modality, image quality, and escalation outcome.
3. Treat asynchronous forms as a triage and information-collection layer, with live telephone fallback and in-person escalation rather than as a universal diagnostic substitute.

## Cognitive load and clinical workflows

Judgment — Cognitive load is the working-memory effort needed to interpret a prompt, retrieve clinical information, enter it correctly, and recover after interruption. In rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, a form or camera task competes with patient interaction, clinical judgment, small screens, limited digital familiarity, and intermittent connectivity. The design goal is therefore not merely fewer taps: it is to make the clinical protocol, current task state, and recovery path visible so providers do not have to hold them in memory.

### Which cognitive load principles inform the design of clinical forms

Cognitive-load theory distinguishes **intrinsic load**—the necessary complexity of a task, which increases when the user has less prior knowledge—from **extraneous load**—avoidable effort imposed by poor presentation, fragmented information, or irrelevant activity. Intrinsic and extraneous load draw on the same limited working memory; reducing extraneous load leaves attention for clinical assessment and decision-making. Expertise matters: guidance useful to novices can become redundant for experienced users.[79][80]

For clinical forms, four practical principles translate this theory into interaction design: **structure, transparency, clarity, and support**. Structure provides a logical path; transparency tells the user what is required and what will happen; clarity makes labels and actions unambiguous; support provides timely help. Each field otherwise requires the user to interpret a question, find information, and enter it in an accepted format, increasing the risk of error, overwhelm, and abandonment.[81]

A low-load form should use a straightforward, stepped, single-column route with small, logically grouped sections, descriptive headings, and only fields relevant to earlier answers. Grouping helps users focus on one clinical category at a time; conditional routing avoids scanning irrelevant fields; a single column gives a stable vertical path on a phone. Questions should progress from familiar, simple information to priority and more complex clinical questions, while dependency rules reveal follow-up questions only when warranted.[82][81][83]

Clinical structure must follow clinical safety, not generic form convenience. The IMCI assessment sequence first asks about the presenting problem and checks general danger signs, then assesses cough or difficult breathing, diarrhoea, fever, and ear problems; it subsequently checks malnutrition, anaemia, immunization status, and other reported problems. It also routes children by age to the appropriate chart and separates assessment, classification, treatment, referral, counselling, and follow-up.[84][85]

**A safe clinical form is external cognitive support, not a blank digital record.** It should make danger signs, clinical branches, and next actions visible while preserving the distinction between observed data, the clinician’s classification, and treatment decisions. Rwanda’s d-IDS similarly places danger-sign screening before integrated symptom screening, uses step-by-step decision trees and referral warning flags, and prevents symptom bypass; 93.8% of 600 assessed CHWs agreed it saved time, 89.0% said it improved knowledge and decision-making guidance, and 81.2% said it improved data accuracy and follow-up.[86]

This makes progressive, protocol-led forms preferable to long questionnaires or conversational chatbot forms for this use case. A cited clinician-facing cardiology-referral comparison found the chatbot form least preferred and below average on SUS among participating nurses, who described it as difficult to understand and use.[87]

Medication reconciliation is a high-load clinical-form task because it requires gathering and verifying current medicines, prescribing, resolving discrepancies, making clinical decisions, and communicating the finalised list to the patient or caregiver. A medication-reconciliation form should therefore externalise comparison rather than present a single undifferentiated list: identify whether information came from the patient, health record, or pharmacy; retain the age of the patient report; and make additions, discontinuations, dose discrepancies, dose changes, and new medicines visible.[88]

A staged reconciliation route should remind patients to bring medicines or a list; screen for risk; obtain and review the current list; compare it with the medical record; reconcile discrepancies; communicate changes and check understanding; document changes; and follow up outstanding issues. For each medicine, collect name, reason for use, route, dosage, and frequency.

A remote medication-reconciliation record can additionally capture medicine strength, intended duration, prescriber, whether treatment has started or stopped, adherence or access problems, allergies or adverse reactions, and whether the medicine was dispensed.[3][5][120]

Medication review should prioritise unresolved discrepancies. The medication-reconciliation application design grouped information by patient-derived, health-record-derived, and pharmacy-derived sources; offered alphabetical and adherence-group sorting; and used type-specific flags so clinicians could scan for changes. It placed detailed information on demand, while making the source of each item visible for comparison.[88]

Ugandan policy and review evidence describe low computer literacy and ICT skills among health workers, particularly in rural areas; Tanzania’s 2019–2024 National Digital Health Strategy likewise identified low digital literacy and inadequate skills using digital solutions among health workers and managers. Ugandan health workers also described ICT as an extra responsibility that could pull them from core duties.[8][7][89]

The interface response is familiar, stable interaction. Use recognisable controls, consistent button placement and terminology, persistent labels outside fields, short plain-language instructions, and clear visual grouping. Do not make the provider remember a label that disappears while typing, remember prior answers from another screen, or discover an error by searching through the whole form.[81][83]

Providers should see only the required path, with optional content explicitly marked and kept out of the critical workflow. Use structured choices, checkboxes, and drop-downs for standard clinical concepts; minimise free text and repetitive typing; allow predictive text or autofill where the local record can safely supply it. Respondents using an offline-first EHR reported that modular workflows and drop-down menus reduced writing, typing time, and encounter-flow burden, while both study sites reported fewer prescription-medication errors after replacing handwritten prescriptions with standardised drop-down categories.[81][83][90][32][91]

The form should preserve the provider’s clinical autonomy. Decision support should prompt and explain rather than silently determine a diagnosis; Tanzania’s ESIDA clinicians worried that rushed or incomplete answers could produce mismatched recommendations, delay urgent care, or weaken clinical judgment. ESIDA’s proposed agree, disagree, or override control is therefore a sound interaction model, provided it shows the basis for the recommendation and retains the clinician’s final decision.[90]

Interruptions create an additional form of extraneous load: a provider must remember where they were, what has saved, and whether to repeat an action. State saving behaviour before a multi-step task; auto-save locally at meaningful boundaries; show the current step, completed steps, pending-sync items, and a clear success, failure, or retry state. W3C guidance recommends avoiding timeouts and data loss, allowing users to return without losing work, providing per-action feedback, and making process status visible.[81][83]

This is more than reassurance. OpenMRS O3’s offline mode permits continued work during a temporary outage only when the relevant patient record and forms were made available offline beforehand; its offline home screen exposes forms, offline patients, and actions pending synchronisation. The Community Health Toolkit similarly allows background update checks to fail or time out without interrupting a workflow and lets a user defer reload while completing a form.[92][30]

**Interruption resilience requires both a visible interaction promise and prior offline preparation.** A provider needs to know that a form is saved; the application must also have cached the patient record, relevant form, terminology, and decision rules locally before service fails. This rules out designs that depend on a live connection for each field validation, branch, or clinical reference.[81][92]

Structured prompting can improve completeness but cannot replace examination or training. In rural India, the Ayu digital assistant used adaptive questionnaires tied to the presenting complaint, prompted clinically relevant history and examination data, and increased adherence to evidence-based care protocols. Yet missing, incorrect, or incomplete CHO information contributed to at least 12 of 27 discordant diagnoses, while remote clinicians missed incidental findings discovered in face-to-face examinations.[57]

The immediate design consequence is to apply local, post-field validation for ordinary entries, real-time validation only for safety-critical values, and field-adjacent error messages that say what needs correction. Urgent danger signs should trigger an explicit escalation state rather than a blocking sequence of administrative questions.[81][85][84]

For medication reconciliation on a phone, make one decision at a time visible: what information is available, where it came from, what changed since the last encounter, the estimated adherence, and what action is needed. Keep pharmacy dispensing history distinct from available refills, and make missing dispensing data explicit. Use structured dose, unit, route, frequency, source, and indication fields with an Other route for exceptions; prompt to save, discard, or cancel when a user leaves an edited medication entry.[88][93]

A remote-consulting workflow also needs practical preparation and training rather than interface simplification alone. REaCH’s Tanzania pilot, digitised, delivered, and evaluated from April to June 2020 with 14 medical officers from five primary-care facilities, adapted the Moodle app for stronger and weaker network capacities. REaCH training covered planning remote consultations, communication when remote from the patient, facility workflow, and remote-care triage; its delivery combined smartphone learning, facilitator support, local-language cascaded training, and airtime/data allowances.[94][95][96]

REaCH trainees used WhatsApp text and voice notes as a portable record for later reference and a forum to seek clarification and peer support. This makes a persistent, reviewable record of instructions and an accessible help route appropriate components of a low-proficiency workflow.[95][97]

For safety-critical mandatory fields, explain why the information is needed and offer an escalation or exception route rather than trapping the provider in the form. Confirm potentially dangerous actions such as closing an unsent urgent referral, but do not add confirmation dialogs to routine taps.

### How do cognitive load principles inform image capture workflows when

Image capture should be a bounded, standardised clinical procedure rather than an open camera tool. Working memory is limited in capacity and duration, and attention is split when people use a mobile device in a noisy, unfamiliar, or stressful environment. Related instructions and actions should therefore appear together: tell the provider what view is needed beside the capture control, show one action at a time, and avoid moving controls or unrelated alerts during capture.[83][80]

A clinically useful dermatology sequence is: first, a distribution or location view; second, a regional or medium-distance view showing configuration; third, a focused close-up showing surface detail. For generalised rash, resource-limited smartphone guidance specifies a vertical view for extent and distribution, a medium-distance view for arrangement and configuration, and a close-up of a representative lesion.[98][99]

The capture workflow should make observable quality criteria explicit. Prepare a private, adequately lit setting with reduced background distraction; clean the lens; centre and focus on the lesion; keep the camera generally perpendicular; include at least one image that identifies the anatomical site; and add a measurement reference where size matters. Take complementary views where the clinical question needs context, not simply more photographs.[99]

The provider should review each image immediately for focus, coverage, exposure, framing, lighting, distance, and clinical relevance before it enters the upload queue. Automated capture checks can flag blur, insufficient lighting, excessive distance, obscuration, or inappropriate camera angle, but their feedback should say what to correct rather than simply reject an image.[98][64][100]

**For images, immediate quality review and recoverable local storage matter more than a high-resolution camera button.** Poor photographs and incomplete clinical data are associated with lower diagnostic reliability, and paediatric teledermatology required live consultation because of inadequate information or image quality in 10% of cases.[60]

Training and embedded guidance improve the conditions for remote interpretation. A teledermatology meta-analysis found mean agreement with face-to-face clinicians of 75.9% where image-acquisition training was reported, versus 62.1% without training; reported mean kappa was 0.77 versus 0.60. The same analysis found 71.7% mean agreement with digital cameras versus 59.8% with smartphones or tablets, so a smartphone-only strategy should compensate with capture guidance, review, and training rather than assume equivalent results.[65]

The image-capture state must survive interruption: retain captured media locally, attach it to the correct patient and clinical question, show whether it is captured, reviewed, queued, uploading, synchronised, or failed, and provide a specific retry action. This is a cognitive-load-informed requirement derived from stable controls, clear status feedback, and the need to resume after distraction; d-IDS demonstrates the related pattern of offline entry with automatic synchronisation when connectivity returns.[83][86]

The 13.8-percentage-point training-associated advantage in teledermatology agreement (75.9% with reported image-acquisition training versus 62.1% without) makes embedded capture coaching and practice feedback a substantive workflow component, not optional help text.[65]

### Synthesis —

Judgment — The combined pattern is clear: build clinical forms and image capture as short, protocol-led, resumable workflows that externalise memory. In forms, this means danger signs first, age- and symptom-based routing, visible prior answers, structured selections, local correction, and distinct assessment, classification, and treatment stages. In image capture, it means a small required set of views, embedded quality instructions, immediate review, and a local queue that survives connectivity loss. Training, local-language support, familiar controls, and reachable human assistance strengthen these patterns for providers with limited smartphone proficiency.[84][81][83][65][86]

1. **Make every consultation workflow locally resumable:** save draft data and media before network transmission, then show pending-sync and retry status.
2. **Use clinical branching, not generic surveys:** screen danger signs first and reveal only clinically dependent fields.
3. **Turn image capture into a guided checklist:** location, regional context, close-up detail, review, then upload.
4. **Test the clinical form against a simple baseline:** compare the protocol-led, locally resumable form with the current paper form or ordinary online entry. Measure completion time, omissions of required danger-sign and symptom checks, correction effort, and successful resumption after a simulated network drop. Tanzania’s electronic algorithm was compared with paper and control workflows on danger-sign checks, symptom checks, and classifications matching experts; the offline-first EHR study identifies writing and typing burden as encounter-flow measures; visible per-step status provides the success and failure feedback needed to assess recovery. [2][32][83]
5. **Test guided capture against an ordinary camera-and-upload workflow:** compare the proportion of clinically usable image sets received by the reviewer, image retakes, provider effort, and recovery after a simulated interruption. Count upload success separately from reviewer receipt and image adequacy. In paediatric teledermatology, poor clinical information or insufficient picture quality required live consultation in 10% of cases; image-acquisition training was associated with 75.9% mean agreement with face-to-face physicians versus 62.1% without reported training; automated checks can identify inadequate lighting, blur or focus, distance, obscuration, and camera angle; and each capture or upload action needs explicit success or failure status. [60][65][64][83]

## Applicability to rural primary care

Rural primary care in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania needs telehealth that works when the network is weak, disappears, or is too costly to use continuously. The applicable lesson is not to treat mobile coverage as continuous broadband. It is to preserve clinical work, patient identity, records, referrals, and diagnostic evidence across interrupted sessions, while retaining voice/SMS and in-person escalation.

### How applicable are the findings to rural primary care providers

Uganda is a mobile-dependent setting. The Uganda Communications Commission reported fixed-line density of about 0.6 subscriptions per 100 inhabitants in 2025 and described mobile networks as the dominant access modality. Its 2025 report also recorded 18.5 million mobile-data subscriptions, while noting that mobile-data subscriptions were only about 39% of active subscriptions and that many users remained on voice-only or basic-data services.[6]

Network expansion does not remove the need for interruption-safe design. Uganda’s fibre network reached 62,941 km in 2025, but 63% of deployed fibre was in the Central Region; fixed subscriptions also grew to 228,718, with deployments concentrated in the Central Region and major urban centres. By December 2025, 53 of 64 subsidized tower sites had been commissioned in previously unserved sub-counties.[6] At the same time, UCC monitoring across eight border towns found that no operator consistently complied across all monitored areas: data speeds could be acceptable, but session success was low, latency high, and packet loss present.[6]

**For rural Ugandan consultations, design for failed sessions rather than nominal coverage.** UCC’s conclusion is operationally important: a fast connection is not necessarily a reliable one, and quality must be measured in the environment where people use the service.[6]

Uganda’s connectivity descriptions differ because they describe different periods and measures. The 2013 National eHealth Strategic Plan stated that data connectivity and networking covered almost 100% of the country through fibre in major towns and mobile wireless elsewhere.[8] A 2017 review reported that 3G and 4G services were unavailable in most rural areas because provision was not economically viable.[7] These claims should not be combined into an assumption that an HCII, HCIII, HCIV, or community provider has stable broadband at the moment of consultation.

Asynchronous communication is particularly applicable. The Uganda eHealth plan identified mobile-phone support for specialist advice to remote professionals and Village Health Teams, electronic sharing of laboratory results to support diagnosis for HCII–HCIV and isolated practitioners, and SMS for referrals, outbreaks, long-term conditions, mental health, and patient reminders.[8] A review of remote sub-Saharan mobile-health deployments found that adding SMS to standard GPRS connectivity significantly reduced connection failures.[7]

This makes SMS a credible resilience layer for Uganda: use it for notification, callback requests, referral receipt, queue status, appointment reminders, and confirmation that a record or image bundle is awaiting upload. It should not be asked to carry detailed clinical history, high-resolution images, or medication lists without a structured fallback workflow.

Offline-first architecture is also directly aligned with Uganda’s operating conditions. The 2017 Uganda review identified power blackouts, lost internet connectivity, and an unskilled health workforce as barriers to eHealth uptake. It also reported low computer literacy and ICT skills among rural health workers, and that ICT could be viewed as extra work that distracts nurses and doctors from core duties.[7] Uganda’s earlier eHealth plan similarly described few and poorly maintained computers in rural facilities, lack of IT staff at lower-level facilities, and limited computer literacy among rural health workers and consumers.[8]

An offline-first pattern means that the provider can first register the patient, capture a consultation history, complete a triage form, select a provisional plan, record medications, and prepare referral material locally on the device. The application then queues encrypted changes and synchronizes when a connection returns. The Community Health Toolkit describes this model: local data storage permits workflows without server synchronization, and data syncs automatically when a connection becomes available.[101] OpenMRS Android likewise documents use in limited-connectivity settings, although offline mode requires a prior sign-in.[102]

Medication reconciliation is especially relevant in Uganda because continuity is not merely access to a facility. A rural Ugandan primary-care study defines continuity as both consistent availability of the medical record and the quality of the relationship with a particular provider.[10] Uganda’s digital-health context includes fragmented and non-interoperable systems, duplicate information, and delayed decision-making when multiple systems must be consulted.[8][11] An offline medication list should therefore show the last synchronized list, the source and date of each item, what the patient reports taking now, changes made during the consultation, and an explicit unresolved-conflict state until synchronization or pharmacist/clinician review.

Uganda has a concrete rural provider-support precedent. During COVID-19, a nurse- and doctor-staffed call centre supported about 3,500 community health workers in 23 rural districts, serving communities at least 7 km from a health facility and an estimated 2.45 million people. The service supported diagnosing, referral, treatment, infection-control practice, and communicating product needs; CHWs reported being better informed and less isolated.[9] This is a useful provider-support model, although it is not a measured consultation-completion or diagnostic-accuracy result.

Remote diagnostic exchange fits Uganda’s stated needs. The national plan specifically proposed teleconsultation and image transfer, including teleradiology and telepathology, and digital sharing of laboratory results.[8] Smartphones can support high-resolution image transfer and store-and-forward telemedicine.[7] Store-and-forward is more compatible with interruption than live video because clinicians can prepare photos and history without internet, then upload later; live video requires both parties to have a simultaneous continuous connection.[52]

Judgment — For Uganda, the appropriate default is an assisted, asynchronous clinical handoff: a local nurse, clinical officer, or CHW collects structured history and measurements; the platform preserves the case locally; a remote clinician reviews when connectivity permits; the platform returns a clear plan, prescription, referral, or request for missing information. This follows the known network and workforce conditions, rather than assuming uninterrupted video is the baseline.

Kenya’s telemedicine landscape is mobile-first. A 2025 review describes moderate adoption through mHealth platforms, AI-enabled SMS interventions, and remote consultation services, with an orientation toward rural outreach.[16] Kenya also included remote consulting in its national self-care guideline for reproductive health, approved by the Ministry of Health in June 2023.[18]

Rural access remains uneven. The Communications Authority’s FY 2022–2023 assessment reported that Airtel and Telkom performed well in urbanized areas but had below-average coverage in rural and far-flung areas. Airtel and Telkom also failed critical unsuccessful-call and data-internet KPIs, measures tied to coverage and internet availability or accessibility.[13] County-level KPI compliance ranged from 93.94% in Nairobi City to 54.54% in Laikipia and 57.57% in Baringo.[13] The same regulatory report recorded industry consumer-experience values of 27.43% for coverage challenges and 14.97% for connectivity challenges.[13]

National growth does not replace rural design constraints. In the third quarter of FY 2025/2026, Kenya had 84.1 million active mobile subscriptions, 84.4% of connected users on mobile broadband, and smartphones representing 63.7% of connected mobile phones; subscriptions on 2G and 3G continued to decline as users moved to 4G and 5G. Yet the rural-access problem remains material: Kenya’s telemedicine review identifies connectivity, affordability, and digital-literacy barriers in rural communities, while the Kenyan literature it reviewed focused predominantly on Nairobi, Kisumu, and Mombasa rather than Northern Kenya and remote coastal regions.[12][16]

Kenya supports mobile-first telehealth, but not a design assumption that rural users can maintain a data-heavy synchronous consultation. The system should treat voice, SMS, and asynchronous data exchange as first-class modes, particularly for remote facilities and households with shared devices, prepaid data, or intermittent coverage.[12][17][16]

Kenyan evidence supports a health-worker-assisted model. A community-linked maternal and newborn care pilot combined virtual doctor consultations, CHW support, and SMS engagement; the article reports enhanced access, improved referral completion, and positive health-seeking behaviour.[17] Its participants also reported low literacy, difficulty using the platform independently, shared or limited phones, inactive SIM cards, missed callbacks, and long waits.[17] The article proposes offline functionality and shared-device protocols as future responses to these access barriers.[17]

Kenya has an example of a facility-assisted diagnostic workflow. The Kiriani dispensary pilot in Mathioya Sub-County uses a spirometer, ECG monitor, thermometer, blood-pressure machine, and glucometer to collect patient information and test readings. After testing, the system uploads readings to a mobile application and central servers for clinician review and real-time alerts.[103] This supports a pattern in which local staff capture standardized measurements and a remote clinician interprets them.

It also shows why local persistence is necessary: the reported workflow depends on automatic upload and immediate review, rather than describing queued synchronization after an outage.[103]

A rural Kenya asynchronous precedent is Mashavu: Networked Health Solutions, field-tested to increase access to preprimary healthcare.[1] A later account of that Kenyan programme reports 78.4% diagnostic concordance and 89.2% treatment concordance between in-person and virtual nurse visits for 102 cases.[56] The result supports asynchronous clinical exchange as a potentially useful model in Kenya, but the diagnostic-concordance figure is below an 85% diagnostic threshold and is not a consultation-completion figure.

Babyl’s low-capability access route is a historical Rwanda precedent, not a currently deployable service: it operated from 2016 until it halted in September 2023 for system redesign.[23] Before the halt, Babyl used text-based USSD available on basic feature phones, text messages, and voice calls; it also developed national-ID verification processes so non-phone owners could register, consult, and receive prescriptions using shared digital or analogue devices.[4][104] That pattern fits Kenyan realities of shared phones and constrained smartphone access.

It should be paired with assisted registration and a clear local ownership model, because Rwanda participants reported registration complexity, limited digital literacy, poor rural network connectivity, agent absence, delays, and poor integration with facility workflows.[23] Babyl halted operations in September 2023 for system redesign after reaching 450 of 510 primary health facilities and enrolling 2 million patients.[23]

mPharma’s Mutti Doctor was announced as available through Mutti pharmacies in Kenya, among other African countries.[42] This makes it relevant as a pharmacy-linked access model, but its interface behaviour under interruptions, store-and-forward diagnostic workflow, and medication-reconciliation flow are not described by the cited service material.

Zipline’s relevant connectivity lesson is operational rather than consultation-interface specific. A Ghana study reports that Zipline’s drone operations depended on electricity and mobile networks that were frequently disrupted in low-resource settings.[47] For Kenyan primary care, the implication is to separate clinical decision-making from real-time logistics confirmation: a prescription, referral, or stock request should remain locally recorded and visibly pending until the supply-side acknowledgement arrives.

Tanzania has direct evidence that rural remote consulting must accommodate unstable networks, device constraints, and power limitations. A Tanzania study on clinician perspectives reports that rural areas still rely on 2G and 3G, where slow speeds hinder data-heavy applications; it also identifies limited smartphone access, unstable networks and internet connections, and lack of electricity in rural areas.[90] Participants in a separate remote-consulting process evaluation reported network instability in remote rural villages and during heavy rain.[18]

National indicators support a mobile- and low-data-tolerant design. In June 2026, Tanzania reported 98.66% 2G geographical coverage and 93.95% 3G geographical coverage, while the corresponding population coverage was 79.86% and 76.41%.[20] The country reported 24,628,003 internet subscriptions using 2G technology, 37,575,797 mobile-broadband subscriptions, 44.74% smartphone penetration, and 83.38% feature-phone penetration.[20] In September 2024, the industry-average data tariff was TZS 9.35 per MB pay-as-you-go and TZS 2.17 per MB in bundles; 99.9% of users subscribed to bundles.[105]

Tanzania’s rural design baseline is not continuous smartphone broadband; it is mobile access that varies by device, data budget, weather, electricity, and locality. Voice and SMS must remain workable consultation channels, while smartphone workflows need graceful degradation to local storage, compressed attachments, and deferred upload.[90][18][20][105]

Tanzania’s regulator reports strong sampled voice performance, but the measurement scope matters. For April–June 2026, all operators met the call-drop target of less than 2% in all 22 measured service areas; network availability exceeded the 99% threshold in all measured areas.[20] These indicators make voice a viable fallback channel in sampled areas, but call success does not guarantee a stable data session for an image upload or a video examination.

Tanzania also has a directly relevant implementation example: REaCH training for remote consulting was delivered through Moodle and WhatsApp in rural Ulanga District in August–September 2020, using a train-the-trainer cascade. Sixty-one of 63 tier-2 trainees completed the training.[21] The programme was carried out in a district with one hospital, two health centres, and 23 dispensaries for a population of 265,203.[21] This supports remote capacity-building in rural Tanzanian primary care, although training completion is not patient-consultation completion.

The REaCH trials provide a more clinically relevant Tanzania finding. In Tanzania, a stepped-wedge intervention incorporating training, mobile-data allowance, and implementation support found no evidence of change in prescribing rates, investigation rates, face-to-face consultation rates, or remote-consultation rates. Prescribing had a rate ratio of 0.92 (95% CI 0.60–1.67; p=0.97), investigations 1.15 (95% CI 0.35–1.64; p=0.58), face-to-face consultations 0.94 (95% CI 0.61–1.67; p=0.99), and remote consultations 1.17 (95% CI 0.56–5.57; p=0.39).[106]

This result makes an important design point: workforce training and data support can enable implementation, but they do not automatically alter prescribing, investigations, or consultation volume. Clinical workflow design must also address suitability screening, case completeness, response responsibility, follow-up, and referral ownership.

Image-based diagnostic workflows need an explicit non-smartphone route. Tanzanian patients and health workers reported that basic push phones restricted remote services, especially sending photos through WhatsApp.[18] Feature-phone penetration exceeded smartphone penetration in June 2026.[20] A platform should therefore allow a provider at a facility to capture images on a shared clinical device, save them offline, associate them with the patient and encounter, and send them later; patients without capable devices should be offered voice/SMS triage or a facility-assisted appointment rather than being excluded.

The closest regional design precedent for low-connectivity clinical data continuity is offline capture followed by synchronization. In rural Ethiopia, a tablet-based HPV workflow with offline mode reported robust technical function, stability, data accuracy, ease of use, no observed data loss, and safe, error-free collection, upload, and synchronization.[107] This supports the architecture for Tanzanian clinical records and forms, although it was screening data collection rather than remote primary-care consultation.

Babyl’s Rwanda access pattern is a historical precedent, not a currently deployable Rwanda service: Babyl operated from 2016 until it halted in September 2023 for system redesign after reaching 450 of Rwanda’s 510 primary health facilities and enrolling 2 million patients. Uganda should adopt only the transferable access patterns—USSD, text, voice, shared-device use, and national-ID-based identity verification—not treat Babyl as an active service option.[23][4][104]

For Tanzania, Babyl is likewise a historical design precedent rather than an active Rwanda service to deploy or integrate: it operated from 2016 until it halted in September 2023 for system redesign, after reaching 450 of 510 primary health facilities and enrolling 2 million patients. Tanzania can reuse its documented low-capability patterns—USSD, text, voice, shared devices, and national-ID-based identity verification—only as design inputs.[23][4][104]

### Synthesis —

Across Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, rural primary care has a stronger basis for asynchronous, assisted, and interruption-resumable workflows than for video-first consultation. Uganda combines mobile dependence with documented weak session reliability in monitored border areas; Kenya combines mobile-first activity with rural coverage, affordability, and digital-literacy barriers; Tanzania combines high reported 2G/3G coverage with continued 2G use, feature-phone predominance, weather-related instability, and data-cost constraints.[6][16][90][18][20]

Judgment — Build one clinical workflow that can begin offline, continue by voice or SMS, and complete when data returns. Use live video only as an upgrade when a preflight network check and clinical need justify it; do not make it the dependency for registration, clinical documentation, medication reconciliation, referral, or diagnostic evidence capture.

### Open questions

The requested above-85% consultation-completion benchmark is not established here for a named interaction pattern, and the same pattern is not shown to meet both that threshold and in-person-comparable diagnostic accuracy. Case-specific product behaviour for mPharma’s telemedicine interface and Zipline’s clinical decision tools remains open for intermittent connectivity, store-and-forward diagnostics, and medication reconciliation. Product-level workflow documentation and outcome data that define completed consultations, the clinical reference standard, patient population, network condition, and medication-reconciliation steps would settle these points.
