# The Iliad and Epic Cycle: Neoanalysis and Oralism

## Executive summary

The Iliad demonstrably shares Trojan-War narrative matter with the tradition later represented by the Epic Cycle, but the surviving evidence does not establish that it directly alludes to or depends on a fixed text of the Cypria.

Neoanalysts use the Cypria dossier to reconstruct meaningful pre-Iliadic narrative patterns, while oralists explain the same correspondences as traditional referentiality within a multiform oral repertoire. The Zeus–Thetis material, Achilles’ prospective death, and proposed Cypria motifs in Iliad 1 and 24 are consequently strongest as signs that the poem activates wider tradition, and only selectively plausible as markers of particular antecedent poems.

P.Oxy. 3829 and post-2000 digital access to papyri have sharpened knowledge of variation in Cyclic transmission, but they have not supplied a securely identified Cypria text or decided the dependence question.

Recent synthetic work has narrowed the opposition by accepting both traditional multiformity and deliberate, patterned engagement with inherited narrative, while leaving direct textual priority and the force of individual parallels disputed.

## The question and surviving Epic Cycle evidence

The Iliad survives as a large, continuously transmitted poem; the Epic Cycle survives as an archive of summaries, quotations, scholia, and later reports. That asymmetry is the reason that the question of relationship remains open. A resemblance between Iliad and Cycle can indicate that the Iliad recalls a particular earlier song, but it can also indicate that both draw upon a shared Trojan-War tradition whose individual performances and texts have mostly disappeared.

The dispute between neoanalysis and oral-traditional scholarship therefore concerns not whether wider Trojan-War traditions existed. Both accept that they did. It concerns what kind of inference can be drawn when a surviving Iliadic scene corresponds to a much later and fragmentary Cyclic witness: deliberate allusion to a particular antecedent poetic realization, or activation of a traditional narrative and dictional repertoire.

### What Epic Cycle evidence survives

The term **Epic Cycle** is used in two related senses. In the broad ancient and modern sense, it can include Theban as well as Trojan narratives. In the narrower Trojan-War sense relevant here, it denotes the sequence conventionally called the *Cypria*, *Aithiopis*, *Little Iliad*, *Iliou Persis* or *Sack of Ilium*, *Nostoi* or *Returns*, and *Telegony*. These poems narrate material before, after, and outside the short span of the *Iliad*: the *Cypria* treats the war’s prehistory and early years; the *Aithiopis*, *Little Iliad*, and *Iliou Persis* cover the war’s final phase and Troy’s destruction; the *Nostoi* and *Telegony* narrate returns and post-war consequences. The conventional label Cyclic poems normally excludes the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, even though the chronological sequence of Trojan myth can place them among these narratives.[1][2][3]

No Trojan-Cycle poem survives as a complete continuous text. What survives is a mixed dossier: titles and ancient author-attributions; prose **epitomes**, meaning compressed plot summaries; direct quotations and putative fragments; testimonia, meaning later reports about what a poem said; scholia, meaning ancient or Byzantine marginal commentaries; and occasional artistic or papyrological comparanda. Aristotle’s contrast between the unified *Iliad* and *Odyssey* and the many-parted *Cypria* and *Little Iliad* is itself a valuable early witness to the poems’ perceived breadth, but it is not a plot-summary and cannot determine their original textual extent.[4][5][6]

The principal framework is the set of summaries attributed to Proclus’ *Chrestomathy*, a lost treatise which West (2013) dates probably to the second century CE and whose summaries were themselves probably drawn from an older source rather than composed from Proclus’ direct reading of the epics.[7] The transmitted manuscripts of the *Cypria* summary read that Zeus deliberates with Thetis; modern editions print Themis, an editorial correction supported by P.Oxy.

3829, where Zeus plans with Themis to destroy an impious heroic race. The papyrus account is not identical to the Proclean summary because it supplies mortal impiety as the motive. In the modern edited summary, the *Cypria* is represented as a poem of eleven books: Zeus deliberates with Themis about the Trojan War; Eris provokes the beauty contest among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis; Paris judges in Aphrodite’s favour; Helen is taken; the Greek expedition gathers; and the narrative reaches the first nine years at Troy.[8][2] At the summary’s end, Achilles receives Briseis and Agamemnon Chryseis; Palamedes dies; Zeus plans to help the Trojans by removing Achilles from the Achaean alliance; and a catalogue of Trojan allies follows.[2][9][10]

This sequence makes the *Cypria* look like an introduction to the *Iliad*. It also contains material that does not fit neatly into that role: the Judgement of Paris, the Teuthranian expedition, the second gathering at Aulis, Iphigenia’s threatened sacrifice, Philoctetes’ abandonment, raids around Troy, and extensive genealogical and heroic material. Aristotle already regarded the *Cypria* as an epic of many parts; modern reassessments have urged that this episodic structure be treated as a positive narrative form rather than simply as failed Homeric unity.[11][12]

The *Cypria*’s direct fragments are few, often short, and uneven in status. A famous first fragment represents Zeus as pitying the overburdened earth and planning the Trojan War so that heroes will die; an English rendering of its closing claim is that the heroes died at Troy and Zeus’s will was fulfilled.[13] This passage is central because it is transmitted through a scholion on *Iliad* 1.5, where the *Iliad* likewise speaks of Zeus’s plan.

Other items preserve or report such matters as Aphrodite’s adornment before Paris’ judgement, the fate of Castor and Polydeuces, the naming of Neoptolemus, and details of Aeneas’ family.[13][8][14][15][4]

The dossier includes later testimony rather than only quotations. Pausanias says that he had read in the *Cypria* that Diomedes and Odysseus killed Palamedes while fishing; a Euripidean scholion reports a Cypria version in which Diomedes and Odysseus killed Polyxena; and a scholion on *Iliad* 17.140 attributes the account of Peleus receiving Achilles’ ash spear to the author of the *Cypria*. Such reports establish that ancient readers associated these stories with a poem named *Cypria*. They do not yield a complete plot, a secure date, or a single recoverable wording for the poem.[6][16]

The two standard modern fragment editions also shape what counts as Cypria evidence. Alberto Bernabé’s *Poetae Epici Graeci* of 1987 and Martin Davies’ *Epicorum Graecorum Fragmenta* of 1988 are generally reliable collections, but they differ over which items are genuine and in what order fragments should be printed. Their disagreement is not merely bibliographical. A numbering sequence can imply a reconstruction of the lost poem’s narrative architecture, so citing Cypria fragment 1 Bernabé or fragment 1 Davies is not neutral shorthand for a fully secure original text.[12][10]

For example, the alleged Scyros material is disputed. A scholion on *Iliad* 1.131 says that Thetis learned from Zeus that Achilles faced two alternatives: long but obscure life if he remained in Phthia, or short life with undying fame if he went to Troy. It adds that Thetis hid him among Lycomedes’ daughters on Scyros. Modern editors have proposed assigning this story to the *Cypria*, but the scholion does not say that Achilles himself knew the prophecy.[4] Proclus, by contrast, places Achilles’ visit to Scyros after the failed Teuthranian expedition.

The mismatch shows that a story can be “Cyclic” in ancient scholia without being securely locatable in the *Cypria* as represented by Proclus.[2][6]

The same problem recurs throughout the Cycle. Proclus’ *Iliou Persis* attributes Astyanax’s death to Odysseus, whereas a *Little Iliad* fragment attributes it to Neoptolemus. Proclus’ *Aithiopis* ends before Ajax’s suicide, but a Pindar scholion indicates that the *Aithiopis* included it. Proclus’ *Little Iliad* ends with the wooden horse entering Troy, although other evidence suggests that the poem could have included the sack itself. These cases do not make Proclus useless; they show that his summaries are selective and that their poem-boundaries may reflect a later organization of material.[2][10]

The *Cypria* is especially difficult because no papyrus fragment can be securely assigned to it. Martin West’s account emphasizes that the primary scaffolding is Proclus’ plot-summary, supplemented by Apollodorus and other reception evidence; the poem’s earlier currency must be inferred from archaic and classical citations, allusions, and visual reflexes. The absence of a secure Cypria papyrus means that even an apparently direct Cypria fragment often reaches us through a later commentator rather than through an ancient copy of the poem.[12]

This does not mean that papyri are irrelevant. A papyrus preserves a summary of material apparently from the *Little Iliad* whose wording and sequence differ from Proclus. It proves that Proclus was not the only form in which Cyclic narrative could circulate. But it is evidence for variant transmission of *Little Iliad*-like material, not a direct Cypria witness.[10][2] Similarly, P.Berol. 13930 belongs in discussion of Scyros traditions, but its presence among testimonia does not establish that it contains the *Cypria*.[12]

The physical transmission of the summaries is also uneven. Venetus A, Marcianus Graecus Z. 454 (= 822), is a tenth-century Byzantine manuscript and the oldest complete *Iliad* now extant. It contains excerpts from Proclus’ *Chrestomathy*: the Life of Homer and summaries of the Cyclic poems other than the *Cypria*. Its Cypria summary is absent because the relevant portion was lost or damaged; later manuscripts preserve the Cypria summary alone. Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles') reports nine manuscripts for the Cypria summary but only Venetus A for the other Trojan-Cycle summaries.[17][18][2][1]

**The surviving Cypria is therefore an evidentiary dossier, not an extant poem.** It provides identifiable narrative clusters—Zeus’s plan, the Thetis–Peleus wedding, Paris’ judgement, Helen’s departure, the Aulis crisis, the early campaign, prize-taking, and Achilles’ withdrawal—but it does not provide a single fixed text against which the *Iliad* can simply be measured.[10][12]

The evidence also preserves substantial variation. Herodotus says that the *Cypria* had Paris and Helen reach Troy directly from Sparta on the third day, with favourable wind and calm sea. Proclus’ summary instead has Hera send a storm that takes them to Sidon, which Paris sacks before sailing to Troy. The two versions matter because the Iliad itself refers to Sidon. One possibility is that the Cypria changed under Iliadic pressure; another is that Herodotus and Proclus knew different poems or versions bearing the Cypria title.[14][10][19][2]

Variation is equally visible in the Palamedes story. Pausanias reports drowning during a fishing expedition at the hands of Diomedes and Odysseus, while Apollodorus preserves a different tale in which Odysseus frames Palamedes for treason, leading to his stoning. Neither report supplies a continuous Cypria passage, but together they demonstrate that the label Cypria does not automatically guarantee a stable episode in a stable form.[20][6]

The resulting rule for interpretation may be called the **motif–text distinction**: the age and diffusion of a mythic event must be separated from the date, shape, and interrelations of the written poem in which it is later attested. Burgess makes this distinction explicit: the Cycle poems need not predate the *Iliad* for much of their material to preserve pre-Homeric Trojan-War tradition.[10][2]

That distinction has a practical consequence. A Cypria parallel can show that an Iliadic motif belonged to the wider Trojan tradition; it cannot, without further evidence, show that the *Iliad* copied a fixed Cypria text or that the surviving Cypria preserved the earliest form of the motif.[21][12][19]

P.Oxy. 3829 is not a post-2000 discovery: it was published in *The Oxyrhynchus Papyri* 57 in 1990. Later digital availability and renewed analysis have made the witness easier to inspect, but they have not supplied a continuous papyrus text of the *Cypria*.

A further manuscript witness confirms that the Cypria argument did not survive only in the nine manuscripts conventionally counted for it: the eleventh-century *Escorialensis* Ω.I.12 (= E4, West F) transmits a complete *Iliad*, commentary and scholia, lives of Homer, Porphyry excerpts, the *Batrachomyomachia*, and a Cypria summary attributed to Proclus.[22][18][1]

The eleventh-century Escorialensis Ω.I.12, also called E4 or West F, transmits a summary of the *Cypria* attributed to Proclus alongside its *Iliad* text and other material.[22]

The *Cypria* summary survives in about twelve manuscripts; Venetus A has lost the folio that contained it, whereas the other Trojan summaries survive only there.[7] Photius confirms that the excerpted summaries derive from the same longer work, Proclus’ *Chrestomathy*.[23]

P. Rylands 22, a first-century papyrus, preserves a narrative in which Helenos tells the Achaeans that the Palladion must be stolen before Neoptolemus is fetched from Scyros.[24]

P.Oxy. 1611 fr. 4 ii 145 preserves a scholarly discussion that quotes a line from the *Aethiopis* addressed to Penthesileia.[7]

### What evidentiary limits frame the debate over the Iliad’s relationship

The central asymmetry is simple. The *Iliad* is a continuously transmitted large-scale poem with ancient variants, scholia, papyri, and medieval manuscripts. The Cycle is reconstructed from late and selective witnesses. Venetus A remains indispensable because it preserves both the oldest complete *Iliad* and a uniquely rich body of ancient scholarship, but it is a tenth-century witness to texts and commentaries that had already undergone long transmission.[18][25][26]

The *Iliad* itself is not a transparent record of an eighth-century performance. Fundamental questions remain unresolved about when, how, and by whom it was written down; how it acquired its present form; and how it was transmitted before the earliest surviving manuscripts. This does not erase the difference from the Cycle, but it prevents scholars from treating the received *Iliad* as a direct recording of a single original event.[26][27]

The Cycle’s evidence has additional layers. Burgess identifies at least three major points at which material could have changed: the formation of a verse Cycle from separate poems; the creation of a prose summary; and the insertion of excerpts from that summary into manuscripts of the *Iliad*. Further alteration could have occurred during transmission of the poems, the verse collection, the prose epitome, and the manuscript excerpts.[10][2] These are not abstract possibilities: conflicting testimony about Paris’ voyage, Ajax’s suicide, the Scyros episode, and the Little Iliad’s endpoint all show why Proclus cannot be treated as a neutral facsimile of archaic poetry.[10][2]

Proclus’ summaries are informative precisely because they preserve ordering and selected narrative highlights. They can also compress motivation, flatten aesthetic structure, and smooth over contradictions. Their apparent arrangement of the *Cypria* as a lead-in to the *Iliad* may result partly from a later chronological organization of Trojan myth rather than from the archaic poem’s original design.[12][11] Yet the summaries cannot simply be dismissed as Homerizing inventions: Burgess argues that their wording preserves information irrelevant to a reader seeking only Iliadic background and that their transmission was careful enough to retain contradictions with Homer.[10]

The Cypria opening illustrates the problem. The fragment transmitted through the scholion to *Iliad* 1.5 links Zeus’s plan with the death of heroes at Troy. The transmitted Proclean manuscripts instead read that Zeus plans with Thetis; modern editions print Themis as an editorial correction supported by P.Oxy. 3829.[8][2] The scholia add Gaia’s request to be relieved of her burden.

These witnesses agree on a broad divine-population-reduction story, but they differ in agency, framing, and detail. Bernabé prints the scholia in his apparatus; Davies includes them with the fragment.[2][8][13]

The linguistic form of the fragment adds another difficulty. The transmitted language contains forms that look late: *pambōtōr* appears to be a hapax; *kenoō* is unattested before the fifth century BCE; and the phrase *polemos Iliakos* has no earlier attestation. These facts do not date the underlying myth to the fifth century. They show instead that the fragment’s extant wording may have been shaped by later transmission and by the cultural pressure of the Iliadic tradition.[8]

The **absence rule** follows. Absence from Proclus is not good evidence that an episode was absent from the poem. Polyxena’s sacrifice and Achilles’ hiding on Scyros are both attested or suspected elsewhere despite not appearing in the Proclean Cypria summary. The *Nostoi* underworld episode is another clear case: multiple testimonia attest it, yet Proclus omits it.[11][10] Conversely, presence in Proclus establishes that the epitomizer knew or reported the material, not that every feature belongs to the earliest performance or composition of the poem.[11][12]

Later mythographic accounts must be used with the same precision. Thomas Nelson (2023, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'Markers of Allusion in Archaic Greek Poetry') argues that Apollodorus probably preserves some Cypria material about Tennes, including a battle omitted by Proclus; he nevertheless states that the conclusion belongs in the language of probability, because Apollodorus is a later mythographic source and Proclus is clipped.[28][11] This is the appropriate model for many reconstructions: the evidence can make a narrative placement plausible without converting it into a recovered line of archaic epic.

The Iliad’s relationship to traditional material creates a second limit. It frequently refers to events outside its dramatic present: Achilles’ death, Troy’s eventual fall, Paris’ judgement, Protesilaus’ death, Philoctetes’ wound and expected return, earlier raids, and future destruction. Such external analepses and prolepses—references backward and forward beyond the narrated time—are a recognized Homeric technique for making a short segment of the war evoke the whole Trojan story.[2][29][6]

The *Iliad*’s narrated action spans fifty-one days, although it presupposes events well beyond that period.[2]

An Iliadic allusion to an external story therefore does not decide whether that story existed in a particular poem, in a performance tradition, or in several competing versions. Achilles’ raid on Aeneas’ cattle appears in Cypria-related material and in a relief amphora dated about 650 BCE. The evidence supports the existence of a broadly circulating raid tradition, but neither the image nor the summary proves that one directly depends on the other.[2]

The same limit applies to verbal correspondence. Bruno Currie (2021, Trends in Classics, 'Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician') observes that *Iliad* 1.5 and Cypria fragment 1.6–7 share the phraseological and thematic nexus of Zeus’s plan and the destruction of heroes.[30] He proposes that the Iliadic proem may allude to earlier Trojan-War poetry whose larger theme was the killing of heroes.

He also expressly allows alternatives: the Cypria or Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* may preserve the older context, the relationship may run in the other direction, or the phrasing may be a conventional hexameter expression.[31]

This is a crucial methodological point. An allusive reading does not require proof that Homer had a written book before him. But it does require an argument that the correspondence is distinctive enough, sufficiently ordered, and meaningful enough in its Iliadic context to be more than a shared formula, typical scene, or mythic commonplace.[32][11]

The corresponding oral-traditional caution is equally precise. John Miles Foley treats the scattered Cycle material not as fixed texts that influence the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* under a literary model, but as other poems in the larger constellation of Greek oral epic. In that model, shared material can signal **traditional referentiality**: a phrase, scene, or plot-pattern gathers meaning from recurrent use across a traditional repertoire rather than by pointing uniquely to a single source-text.[33][32]

The contrast is not between “text” and “no text.” Early Greek epic could be oral-derived and still know written records, memorized performance, recurring repertoires, and potentially specific allusions. Currie notes that writing may have contributed to the *Iliad*’s composition and that Foley’s term oral-derived allows oral and written techniques to coexist.[31] Nelson similarly argues that no single watershed separates mythological intertextuality from textual intertextuality: explicit naming of poets becomes more common, but direct allusion can still be plausible earlier.[34][35]

In oral performance, listeners cannot pause to compare written texts; they must recognize possible references to other stories from memory as the song unfolds.[87]

The problem is evidentiary rather than merely theoretical. Finkelberg argues that Cypria-related testimony displays material variation in episode order and character details—Paris and Helen’s route and Palamedes’ death are examples—whereas no comparable fluctuations in names or episode order have been attested for Iliadic subjects. Her conclusion is that the Cypria functioned as one multiform traditional poem among many, while the *Iliad* acquired a different, more definitive status.[20] This is a conclusion about the surviving record, not proof that the *Iliad* never varied in performance.

Nagy’s model pushes in another direction. He describes Homeric poetry as undergoing **crystallization**, a gradual stabilization through performance and institutional transmission rather than a single moment of textual fixation. In his chronology, a Pan-Ionic Homer developed from the eighth to early seventh century BCE; the Iliad–Odyssey tradition and Cycle traditions still coexisted in the early seventh to mid-sixth centuries; and textualization began in the mid-sixth through later fourth centuries BCE, when written forms increasingly approximated the later textual tradition.[36][13]

Finkelberg objects that the different Cypria versions attested over roughly a thousand years, from Herodotus in the fifth century BCE to Proclus in the fifth century CE, do not display the gradual movement from fluidity to rigidity that Nagy’s model would predict.[20] The disagreement is significant, but both positions reject a simple inference from the received Cypria summary to a fixed archaic original.

Venetus A’s scholia must be handled within this layered framework. The manuscript contains several strata of annotation, including material associated with Alexandrian scholarship. Its lemmata—the snippets of Homeric text quoted to identify the line being discussed—sometimes differ from the Iliad text written in the same manuscript. Such discrepancies may preserve alternative textual forms or traditional material that had fallen out of the common text by the ninth or tenth century.[37][26]

A scholion can therefore be evidence for ancient reception, variant Homeric text, and extra-Homeric myth. It is not automatically evidence that an Iliadic line derives from a Cyclic poem. The scholion on *Iliad* 1.5 transmits the Cypria fragment because it interprets Zeus’s plan in the *Iliad*; its very survival is shaped by Homer-centred commentary.[8] The scholion’s fuller narrative, including impious humankind and Zeus’s alternative disasters, may contain elements imported by the commentator rather than preserved from the Cypria itself.[8]

**The evidentiary limit is thus not that comparison is impossible; it is that overlap alone cannot identify its mechanism.** A proposed Cypria motif may be a particular allusion, a shared story-pattern, a common formulaic resource, a later Cypria adaptation to Homer, or more than one of these at different stages of transmission.[31][33][11][12]

The question remains unsettled because it requires evidence that would discriminate among these possibilities: an early securely dated quotation with identifiable wording, a papyrus attributable to a Cyclic poem, or a correspondence so dense and contextually marked that shared tradition is less economical than deliberate engagement.

### How did the Iliad’s transmission

Neoanalysis arose against older **analysis**, the school that divided Homeric poems into smaller compositional layers and explained inconsistency through interpolation. Neoanalysts remain unitarians: they usually treat the *Iliad* as a coherent poem, often by one poet, but interpret apparent narrative inconcinnities as transformations of material from other epic contexts. The classic example is the proposal that the *Iliad* reshapes the story of Achilles’ killing of Memnon after Antilochus’ death, as preserved in the *Aithiopis*, into Achilles’ vengeance for Patroclus.[38][2]

Classical *Quellenforschung*, or source-research, often treated Homer’s antecedents as written proto-Cyclic poems; later neoanalysis also allows earlier oral poems or relatively stable story-patterns.[12][75][88]

Modern neoanalysis is no longer equivalent to the claim that Homer read a pre-existing written Cycle. Burgess states that leading neoanalysts do not generally argue that Cyclic poems preceded Homer or that written Cyclic texts were available to the Iliadic poet. Rather, they posit pre-Homeric traditional motifs, episodes, and plots, reconstructed from later poems that continue those traditions.[10][2] Kullmann similarly distinguishes the later written record of Cyclic poetry from the earlier oral circulation of much of its contents.[39]

Older Neoanalysis often proposed written, pre-Homeric “proto-Cyclic” poems; Kullmann and later scholars also allow antecedent oral poems or stabilized oral traditions whose surviving Cyclic forms may be later manifestations.[89][75][13]

Wolfgang Kullmann’s revision of earlier neoanalysis allowed the antecedent poems proposed as sources for Homeric scenes to have been orally transmitted rather than written texts.[75]

This revision matters because it has reduced the apparent opposition between neoanalysis and oral theory. Neoanalysts ask what stories, scenes, and narrative structures supplied the *Iliad* with meaningful antecedents. Oralists ask how formulaic diction, type-scenes, repeated patterns, performance, and traditional referentiality enable Homeric composition and audience understanding. Burgess’s formulation is useful: oralists focus chiefly on the poetic craft of tradition, neoanalysts on its narrative contents.[10][13]

Seth Schein treats Parry’s oral-formulaic insights and Kakridis’s Neoanalytic attention to transformed myth as complementary approaches to Homer’s language, meter, style, and adaptation of traditional mythology.[90]

The divide remains real because the two approaches infer different things from the same resemblance. Neoanalysis tends to treat a distinctive correspondence as a sign that the Iliad deliberately evokes an external story, often with an effect of reversal, compression, anticipation, or rivalry. Oral-traditional criticism treats resemblance first as potentially conventional: the performer may be using a traditional resource without directing the audience to a single external poem.[32][13]

Burgess occupies an important position within this field because he accepts much of neoanalysis’s insight while resisting simple source-hunting. He accepts that the *Iliad* is an idiosyncratic shaping of inherited Trojan-War material, but argues that its relation is usually to mythological traditions expressed in different media rather than necessarily to specific Cycle poems. He calls this relation paradigmatic correspondence or motif transference: a Homeric scene may activate an external traditional pattern without reproducing one determinate literary source.[10][40][29]

That position explains why Burgess can call the Iliadic quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon traditional while declining to derive it straightforwardly from the *Cypria*. Proclus reports a quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon at Tenedos because Achilles had received a late invitation. Burgess argues that the recurrence of such quarrels in heroic myth makes the Iliadic wrath more plausibly a traditional episode massively expanded by Homer than a wholly Homeric invention or a copy of one fixed Cypria scene.[2]

Tsagalis (2025, Scripta Classica Israelica, 'Proemial Bridges: An Intratextual Association Activating an Intertextual Reference') represents a more explicitly plural and stratified use of traditional material.[41] In his work on the *Odyssey*, he distinguishes three levels: independent typical elements; typical elements that interact with other parts of the same poem; and features that represent Cyclic motifs.

He therefore neither reduces every recurrence to a generic motif nor treats every resemblance as an allusion to a fixed poem.[13] His analyses repeatedly stress the difficulty of separating a poem’s own inherited tradition from material stemming from other epic traditions.[13]

Tsagalis also treats Cyclic poetry as capable of preserving older local or epichoric traditions even where its surviving written form is post-Homeric. He suggests, for example, that the post-Homeric *Telegony* may preserve older western-Greek traditions about Odysseus’ journeys to Elis and Thesprotia.[13] This is a version of the motif–text distinction: later textual attestation does not settle the antiquity of the story.

Currie, by contrast, presses harder for intentional allusion and for the possibility of reconstructing specific poetic relations. His formulation that the quality and quantity of connections between *Cypria* and *Iliad* make a purely typological relationship problematic gives a clear neoanalytic rationale.[11] In *Homer’s Allusive Art*, he reads the proem’s Zeus-plan language as potentially evoking an earlier Trojan-War poetic horizon and argues that the restricted Iliadic theme is thereby modelled against the whole war.[31]

Currie does not claim certainty about direction in the Zeus-plan case. He expressly recognizes that Cypria and Hesiodic material may preserve an older context, may be later than the *Iliad*, or may share a traditional convention.[31] His methodological difference lies in treating the verbal and thematic pattern as potentially purposeful enough to require allusion as an explanatory option, rather than treating common tradition as the default terminus of inquiry.

Nagy’s oralist model frames the Cycle and Homer as evolving repertoires rather than bounded texts. In his reconstruction, the Cycle coexisted tenuously with the evolving Iliad–Odyssey tradition in the early seventh through mid-sixth centuries BCE. In the subsequent period, transmitters increasingly separated the Cycle from Homer and attributed Cycle poems to named non-Homeric poets.[36] The later opposition between Homeric and Cyclic poetry is thus an effect of institutional differentiation, not necessarily an archaic fact.

Nagy also connects this differentiation to festival performance. He reconstructs the archaic Panathenaia as featuring the Epic Cycle, including the repertoires that developed into the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*; by the classical period, the festival featured only the Homeric poems and excluded the Cycle.[36] He agrees with Burgess that no plausible reconstruction permits full performances of both received Homeric epics at the Panathenaia, making selections more likely in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE.[36]

This model gives oralists an explanation for the later “prequel” and “sequel” arrangement. Once the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* had become charismatic and comparatively fixed texts, other Trojan narratives could be organized around them as preliminaries and continuations. Holmberg argues that this process constructed fixed limits, authors, and exclusions for traditions that had earlier existed as more fluid intertextual narratives.[5]

Foley’s version of oralism focuses less on a detailed historical chronology and more on how traditional art functions. He insists that the Cycle should be treated as part of a constellation of oral epic rather than as a collection of literary sources for Homer.[33] His comparative work on return songs—songs whose patterns resemble the *Odyssey*’s *nostos*, or homecoming—demonstrates why recurrent plot shapes can be traditional rather than evidence of direct borrowing.[33]

Oral-traditional scholarship does not deny allusion. It rejects the inference that similarity alone proves it. A recurrent lament, battle over a corpse, heroic withdrawal, catalogue, divine council, or travel-and-return pattern can be an inherited conventional sequence. Burgess notes that a battle over a slain warrior is a typical motif; therefore, the battle over Patroclus’ body cannot by itself demonstrate dependence on a story about Achilles’ corpse, even though the neoanalytic correspondence may be compelling in its fuller context.[10]

The dispute over Thetis’ mourning in *Iliad* 18 makes the methodological contrast especially visible, though its detailed implications belong to the later discussion of disputed passages. Neoanalysts compare Thetis and the Nereids’ lament for Achilles in *Iliad* 18 with the actual mourning of Achilles in the *Aithiopis* and *Odyssey* 24. Kelly (2012, De Gruyter eBooks, 'The Mourning of Thetis: ‘Allusion’ and the Future in the Iliad') argues instead that the Iliadic episode is a **prospective lamentation**, a recurring Homeric sequence in which lament anticipates rather than follows death.[42][13][43] The disagreement is not about whether Achilles’ future death is relevant; it is about whether the particular scene needs an external source-story to be intelligible.

The evidence for early reception supports Burgess’s resistance to a Homer-centred linear model. He argues that general Trojan-War myth is attested from the late eighth century BCE, while probable evidence for knowledge of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* does not emerge until the late seventh century BCE. Early Greek art, on his account, represents non-Homeric Trojan themes more often than Homeric ones.[2][10] These observations do not date specific Cyclic poems before Homer, but they make it risky to assume that later Cyclic material necessarily derives from a dominant Iliadic text.

At the same time, later Cyclic forms could respond to Homer. Nelson’s model makes this double movement explicit: the written *Cypria*, perhaps of the seventh or sixth century BCE, can preserve pre-Homeric mythical material and also allude to the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*. In individual cases, he says, the direction of influence may be impossible to determine.[11] This is not indecision.

It is a historically differentiated account in which a story may be old, a particular Cypria realization later, and the later realization nonetheless artistically responsive to Homer.

Recent work increasingly adopts this kind of plural framework. The study of the *Cypria* now uses source research, oral tradition, reception history, linguistics, meter, and narratology rather than treating the poem merely as an appendix to Homer.[12] Linguistic work by Ercolani and Fina argues that Cypria forms belong to the broader continuum of archaic epic diction and warns that common diction can result from singer mobility, education, and professional networks rather than direct textual dependence.[21][12]

This methodological convergence does not abolish the basic disagreement. Kelly argues that newer neoanalysts increasingly use terms such as interaction, allusion, versions, songs, and story traditions rather than “errors” or mechanically borrowed sources; nevertheless, he sees their continuing objective as establishing the priority of non-Homeric material.[13] Currie’s critics object that an allusive model risks assuming that Homer or another canonical poem dominated a poet’s and audience’s memory in a performance culture where listeners encountered many songs and traditions.[19][14]

**The present divide is therefore a dispute over explanatory priority.** Neoanalytic interpretation asks whether a marked Iliadic configuration is best explained as engagement with a particular prior or parallel Trojan-War realization; oralist interpretation asks first whether the configuration can be explained by the flexible resources of a shared tradition and performance ecology.[32][13][33]

Bakker’s oralist account adds a discourse-oriented dimension: Homeric diction and narrative are shaped by the real-time dynamics of oral composition and audience processing. On this view, an audience can recognize a traditional narrative resonance without treating it as a reference to one fixed Cyclic text.

### The transmission history that made the problem acute

The familiar idea that the *Cypria* precedes the *Iliad* and the *Aithiopis* follows it derives in part from the order in which later readers received the corpus. Rousseau notes that this arrangement has roots in an edition in which the *Cypria* preceded the *Iliad* and the *Aithiopis* followed it. That order is highly useful for chronological orientation, but it does not prove a chronology of composition or direct poetic dependence.[44]

Burgess argues that the Cycle as transmitted could duplicate Homeric material, contradict it, or pass over it without concern. The poems do not consistently behave like carefully designed appendices that fill every gap in the Homeric poems.[10] The *Cypria*’s Trojan catalogue is one test case: it contains no Greek contingents or ships and foregrounds Trojan and Asia Minor allies, including figures absent from the *Iliad*. Burgess therefore rejects the inference that it was composed simply to introduce the Iliadic catalogue.[12][2]

Another reading treats the Trojan-allies catalogue at the end of the *Cypria* summary as duplicating the Trojan Catalogue in *Iliad* 2, complicating the idea of a seamless junction between the poems.[6][9]

Similarly, the Cypria’s final Zeus-plan differs structurally from the plan in *Iliad* 1. In the Cypria summary, Zeus plans to benefit the Trojans by separating Achilles from the Achaeans, before the quarrel. In the *Iliad*, Zeus’s commitment follows the quarrel and responds to Thetis’ request to honour Achilles. The resemblance is real, but the sequence and purpose are not identical.[6][2] This difference supports independent traditional development or later reciprocal shaping more readily than a simple prequel-to-main-text model.

Finkelberg’s multiformity argument makes the same point from a different angle. The Cypria’s reports preserve different routes, different episode orders, and different narrative versions. The comparatively stable profile of Iliadic material in the surviving record suggests that Homeric canonization—including public recitation at the Panathenaia and school use—gave the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* a distinctive status not shared by the Cyclic poems.[20] The conclusion is not that the Cypria was pure oral flux while the *Iliad* was always fixed. It is that the later reception history preserved them in radically different forms.

Burgess disagrees with using active multiform performance to explain every Cypria discrepancy. Regarding Herodotus and Proclus on Paris’ voyage, he argues that the Cypria was probably already a fixed text by the Classical period and therefore seeks other explanations, including alteration and the possibility of multiple poems with similar titles.[2][10] This is an internal disagreement among scholars who all reject the naive equation of the surviving Proclean summary with an original Cypria.

Sbardella offers a third historical model. Rather than treating the Cycle as either a Hellenistic construction or a stable early corpus, he describes the incorporation of the *Cypria* into a Trojan Epic Cycle as a long, layered development from regional poetic traditions through changing cultural, religious, and performative contexts, culminating in late-Archaic rhapsodic consolidation.[21][12] This account is compatible with the fact that particular poems could preserve old material while their placement in a comprehensive sequence remained historically contingent.

The distinction between **story chronology** and **text chronology** follows. Story chronology orders Zeus’s plan, Paris’ judgement, Helen’s abduction, Aulis, the war, Achilles’ death, Troy’s fall, and the returns. Text chronology asks when particular performances, poems, written copies, summaries, and manuscript compilations took shape. The former is comparatively stable; the latter is precisely what the surviving evidence rarely permits scholars to establish.[34][13][12]

This distinction also protects against circularity. If one uses the *Iliad* to reconstruct an earlier Cypria tradition and then uses the reconstructed tradition to explain the *Iliad*, the argument may simply reproduce its own premise. Nelson identifies circularity as a risk whenever the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are mined for traditions that they may themselves have shaped.[34] The motif–text distinction and absence rule are safeguards against that circularity.

The material history of the Cycle gives Venetus A a special but limited role. Its appended summaries show that Byzantine readers encountered Trojan-Cycle material alongside the *Iliad*, as background for Homeric reading. Burgess argues that the summaries were excerpted from the larger *Chrestomathy* and inserted into Iliad manuscripts for that purpose.[10][2] This proves a powerful later reception practice: the Cycle was framed as Homer’s narrative context. It does not prove that archaic singers conceived the Cycle as a prequel-and-sequel set.

The scholia reinforce the same point. They preserve ancient scholarly efforts to explain Homer through external myth, including Cyclic material. Aristarchus athetized, or marked as suspect, *Iliad* 24.28–30 because it refers to the Judgement of Paris; later analysts likewise called such material Cyclic and therefore late.[2][6] Modern scholarship generally resists that inference: an Iliadic reference to a wider myth does not make the passage secondary, because the poem can deliberately invoke a tradition beyond its immediate plot.[44][2]

The current question is thus inherited from two linked historical processes. First, the *Iliad* became a canonical, heavily copied text with a massive commentary tradition. Second, the Cycle was preserved selectively and largely because it could be read beside Homer. The resulting archive makes Homer look primary and the Cycle supplementary even when individual myths may have circulated independently of either text.[8][14]

This history creates the temptation that both schools seek to manage. Neoanalysis risks treating late Cyclic witnesses as transparent windows onto earlier poems. Oralism risks treating every marked correspondence as a generic traditional resonance and thereby losing the possibility of specific poetic interaction. The strongest current work avoids both reductions by asking, case by case, whether the evidence points to a broad narrative cluster, a particular performance tradition, a later Homer-facing Cyclic text, or a marked allusive relation.[11][21][19][13]

### Synthesis

The surviving Epic Cycle establishes a broad and ancient Trojan-War story-world, but not a continuous pre-Homeric Cypria text. Proclus, Bernabé’s and Davies’ fragment collections, scholia, testimonia, art, and papyri preserve overlapping yet non-identical traces of that world; Venetus A records a later Homer-centred arrangement of much of it.[12][10][2][26] Neoanalysis is strongest when distinctive correspondences make inherited narrative materially active in Iliadic interpretation; oralism is strongest when formulaic diction, typical scenes, and variant traditions make direct dependence unnecessary.[31][33][10] The defensible starting point is therefore neither fixed-source dependence nor undifferentiated oral fluidity, but the motif–text distinction: an old motif may be shared by the *Iliad*, several performances, and a later Cyclic poem, while a particular surviving formulation may still respond to Homer.[11][21][2]

## Neoanalytic accounts

### What is Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles') [17]’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

Jonathan S. Burgess reworks neoanalysis—the study of how Homeric narrative may reuse material from earlier epic—inside an oral-traditional model. His central claim is that the *Iliad* is both a unified, distinctive poem and an idiosyncratic reshaping of inherited Trojan-War narrative. Homeric and Cyclic performance traditions could have coexisted in the Archaic period, drawing on the same general mythological heritage while differing in subject, style, purpose, and eventual textual formation.[10]

For Burgess, the important object is not a prior written *Cypria* from which Homer copied. Contemporary neoanalysis, as he describes it, no longer requires that the Cycle poems predate Homer or that written Cyclic texts were available to the Iliadic poet. Instead, it infers pre-Homeric traditional motifs—recognizable episodes, actions, and character-bound story patterns—from later witnesses, including Cyclic fragments, testimonia, summaries, art, and later poetry.[10][2] That distinction matters because a resemblance between the *Iliad* and the *Cypria* can show that both participate in an inherited story-world without showing that one extant poem copied the other.

Burgess calls the Iliad’s transformative reuse of such material **metacyclic**. A metacyclic passage transfers a traditional narrative motif into a new Iliadic setting in a way that evokes its wider Trojan-War context for an audience already familiar with that context. What moves is not wording from a fixed text but a notional motif: a sequence of actions traditionally associated with a particular hero or episode. The transfer may be temporally displaced: material proper to Achilles’ later death, funeral, or Troy’s fall can be made meaningful within the Iliad’s earlier narrative.[29][40] **Burgess therefore makes meaningful Iliadic allusion compatible with oral composition by locating its reference in shared mythological tradition rather than in a single recoverable source text.**[10][29][40]

Burgess distinguishes three levels: cyclic myth, the broad Trojan-War tradition; Cyclic epic, its poetic realization; and Homeric epic, which can use both allusively.[88]

Burgess distinguishes “cyclic myth,” the broader mythic tradition, from “cyclic epic,” particular epic presentations of it, and calls Homeric epic “metacyclic” when it allusively uses material from those levels.[88]

Burgess distinguishes cyclic myth, the wider body of Trojan-War stories, from cyclic epic, its particular poetic realizations; he calls Homeric epic “metacyclic” insofar as it mobilizes both.[88]

This model nevertheless preserves a neoanalytic criterion stronger than mere resemblance. Burgess distinguishes highly repeatable elements—formulas, type-scenes, and broad thematic plots—from mid-level motifs specific to a hero or narrative. A proposed transfer must involve details that fit the presumed primary setting substantially better than the Iliadic reuse; generic resemblances such as anger, mourning, battle over a corpse, or heroic withdrawal do not suffice.[29] His treatment of the Patroclus narrative illustrates the distinction.

He finds the correspondence between Patroclus’ death and the traditional death of Achilles compelling where the shared configuration includes death near Troy’s walls, the joint action of mortal and god, and Apollo’s secondary but decisive role. But he allows that the battle over Patroclus’ corpse could be a conventional battle-for-the-body motif rather than a displaced Achilles episode.[10]

Burgess’s reconstruction of Achilles’ death uses a **fabula**, a composite traditional narrative inferred from literary and artistic evidence rather than preserved whole in any one source. In that reconstructed sequence Achilles kills Memnon, attacks Troy, and is killed by Apollo and Paris; Thetis and Eos secure exceptional posthumous fates for their sons. Burgess acknowledges the danger of circularity in using Homer to reconstruct the fabula, but argues that substantial non-Homeric evidence indicates that the story was known independently of Homer.[29] He consequently reads the Iliad’s repeated concern with Achilles’ death as allusive: the poem does not narrate the death, but expects its audience to know the story and turns that external knowledge into foreboding.[29]

This does not make Burgess an uncritical heir of classical neoanalysis. He rejects older arguments that reconstruct a pre-Iliadic *Memnonis* or *Aethiopis* as a determinate source from which the Iliad mechanically borrowed. For example, he judges the claim that Achilles’ Iliadic withdrawal derives from a withdrawal before his encounter with Memnon insecure: heroic withdrawal is a common motif, and it is uncertain whether the *Aethiopis* itself contained the alleged withdrawal.[2][10] His more limited claim is that Achilles’ death-tradition can illuminate Iliadic effects: Thetis and the Nereids’ mourning in *Iliad* 18 resembles Achilles’ future funeral, while Patroclus functions as an altera persona, an alternate embodiment, of Achilles in a cluster of death-and-funeral motifs.[29]

Burgess applies the same logic to the *Cypria*. Proclus’ summary reports that Achilles took Briseis, Agamemnon Chryseis, and Zeus planned to help the Trojans by removing Achilles from the Greek alliance.[10][2] Burgess emphasizes the consequential difference from *Iliad* 1: in the *Cypria* Zeus’s plan includes the quarrel and aims at Trojan advantage, whereas in the *Iliad* Zeus responds after the quarrel to Thetis’s request to honour Achilles; Trojan success is the means, not the ultimate aim.[10][2] He concludes that the Achilles–Agamemnon quarrel was traditional and that the *Cypria* independently told a somewhat different version. Thus the parallel is not evidence that the *Cypria* was composed as an introduction to *Iliad* 1.[10][2]

The phrase *Dios boulē*, Zeus’s plan, illustrates Burgess’s wider comparative method. The *Cypria*’s plan to reduce humanity and the Iliadic proem’s reference to Zeus’s plan participate, he argues, in old Near Eastern and Indo-European patterns of divine planning and mass destruction. That comparative background undercuts a simple claim that the *Cypria* merely expanded Iliad 1.5. Burgess permits the Iliadic phrase to denote the poem’s immediate plot while connoting the broader Cyclic plan.[10][2] Comparative Indo-European material is therefore contextual evidence for the antiquity and recurrence of a mythic structure, not proof of direct poem-to-poem borrowing; his discussion of the possible Indo-European unequal-brothers pattern behind Paris and Hector is expressly suggestive rather than demonstrative.[2]

Burgess gives the transmitted Epic Cycle substantial evidentiary value but sharply limits what its form can prove. The Cycle survives through fragments, testimonia, and Proclus’ prose summaries; these are useful windows onto ancient Trojan-War myth.[10] Yet he reconstructs the familiar Cycle as a Hellenistic editorial collection of previously independent poems, possibly created by abridging and joining sections whose surviving transitions remain awkward.[10] The summaries embedded in Iliad manuscripts, including the tenth-century Venetus A, were placed there as background for Iliad readers; Venetus A’s *Cypria* summary is lost through damage, although later Iliad manuscripts preserve it.[10][2] Consequently, the apparent sequence from *Cypria* to *Iliad* in Proclus does not establish an archaic prequel–sequel design.

Burgess does not dismiss Proclus as useless. He argues that Proclus provides a functional approximation of a centre for reconstruction, while warning that the summary’s poem-boundaries and scope may reflect later cropping and collection.[6][10] The mismatch between Proclus and other evidence is central to the point: a Pindar scholion indicates that the *Aethiopis* included Ajax’s suicide even though Proclus ends it earlier, and a scholion attributes Achilles’ concealment on Scyros to the Cycle despite its absence from Proclus’ *Cypria* synopsis.[2] The result is a method that uses Bernabé’s fragments and scholia as witnesses to wider tradition, not as a stable map of original poem-limits.

Burgess’s position is thus internally double: he treats the Cyclic poems as independent, orally developed poetic traditions that later acquired relatively fixed forms, but he also treats their transmitted collection as a moving target shaped by editorial and manuscript history.[10] This lets him argue that Cyclic material can preserve pre-Homeric traditions even if the familiar Cycle collection is later than the *Iliad*.[10][2] It rules out both extremes: the Cycle is neither simply Homeric supplementation nor securely a set of complete pre-Homeric source texts.

Burgess’s rejection of a designed Cypria-to-Iliad handoff also rests on the Trojan catalogue. The Cypria catalogue has neither ships nor Greek contingents, but emphasizes Trojan and Asia Minor allies, including figures absent from the Iliad; it therefore does not operate as a straightforward introduction to the Iliadic catalogue. Burgess’s claim that Cyclic poems were not composed with one another in mind rejects an original continuous mega-narrative, not the existence of shared motifs, episodes, or correspondences.[45]

This account directly conflicts with Martin L. West’s reconstruction of the Cypria as a deliberately pre-Iliadic poem: West holds that its final form was tailored to cover the events before the Iliad and to incorporate events to which the Iliad refers or which it presupposes. West accordingly reads the allocation of Briseis to Achilles and Chryseis to Agamemnon, and Zeus’s plan to remove Achilles from the Greek alliance, as preparation for the Iliad; he nevertheless judges the Cypria’s Trojan catalogue dependent on the Iliadic catalogue.[7]

Burgess’s treatment of a shared quarrel is also supported by variant local detail: a scholion to Iliad 16.57 places Briseis’ capture at Pedasos in the Cypria-poets’ version, whereas the Iliad places it at Lyrnessos. Such divergence accords with his model of differently realized traditional narratives rather than a Cypria made to reproduce Iliad 1.[7][45]

Burgess had set out the larger basis of this position in his 2001 monograph *The Tradition of the Trojan War in Homer and the Epic Cycle*: the Homeric poems presuppose a large and coherent Trojan-War tradition beyond the plots they choose to narrate. On this view, the pre-war divine history, including Thetis’s status and Zeus’s plan, is relevant to *Iliad* 1 without making the later transmitted *Cypria* Homer’s source-book.

Burgess’s Achilles material also specifies how the *Iliad* represents knowledge of the hero’s death. Achilles’ foreknowledge of the choice between a short, glorious life and a long, obscure one is exceptional among Iliadic warriors; by the poem’s end, its increasingly intense prophecies have made the circumstances of his death and burial familiar.[46] Burgess reads Patroclus as an *altera persona*: Patroclus performs deeds ordinarily belonging to Achilles, and his death reflects Achilles’ traditional death, making Achilles a dead man walking.[46] The Patroclus and Achilles sequences interlock: Patroclus enacts most death motifs, while Achilles’ own sequence briefly brings his approaching fate to the foreground before the poem returns to Patroclus.[46]

Burgess’s criterion of specificity controls this argument. Death before Troy’s walls with Apollo involved is specific to the Achilles-death *fabula*; funeral games are a more generally heroic motif and cannot by themselves establish a transfer.[46] He therefore denies that Antilochus is simply Patroclus’ model, even while accepting a general correspondence between the two configurations.[46] The single suit of Achilles’ armour in Cyclic tradition, against the *Iliad*’s doubled armour after Patroclus receives the first, likewise makes the Iliadic arrangement a poetically purposeful innovation: it enables Patroclus to substitute for Achilles and makes Hector’s wearing of that armour ominous.[46]

Burgess identifies the surviving Trojan-War account of the Cycle as a summary attributed to Proclus in the *Chrestomathy*, while maintaining that the identity and date of this Proclus are uncertain.[2]

The Zeus–Thetis scene crystallizes Burgess’s double scale of reference. Its immediate plot-function is Thetis’s request that Zeus honour Achilles after Agamemnon’s insult, with Trojan success serving that honour; its wider mythic function joins Thetis’s marriage to Peleus and Achilles’ birth to the Cyprian and Venetus A tradition of Zeus’s plan to reduce the heroic population. Burgess treats both levels as co-present.[47]

Burgess treats the Trojan-War tradition as extending beyond epic performance to other verse, unmetrical narratives, folktales, and visual representations, with these media interacting.[2]

### What is Currie (2021, Trends in Classics, 'Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician') [30]’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

Bruno Currie’s **expanded neoanalysis** is more willing than Burgess’s model to posit deliberate, specific, and potentially directional poetic interaction. His 2016 monograph *Homer’s Allusive Art* asks how allusion can function in oral-derived poetry and answers that oral derivation does not exclude specific reference to a particular earlier or contemporary song, poem, or passage.[31][48] Currie therefore treats traditional referentiality—meaning generated by recurrent traditional diction and patterns—and allusion in the stricter sense as complementary rather than rival explanatory modes.[31]

Currie’s account retains oral tradition but rejects the inference that oral performance necessarily produces only anonymous, non-specific resonance. He argues that Greek epic may have been hybrid: orally traditional in its compositional resources while capable of deliberate differentiation by individual poets.[31] Performance rivalry—the competition of works performed on the same or closely connected occasions—supplies one mechanism by which poems could respond to, rival, or revise one another without requiring modern-style mass circulation of books.[30] **Currie’s decisive methodological claim is that poem-specific allusion remains possible under oral conditions and must be tested rather than ruled out in advance.**[31][49]

His treatment of Iliad 1 and the *Cypria* shows both the ambition and restraint of that approach. Currie compares *Iliad* 1.5’s *Dios boulē* with *Cypria* fragment 1 Bernabé, which describes heroes dying at Troy as Zeus’s plan was being fulfilled, and with the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, where Zeus intends many heroes’ heads to be sent to Hades. He suggests that the Iliadic poet’s deployment of this language in the narrow setting of Achilles’ quarrel may signal that the poem’s restricted theme is modelled on the whole Trojan War.[31] The comparison is strengthened by the recurrence of language about many heroic deaths and Zeus’s will, but Currie explicitly leaves three possibilities open: the *Cypria* and *Catalogue* may preserve the earlier context; the relationship may run from the *Iliad* outward; or all three may exploit a conventional hexameter formulation.[31]

Currie consequently does not simply revive the old claim that Homer copied a fixed *Cypria*. His method permits oral or partly oral precursors, and his own discussion of the Zeus-plan parallel suspends a final decision about direction.[31] But he insists that the quality and quantity of observable *Cypria*–*Iliad* connections make a purely typological explanation unsatisfactory.[11] A type-scene is a repeatable conventional scene-pattern; Currie’s point is that dense verbal, narrative, and contextual correspondences can exceed what a broad shared pattern alone explains.

This stronger account of allusion is visible beyond the *Cypria*. Currie investigates possible Iliadic engagement with earlier poetry about Memnon and considers whether a hypothetical *Memnonis* stood behind the Iliad’s Patroclus–Hector–Achilles sequence.[49][48] He also connects Homeric allusion to Ancient Near Eastern epic, especially the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, and proposes continuity in sophisticated allusive practices across very different media and cultures.[48] This comparison expands the possible archive of Homeric poetic memory, but it has attracted objections that the required textual dominance of Homer or Gilgamesh in a poet’s and audience’s mind is historically uncertain.[14][19]

Currie is willing to posit a pre-Iliadic *Memnonis* as a model for elements of Patroclus’ death. He also compares the Sumerian *Death of Bilgames* with the Babylonian *Gilgamesh* to argue that an epic can imitate both an earlier narrative and that narrative’s own imitative practice.[75]

The difference from Burgess is therefore one of explanatory preference. Burgess normally explains correspondences through shared traditional story-material and reserves specific allusion for strongly character-bound motifs. Currie gives greater weight to individual poetic agency and to the possibility that a marked verbal or narrative pattern signals a particular predecessor. A review of his work accordingly describes the project as focused on allusive poetics rather than reconstructing composition or genesis, although it uses both extant and hypothetical texts as possible partners in interaction.[49]

Currie’s critics, especially oral-traditional scholars, object that the surviving Cycle is too fragmentary to support secure directional claims. The objection is not that Homer lacked knowledge of wider Trojan-War traditions. Rather, it is that a surviving parallel may reflect a shared oral repertoire, repeated reperformance, or later memorialization rather than a source–recipient relationship between identifiable poems.[49][50] Kelly (2012, De Gruyter eBooks, 'The Mourning of Thetis: ‘Allusion’ and the Future in the Iliad')’s criticism of Currie’s *Cypria* reading is pointed: beginning from specific allusion can assume too readily that Homer or Gilgamesh dominated the Cypria poet’s and audience’s interpretive horizon, while performance tradition offers other ways to understand the same material.[42][14][19]

Currie’s account makes a productive demand on the debate: oral-traditional explanation cannot dismiss specificity merely by naming typology, and neoanalysis cannot infer direction merely from similarity. The question becomes whether a correspondence is sufficiently marked, concentrated, and meaningful to identify an intentional relationship rather than a shared conventional resource.[49] That demand pushes discussion from an abstract opposition between orality and textuality toward case-by-case arguments about particular passages.

Currie’s 2015 chapter on the Cypria situates its study in a plural field comprising source-research, oral tradition, reception history, linguistics, and metre. That framework treats Cyclic epic as a central component of Greek epic culture rather than merely Homer’s appendix, while leaving his 2016 case-by-case test for specific allusion intact.[45]

Currie’s model also permits Achilles’ awareness of his coming death to activate the later Achilles narrative without requiring the *Iliad* to narrate it. The resolution of *Iliad* 24 gains additional force, on this reading, because the audience can set Priam’s recovery of Hector against Achilles’ own imminent death and the future fall of Troy.

### What is Tsagalis (2025, Scripta Classica Israelica, 'Proemial Bridges: An Intratextual Association Activating an Intertextual Reference') [41]’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

Christos Tsagalis develops what he calls **oral intertextual neoanalysis**, especially in *The Oral Palimpsest* (2008) and later methodological work. The phrase joins two commitments: oral traditions preserve many variant tellings, but those tellings can still enter meaningful, recognizably intertextual relationships. Oral intertextuality, in his definition, is an inherent feature of a web of myth through which epic audiences can access a horizon of diverse story-variants.[51][52] Homeric epic is thus neither isolated from the Cycle nor reducible to direct dependence on a fixed Cyclic text.

Tsagalis’s oral intertextuality allows recognizable allusive relations to emerge when sequences of events, character associations and versions become sufficiently stable within an oral tradition.[89][91]

Tsagalis’s larger historical model treats Homer as a Panhellenic construction: a tradition tending toward a broadly Greek canon while retaining *poikilia*, varied and interwoven materials from recognisable song-traditions and older Indo-European strata.[51][52] This model differs from Currie’s greater readiness to infer interaction with individual passages or texts, but it also differs from an oralism that regards all recurrence as merely generic.

Tsagalis allows that oral epic can make deliberate allusion and proposes **markedness** and **meaningfulness** as positive criteria: a recurrence must stand out and make an interpretively consequential contribution in its new context.[53]

For Tsagalis, an oral audience’s recognition of a particular allusion requires some stability: an established pool and order of events, persistent associations between characters and episodes, and broad diffusion of an authoritative version.[89]

His method distinguishes three levels that are too often conflated: cyclic myth, the broad traditional story-material; cyclic epic, particular poetic realizations of that material; and Homeric epic, which deploys both for its own design.[13] A motif may have a typical core while its particular form is shaped by a Cyclic tradition.

Thus Tsagalis can identify motifs in the *Odyssey* as transferred from oral precursors of the *Cypria* and *Nostoi*, while refusing to assign every recurrent feature to a particular source.[13] This is a middle position: oral variation supplies the medium, but distinctive configurations can still preserve contacts with identifiable traditions.

Tsagalis’s reassessment of the Scyros material attributed to the *Cypria* offers a model case. He assembled scholia to *Iliad* 19.326 and 9.668, P.Berol. 13930, Ptolemaeus Chennus, Pausanias, Homeric references to Scyros and Neoptolemus, and Proclus’ summary. He concluded that later scholarship and scholiasts had conflated three rival narratives: Achilles’ Iliadic sack of Scyros; a Cyclic story in which a storm after the Teuthranian expedition takes Achilles to Scyros, where he fathers Neoptolemus; and the separate story of Achilles concealed in women’s clothing until Odysseus exposes him.[12] Proclus’ silence about a problematic concealment-and-recruitment story, alongside the D-scholium’s likely limited attribution to Neoptolemus’ birth and naming, leads Tsagalis to exclude the cross-dressing episode from the *Cypria*.[12]

This procedure displays Tsagalis’s distinctive philological restraint. Scholia, papyri, Proclus, and Bernabé’s fragment assignments are not accepted as mutually transparent witnesses; each must be tested against rival traditions and the narrative logic of the poems. The result is not scepticism about the Cycle’s relevance but a more granular account of how Cyclic traditions circulated and were later conflated.[12] It makes the Cycle valuable evidence for oral narrative diffusion without treating every ancient attribution as a secure report of a single poem’s contents.

Tsagalis also uses comparative Indo-European poetics selectively. In his discussion of a Cyprian Achilles–Helen episode, he compares Aphrodite, Thetis, and Eos as goddesses who rescue or transport beloved mortals and relates Eos to the Indo-European dawn goddess *Ausos*. This supports the plausibility of divine mediation in the reconstructed scene; it does not independently establish the scene’s presence in the *Cypria*.[12] His reconstruction from Proclus has Achilles desire to see Helen, Thetis mediate with Zeus, and Aphrodite bring Helen to Achilles in concealment, perhaps under mist.[12] He then reads the *Iliad*’s avoidance of such a meeting as an artistic choice: the episode belongs to the Cypria’s romantic logic and would conflict with the Iliad’s tragic concentration on wrath and heroic death.[12]

Tsagalis’s reconstruction of the *Cypria*’s Achilles–Helen episode combines Proclus’ brief notice with comparative literary and visual evidence; it is a plausible reconstruction, not a continuously preserved text.[12]

Tsagalis’s general interpretive position is therefore more integrative than either a fixed-source neoanalysis or a purely diffuse oralism. He holds that a Homeric poem can reshape the particular forms of Cyclic myth for ideological and narrative purposes, while preserving traces of alternative song-traditions.[13][51] His work also recognizes a genuine interpretive difficulty: the scholar must distinguish features generated within a poem’s own tradition from features brought in from other traditions, a problem he calls exceptionally slippery.[13] The answer is not to abandon allusion, but to separate generic motifs, intratextual patterning, and marked intertraditional reference.

For *Iliad* 1 and 24, Tsagalis’s approach treats the Zeus–Thetis encounter, Achilles’ mortality, and Priam’s encounter with Achilles as nodes that activate a larger Trojan narrative. Their effect depends on the audience’s access to layered traditions about Thetis, Zeus, Achilles’ death, and Troy’s destruction, rather than on quotation from a single Cyclic book.

### Synthesis

Burgess, Currie, and Tsagalis all reject the old Homer-centred picture in which the Epic Cycle is merely a late supplement to autonomous Homeric masterpieces. All three accept that Homer works within a larger Trojan-War tradition and that Cyclic evidence can illuminate the *Iliad*.[10][31][51] Their disagreement concerns the level at which a parallel should be identified: Burgess usually locates it in a shared, hero-specific mythic repertoire; Currie is readier to identify deliberate interaction with particular poems or passages; Tsagalis places such interaction within a layered field of cyclic myth, oral song-tradition, and Panhellenic recomposition.[29][31][13]

The strongest common conclusion is that neither “direct borrowing from the *Cypria*” nor “mere generic typology” is adequate as a universal explanation. Burgess’s metacyclic model and Tsagalis’s oral intertextual neoanalysis show how distinctive narrative traditions can matter without a fixed source-text; Currie’s work insists that oral conditions do not, by themselves, rule out specific allusion. The live disagreement remains passage-specific: how much marked correspondence is enough to move from shared tradition to directed poetic engagement.[29][31][53]

## Oral-traditional accounts

Judgment — Oral-traditional scholarship does not deny that the *Iliad* and the Epic Cycle share Trojan-War narratives. It asks a different question from classical Neoanalysis: not which fixed Cyclic poem Homer used, but how a performance-based tradition made phrases, scenes, and story-patterns meaningful to singers and audiences. Nagy, Foley, and Bakker agree on that broad reorientation, but they differ markedly in their accounts of transmission, textuality, and the kinds of recurrence that can count as meaningful.

### What is Nagy’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

Gregory Nagy’s evolutionary model treats the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey*, and cyclic epic as outcomes of long-lived and changing traditions of composition-in-performance, not as stable, separately authored books whose mutual relations can normally be reduced to one-way borrowing. He rejects the claim that a passage in one traditional poem can directly refer to a passage in another; instead, traditions interact through what he calls diachronic cross-reference: evolving performance traditions influence and respond to one another over time. This does not make Homeric–Cyclic connections unreal; it moves them from text-to-text dependence to interaction among traditions.[40][34]

Nagy’s account depends on *multiformity*, the capacity of an oral tradition to produce variant realizations of a song. His criterion is relatively expansive: variation in wording can suffice for treating a poem as multiform, whereas the Lordian criterion used by critics also expects variation in nonessential narrative detail. On Nagy’s reconstruction, Homeric poetry continued to crystallize—becoming progressively more stable without becoming instantly fixed—through a period extending from the eighth to the sixth centuries BCE; textualization began in his Period 3, from the middle of the sixth century to the later fourth century BCE, but possible written transcripts did not terminate oral reception or control performance.[20][13][36]

Oral multiformity does not require unconstrained improvisation: a tradition can preserve a stable narrative skeleton while names, sequence, wording, and local details vary.[20][92]

This reconstruction has a direct consequence for the *Cypria*. A shared narrative element between Iliadic and Cyclic evidence cannot, by itself, establish that the *Iliad* borrowed from a fixed *Cypria* text, because the later record may preserve different products of interacting oral traditions. Nagy’s position therefore differs both from a simple source model and from the view that similarities have no historical significance: traditional overlap is meaningful, but priority between particular versions is methodologically insecure.[40][14]

Nagy’s evolutionary model also permits the possibility of several *Cypriae*, rather than a single authoritative poem, given divergent versions of Cyclic episodes.[20]

Nagy’s multiform model also permits the possibility of several “Cypriae,” rather than a uniquely authoritative poem behind the later testimonia.[20]

Nagy also explains the eventual separation of Homer from the Cycle through *Panhellenization*, the historical selection and reshaping of traditions toward a broad Greek rather than local constituency. In his formulation, Homeric poetry becomes Panhellenic through an evolutionary synthesis that emphasizes materials shared across many communities, while Cyclic poetry is relatively more local or *epichoric*—that is, tied more closely to particular places and communities. He envisages the archaic Panathenaia as including the Epic Cycle alongside the repertoires later recognized as the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, whereas its classical phase featured only Homeric epic; he also characterizes the Cycle’s sequencing as less continuous than that of the Homeric poems.[54][36]

Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles') accepts that Nagy’s Panhellenic/local distinction is analytically useful but warns against turning it into an absolute division.[17] The transmitted *Nostoi*, for example, appears to offer a Panhellenic perspective, and local traditions could cross-pollinate within a larger Panhellenic Trojan-War tradition.

Rousseau similarly regards Nagy’s account as illuminating but argues that Panhellenization includes positive incorporation and reconfiguration of inherited material, not merely the elimination of local matter.[10][44]

Nagy’s interpretation of Achilles illustrates the consequences of this model. He reads Patroclus as Achilles’ ritual and narrative surrogate: Patroclus’ death at Apollo’s hands in *Iliad* 16 prefigures Achilles’ death beyond the poem. The incomplete shared tomb of Patroclus and Achilles in *Iliad* 23 is correspondingly completed only after Achilles’ death, as described in *Odyssey* 24. These are not, in Nagy’s analysis, bare references to a separate Cyclic plot; they are signs of an implicit Homeric language of hero cult, the ritual honouring of dead heroes.[13]

Nagy thus treats Homeric reticence about Achilles’ posthumous fate not as evidence that cult or immortalization traditions were absent, but as a Panhellenic shading-over of local cult language. His reading of *sēma*, meaning both sign and tomb, in *Iliad* 23 makes the term a cue to death, continued life, and hero cult; similarly, Hector’s inaccurate prediction in *Iliad* 7 of a tomb for the best Achaean receives long-range fulfilment in Achilles’ tomb.[13] **Nagy’s model therefore permits the *Iliad* to evoke Achilles’ death and cultic future without making those evocations evidence of direct dependence on a particular Cyclic text.**

This account does not make Nagy indifferent to Cyclic narratives. He reads Nestor’s *sēma* for Antilochus in *Iliad* 23 as pointing both to the immediate chariot race and to the later story, known from the *Aethiopis* summary and Pindar, in which Antilochus dies while saving Nestor. The relationship is one of traditional resonance across the wider epic continuum, not a claim that an Iliadic passage quotes a fixed *Aethiopis*.[13] What follows is that Nagy’s model can recognize external epic horizons while rejecting neoanalytic claims of demonstrable textual direction.

Nagy restated this position on 22 November 2021: oral poetic traditions shaped both the Homeric poems and the Cycle, and the Cycle’s traditions cannot be detached from their Homeric counterparts. In his account, composition and reception coincide in performance, so that each performance recomposes the poem; during the archaic period, from the eighth through the sixth centuries BCE, Homeric, Cyclic, and Hesiodic poetry circulated through oral poetics rather than widespread written dissemination. He distinguishes subsequent stages of writing down such poetry as transcript, script, and scripture.[55]

Nagy consequently historicizes the category Epic Cycle itself. He argues that kúklos originally denoted all poetry composed by Homer, including what are now called the Iliad and Odyssey, and that the distinction of those poems from a non-Homeric Cycle emerged gradually and is clearest in fourth-century BCE sources such as Aristotle’s Poetics. His performance history places prototypical Iliad and Odyssey traditions at the Panionia in the late eighth and early seventh centuries BCE, while the Panathenaic Regulation toward the end of the sixth century BCE made the Iliad and Odyssey Athens’ sole epic-performance repertoire.[55]

This historical account sharpens Nagy’s claim about Cyclic parallels: he treats a Homeric–Cyclic connection as a diachronic, evolutionary cross-reference between traditions of composition-in-performance, not as one textual passage referring to another. In particular, he maintains that orally transmitted reference reaches analogous contexts heard in earlier performances as well as the immediate context.[55]

Nagy applies this approach to the Demodokos episode in Odyssey 8. He reads the first song (Odyssey 8.72–83) as a micro-Iliad: Achilles and Odysseus quarrel, Zeus’s plans are engaged, and Apollo participates, paralleling the macro-Iliad’s quarrel of Achilles and Agamemnon, Zeus’s plan, and Apollo’s role. He rejects Martin West’s proposal that the oracle refers instead to the Iliadic Achilles–Agamemnon quarrel, possibly from a Cypria narrative, with Odysseus substituted for Agamemnon. For Nagy, the third Demodokos song, on the Wooden Horse and Troy’s capture by Odyssean intelligence, is morphologically parallel to the Cyclic Iliou Persis; ring composition links its recurrent themes of pain to the first song and produces the effect of a cycle.[55]

Nagy accordingly describes the first and third Demodokos songs as a general epic form exemplified by the Cycle, set against the special epic form of the Iliad and Odyssey. He associates the Cyclic form with metábasis, a forward shift to a later starting point, and contrasts it with the regulated continuation of Iliadic and Odyssean performance at the Panathenaia from the late sixth century BCE onward.[55]

Nagy’s oralism also governs his use of manuscript evidence. The Homer Multitext presents the Iliad and Odyssey as multiform traditions and records variants in their historical settings rather than subordinating them to a single Urtext, or reconstructed original text. Nagy identifies the tenth-century CE Venetus A as a particularly informative collection of Homeric scholia, whose images are available through that project; he treats non-Koine variants that fit formulaic diction as products of an earlier phase of Homeric oral tradition, while the Athenian tradition of the fifth and fourth centuries BCE and late sixth-century BCE was more tightly regulated and Aristarchus’s Koine became the basis of the medieval vulgate.[56]

This is an editorial consequence of Nagy’s oral-performance model: Homeric variants are historical forms of the tradition, not simply errors, and the Homer Multitext supplies texts, images, machine-readable indices, and tools for synchronic and diachronic comparison.[56]

Comparative poetics gives Nagy a further account of epic totality without reducing it to a fixed Cyclic text. He connects kúklos, understood through the chariot-wheel metaphor of a well-made song, with an explicitly attested Indo-European poetic metaphor in Rig-Veda 1.130.6; he also compares Greek poetic craft to the work of the téktōn, the master carpenter or joiner. He proposes that Hómēros itself means the one who fits or joins together.[55]

Nagy also uses comparative mythology to interpret Achilles rather than to derive Achilles’ fate from a Cyclic source. He argues that Greek myths of Apollo and Achilles, including their names and Homeric roles, have an Indo-European background, and his broader comparison places Achilles and Odysseus beside Gilgamesh and Enkidu, Aeneas, and Arjuna while retaining differences among the heroes.[57][58]

Nagy’s major formulations of this diachronic model include *Greek Mythology and Poetics* (1990), *Poetry as Performance* (1996), and *Homer the Preclassic* (2010). His comparative Indo-European analysis of Achilles’ choice in *Iliad* 9 treats *kleos aphthiton*, imperishable fame, as an inherited Greek–Indo-Iranian formula and ideological complex; this establishes the traditional depth of Achilles’ fate, not dependence on the *Cypria*.

Nagy’s reading of the proposed Cypria material at *Iliad* 1 begins with *Dios boulē* at 1.5, which he normally translates as the Will of Zeus.[59] In his May 2016 formulation, the Iliad’s and *Cypria*’s divergent plots are distinct plans that converge in Zeus’s single Will: both poems foreground mass death among Achaeans and Trojans, while the *Cypria* makes Earth’s relief from human overpopulation explicit.[59] The Zeus–Thetis scene therefore has both the immediate function of securing honour for Achilles after Agamemnon’s insult and a wider mythic horizon in which Thetis’s marriage to Peleus and Achilles’ birth belong to Zeus’s plan for the heroic generation; Nagy reads these levels as elements of the Will of Zeus rather than as proof of textual priority.[47][59]

On Achilles’ death, Nagy makes the larger traditional horizon internal to Iliadic poetics: Patroclus’ death with Apollo’s active help prefigures Achilles’ offstage death at the hands of Paris with Apollo’s active help.[55] Achilles’ awareness is therefore a development in heroic identity: he achieves maturity or seasonality when he comes to terms with his impending death, choosing kleos, both glory and the epic song that conveys it, over life.

Nagy calls Achilles pan-a-(h)ōr-ios, the most unseasonal of all, at Iliad 24.540, and explains his antagonism toward Apollo through their uncanny resemblance.[55][60]

For Achilles’ foreknowledge, Nagy distinguishes the poet’s theological perspective from that available to characters: Zeus’s Will is difficult for mortals, including heroes, to understand, whereas poets possess an inner theological vision.[59] Achilles’ choice of *kleos* rather than life at *Iliad* 9.413 thus establishes his acceptance of the heroic alternative, while Nagy’s account of the poem treats Achilles’ eventual immortalization as implicit.[59][58]

Nagy says that Achilles achieves maturity or seasonality when he comes to terms with his impending heroic death.[60]

### What is Foley’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

John Miles Foley calls the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* oral-derived traditional texts: works surviving as texts but shaped by oral-traditional registers and modes of reception. His key concept is *traditional referentiality*, also called traditional resonance: a formula, typical scene, or story-pattern can point beyond its immediate occurrence to a much larger body of shared tradition. Foley describes such signs as metonymic, meaning that a part—an epithet, phrase, scene, or narrative pattern—stands for a larger traditional whole.[31][33][54]

Foley prefers the description “traditional” to “oral” because his object of study is an evolving poetic language shared by performers and audiences, not a single poet or performance.[92]

For Foley, then, Homeric diction does not merely make rapid oral composition possible. It communicates accumulated associations to an audience fluent in the traditional register. Formulae, themes, and story-patterns are not inert repetitions or clichés; they can carry what Foley calls *word-power*, meaning made possible by performance and by the tradition that the performance activates. Homeric hexameter itself signals the communicative world of Greek epic, and the traditional register supplies meanings that exceed a passage’s literal surface.[54]

Applied to the Epic Cycle, Foley explicitly treats its fragments and summaries not as fixed source-texts that influenced Homer according to a literary model, but as other poems within the larger constellation of ancient Greek oral epic. Consequently, an Iliadic–Cyclic parallel may show a shared traditional pattern without proving that Homer derived one version from a particular Cyclic poem, or that either poem is primary. Foley specifically resists ranking analogous traditional patterns in epic and hymn as primary and derivative merely because they resemble one another.[33][54]

Foley’s method is not an invitation to infer unlimited meanings from every repetition. First, it requires identification of a tradition-specific register and its rules of phraseology and narrative pattern. Second, it compares multiple instances—different witnesses where available, otherwise passages within and across texts—to determine whether a repeated feature bears a recurring associative value. Third, it calibrates the result to the relevant tradition, genre, and individual text.[54]

In Foley’s account, traditional stability lies chiefly in themes and story-patterns, not in verbatim wording or every incidental detail.[20]

Foley’s model allows themes and narrative outlines to remain relatively stable even when wording, names, episode order, and non-essential details vary.[20]

The result is an account of the Iliad–Cycle relationship that shifts attention from origin to reception. Instead of asking primarily whether the *Iliad* borrowed a Cyclic episode, the Foleyan question asks how Homeric wording or structure could evoke a larger Trojan-War repertoire for a tradition-competent audience. A surviving Cyclic summary can help reconstruct that repertoire, but it cannot automatically be treated as the precise source of an Iliadic scene.[33][54]

Foley nevertheless does not posit an absolute opposition between oral performance and written transmission. A surviving text is not identical with a performance, and later readers cannot recover the complete performance arena, including voice, pauses, music, gesture, or the full competence of ancient audiences. Yet texts can retain rhetorical traces of oral tradition: formulaic phraseology, themes, story-patterns, and other signals may preserve a reduced but interpretable traditional register.[54]

This makes Foley more flexible about textuality than a caricature of oral theory suggests. He rejects the old “Great Divide” between orality and literacy and argues for a spectrum on which oral and written strategies coexist. His proposed *syncretic poetics*—a combined approach—requires interpreters to consider traditional oral and specifically textual or intertextual strategies together rather than flattening an oral-derived work into either purely literary or purely oral signification.[54]

The disagreement with Currie (2021, Trends in Classics, 'Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician') concerns explanatory priority.[30] Currie characterizes Foley’s position as holding that Homeric poetry characteristically uses traditional referentiality rather than literary-style allusion, whereas Currie argues that traditional referentiality and specific allusion can coexist.

Foley’s own syncretic model allows textuality to matter, but it does not license a directional source claim from a parallel alone.[31][54] **Foley’s approach therefore makes shared tradition the default explanation for Iliad–Cycle resemblance, while requiring a distinct demonstration before an interpreter posits a particular source or one-way allusion.**

Foley developed traditional referentiality most fully in *The Immanent Art* (1991) and *Homer’s Traditional Art* (1999). On this account, prophecy, Thetis’ lament, Achilles’ choice, and anticipations of Troy’s fall can evoke a traditional story-world larger than the *Iliad*’s present action; in Iliad 1 and 24 the Zeus–Thetis material may similarly activate inherited patterns of divine counsel, mediation, mortality, and reciprocal obligation without identifying the evoked background with a fixed *Cypria*.

### What is Bakker’s methodological and interpretive account of the relationship

Egbert Bakker’s oralist approach begins not from a catalogue of detachable formulas but from Homeric discourse as *poetry in speech*: elevated speech shaped by rhythm, syntax, phrase-building, particles, and discourse markers. His work treats Homeric hexameter as developing from ordinary speech and analyzes Homeric discourse as processual—in motion—rather than as a written chain of logically related sentences.[36][61][62]

This matters for the Iliad–Cycle issue because it resists a mechanical model in which repeated wording automatically proves copying. For Bakker, formulaic language is not a stockpile used without thought. Formula use depends on a singer’s and audience’s judgment that two contexts are similar; its recurrence is meaningful because it recognizes, constructs, or tests an analogy between situations.[63]

Bakker’s term *interformularity* names this graded relation among formulas. On his scale, the more specific a formula and the more restricted its distribution, the greater the potential for performers and audiences to notice its recurrence and for that recurrence to signal meaningful similarity. But the scale does not tell a modern scholar how probable allusion or quotation is, and a rare phrase may simply be under-attested in the surviving record.[34][63]

Bakker also emphasizes the memorability of contexts: formulas can be more or less closely associated with memorable situations, allowing a singer to recognize similarities between scenes without quoting an earlier poem.[50]

Bakker thus leaves open the possibility that Homeric recurrence can be pointed and significant. His approach does not reduce all resemblance to generic similarity, as his reading of the *Odyssey*’s final battle in relation to Achilles’ return to battle demonstrates: the relation can cast Odysseus in an Achilles-like heroic role. But the evidentiary logic is different from neoanalysis. Interformularity identifies perceived contextual affinity within a living traditional system; it does not, on its own, establish that one determinate poem supplied another’s source.[64][34][63]

In relation to the Cycle, Bakker’s recorded contribution is therefore methodological rather than a detailed reconstruction of the *Cypria*’s influence on particular Iliadic scenes. His general reassessment of Homeric intertextuality and intratextuality supports treating repetitions as potentially meaningful within oral tradition, while withholding a source–recipient conclusion unless further evidence establishes it.[64][34][63]

Bakker also differs from Foley on a particular issue of transmission. Currie reports that Foley, following Lord, argued that the Homeric poems arose through dictation on an external stimulus; Bakker criticized this inference. Bakker’s criticism does not reject Homeric orality. It rejects the claim that calling the poems oral-derived entails one necessary scenario for their recording.[31] **Bakker’s oralism consequently directs interpretation toward discourse, contextual similarity, and graded recurrence, while leaving specific Iliad–Cycle dependence as a further claim requiring evidence beyond formulaic resemblance.**

Bakker’s performance-pragmatic account is developed especially in *Poetry in Speech* (1997) and *Pointing at the Past: From Formula to Performance in Homeric Poetics* (2005). His analysis of particles, deixis, and narrative communication requires Iliadic pointers to Achilles’ fate, Zeus’ plan, and Thetis’ mediation to be read first as acts of performed narration rather than as citations of a fixed external poem.

### Synthesis —

Nagy, Foley, and Bakker agree that the *Iliad* and Cyclic poems belong within a broader and changing field of Greek epic tradition, so shared motifs are not automatically evidence of a fixed *Cypria* source. Nagy explains their differentiation historically through crystallization, Panhellenic selection, and diachronic cross-reference; Foley explains their communicative relation through traditional referentiality and oral-derived textual rhetoric; Bakker explains meaningful recurrence through discourse and interformularity.[40][54][33][34][63]

Their shared conclusion is stronger than a generic appeal to orality: Homeric–Cyclic parallels can be meaningful and can evoke external narrative horizons. Their disagreement with neoanalysis concerns what such parallels establish. Neoanalysis may infer a specific inherited motif and adapted narrative context; oralist methods require that direction, source, and textual priority be separately demonstrated rather than derived from resemblance itself.[10][39][54][63]

## The disputed Iliad passages

The passages at issue show why the debate cannot be reduced to whether Homer knew the Trojan-War story outside the *Iliad*. Both approaches accept that the poem activates such knowledge. They disagree over the mechanism: whether a distinctive detail permits reconstruction of a prior Cypria-like poem or whether it works through a shared, variable repertoire of stories, formulas, and performance patterns.

### How does each school interpret the proposed Cypria motifs in

The principal Cypria-related cluster in *Iliad* 1 comprises the proem’s Διὸς βουλή, Zeus’s plan (1.5); the prior allocation of Briseis to Achilles and Chryseis to Agamemnon; and Zeus’s ensuing support of Troy while Achilles is absent. Proclus’ Cypria summary likewise has Achilles receive Briseis, Agamemnon Chryseis, and Zeus plan to help the Trojans by detaching Achilles from the Greek force.[10][2]

Currie (2021, *Trends in Classics*, *Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician*) treats the verbal convergence between *Iliad* 1.5 and Cypria fragment 1.6–7 Bernabé—heroes dying at Troy as Zeus’s plan is fulfilled—as a potentially deliberate macro-allusion. Nagy instead calls Διὸς βουλή the Will of Zeus and treats it as the plot-shaping principle of each epic: the *Iliad*’s Will is coextensive with the *Iliad*’s plot, the *Cypria*’s with the Trojan-War plot, and their divergent plots converge in the loss of lives on both sides.[31][59] On this reading, the restricted story of Achilles’ wrath is modelled against the whole Trojan War and its destruction of heroes; Currie nevertheless expressly leaves open whether the Cypria preserves the earlier context, the *Iliad* does, or both draw on a conventional hexameter expression of Zeus’s will.[31]

Kullmann reads the compressed plan of Zeus in *Iliad* 1.5 against the fuller Cyprian plan for war and heroic destruction, but argues that the *Iliad* refers to an older oral tradition that also inspired the *Cypria*, not necessarily to the surviving poem.[13]

Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles') reaches a different neoanalytic conclusion.[17] He holds that the Achilles–Agamemnon quarrel was traditional and that the Cypria and *Iliad* independently narrated slightly different versions.

The functional difference matters: in the Cypria summary the quarrel and Achilles’ removal serve Zeus’s aim of aiding Troy; in *Iliad* 1 Zeus responds after the quarrel to Thetis’s request to honour Achilles, with Trojan success as the means rather than the end.[10] Burgess therefore permits *Iliad* 1.5 to denote the Iliad’s immediate plan while connoting the wider Cyclic plan to reduce humanity.[10][2]

Tsagalis (2025, Scripta Classica Israelica, 'Proemial Bridges: An Intratextual Association Activating an Intertextual Reference') also reads the junction as meaningful transformation rather than simple duplication.[41] He describes the Cypria–*Iliad* relation as a compression of the broad relief-of-earth motif into the Iliadic objective of satisfying Achilles through temporary Trojan victory. His method of oral intertextuality allows the *Iliad* to engage particular traditional narratives while reshaping them for its own tragic economy.[13][52]

Tsagalis identifies the *Dios boulē*, Zeus’s plan, as a pre-Homeric motif absorbed and given a new function in the *Iliad*’s plot of Achilles’ wrath.[89]

For Tsagalis, the inherited Will-of-Zeus motif is refunctionalized so that Zeus’s assistance to the Trojans becomes an expression of the *Iliad*’s governing theme, Achilles’ *mēnis* or wrath.[89]

The oralist alternative, especially Foley’s traditional referentiality, understands formulas, type-scenes, and story patterns through their accumulated uses across the tradition. A knowledgeable audience can hear Zeus’s plan, Briseis’ loss, and Aulis-like impediments as resonant with wider Trojan-War material without inferring dependence on a fixed Cypria text.[34][19] Nagy’s performance-centered language of Homeric cross-reference similarly shifts the question from poem-to-poem borrowing to engagement with a Cyclic tradition of performance.[36]

Further proposals intensify the difference. Nelson (2022, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 152, *Iphigenia in the Iliad and the Architecture of Homeric Allusion*) argues that *Iliad* 1.8–487 sustains an allusive dialogue with the Iphigenia-at-Aulis narrative: Agamemnon offends a god, Calchas identifies divine anger, the expedition is obstructed, and the dispute over Chryseis and her return to her father replay and rework the sacrifice story.[65] Nelson (2023, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'Markers of Allusion in Archaic Greek Poetry')’s proposal makes the opening a sustained reworking of traditional material, not a demonstration that Homer copied the surviving Cypria.[32][28] The same applies to suggested Tenedos allusions: Chryses’ mention of Tenedos at 1.38 may recall Achilles’ killing of Apollo’s son Tennes, and Agamemnon’s claim that strife is dear to Achilles may recall an earlier quarrel at Tenedos.[11]

Nelson describes the Iphigenia parallel as a *fabula*, a recognizable sequence of core events rather than recoverable Cypria wording. Among the correspondences he identifies are Achilles’ associated loss of a woman, Odysseus’ conduct of the woman to her father at an altar, the ensuing sacrifice, and a favourable wind for the Greeks.[32]

On a Foleyan reading, the Apollo-plague sequence can evoke the wider Aulis and Iphigenia pattern without implying that a particular source poem supplied it.[92]

**The evidence makes Iliad 1 a strong case for engagement with wider Trojan-War tradition, but not for direct dependence on a determinate Cypria text.** Currie sees potentially pointed macro-allusion; Burgess accepts allusive contact while denying textual priority; Foley and Nagy explain the same intelligibility through traditional resonance and performance.[31][10][34]

The clearest Cypria-related passage in *Iliad* 24 is 24.25–30, the poem’s sole explicit reference to the Judgement of Paris. Hera and Athena continue to hate Troy because Paris insulted the goddesses and favoured the deity who granted him destructive sexual desire. The Cypria summary likewise places the Judgement after Eris’s quarrel at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis and Aphrodite’s promise of Helen.[66][15]

The *Iliad*’s narrated action spans fifty-one days, while its reference to Paris’ judgement presupposes a much longer Trojan story.[2]

For neoanalysis, such compressed references can be traces of a fuller narrative outside the poem. Motif transference is the method of identifying a motif that appears more fully or naturally situated in another account and inferring that Homer has redeployed it.[34] Kullmann accordingly takes Helen’s statement that she left home twenty years earlier at 24.765–766, alongside Calchas’s words in Book 1, as an allusion to the Teuthranian expedition.[13]

Kullmann’s *Faktenkanon* holds that early epics could preserve fundamental traditional events even when they suppressed, substituted, or radically reinterpreted them; on this account, Helen’s twenty-year chronology retains an otherwise unnarrated event of the war’s prehistory.[13]

Kullmann reads *Iliad* 1.106–110 as retaining a suppressed reference to the oracle and sacrifice associated with the failed first expedition from Aulis. He also connects Helen’s twenty-year interval at 24.765–766 with that omitted expedition.[13]

Burgess accepts the Judgement and Andromache’s forecast of Astyanax’s death as recognisable pre-Iliadic myth. He argues, however, that Astyanax’s presence in later Cycle narratives need reflect his traditional status rather than Homeric influence or proof that the *Iliad* borrowed from a fixed Cypria.[2] His oral, intertextual neoanalysis thus preserves the idea of specific inherited *fabulae*—recognisable story cores—while refusing to infer a stable source-poem behind every Homeric resonance.[34]

Foley’s approach clarifies why Book 24 does not require a Cypria source even where it unmistakably opens onto the larger war. He reads the delayed reconciliation and meal of Priam and Achilles as the culmination of a traditional sequence: movement from lament and refusal through purification and feast to mediation.[54] The forward reach of Book 24 similarly depends on audience knowledge of Priam’s impending losses, Troy’s fall, and Astyanax’s death, but that knowledge can arise from a performed repertoire rather than one textual predecessor.[50]

Several relevant Book-24 motifs are not Cypria motifs in the strict sense. Andromache’s prospect of Astyanax’s death points toward the *Little Iliad* and *Iliou Persis* tradition; Priam’s supplication has only conjecturally been assigned also to the *Aethiopis*.[34][5] Aristarchus athetized the Judgement passage, while most modern scholars accept it; West deletes 24.29–30 as rhapsodic interpolation.[6]

Book 24 supports the proposition that the Iliad presupposes a broad Trojan-War horizon, but it does not establish Cypria-to-Iliad borrowing. The competing explanation is between neoanalytic reconstruction from compressed traces and oral-traditional resonance in a flexible mythic repertoire.[34][6]

The closest surviving Cypria parallel to Iliad 1 is the ordered sequence in which Chryses ransoms Chryseis, Agamemnon refuses, Apollo sends plague, the girl is restored, and Agamemnon’s compensation claim ends in his taking Briseis. Bakker’s performance-oriented approach treats that sequence as a realization of a shared narrative repertoire, not evidence that the Iliad adapted a fixed Cypria text.

West (2013) supplies an important counter-direction to source-oriented neoanalysis. He holds that the concept of the Διὸς βουλή was common epic property, but that the Cypria proem’s Διὸς δ᾿ ἐτελείετο βουλή imitates *Iliad* 1.5; he likewise identifies the Cypria’s Tenedos quarrel as a μῆνις duplicating the Iliadic anger and as an innovation.[7] West also treats the allocation of Briseis and Chryseis as preparation for the Iliad while identifying Cypria borrowings from the *Iliad*, including the Tenedos quarrel, the Achaeans’ wish to sail home, and scenes in which Thetis advises Achilles or fulfils his requests.[7] This reverse-dependence model sharpens Burgess’s objection to treating the apparent junction as a designed textual handoff.[45][7]

West adds a compositional alternative to both a simple prequel model and a model of wholly independent poems: the *Cypria* was shaped, whether by incorporating smaller epics or by later redaction, to align with Iliadic presuppositions, notably the allocation of Chryseis and Briseis and the coalition configuration. This model makes Cyprian-Iliadic agreement compatible with Cyprian adaptation to Homeric material.[45]

Nagy’s oral-traditional account gives the Διὸς βουλή a stronger cosmic coherence than the preceding formulation makes explicit: the *Iliad* and *Cypria* are convergent expressions of the larger Will or Plan of Zeus, whose object is the destruction of many heroes. The Iliad’s locally activated plan therefore participates in, rather than necessarily borrows from, that overarching epic theology.[47]

At *Iliad* 24.58–63 Zeus recalls the marriage of Peleus and Thetis, the wedding at which the Cypria places Eris’s intervention and the beginning of the sequence leading to Paris’s Judgement; it is therefore a second Book-24 opening-of-the-war resonance, alongside 24.25–30.[44]

The Cypria background itself contains incompatible local versions of the Briseis material: a scholion to *Iliad* 16.57 assigns her capture to Pedasos to the Cyprian poets, whereas the *Iliad* places it at Lyrnessos; a scholion to *Iliad* 1.366 attributes Chryseis’ presence at Thebe to her attendance at an Artemis sacrifice with Eetion’s sister.[7] These variants fit Burgess’s account of a traditional quarrel and prizes circulating in forms that differ from the Iliadic narrative.[45][7]

West (2013) also connects Agamemnon’s accusation that Calchas always prophesies disaster at *Iliad* 1.106–8 with the Cypria’s Iphigeneia sacrifice, furnishing a more specific Cypria-oriented basis for the proposed Aulis echo.[7]

Nelson’s 2026 reconstruction makes the Tenedos material more specific. Proclus’ *Cypria* summary has Achilles quarrel with Agamemnon after a late invitation at Tenedos; Apollodorus preserves Thetis’ warning that killing Tennes would bring Achilles’ death at Apollo’s hand. Nelson reads Chryses’ naming of Tenedos at *Iliad* 1.38, which ancient A and D scholia connect with Tennes, and Agamemnon’s claim that strife is dear to Achilles at 1.177 as activation of this pre-Iliadic complex. He also identifies the Cyprian Tenedos sacrifice and feast as structurally parallel to the Chryse sacrifice and feast in *Iliad* 1.[67]

This reconstruction sharpens the contrast over Achilles’ fate. Nelson places the killing of Tennes within a Cyprian sequence that supplies an aition, a narrative cause, for Apollo’s hostility and participation in Achilles’ death; Ward’s formulation makes the *Cypria*’s fatal certainty oppose the initially open ethical choice of *Iliad* 9.410–416. Nagy reads that Iliadic choice as Achilles’ choice of *kleos*, glory as conferred by epic song, over life.[67][58]

Rousseau distinguishes the interpretation that identifies the proem’s Διὸς βουλή with the Cypria-associated plan to end the generation of heroes through war from the Aristarchean interpretation that identifies it with Zeus’s Book-2 plan to fulfil his promise to Thetis.[44]

The ancient local interpretation of *Dios boulē* as Thetis’ request in Book 1 was associated with Aristophanes as well as Aristarchus.[8][2][47]

### How does each school treat Achilles’ awareness of his own

Burgess argues that Achilles is the only major Iliadic character with foreknowledge of his own death. Thetis has informed him before the poem’s dramatic present that his life will be short, though the poem distributes the information across general warnings, Achilles’ two fates, Apollo’s weapons, Xanthus’ prophecy, Hector’s prediction of Paris and Apollo at the Skaian gates, and Patroclus’ shade.[29][11] Burgess distinguishes this character-level knowledge from the audience’s fuller traditional knowledge that Achilles will kill Memnon and then die at Troy through Apollo and Paris.[29]

In Book 1 Thetis calls Achilles *ōkymoros*, “doomed to an early death”; later, Achilles identifies Apollo’s arrows beneath Troy’s walls, his horses predict death “by god and man,” and Patroclus’ ghost says that Achilles too will die beneath the Trojan wall.[11][38]

Burgess argues that, because other Iliadic statements make Achilles’ death fixed, the alternative fates in Book 9 create an “illusion of uncertainty” for characterization rather than a genuinely open future.[93]

This is neoanalysis in a modified form. Burgess does not claim that the *Iliad* copied the plot of the *Aethiopis*. Rather, he argues that the Iliad allusively transfers elements of Achilles’ later death-story into Patroclus’ story: combat with a foreign defender, Apollo’s complicity in a death, funeral lament, and funeral games. Patroclus thereby becomes an *altera persona*, another version, of Achilles.[29]

Burgess reads Thetis and the Nereids’ lament for the still-living Achilles in *Iliad* 18 as transferred material from his future funeral, integrated into the immediate mourning for Patroclus.[38]

Tsagalis and more source-oriented neoanalysis give greater weight to the displacement. Thetis’ warning in 18.95–96 that Achilles will die immediately after Hector seems, on this approach, more fitting before Achilles’ later fight with Memnon, after which he dies in the Aethiopic story.[13] The same logic underlies comparisons between Patroclus’ death and Achilles’ later death: both occur by Troy’s walls and involve a mortal killer assisted by Apollo.[10][39]

Tsagalis allows multiple overlapping correspondences in the Patroclus narrative rather than mapping Patroclus onto Antilochus, or Achilles onto a single prior figure, one-to-one.[38]

Currie argues that *Iliad* 18.96 simultaneously “quotes” the *Memnonis* and negates its plot: Thetis’ prediction evokes an expected continuation that the *Iliad* does not narrate.[70]

Nagy’s oral-traditional reading reaches the future of Achilles through structural patterning rather than a reconstructed source. Patroclus is Achilles’ ritual substitute and narrative surrogate: his Apollo-caused death prefigures Achilles’ death beyond the poem, while the incompletely constructed common tomb in *Iliad* 23 anticipates its completion after Achilles’ death in *Odyssey* 24.[13] Nagy’s cult-oriented account makes Achilles’ mortality and posthumous status integral to the Iliad’s architecture, even though the hero’s death lies outside its narrated time.[13]

Foley’s pattern-based analysis supports this kind of explanation. His work identifies recurrent sequences of suspicion of death, recognition, self-defilement, lament, purification, and mediation; such patterns can explain anticipatory grief and mourning without treating every unusual feature as residue from another epic.[54] Kelly (2012, De Gruyter eBooks, 'The Mourning of Thetis: ‘Allusion’ and the Future in the Iliad')’s oralist critique makes the point directly: Thetis’ mourning in *Iliad* 18 is prospective lamentation, a recurrent Homeric sequence, rather than necessarily transferred material from the *Aethiopis*.[42][13]

The scholion on *Iliad* 1.131 preserves the famous choice between long, obscure life in Phthia and brief, glorious life at Troy. It says Thetis learned this from Zeus and hid Achilles at Scyros; some scholars have assigned the material to the Cypria, but the scholion attributes the knowledge explicitly to Thetis, not Achilles.[4]

**Both approaches accept that Achilles is death-bound and that the poem makes his fate present; they differ over whether its anticipations reconstruct an external Achilles-death narrative or arise primarily from Iliadic surrogate, lament, tomb, and cult patterns.**[29][13]

Achilles himself states the alternative at Iliad 9.410–416: return to Phthia for a long life without fame, or remain at Troy for imperishable fame and an early death. After Patroclus’ death he says that his own death follows Hector’s, and when Xanthus predicts his approaching day of death at 19.416–417 he answers that he already knows it. Bakker accordingly treats these recurrent reorientations from the performed present toward foreknown consequences as an oral-poetic procedure, not as proof of consultation of the Aethiopis, where Achilles’ death is narrated.

Burgess calls the apparent choice between Achilles’ two fates in *Iliad* 9 an “illusion of uncertainty,” because other Iliadic passages present his death as already determined.[94][93]

### How does each school interpret the Zeus-Thetis scene?

In *Iliad* 1, Achilles invokes Thetis’ prior rescue of Zeus from an attempted Olympian binding, and Thetis then wins Zeus’s irreversible nod: Trojan success will compel the Achaeans to restore Achilles’ honour.[38][68] In *Iliad* 24, the relation reverses: Zeus sends Thetis as his messenger to command Achilles to release Hector, and Achilles accepts Zeus’s order.[69]

In her formal supplication at *Iliad* 1.493–533, Thetis sits before Zeus, grasps his knees and takes his chin as she asks him to restore her short-lived son’s honour (*timē*) by temporarily favouring the Trojans.[95]

Burgess treats the Cypria and *Iliad* as related but functionally distinct. The Cypria has Zeus deliberate with Themis about the Trojan War and later plan to aid Troy through Achilles’ removal; *Iliad* 1 presents a reactive supplication after Achilles’ quarrel.[2][10][44] This distinction rules out equating the Cypria’s pre-war divine plan with the Iliadic bargain between Zeus and Thetis.[10]

Tsagalis identifies a closer transformational relationship. He sees the Iliad as converting the broad Zeus-plan of population reduction into Achilles’ personal satisfaction through Trojan success. His reconstruction of the Cypria’s Achilles–Helen scene likewise has Thetis mediate between Achilles and Zeus, after which Zeus orders Aphrodite to bring Helen to Achilles.[13][12]

Tsagalis reads Thetis’ supplication as the first step in fulfilling Achilles’ will: it translates Achilles’ *mēnis*, or wrath, into Zeus’ plot.[96]

Foley’s traditional referentiality and Nagy’s performance model allow the scene to carry mythic depth without requiring source dependence. Foley presents Slatkin’s Thetis as a figure whose latent traditional power exceeds her immediate dramatic role; her ability to affect Olympian stability is conveyed by oblique cues intelligible to a tradition-competent audience.[33] Thetis’ continuing grief for Achilles’ approaching death also motivates her appearances within the Iliad itself.[70]

Slatkin relates Thetis’ leverage over Zeus to divine-succession traditions, including a prophecy that her son would exceed his father; she reads the *Iliad*’s reticence about this alternative as part of its Panhellenic and Olympian ordering.[94][32]

The Cypria tradition is not stable enough to be treated as a simple counterpart. Proclus’ manuscripts read that Zeus planned with Thetis, while modern editors print Themis on papyrological grounds; P.Oxy. 3829’s related account has Zeus judge the heroic race impious and plan with Themis to destroy it, a motive absent from Proclus’ summary.[8] A separate Cypria fragment says Thetis avoided union with Zeus to please Hera, prompting Zeus to decree her marriage to a mortal.[71]

**The Zeus–Thetis scene is therefore not a Cypria episode transplanted into the Iliad: it is an Iliadic refunctionalization of larger Zeus-plan and Thetis traditions.** Burgess stresses independent traditional variants, Tsagalis stresses compression and transformation, and oralists stress traditional resonance and performance-based meaning.[10][13][34]

The first encounter, at Iliad 1.493–530, authorizes the consequences of Achilles’ withdrawal; the second, at 24.100–119, authorizes the reversal concerning Hector’s corpse. Their deliberate framing relation is central to the oralist case that Book 24 completes an internal Iliadic design rather than reproducing an episode extracted from the Cypria.

### Synthesis —

Taken together, these passages show a real but qualified divide. Currie and Tsagalis are read most fruitfully as arguing for purposeful, often highly structured engagement with prior Trojan-War narratives; Burgess accepts such engagement but replaces dependence on a fixed source with oral, intertextual neoanalysis. Nagy and Foley explain the same density of reference through multiform tradition, traditional referentiality, and audience competence.[31][34][52] The strongest common ground is that *Iliad* 1 and 24 make sense against stories beyond their immediate action.

The unresolved issue is whether a particular correspondence—especially Zeus’s plan, Achilles’ fate, or a funeral-like lament—identifies a recoverable prior poem or only a traditional story-pattern; that question turns on the specificity of each correspondence, not on a general opposition between oral poetry and allusion.[34][13]

The evidence is asymmetrical: Iliad 1 has a close parallel to the Cypria argument, whereas no surviving Cypria summary narrates Priam’s supplication or the ransom of Hector in Iliad 24. The Cypria concerns the war’s origins and early expedition, while the Aethiopis begins after Hector’s burial; Book 24’s relevant correspondences are therefore wider Trojan-War and cyclic resonances rather than a comparably secure Cypria narrative parallel.

## Competing uses of ancient and comparative evidence

The dispute is not over whether the *Iliad* and the Epic Cycle share stories, phrases, and characters. They plainly do. It concerns what a resemblance licenses us to infer: a deliberate allusion to an earlier, identifiable poem; a transformation of a pre-Homeric narrative sequence; or a resonance within a broad, variable tradition of performance.

### How do the schools use the Bernabé fragments

Alberto Bernabé’s *Poetae Epici Graeci* (1987) is an edition of testimonia and fragments, not a recovered continuous *Cypria*. Its arrangement and numbering reconstruct a poem from quotations, scholia, and prose reports; Martin Davies’s 1988 edition differs both in which fragments it accepts and in their order. Bernabé himself reordered material partly in relation to Proclus’ summaries. The other Bernabé numbers invoked here are likewise differently transmitted items: fr. 19 is the Scyros dossier assembled from scholia on *Iliad* 19.326 and 9.668, P.Berol. 13930, Ptolemaeus Chennus, Pausanias, Homeric passages, and Proclus’ summary, and concerns the disputed attribution of Achilles’ concealment in women’s clothes; fr. 24 is a reported Cypria account naming both Iphigeneia and Iphianassa among Agamemnon’s four daughters; and fr. 28 combines a scholion’s report that Chryseis went to Thebe for an Artemis sacrifice with a testimonium that places Briseis’ capture at Pedasos rather than Iliadic Lyrnessos. They are indispensable evidence, but they are not consecutive leaves of an archaic manuscript.[12][10][4]

The focal item is *Cypria* fr. 1 Bernabé, a quotation embedded in the scholion on *Iliad* 1.5: its surviving hexameters say that Zeus planned to lighten Earth through the Trojan War and that the heroes died at Troy as Zeus’ plan was fulfilled. The same scholion supplies, but does not thereby make part of the quotation, the fuller Earth-burden, Momos, Thetis-marriage, Helen-birth, and alternative-flood-or-thunderbolt narrative.[31][13][8] Currie (2021, Trends in Classics, 'Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician') compares this wording with the *Iliad*’s proem and with *Iliad* 11.55, where Zeus plans to send many strong heads to Hades.[30] He proposes that the poet of the *Iliad* may have used traditional hexameter wording about Zeus’ destruction of heroes to signal that Achilles’ wrath is a concentrated model of the whole Trojan War.

Currie explicitly leaves open the alternatives: the *Cypria* and Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* may preserve the older context, the direction may be reversed, or the phrase may have been conventional.[31]

Neoanalysis, in its broader modern sense, explains anomalous or strategically incomplete Homeric narrative by reference to earlier Trojan-War narratives that Homer presupposes, reworks, or suppresses. On that model, the *Iliad*’s backward references to episodes later narrated in the *Cypria* are traces of an antecedent epic event-sequence, often called a *Faktenkanon*: a standard list of story-events shared by poet and audience. Currie’s more literary version seeks likely directions of influence; it can therefore treat a distinctive verbal and narrative nexus as an intentional allusion rather than merely a common motif.[12][13][49]

Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles') accepts the antiquity and importance of the underlying Trojan-War material but rejects the inference from correspondence to one-way dependence.[17] His reading of fr.

Fr. 24, a reported Cypria account, gives Agamemnon both Iphigeneia and Iphianassa among his four daughters.[10] Such mixture of overlap and divergence, Burgess argues, is better explained by independent versions within a shared mythological tradition than by a Cypria designed to fill Homeric gaps.[10][2]

Tsagalis (2012) applies this caution at the level of individual attributions. *Cypria* fr. 19 Bernabé/West is a dossier rather than a continuous Cypria extract: its transmitting evidence comprises scholia on *Iliad* 19.326 and 9.668, P.Berol. It had been used to assign Achilles’ pre-war concealment in women’s clothing on Scyros to the *Cypria*, but Tsagalis (2025, Scripta Classica Israelica, 'Proemial Bridges: An Intratextual Association Activating an Intertextual Reference') identifies several rival Scyros narratives.[41][12] He concludes that the D-scholion’s Cypria attribution most likely applies only to Neoptolemus’ birth and naming, not to the entire concealment-and-discovery narrative.[12]

Foley’s concept of traditional referentiality gives the oral-traditional counter-model its clearest terminology. A formula, type-scene, or story-pattern can evoke the accumulated associations of its prior uses without pointing to one source-text; inherited poetic language carries resonance for an experienced audience. On this account, the Bernabé dossier preserves partial, differently mediated witnesses to a many-stranded repertoire, while similarities between Homer and Cycle remain compatible with independent traditional variants.[14][34][50][12]

**The Bernabé fragments therefore support neoanalytic reconstruction of an older Trojan-War narrative horizon, but they do not decide whether the *Iliad* alludes to a particular Cypria text or activates a shared traditional repertoire.** The decisive divide is inferential: Currie treats selected verbal and structural correspondences as possible purposeful allusion, whereas Burgess and Foley’s model makes resemblance the normal result of common tradition unless more specific markers can establish a particular reprise.[31][10][19][34]

The Proclean argument of the *Cypria* also preserves the Chryses–plague–Briseis sequence, making it a particularly close Cyclic counterpart to the opening action of *Iliad* 1. Neoanalytic reconstruction treats that sequence as evidence for a Cypria-related narrative background, whereas oralist interpretation treats it as a shared traditional narrative node.

West judges Zeus’ plan to be common property among epic poets, but judges that the wording of the Cypria proem, especially its ending, looks very much like an imitation of *Iliad* 1.5.[7]

A scholion on *Iliad* 1.366 reports that the *Cypria* explained Chryseis’ presence at Thebe by saying that she attended an Artemis sacrifice with Eetion’s sister; West holds that this version prepares for the Iliadic Chryseis episode.[7]

### How do the schools use the Venetus A scholia

Venetus A, Marcianus Graecus Z. 454 (= 822), is a late tenth-century Byzantine manuscript and the oldest complete surviving *Iliad*. It transmits the *Iliad* with several layers of scholia, marginal notes assembled from ancient and later scholarship, and excerpts from Proclus’ *Chrestomathy*. Its Cycle summaries cover the poems except the *Cypria*, whose summary was missing through manuscript damage and survives in other manuscripts. Venetus A is consequently a major witness to the reception and transmission of Homer and Cyclic material, not a direct manuscript of the *Cypria*.[18][2][72]

This distinction matters especially for the scholion on *Iliad* 1.5. It embeds the brief Cypria quotation about Zeus’ plan in a much fuller account: Earth is burdened by humanity; Zeus destroys many in the Theban war; Momus proposes Thetis’ marriage to a mortal and the birth of Helen; the Trojan War then lightens Earth through mass death. That scholion has made the phrase *Dios boulē*, Zeus’ plan, central to debate about Iliad 1.5. But its motifs of human impiety and Zeus’ alternative destruction by flood or thunderbolt need not belong to the quoted *Cypria*: they may be the scholiast’s explanatory enlargement.[8][73]

Burgess uses such scholiastic material positively but not naïvely. He holds that the scholia’s Cyclic labels and their divergences from Proclus indicate more overlap between Homeric and Cyclic traditions than the conventional Homer-versus-Cycle divide admits. At the same time, he argues that Proclus’ summaries were excerpted into *Iliad* manuscripts as background for readers, not as proof that Homer and Cycle were originally one continuous mega-poem.[2][10]

The Scyros scholia expose the methodological disagreement. A D-scholion on *Iliad* 19.326 attributes Achilles’ hiding in women’s clothing to cyclic poets, although Proclus places Achilles’ stay on Scyros after the failed Teuthranian expedition. Burgess considers it plausible that the concealment episode was in the *Cypria* despite Proclus’ omission. Tsagalis instead treats the scholion’s final attribution as limited and argues that the concealment story did not belong to the *Cypria* as Proclus knew it.[6][2][12]

The scholion at *Iliad* 1.131 offers another relevant tradition: Thetis learns from Zeus that Achilles may remain in Phthia for a long but obscure life or go to Troy for brief life and lasting fame; she therefore hides him on Scyros. Some scholars have assigned this narrative to the *Cypria*, but the scholion makes Thetis—not Achilles—the recipient of Zeus’ information. It is thus evidence for a tradition of Thetis’ foreknowledge and Achilles’ alternatives, not direct proof that Achilles himself received the prophecy in the Cypria.[4]

At the opposite end of the *Iliad*, a scholion on 24.804 adds Penthesilea’s arrival after Hector’s burial. The notice has been proposed as the beginning of the *Aethiopis*, thereby making the end of *Iliad* 24 a physical seam with the next Trojan-War song. Holmberg stresses that no internal evidence establishes that identification; the line may instead be a rhapsodic or grammatical joining device, or simply a comment that expands the poem’s horizon.[5][2]

Digital diplomatic editing has sharpened this methodological issue by preserving each scholion’s wording, lemma, and physical placement rather than merging it into a synthetic edition. Venetus A’s lemmata sometimes differ from the main *Iliad* text, potentially preserving alternative textual or traditional material, while earlier editions often abbreviated, emended, or conflated scholia from several manuscripts. Nagy’s work on the scholia treats them particularly as traces of ancient reading and performance practices; that use differs from Currie’s search for textualized allusion and from Tsagalis’ passage-by-passage reconstruction.[26][25]

**The scholia support the existence of expansive ancient networks of Homeric and Cyclic interpretation, but they cannot by themselves turn a late explanatory note into an archaic Cypria passage or demonstrate direct Homeric borrowing.** Burgess extracts recoverable traditional overlap from their variants and attributions; oral-traditional approaches emphasize their layered reception history and their evidence for multiform tradition.[2][8][37]

When Bessarion acquired Venetus A, 19 of its 654 pages were missing, a loss of 2.9%; the replacement leaves restored the Iliadic text but not their scholia. This quantifies why the manuscript’s surviving annotation, including its lack of a Cypria summary, must be read as materially incomplete.[74]

The Cypria-related Zeus-plan explanation specifically has Zeus deliberate with Themis about bringing on the Trojan War. This Zeus–Themis consultation supplies a larger mythic frame against which neoanalytic readings set the *Iliad*’s Zeus–Thetis scene.

### How do the schools use comparative Indo-European poetics

Comparative Indo-European poetics studies inherited linguistic, metrical, mythic, and narrative structures across Indo-European traditions. It differs from identifying literary influence: a Greek and an Indic parallel may reflect common inheritance, later contact, or independent adaptation. In this debate, comparisons illuminate the depth and traditionality of a motif, but they do not directly establish that the *Iliad* borrowed from a Cyclic poem.[13][54][10]

Burgess deploys comparative evidence chiefly against the claim that the *Cypria*’s Zeus-plan merely expands *Iliad* 1.5. The divine decision to relieve Earth by killing heroes resembles long-standing Near Eastern and Indo-European catastrophe narratives; this makes it historically implausible to treat the Cypria motif as a simple post-Iliadic invention. Yet Burgess simultaneously explains Homeric–Cyclic verbal resemblance through oral-formulaic poetics, not quotation: similar phraseology can show common poetic tradition rather than text-to-text copying.[10]

The comparative problem remains contested. The Cypria’s population-cull has been explained either as a seventh-century BCE Mesopotamian import related to *Atra-ḫasīs*, or as inheritance from an ancient narrative also represented by the *Mahābhārata*. A newer proposal rejects that strict alternative and argues for shared ancestry through multilingual transmission in a Eurasian-Steppe cultural zone. The comparison therefore establishes that Greek epic participated in a broad field of catastrophe stories; it does not settle a single genealogy for Cypria fr. 1.[73]

Tsagalis uses Indo-European comparison more locally. In reconstructing the *Cypria* episode in which Thetis and Aphrodite arrange Achilles’ meeting with Helen, he compares Aphrodite, Thetis, and Eos as goddesses who save, remove, or transport favored mortals. Since Eos preserves features of the Indo-European dawn goddess *Ausos*, and archaic poetry transfers some such traits to Aphrodite, Tsagalis argues that Aphrodite’s role in the reconstructed scene is poetically plausible. This supports a functional reconstruction, not direct proof of the scene’s wording or of Iliadic dependence.[12]

Currie takes a more source-specific comparative route. He argues that Aphrodite seduction scenes are not merely a recurring type-scene but specific reprises of an earlier oral Aphrodite-and-Anchises poem, and he further compares that hypothetical poem with Sumerian Inanna–Dumuzi songs. This is not an Indo-European argument, and the reported critique judges the proposed Sumerian translation weak because the shared goddess-and-shepherd motif is superficial relative to the variability of the Sumerian corpus.[6][75]

Nagy’s comparative work instead supports a diachronic account of Panhellenization: the Homeric poems suppress or shade over localized hero-cult and immortalization traditions as they become Panhellenic, whereas less Panhellenic Cyclic traditions may preserve them more openly. His comparison with Indic traditions helps explain why Achilles’ special afterlife may be muted in Homer while remaining prominent in later Cyclic and local traditions. This is a model of changing traditional systems, not a claim that a single author borrowed a particular Cyclic text.[10][75][2]

Foley’s comparisons, especially with South Slavic oral epic and widely distributed return-story patterns, similarly prioritize how traditional forms make meaning in performance. He warns that South Slavic evidence is an illuminating analogy rather than a universal template, and that geographical distribution of a story-type does not compel Proto-Indo-European inheritance because contact can also transmit narratives. Bakker’s oralist emphasis on discourse and performance is compatible with this methodological restraint, but the evidence here does not document a distinctive Bakker intervention on the Cypria’s Zeus-plan or the specific Iliadic passages at issue.[33][54]

**Comparative poetics most strongly demonstrates that Zeus’ plan, divine mediation, hero substitution, and immortality motifs have histories larger than any surviving Cyclic poem; it does not choose between Neoanalysis and oralism on its own.** Neoanalytic arguments can use comparative depth to defend the independence or priority of a reconstructed motif, while oralist arguments use it to explain recurrence without inferring a one-to-one source relation.[10][12][34][13]

In *The Best of the Achaeans* (1979), Nagy compares the Iliadic phrase *kleos aphthiton*, imperishable fame, with Indo-Iranian and especially Vedic ideals of imperishable fame. The comparison makes Achilles’ choice of fame inseparable from his death and posthumous song a traditional heroic pattern rather than a narrative irregularity requiring Cyclic explanation.

### Synthesis

The three evidentiary domains point in the same broad direction: the *Iliad* and the Cycle belong to a connected Trojan-War tradition, but the surviving materials do not preserve a fixed pre-Homeric Cypria against which Homer can simply be measured. Bernabé’s dossier supports real parallels and real divergences; the Venetus A scholia preserve both ancient Cyclic associations and later interpretive expansions; comparative poetics gives several motifs a depth beyond either poem.[10][12][8][13]

The remaining disagreement is therefore productive but unresolved. Currie’s neoanalytic approach asks when verbal precision and narrative placement justify identifying an allusive predecessor; Burgess, Nagy, Foley, and compatible oral-traditional work require that such specificity exceed what shared formulaic and mythic tradition would already predict. Tsagalis’ reclassification of the Scyros material shows why passage-level attribution remains the point at which the two approaches continue to diverge.[31][10][12][34]

## Papyri and digital analysis since 2000

### What is P.Oxy. 3829

P.Oxy. LVI 3829 is not a fragment of the *Cypria*. It is an Iliadic papyrus, edited by L. Kappel, containing: the end of a catechism—a question-and-answer teaching text—on *Iliad* characters; an *antehomerica* narrative, or account of events before Homer’s poem; and *Iliad* 1.1 followed by a Book 1 hypothesis, or plot-summary.[76]

Its *antehomerica* runs from Zeus’ plan to destroy the heroic generation through the Judgement of Paris. At ii.9 Zeus judges the heroic race impious and plans, with Themis, to destroy it. This wording has supported modern editors’ emendation of Proclus’ *Cypria* summary from Thetis to Themis.[8] The papyrus was first published in 1989 in *The Oxyrhynchus Papyri* volume 56, which comprises nos.

3822–3875.[77] **It is therefore a pre-2000 publication whose importance lies in a related pre-Iliadic narrative, not in recovery of a Cypria manuscript.**[76][8]

Its ancient date, the late-nineteenth-century recovery of Oxyrhynchus material, and the 1989 modern edition are distinct chronological facts; digitization changes none of them.

P.Oxy. 3829 comes from Oxyrhynchus in the Oxyrhynchite nome of Roman Egypt and is dated to the later second century CE.[78][47]

[12]

- **P.Oxy. 4708:** Archilochus’ *Telephos* elegy is central to debate over the Teuthranian expedition. Kullmann takes it as confirmation of an old, ultimately pre-Homeric oral-epic story; the contrary objection is that Archilochus’ knowledge cannot prove Iliadic dependence on a Cyclic poem. [13][75]

The Archilochus papyrus relevant to the Teuthranian expedition was published in 2005.[13]

- **P.Oxy. 2513 and P.Oxy. 2434:** The former contains hexameters on Iphigenia, though it has also been associated with Eumelus’ *Korinthiaka*; the latter may be Simonidean commentary and plausibly concerns Iphigenia’s sacrifice. [79][32]

- **P. Rylands 22 and the Louvre Papyrus:** Rylands 22 narrates the night of Troy’s fall differently from Proclus but claims no direct Cyclic affiliation; the Louvre Papyrus is listed as *Cypria* fr. 16 and preserves an appeal to Achilles. [6][16]

West’s decisive constraint remains that no papyrus fragment can be securely assigned to the *Cypria*: its narrative framework rests chiefly on Proclus’ summary, supplemented in places by Apollodorus and indirect testimonia.[12]

The principal evidence for the Epic Cycle is nevertheless indirect: the Proclus/Chrestomathy prose summaries, quotations and notices in later authors, scholiasts and lexicographers, and artistic evidence, alongside a very small and often disputable papyrus record. Papyri are consequently important comparanda but cannot bear the whole reconstruction of a Cyclic poem.

- **P.Oxy. 5094:** A citation in this papyrus connects a newly discussed fragment with the *Cypria*; the proposed identification of the daughter of Dymas as Hecabe still permits the verse to derive from another Cyclic poem. [7]

- **P.Oxy. 1611 fr. 4 ii 145:** This scholarly text quotes a line on Penthesileia’s death attributed to Arctinus’ *Aethiopis*, although the identity of the line’s speaker cannot be determined. [24][7]

- **P.Oxy. 2510:** West identifies this with Bernabé’s doubtful Little *Iliad* fragment 32, rejects assignment to either the *Aethiopis* or the Little *Iliad*, and judges it highly unlikely that this fourth-century papyrus preserves Cyclic epic. [7]

### How has the digital analysis of P.Oxy. 3829 and other

Since 2000, digitization has transformed access rather than supplied a new Cypria text. The Homer Multitext has produced TEI-XML diplomatic editions of Venetus A’s text and scholia and, alongside its 2010 *Iliad* 10 volume, online editions of Venetus A and three Homeric papyri.[80][26] Oxyrhynchus Online and Ancient Lives use imaging, computational processing, and consensus transcription to accelerate identification, transcription, and cataloguing; Ancient Lives began in 2011.[81][82]

Ready (2019, Orality  Textuality  and the Homeric Epics, 'Oral Texts and Entextualization in the Homeric Epics')’s model of the Ptolemaic “wild” Homeric papyri treats copying as a form of performance, strengthening questions about continuing oral performance in the Classical and Hellenistic periods.[83][84] **Digital work has thus made the witnesses easier to compare, but it has not adjudicated neoanalytic Cypria-linkage against oralists’ model of a shared multiform tradition.**[26][12][84]

### Synthesis

P.Oxy. 3829 strengthens the case for reading Themis in Proclus’ Zeus-plan, but its distinctive motive of human impiety means that it cannot simply be equated with Proclus’ *Cypria* summary.[8] The other papyri demonstrate broad circulation and variation of stories about Telephus, Iphigenia, Scyros, and Troy’s fall.

They therefore aid oral-traditional accounts of multiform myth while supplying neoanalysis with comparanda for possible Iliadic allusion; none independently establishes direct dependence on a fixed *Cypria* text.[12][75][79][32][6]

## Recent monographs and prospects for synthesis

Recent book-length work no longer treats the choice as simply between a fixed lost source and free oral variation. Instead, it tests how inherited story-patterns, relatively stable performances, and pointed reference to particular narratives may coexist. The result is a developing rapprochement, not an agreed account of any individual Iliadic–Cyclic correspondence.[12]

### Which recent monographs attempt a synthesis between these positions?

Jonathan S. Burgess (2009, no venue recorded, 'The Death and Afterlife of Achilles')’s *The Death and Afterlife of Achilles* (2009) makes Achilles’ post-*Iliad* fate—a central area of overlap between Homer and Cyclic tradition—its subject.[17] Burgess’s broader revision of Neoanalysis replaces earlier claims of Homer’s use of written Cyclic poems with pre-Homeric traditional motifs, bringing his model closer to oral-traditional premises while retaining Neoanalysis’s interest in reconstructing narrative content.[17][2]

James W. Nelson (2023, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'Markers of Allusion in Archaic Greek Poetry')’s *Markers of Allusion in Archaic Greek Poetry* (2023) explicitly combines Neoanalysis with Foley-style traditional referentiality: traditional referentiality means the resonance generated by shared traditional patterns, while allusion is a more pointed reference to a particular prior poetic context.[28] Nelson presents Burgess’s oral, intertextual neoanalysis as a principal model for this combination.[28][34]

Other book-length interventions broaden rather than settle the synthesis. Sammons’s *Device and Composition in the Greek Epic Cycle* (2017) treats Cyclic narrative design as purposeful composition, not failed Homer; Zieliński’s recent *The Iliad and the Oral Epic Tradition* combines oral performance and allusion with a primary traditional version behind some Iliad–Cycle parallels, without postulating a written or fixed source-text.[12][85] **These monographs make mixed models intellectually viable, but they do not produce a common test for deciding that a particular resemblance derives from one determinate prior narrative.**[49][85]

Burgess’s *The Tradition of the Trojan War in Homer and the Epic Cycle* (2001) is the foundational book-length version of this rapprochement: it treats Iliadic–Cyclic correspondences as evidence for a broad, multiform Trojan-War tradition rather than straightforward use of the surviving Cyclic poems as written sources.

Christos C. Tsagalis (2025, Scripta Classica Israelica, 'Proemial Bridges: An Intratextual Association Activating an Intertextual Reference')’s *The Oral Palimpsest: Exploring Intertextuality in the Homeric Epics* (2008) explores how intertextual interpretation can operate within orally transmitted Homeric poetry.[41][11][86]

### What is the current status of attempts at synthesis between

The programmatic formulation came with *Homeric Contexts* (2012): orally transmitted poems could become sufficiently stable through memorization to function as models, while entirely traditional material could still form a distinctive motif within one poem. Montanari therefore separated theoretical compatibility from agreement over particular passages.[13]

Currie (2021, Trends in Classics, 'Intertextuality in Early Greek Poetry: The Special Case of Epinician')’s *Homer’s Allusive Art* (2016) argues that specific allusion and traditional referentiality may both operate, but insists that each method first establish its own validity.[30] Kelly (2012, De Gruyter eBooks, 'The Mourning of Thetis: ‘Allusion’ and the Future in the Iliad')’s alternative bridge gives methodological priority to performance and traditional scene-types, then admits specific allusion when independently demonstrated; he thus rejects an either-or model without making allusion the default explanation.[42][31][19][14] Porter similarly rejects source–recipient hierarchy as the default, whereas Zieliński maintains that some correspondences require a primary version rather than merely a shared pattern.[50][85]

**The live disagreement is now chiefly evidentiary: what threshold converts a shared motif into allusion to a particular poem or stabilized oral narrative?** Neoanalysis remains useful for locating meaningful correspondences; oral theory explains how those correspondences can circulate, vary, and carry meaning in performance.[13][75]

A limited consensus holds that the *Iliad* presupposes a wider Trojan-War story-world, whereas the extant Epic Cycle is late, fragmentary, and textually mediated evidence for that world. This rejects both the claim that Homer knew no extra-Iliadic narratives and the claim that every parallel proves dependence on the particular *Cypria* later summarized by Proclus.

### Synthesis

The current field is plural rather than reconciled. Burgess, Nelson, Montanari, Kelly, and Zieliński show that oral composition does not logically exclude stable narrative models or pointed allusion; conversely, a possible model does not make every parallel an act of dependence.[2][34][13][14][85] The *Cypria* is consequently studied through source research, oral tradition, reception, language, meter, and compositional technique, rather than reduced to Homer’s source or inferior prequel.[12] What remains disputed is passage-specific: whether resemblance reflects direct dependence, allusion to a relatively stable oral version, or resonance within a multiform Trojan-War tradition.[12][19][14]

### Open questions

Open are the precise pre-Iliadic form, date, and circulation of the Cypria; whether particular Iliadic correspondences derive from a distinct Cypria-like realization rather than shared tradition; and how far Achilles’ death-knowledge and the Zeus–Thetis scene preserve older narrative configurations. A securely dated early Cypria witness, or a demonstrably distinctive and ordered verbal correspondence between an Iliadic passage and an independent Cyclic text, would most directly resolve these questions.
