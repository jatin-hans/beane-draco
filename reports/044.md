# Carbon Pricing Implementation and Outcomes in California, the EU and China

## Executive summary

The three mechanisms should not be ranked on a single 2020–2024 performance metric because California and the EU ETS were mature cap-and-trade systems, while China’s national market began trading only in July 2021 and used output-linked intensity benchmarks rather than a fixed absolute cap.

California combined economy-wide upstream fuel coverage with a steepened post-2020 cap, falling by about 13.4 million tCO₂e annually in 2021–2030, while the EU ETS tightened toward a 62% reduction in covered-sector emissions below 2005 by 2030 and added maritime transport in 2024.[1][2][3] Both mature systems preserved banking and their standing supply-management rules through COVID-19 rather than adopting pandemic-specific banking changes, whereas China’s later launch and power-sector-only initial scope make its price, emissions, revenue, and compliance observations inherently less comparable.[4][5][6] Revenue and leakage protection also followed different models: California recycled auction value through ratepayer credits and climate programmes, the EU auctioned most allowances while protecting listed industrial sectors and introducing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism transition, and China’s free-allocation national market did not generate comparable auction revenue.[7][8][2] The decisive conclusion is that complementary renewable deployment and coal-policy choices, not allowance prices alone, explain much of the observed emissions change, and measured industrial relocation must not be inferred merely from the existence of free allocation or a border measure.

## Scope and implementation

This report measures each system over 1 January 2020–31 December 2024. An allowance price is the price of a compliance instrument, normally representing one tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e); covered emissions are emissions for which an entity must report and, where applicable, surrender instruments. A baseline projection is a forward-looking estimate of emissions without the policy, so an achieved reduction versus baseline equals projected emissions less observed emissions on the same scope, geography, and time basis. Auction receipts are public proceeds from selling allowances, distinct from the value of free allowances or private secondary-market trades. An entity-based compliance rate is the proportion of regulated entities meeting an obligation, whereas an emissions-weighted compliance rate is the proportion of covered emissions associated with compliant operators; these measures are not interchangeable. Banking is the retention of unused allowances for future compliance. Carbon leakage is a shift of production and associated emissions to jurisdictions facing lower carbon costs, rather than simply a change in trade or in the location of an allowance holder. California began operating in 2012 and imposed compliance obligations from January 2013; the EU ETS began in 2005; China officially launched its national system in January 2021 and began national trading on 16 July 2021.[9][2][10][11][12][6] Its practical effect depends first on who must participate, where in an economic supply chain the legal obligation falls, how allowances are distributed, and whether later reforms change the effective scarcity of allowances. Those design choices make the three systems materially different before any comparison of prices, emissions, revenue, or compliance is attempted.

Judgment — California and the EU ETS operated declining-cap, cap-and-trade systems in 2020–2024. China’s national system allocated free allowances against output-based emissions-intensity benchmarks, so it more closely resembled a tradable performance standard than an absolute-cap system.[13][14] A tradable performance standard instead gives regulated firms output-linked allowances against an emissions-intensity benchmark; total allowance supply can therefore rise with output.

California and the EU ETS were cap-and-trade systems throughout this period. China’s national market operated principally as the latter, despite often being described as an ETS.

### When did California's Cap-and-Trade Program operate

California’s Cap-and-Trade Program began operating in 2012 when its tracking system opened for allocation, auction distribution, and trading. Binding compliance obligations began in January 2013, when the emissions cap took effect. The program therefore operated throughout 2020–2024.[9][15][1]

The requested period spans three compliance periods rather than one continuous regulatory block: the final year of the third compliance period in 2020, the full fourth compliance period in 2021–2023, and the opening year of the fifth compliance period in 2024–2026. A compliance period is the multi-year interval over which entities make annual partial surrenders and then a final surrender covering their remaining emissions.[1]

California’s program was authorized initially through 2020, but Assembly Bill 398 (AB 398), enacted in 2017, extended it through 2030. The continuation is important because the 2020–2024 period was not a short-term continuation following the state’s 2020 target; it was the opening stage of a more stringent post-2020 cap trajectory.[7][16]

California and Québec remained linked during the period. Linkage means that the two jurisdictions mutually recognize allowances and offsets and hold joint auctions, enlarging the compliance market beyond California alone. California, Québec, and Washington announced interest in exploring a shared market in 2024, but this was not a new operative linkage by 31 December 2024.[4][17]

California and Québec began holding joint allowance auctions in November 2014.[75][112]

California and Québec held their first joint allowance auction in November 2014.[75]

California maintained its own allowance budget within the linked California–Québec market; the California budget was distinct from the combined linked-market budget.[75][113]

**California therefore entered 2020–2024 as a mature, linked market with more than seven years of compliance history, not as a newly introduced carbon-pricing experiment.** This makes its observed market behaviour structurally different from China’s market, which did not begin trading until July 2021.[9][6]

### Which sectors did California cover during 2020–2024?

California’s established scope during 2020–2024 covered fuel-combustion emissions in mining and extractives, power, buildings, transport, industry, agriculture, and forestry, as well as industrial-process emissions from about 400 covered facilities. The program also covered fuel use in fishing operations upstream, through the fuel supplier rather than the final user.[9][8][1]

The legal point of regulation differed by source type:

- **Point-source coverage:** Mining and extractive activities, industrial facilities, and in-state power generation were regulated at the facility or source. This means the installation operator has the compliance obligation. [9][1]

- **Imported-electricity coverage:** Electricity imported into California was regulated at the first delivery onto California’s grid. This design prevents imported generation from escaping the carbon price simply because the emitting plant lies outside state borders. [9][1]

- **Upstream fuel-supplier coverage:** Buildings, road transport, agriculture, forestry, and fishing were covered upstream. The supplier of natural gas, petroleum, gasoline blendstock, diesel, liquefied petroleum gas, or liquefied natural gas faced the obligation, rather than each household, motorist, farm, or business consuming the fuel. [9][1]

The general facility threshold was annual emissions of at least 25,000 tCO₂e. Electricity imported from specified sources was covered when connected to generators meeting that threshold, while emissions from unspecified imported electricity were covered without a threshold using a default emissions factor. Smaller facilities in covered sectors could opt in voluntarily.[1]

The sectoral architecture was already in place before the requested period. The first compliance period covered electricity generation, including imported electricity, and industrial facilities emitting at least 25,000 tCO₂e annually. In 2015, the second compliance period added transportation-fuel and natural-gas suppliers; that expansion created the broad fuel-supplier coverage in place from 2020 onward.[17][1]

Sources report different shares of California emissions covered because they use different dates and coverage conventions. CARB describes approximately 80% of statewide GHG emissions; ICAP reports approximately 76%; another account places coverage at roughly 85%; and a 2021 account reports about 74%. The figures should therefore be read as approximate descriptions of broad economy-wide reach, not as interchangeable annual measurements.[21][1][16][24]

Reported counts also use different units. CARB staff analysis describes approximately 400 covered entities, about 250 of them stationary-source facilities, with the remainder fuel suppliers and electricity providers. A CARB presentation refers to about 450 covered entities, while ICAP describes industrial-process emissions from about 400 facilities. A facility, a regulated entity, and a fuel supplier are not the same unit of observation.[22][4][8]

California covers carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases, including methane, nitrous oxide, hydrofluorocarbons, perfluorocarbons, sulfur hexafluoride, nitrogen trifluoride, and other fluorinated gases. One allowance represents one metric tonne of CO₂e using the 100-year global-warming-potential metric.[17][21]

**The economy-wide character of California’s market comes from regulating different points in the supply chain, rather than from placing direct compliance obligations on every emitter in buildings, transport, agriculture, and forestry.** That design gives California broader sectoral reach than a conventional installation-only ETS.[9][8]

### What was California’s allocation approach during 2020–2024?

California used a mixed allocation model. Covered entities could obtain allowances through quarterly auctions, limited free allocation for eligible recipients, and trading with other entities; a majority of allowances were offered through quarterly auctions.[21][25]

An allowance budget is the annual quantity of allowances issued under the cap. California distributed this budget through three channels: free allocation, free allocation with consignment, and auctioning. Free allocation with consignment means that an entity receives allowances without charge but must sell some or all of them at auction, with the proceeds directed to a specified public purpose.[9][1]

Industrial facilities received free allowances to address carbon leakage—the risk that production and associated emissions move to jurisdictions without comparable climate policy. For nearly all industrial facilities, the quantity reflected product-specific benchmarks, recent production volumes, a cap-adjustment factor, and an assistance factor based on leakage risk.[7][8]

The industrial benchmark approach rewarded comparatively efficient production rather than treating all firms in a sector alike. The formula incorporated an industry-wide product-efficiency benchmark, the declining annual allowance budget, and a facility’s annual production. More production generally resulted in more free allowances, which was intended to preserve incentives to keep efficient, emissions-intensive, trade-exposed production in California rather than relocate it.[7][17]

California’s industrial free-allocation true-up could account for production changes using output from two years earlier.[114]

AB 398 set industrial assistance factors at 100% through 2030, citing continued vulnerability to carbon leakage. This did not mean industrial free allocation was fixed in absolute quantity: the cap-adjustment factor and the declining allowance budget continued to reduce the overall allocation trajectory.[8][17][22]

California’s listed high-leakage-risk industrial activities included cement, paper and steel; their assistance factor was 100% for 2021–2030.[114]

Electrical distribution utilities and natural-gas suppliers also received free allowances, but on behalf of ratepayers rather than as unrestricted compliance support. The allowance value had to benefit retail customers and support GHG reductions.[4][9]

Investor-owned electric utilities had to consign 100% of their allocated allowances to quarterly auctions. They then purchased allowances for their own compliance needs, while the value from their initial allocation was returned to customers through the California Climate Credit, generally as biannual credits on electricity bills. Natural-gas suppliers consigned an annually increasing share of their allocations; in 2023, the stated consignment share was 65% for California natural-gas suppliers.[22][7][19]

Publicly owned utilities and cooperatives could use free allowances for compliance or consign them to auction. If they sold them, the proceeds could support ratepayer benefits or GHG-reduction projects, including renewable-energy projects.[1][26]

California’s allocation model also included cost-containment features. The auction reserve price—the minimum price at which allowances can sell—rose each year by 5% plus inflation. In May 2024, the reserve price was US$24.04 per allowance and the settlement price was US$38.35. CARB also held allowances in the Allowance Price Containment Reserve, with 2024 prices of US$56.20 for Tier 1 and US$72.21 for Tier 2; no reserve sale had occurred by the period described.[7]

California’s Allowance Price Containment Reserve Tier 1 and Tier 2 prices were US$41.40 and US$53.20 per allowance in 2021, respectively.[115][116]

California’s Allowance Price Containment Reserve tier prices were US$41.40 and US$53.20 per allowance in 2021; these were contingent supply prices, not observed auction settlement prices.[115][116]

**California’s allocation design combined auction-led scarcity with targeted protection: industry received production-linked free allowances to reduce relocation incentives, while utilities received consigned allowances whose value was directed principally to customers.** The design therefore sought to retain an economy-wide carbon-price signal without fully passing compliance costs through to leakage-exposed firms or retail energy consumers.[21][7][22][9]

CARB’s 2024 auction records illustrate the consignment channel. At the 14 February 2024 joint auction, all 19,613,489 current-auction allowances consigned by California entities, all 25,271,454 CARB current-vintage allowances, and all 6,015,000 CARB advance-vintage allowances listed as offered were sold.[27] At the May 2024 joint auction, investor-owned utilities received US$650,550,386.88 for 17,572,944 consigned allowances and publicly owned utilities received US$89,355,581.22 for 2,413,711; at the November 2024 joint auction, the corresponding figures were US$578,153,931.88 for 18,118,268 investor-owned-utility allowances and US$90,410,954.01 for 2,833,311 publicly owned-utility allowances.[28][29]

### What relevant implementation changes occurred in California during 2020–2024?

The most important in-period implementation change was the transition to the post-2020 cap trajectory. For 2021–2030, the cap was set to fall by about 13.4 million tCO₂e annually, averaging approximately 4%, and to reach 200.5 million tCO₂e in 2030. CARB described 2021 as a year in which the program doubled in stringency through much steeper cap reductions.[9][1][11]

California’s annual allowance budget fell from 334.2 million allowances in 2020 to 320.8 million in 2021, 307.5 million in 2022, 294.1 million in 2023 and 280.7 million in 2024.[114]

The combined California–Québec annual emissions-unit budgets were 388.94 million units in 2020, 376.06 million in 2021, 361.52 million in 2022, 346.89 million in 2023 and 332.25 million in 2024.[113]

Some accounts describe the post-2020 annual decline as about 5%, rather than approximately 4%. The difference reflects differing descriptions of the cap schedule, but both establish that the post-2020 program tightened substantially compared with its earlier trajectory.[30][1]

CARB established a hard price ceiling in 2021 under AB 398. A hard price ceiling permits CARB to sell an unlimited quantity of additional compliance instruments when demand reaches the ceiling; if activated, it can relax the effective emissions cap. The 2024 ceiling was US$88.22, and auction settlement prices had not reached it by the reporting date.[7]

Offset rules tightened from 2021. An offset is a verified GHG reduction or removal generated outside the capped sector that a covered entity may use for a limited portion of its compliance obligation. The allowable share fell from 8% through 2020 to 4% for 2021–2025 emissions. Beginning with 2021 compliance obligations, no more than half of that 4% could come from projects that did not provide direct environmental benefits to California.[21][9][16]

California retained banking, meaning that entities could hold unused allowances for later compliance. Banking remained subject to holding limits. The supplied record identifies no temporary banking-rule modification in response to COVID-19 or the subsequent recovery.[4][24]

California’s automatic unsold-allowance mechanism remained in place. Allowances that did not clear at auction were withheld until two consecutive auctions cleared above the reserve price; previously unsold allowances could return gradually, capped at 25% of allowances offered at an auction. Allowances remaining unsold after 24 months were removed from circulation and transferred to the price-containment reserve.[4]

The 2022 small-business electricity-credit change was distributional rather than a change to the cap. California replaced a volumetric credit, which lowered the per-kilowatt-hour price, with a flat credit equal to the residential credit. The stated purpose was to maintain customer protection while strengthening incentives to conserve electricity.[24]

CARB began a broader program review in 2023. The review examined cap recalibration using updated emissions inventories, trajectories aligned with 2022 Scoping Plan scenarios, and potential changes to allowance-allocation formulas for utilities and emissions-intensive, trade-exposed firms.[30]

In 2024, CARB initiated rulemaking to consider removing allowances from the cumulative 2025–2030 budget and revising post-2030 budgets. These were proposals under consideration, not changes adopted and in force by 31 December 2024.[17]

**California’s changes in 2020–2024 were structural post-2020 reforms—steeper cap decline, a hard price ceiling, and stricter offset rules—not temporary pandemic relief.** Its banking and unsold-allowance mechanisms continued as standing flexibility and supply-management rules rather than COVID-specific interventions.[1][7][4]

### When did the EU ETS operate

The European Union Emissions Trading System began in 2005. It operated in Phase 3 in 2020 and entered Phase 4, running from 2021 to 2030, on 1 January 2021. It therefore operated throughout the requested period.[2][5][31]

In 2020, the EU ETS applied in the 27 EU Member States, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and the United Kingdom. From 2021, the wider United Kingdom ceased participating, except for electricity-generation plants in Northern Ireland.[2][20]

The EU ETS has been linked to the Swiss ETS since 2020. Linkage permits an allowance issued in one scheme to be surrendered against emissions in the other, giving operators access to a larger market and a wider set of abatement options.[3][2]

The EU ETS is an EU-wide cap-and-trade system. It uses a single cap across covered activities, a declining annual allowance supply, auctions as its primary distribution method, free allocation for selected sectors, and secondary-market trading of unused allowances.[13][3]

**The EU ETS was the oldest and most institutionally mature of the three systems during 2020–2024.** Unlike California’s subnational market and China’s newly trading national market, it operated across 30 European countries and entered the period with a long-established EU-wide cap, registry, auction, and market-stability architecture.[2][20]

The United Kingdom’s 2021 departure creates a geographic break in the installation-emissions series. The European Commission recorded 1,356 million tonnes of CO₂e of verified installation emissions in 2020, or 1,253 million tonnes excluding the UK, followed by 1,337 million tonnes in 2021, 1,313 million tonnes in 2022, and 1,096 million tonnes in 2023.[2]

### Which sectors did the EU ETS cover during 2020–2024?

From 2020 through 2023, the EU ETS covered electricity and heat-generation plants, manufacturing installations, and specified aircraft operators. Aviation coverage included operators flying between airports in the European Economic Area (EEA), from the EEA to Switzerland, and from the EEA to the United Kingdom.[2][20]

The industrial scope included energy-intensive activities such as oil refining; iron and steel; aluminium and other metals; cement, lime, glass, ceramics, pulp, paper, and cardboard; acids; and bulk organic chemicals. The system also covered specified nitrous oxide emissions from nitric, adipic, and glyoxylic-acid production and perfluorocarbon emissions from aluminium production.[32]

Aviation had been regulated since 2012. Although the EU ETS’ legal scope covered incoming and outgoing EEA flights, actual obligations were limited from 2013 to flights within the EEA in support of international aviation arrangements.[2]

On 1 January 2024, maritime transport entered the EU ETS. It covered 100% of emissions between two EEA ports and while ships were in EEA ports, plus 50% of emissions from voyages beginning or ending outside the EEA. The initial maritime scope covered CO₂; methane and nitrous oxide were scheduled to enter from 2026.[2][8]

The maritime extension applied to large ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above entering EU ports, irrespective of flag. The EU’s 2024 carbon-market report estimated that, under its defined scope, the EU ETS covered 85 Mt CO₂ from maritime transport in 2023, compared with over 126 Mt CO₂ when counting all emissions from voyages to and from EEA ports.[8][2]

Aviation coverage also expanded from 1 January 2024. The EU ETS added most flights between the EEA and the EU’s nine outermost regions, plus departing flights from those regions to Switzerland and the United Kingdom. The Commission estimated that the added aviation scope extended ETS carbon-pricing coverage by around 7%.[2]

Municipal-waste incineration installations above 20 MW thermal output had to monitor and report emissions from 1 January 2024. They were not required to surrender allowances, so this was an MRV—monitoring, reporting, and verification—extension rather than full carbon-pricing coverage.[2][8]

The EU ETS should not be conflated with ETS2. The 2023 revision created ETS2 as a separate system for upstream fuel-combustion emissions in buildings, road transport, and additional sectors. ETS2 was not operating during 2020–2024; its surrender obligation was scheduled for 2027, or 2028 if exceptionally high gas or oil prices occurred in 2026.[2]

**The EU ETS’s scope was stable from 2020 through 2023—power and heat, manufacturing, and specified aviation—but broadened substantially in 2024 through maritime transport and additional aviation routes.** Buildings and road transport remained outside ETS1 during the requested period.[2]

### What was the EU ETS allocation approach during 2020–2024?

The EU ETS combined auctioning and free allocation throughout 2020–2024. Auctioning was the main distribution method in Phase 4 and could account for up to 57% of the cap in 2021–2025. Of the allowances auctioned, 90% were distributed to Member States according to their shares of verified emissions and 10% to lower-income Member States through the solidarity provision.[20][8][2]

The European Commission says that, although 57% of the Phase 4 cap was in principle to be auctioned during 2021–2030, the initial annual auction share for 2021–2025 was about 51.5% after other allocation rules and could change with Market Stability Reserve operations.[102]

Free allocation remained significant for industry. Its purpose was to manage carbon-leakage risk: the risk that emissions-intensive, trade-exposed production moves from the EU ETS area to places facing lower carbon costs. The Phase 4 carbon-leakage list identified 63 sectors and subsectors, covering about 94% of industrial EU ETS emissions.[2]

Free allocation was benchmark-based. Product benchmarks represented the average emissions intensity of the 10% most efficient installations in each sector. An installation emitting above the benchmark required additional allowances purchased through auction or the market.[2][32]

Power generation generally received no free allocation; generators had to buy allowances. A transitional exception allowed lower-income Member States to provide limited free allocation for electricity-sector modernisation. Three Member States—Bulgaria, Hungary, and Romania—continued using that option in Phase 4, and it could operate until the end of 2024.[33][8]

Manufacturing and aviation continued to receive free allocation during the period. For industry, the 2021–2025 benchmark update incorporated annual technological-progress adjustments. The uniform cross-sectoral correction factor was one, meaning no general proportional reduction was needed to bring free allocations within the available budget.[8]

Free-allocation volumes became more responsive to production in Phase 4. Where annual production rose or fell by at least 15%, operators submitted annual production reports and their allocations could be adjusted through the New Entrants’ Reserve. From 2021 through 2023, the average number of adjustment applications exceeded 3,800 annually, around three times the annual average up to 2020.[2][8]

The initial free allocation for 2021–2025 was calculated at 2,791 million allowances for 7,430 installations. By mid-2024, production-related reductions and Commission corrections had reduced the total by a net 112.2 million allowances relative to that initial amount.[2]

The 2023 revision changed the direction of free-allocation policy. It scaled down free allocation with the tighter cap and made access conditional on companies’ decarbonisation efforts. A January 2024 revision to the Free Allocation Regulation introduced energy-audit conditions, climate-neutrality-plan conditions for certain installations, and revised benchmark definitions, notably for hydrogen and steel.[3][2]

For aviation, the revised system phased out free allowances by the end of 2025. In 2024, aircraft operators received 25% fewer free allowances; full auctioning was scheduled for 2026 and therefore remained outside the requested period.[2][34]

**The EU ETS was auction-led at the system level but retained extensive, performance-based free allocation for leakage-exposed industry.** The result was a stronger direct purchase obligation for electricity generation than for protected industrial production, while Phase 4 increasingly tied free allocation to actual output, efficient performance, and decarbonisation conditions.[8][2]

### What relevant implementation changes occurred in the EU ETS during

The Phase 3–Phase 4 transition was the first major implementation shift. Phase 4 began in 2021 with updated free-allocation benchmarks for 2021–2025, annual technology-progress adjustments, and the 15% production-change rule.[10][8]

Banking continued through the phase transition. Third-phase allowances remained valid indefinitely and account holdings were retained after 1 January 2021. Fourth-phase allowances could be surrendered only for emissions from 2021 onward, whereas third-phase allowances remained usable without that restriction.[5]

Registry rules changed on 1 January 2021. The default rule required approval by a second account representative for transfers, including transfers to trusted accounts, though account holders could ask national administrators to remove that requirement for trusted-account transfers. This was a transaction-security change, not a change to the quantity of bankable allowances.[5]

The end of international-credit eligibility also took effect in 2021. Eligible international credits could be exchanged for EU allowances through 30 April 2021, the end of compliance for 2020 emissions; from 1 May 2021, they could no longer be used in the EU ETS.[5]

The European Commission presented the Fit for 55 package on 14 July 2021. The Parliament and Council completed approval of ETS-related measures by June 2023, and the revised ETS Directive entered into force in mid-June 2023.[3][35]

The 2023 revision raised the 2030 target for ETS-covered sectors to a 62% reduction below 2005 levels, replacing the previous 43% target. It also strengthened the Market Stability Reserve, updated aviation rules, established the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), expanded ETS1 to maritime transport, and created the separate ETS2.[33][8][2]

The first cap changes under the revision took effect in 2024. The 2024 cap was reduced by 90 million allowances, and the Linear Reduction Factor—the annual cap-decline rate—rose to 4.3% per year for 2024–2027, equal to 87,924,231 allowances per year.[2]

The EU ETS general cap fell from 1.572 billion allowances in 2021 to 1.386 billion allowances in 2024; aviation was treated separately in these cap figures.[2]

The EU ETS linear reduction factor was 1.74% annually through 2020 and 2.2% annually from 2021, before rising to 4.3% annually for 2024–2027 under the 2023 reform.[117]

The EU ETS added 78.4 million allowances to its 2024 cap to incorporate maritime transport, alongside the separate downward rebasing of 90 million allowances. The general cap for power, heat, industry and maritime declined from 1.572 billion allowances in 2021 to 1.386 billion allowances in 2024; aviation had a separate cap.[2]

Before the 2024 increase, the EU ETS Phase 4 linear reduction factor was 2.2% annually from 2021.[117]

The Market Stability Reserve is a standing supply-control mechanism. It adjusts auction volumes according to the total number of allowances in circulation (TNAC). When TNAC exceeds 1.096 billion allowances, 24% is withdrawn from auctions and placed in the reserve; if it falls below 400 million, 100 million allowances are released.[20][8]

Unlike California, the EU ETS had no general auction-price floor or ceiling in 2020–2024; it formed prices in the market, subject to an exceptional supply provision for extreme price movements.[118]

The MSR’s rules applied during the recovery period without a separate COVID-specific intervention. The 2023 TNAC was 1.11 billion allowances, above the withdrawal trigger. Consequently, 267 million allowances were scheduled to be withdrawn from auctions between September 2024 and August 2025. This implementation action began within 2024, although part of its scheduled period extended beyond the client’s cutoff.[2]

The MSR also invalidated surplus allowances. From 2024, the applicable reserve threshold was fixed at 400 million allowances. On 1 January 2024, the MSR invalidated 382 million allowances and retained 400 million; cumulative invalidations since 2023 totalled 2.9 billion allowances.[2]

CBAM entered its transitional reporting phase in October 2023. Importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen, and related products had to report embedded emissions but made no carbon payments during 2020–2024. Payments and the linked gradual phase-out of free allocation for relevant sectors were scheduled from 2026.[33][2][8]

The revised Directive also changed revenue-use requirements. Since June 2023, Member States have been required to use 100% of collected EU ETS revenue, or an equivalent financial value, for specified climate action and energy-transformation purposes, except revenue used to compensate electricity-intensive industries for indirect carbon costs.[36]

**The EU ETS became both tighter and broader during 2023–2024: the cap fell faster, maritime transport entered ETS1, free allocation became more conditional, and CBAM began the transition from internal leakage protection toward border adjustment.** These changes were structural Fit for 55 reforms, not temporary pandemic measures.[2][8]

### When did China's national carbon market begin operating and trading

China announced its national ETS in 2017 after provincial and municipal pilot programs. In 2020, the system was expected to begin operating, but the International Energy Agency identified COVID-19 as a potential cause of delay.[10][37][38]

China formally launched the national ETS in January 2021. Trading began on 16 July 2021 on the Shanghai Environment and Energy Exchange. The distinction matters: policy launch, regulatory operation, and actual market trading are separate events.[10][6]

The national market’s first compliance cycle combined 2019 and 2020 emissions. It completed on 30 December 2021, after trading began in July. That first cycle allowed inter-year banking of allowances.[6]

China’s national system followed regional pilots established from 2013 in Beijing, Chongqing, Guangdong, Hubei, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Fujian, and Tianjin. Those pilot systems were not the national market; their coverage grew from roughly 5% of total emissions at inception to roughly 15% by 2017.[18][14]

**China’s 2020–2024 national-market record is not a five-year trading history.** The national market was pre-trading in 2020, started trading only in July 2021, and was still developing its legal, MRV, and enforcement architecture through 2024.[38][6][18]

### What relevant implementation changes occurred in China's national carbon market

The national ETS initially covered the power-generation sector. In its first compliance cycle, it regulated 2,162 key compliance entities from power generation. Eligibility covered power-sector enterprises with annual emissions of at least 26,000 tCO₂e and total energy consumption of approximately 10,000 tonnes of standard coal in any year from 2013 to 2019.[6][39]

For China’s second compliance cycle covering 2021–2022 emissions, covered power-sector entities generally met a threshold of at least 26,000 tonnes CO2e in either 2020 or 2021.[119]

Power-sector coverage included combined heat-and-power plants and captive power plants operated by other industrial sectors. It included coal-fired and gas-fired power generation, although gas plants were exempt from surrendering allowances in the first compliance period.[40][41]

The market was widely described as covering approximately 2,200 to 2,250 power-sector entities. The first batch comprised 2,162 key power-generation entities and covered approximately 4.5 billion tonnes of CO₂ annually.[42][43]

For China’s second national-market compliance cycle, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment reported 2,257 key emitting entities and approximately 5.1 billion tonnes of CO2e in annual coverage, exceeding 40% of China’s CO2 emissions.[61]

China’s second national-market compliance cycle covered 2,257 key emitting entities, including captive power plants, and approximately 5.1 billion tCO2e annually—more than 40% of China’s CO2 emissions.[61][43][120]

Other estimates put annual emissions within scope at about 5.0 billion to 5.1 billion tonnes of CO₂, described as more than 40% of China’s total emissions in some accounts.[18][14][10]
These are coverage estimates, not emissions reductions.[18][14][10]

China’s national market was deliberately introduced through a power-sector-first strategy. National planning identified eight eventual sectors: electricity; building materials; iron and steel; non-ferrous-metal processing; petroleum refining; chemicals; pulp and paper; and aviation. These were plans for eventual coverage, not a statement that all were subject to allowance obligations during 2020–2024.[18][14]

In March 2021, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE) required emissions reporting and MRV preparation for power, petrochemicals, chemicals, building materials, steel, non-ferrous metals, papermaking, and aviation. This was a precondition for later expansion, not evidence that all these sectors were already in the compliance market.[18][14]

China’s second national-market compliance cycle covered 2021–2022 emissions and was completed in 2023. MEE proposed significantly tighter benchmarks for that cycle in March 2023, particularly for coal-fired power plants; smaller installations faced relatively stricter tightening than larger installations.[18]

China’s second national-market compliance cycle covered 2,257 key emitters.[61]

The evidence is inconsistent about industrial expansion in 2024. One account states that the national ETS had expanded to industrial sectors since 2024 but does not identify them. Another treatment models the 2024 extension as a reporting obligation with no carbon-pricing coverage, while several 2024 accounts describe the operational market as power-only through its first four years.[44][45][19][14][46]

The narrower interpretation is consistent with the first-cycle record and with the distinction between MRV coverage and allowance-surrender obligations. Reporting obligations, preparation for expansion, and references to future industrial inclusion should not be treated as proof that cement, steel, aluminium, or other industrial sectors faced a national carbon price by the end of 2024.[6][18][19]

**China’s national compliance market was power-sector based throughout the core 2021–2024 period, even though industrial-sector MRV and expansion preparations were advancing.** It was therefore far narrower in sectoral scope than California’s economy-wide upstream design and the EU ETS’s power, industrial, aviation, and 2024 maritime scope.[46][8][2]

### What was China’s allocation approach during 2020–2024?

China allocated national ETS allowances free of charge during the period. The system used output-based benchmarks rather than auctioning, with allocation linked to electricity generation and emissions intensity.[23][47]

The system is more accurately described as a tradable performance standard. A facility’s free allowance allocation was determined by verified emissions or actual output against the applicable benchmark factor. Firms could trade allowances, but the output-linked allocation meant that the volume of allowances could adjust with production rather than being fixed by an economy-wide absolute cap.[14]

China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment stated that its output-based benchmarking approach did not require firms’ absolute CO₂ emissions to decline and was designed not to constrain electricity supply.[121][120]

Four benchmark groups applied in the power sector: conventional coal-fired units at or below 300 MW; conventional coal-fired units above 300 MW; unconventional coal-fired units; and gas-fired units. Coal-fired plants generally faced less stringent benchmarks than gas sources.[14][23]

For the 2021–2022 Chinese compliance cycle, the allowance formula combined the electricity-supply benchmark, electricity supplied and adjustment coefficients with the heat-supply benchmark and heat supplied.[121]

China’s power-unit allowance allocation depended on electricity and heat output, fuel type, capacity category and adjustment factors. The Ministry stated that its 2021–2022 arrangement did not require firms’ absolute CO2 emissions to fall and was designed not to constrain electricity supply.[120][121]

The first allocation process used pre-allocation and later true-up. Entities initially received allowances equal to 70% of 2018 output multiplied by the applicable benchmark; allocations were then adjusted for actual generation in 2019 and 2020.[6]

The same structure was used in the 2023–2024 plan. Power-sector entities initially received allowances equal to 70% of the preceding year’s verified emissions, then received an ex-post adjustment reflecting actual 2023 and 2024 generation.[40]

The 2023–2024 plan changed the allocation basis from electricity supplied to generator-metered electricity generated and removed indirect emissions from purchased electricity. Those indirect emissions were estimated at under 5 MtCO₂ annually, or less than 0.1% of sector emissions.[120]

The 2023–2024 plan constrained the effective shortage faced by some generators. Gas-fired plants needed to surrender allowances only up to their benchmark-based free allocation. Coal-fired plants with free allocation below 80% of verified emissions had their allocation adjusted up to 80%, so the maximum shortfall was 20% of verified emissions.[40]

In March 2023, MEE proposed substantially tighter benchmarks for the 2021–2022 compliance period than those used for 2019–2020, particularly for coal-fired power plants. Smaller installations faced relatively stricter tightening than larger installations.[18][14]

China’s national-market benchmark for 2023 declined by about 1% on a like-for-like basis.[120]

China’s 2023–2024 allocation plan set a 2023 benchmark decline of about 1% on a like-for-like basis, compared with the previous 0.5% annual decline.[120]

The 2024 Interim Regulations signalled a gradual future transition from free allocation to a combined free-and-paid method. They did not establish an auction share or a timetable for paid allocation within 2024.[18][19]

**China’s allowance system was free, output-linked, and intensity-based, not auction-financed and not a fixed absolute-cap system.** As a result, it provided incentives to improve emissions efficiency per unit of generation but did not impose the same economy-wide scarcity condition as California’s and the EU’s declining caps.[14][23][18]

### What relevant implementation changes occurred in China’s national carbon

China’s first compliance cycle combined 2019 and 2020 emissions and permitted inter-year banking. It was a condensed, power-sector-only cycle with free allocation, CCER offsets, and spot trading in China Emission Allowances.[6]

China’s Measures for the Administration of Carbon Emissions Trading (Trial), which established rules for the national market’s registry, trading, settlement, emissions reporting and verification, took effect on 1 February 2021.[43]

Covered firms could use China Certified Emission Reductions (CCERs) to offset up to 5% of annual verified emissions. In the first compliance cycle, regulated firms used more than 32 million tonnes of CCERs, valued at more than CNY 900 million.[6]

MEE strengthened power-sector MRV in 2022 and 2023. December 2022 accounting and verification guidelines increased facility-level specificity, while a February 2023 reporting notice reinforced management of power-sector emissions reporting. Since 2022, key emitting entities had to submit monthly emissions reports and supporting documents within 40 calendar days after each month ended.[23]

The March 2023 benchmark proposal was a material tightening of allocation parameters, especially for coal plants. It was a scheduled implementation development rather than a temporary COVID-response measure.[18]

The State Council issued the Interim Regulations on Carbon Emissions Trading Management in February 2024, effective 1 May 2024. The regulations provided a legal framework for the national ETS and signalled possible future expansion by referring to carbon accounting in aviation.[18][14]

The regulations also strengthened enforcement. Data-reporting failures or falsification could attract fines from CNY 500,000 to ten times illegal gains. Failure to meet compliance obligations could lead to fines of five to ten times the market value of the shortfall, replacing a previous maximum fine of CNY 30,000; refusal to surrender after warning could also trigger deductions from the following year’s allocation and potential production suspension.[40][23]

China relaunched the CCER market in January 2024 after a six-year pause. Initial project types included forestation, mangrove cultivation, solar thermal power, and grid-connected offshore wind. ETS entities could use eligible CCERs for up to 5% of emissions.[18][14]

Banking rules changed in 2024. Banking had been unlimited in the first three compliance periods. From 2024, power-sector entities could bank up to 10,000 tonnes plus 1.5 times net sales over 2019–2024.[40]

China’s 2023–2024 allocation plan specified that entities with surplus allowances could carry into 2025 a basic amount of 10,000 tonnes plus 1.5 times their net sales in 2024–2025.[120]

China also had a daily price-stability mechanism that limited price swings to within 10%. MEE had announced possible tools—permit buybacks, auctioning, and CCER-rule adjustments—to cushion large price changes, but the record identifies these as options rather than an actual temporary intervention in response to COVID-19.[41][18]

**China’s implementation path from 2021 to 2024 centred on building a functioning power-sector compliance system: tighter benchmarks, more granular MRV, stronger penalties, a CCER relaunch, and an explicit banking limit for power-sector entities.** The market became more administratively robust, but auctioning, a broader paid-allocation system, and an absolute emissions cap remained prospective reforms rather than completed 2020–2024 changes.[23][18][40]

### What in the history

California’s design reflects state-level climate legislation and an explicitly multi-policy strategy. Assembly Bill 32, enacted in 2006, required California to return statewide emissions to 1990 levels by 2020. CARB’s scoping plan combined cap-and-trade with renewable-electricity standards, vehicle rules, low-carbon fuel standards, and building-energy-efficiency policy. CARB expected cap-and-trade to provide only 20% of the abatement required under AB 32, with complementary policies providing much of the remainder.[48][49]

California’s post-2020 context is SB 32’s target of 40% below 1990 GHG emissions by 2030 and the broader 2045 carbon-neutrality objective. The 2021–2030 cap decline was calibrated against that policy environment.[7][9][24]

The EU ETS arose from a different institutional setting: an EU-wide system operating since 2005, using common allocation rules across Member States. Its Phase 4 design made auctioning the central distribution mechanism while retaining free allocation where carbon-leakage risk was considered high.[32][2]

The EU ETS’s 2023 reform aligned it with the EU’s 2030 climate target and raised the ETS-specific objective to a 62% reduction below 2005. It also paired tighter internal carbon pricing with CBAM, which was intended gradually to replace free allocation for selected imports-exposed sectors.[35][2]

China’s national market followed a longer period of regional experimentation but began national trading only in July 2021. Its power-sector-first design reflected the centrality of coal-fired generation, the regulated structure of electricity markets, the need to improve emissions data, and the policy preference for intensity control while electricity demand continued to grow.[37][6][47]

China’s broader climate commitments were to peak carbon emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality by 2060. Its 2030 energy targets included 25% non-fossil fuel in primary energy and 1,200 GW of installed solar and wind capacity. These commitments existed alongside a national ETS that, during the requested period, principally rewarded efficiency improvements per unit of output rather than fuel switching from coal to renewables.[50][18]

**The central comparative point is that California and the EU used declining caps with mixed auction and free-allocation systems, while China began with a power-only, output-based performance standard.** Their different start dates, policy institutions, electricity-market structures, and complementary-policy roles mean that their 2020–2024 results cannot be treated as like-for-like observations.[9][8][14][2][6]

### Synthesis —

California entered 2020–2024 with broad upstream and point-source coverage, a declining post-2020 cap, quarterly auctions, industrial leakage protection, and utility consignment for ratepayer benefit. The EU ETS entered the same period as a mature multinational cap-and-trade system, then tightened its cap, revised free allocation, and added maritime transport in 2024. China differed fundamentally: its national market began trading only in July 2021, covered power generation, allocated allowances free through output-based benchmarks, and spent 2022–2024 strengthening MRV, enforcement, offsets, and banking rules.[8][1][22][2][14][6][23][40]

The relevant comparison is therefore not simply three carbon markets of different scale. It is between two mature declining-cap systems with auction revenue and one newer power-sector tradable performance standard whose allowance supply remained connected to output.

### Synthesis —

Across 2020–24, the EU ETS delivered the highest observed price levels and the sharpest market swings: it fell to €16.03/tCO2 during the March 2020 COVID-19 shock, exceeded €80/tCO2 on average in 2022–23, and fell to €64.8/tCO2e in 2024.[54][2][36] California’s auction price rose more gradually from floor-adjacent levels through 2021 to $38.35/tCO2e in May 2024, remaining far below its $88.22 hard ceiling.[7] China started later and remained lower priced in international terms despite reaching CNY104.25/tCO2 in October 2024.[59][10] **The period therefore shows three distinct price signals: EU high and volatile, California steadily rising within an explicit price collar, and China newly established and rising from a much lower base.**

## Emissions reductions versus baseline projections

Judgment — A baseline projection is a forecast of emissions without the policy; comparing it with observed emissions is the standard way to estimate abatement, meaning emissions avoided. A fall relative to an earlier year or to an emissions cap is an outcome indicator, but it is not by itself a carbon-price-attributable reduction against that counterfactual.

### What total emissions reductions did California's Cap-and-Trade Program achieve versus

California’s Legislative Analyst’s Office found no reliable estimate of emissions reductions achieved by California’s Cap-and-Trade Program alone, because direct regulations, economic conditions, and other climate policies interact with the program. In 2022, emissions subject to California Cap-and-Trade compliance obligations were 282 MMTCO2e, while California’s economy-wide GHG Inventory was 371 MMTCO2e; these are distinct covered-emissions and economy-wide inventory series.[64] Reported covered-entity emissions were consistently below the cap and fell faster than anticipated.[17]

CARB’s historical facility and entity emissions archive provided annual data through 2023 at the 31 December 2024 cutoff; its 2024 dataset was published only in November 2025.[80]

The 2017 Scoping Plan scenario assigned the Cap-and-Trade Program 236 MMTCO2e of cumulative reductions in 2021–2030, and the plan expected cap-and-trade to provide about 38% of its required reductions. The Standardized Regulatory Impact Assessment (SRIA) estimated 385 MMTCO2e of cumulative reductions from non-Cap-and-Trade measures and 621 MMTCO2e in total over 2021–2030.[24][30][65] The SRIA’s 2030 Reference Scenario was 389 MMTCO2e, compared with the 260-MMTCO2e SB 32 limit; it estimated non-Cap-and-Trade policies at 320 MMTCO2e in 2030 and consequently assigned the program 60 MMTCO2e of reductions in that year.[65] These are prospective pathway assumptions, not measured 2020–2024 outcomes against a no-program baseline.[24][30][65]

AB 32 required California to return statewide greenhouse-gas emissions to 1990 levels by 2020. CARB reported that California achieved this target in 2016, four years early, and stated a 2030 target of a further 40% reduction below 1990 levels; a Congressional Research Service account reports achievement in 2014, six years early.[11][7][15] **The California result is therefore a demonstrated statewide and covered-emissions decline, not a quantified 2020–2024 carbon-price abatement total.**

California’s reported covered-sector emissions dropped during the COVID-19 disruption in 2020 and rebounded in 2021. A year-on-year increase from the unusually low 2020 level does not, by itself, show that the Cap-and-Trade Program weakened.

California’s statewide GHG emissions had decreased by approximately 18% since the emissions cap first took effect in 2013, as of 2022, the most recent emissions year.[7]

California estimated that GHG reductions from projects funded by cap-and-trade allowance auctions totalled nearly 110 million metric tons of carbon-dioxide equivalence by December 2024.[17]

CARB set the 2021–2030 caps using extensive modelling that took account of reductions from companion policies in the 2017 Scoping Plan Update and permanently removed allowances from general circulation because emissions were expected to be below the 2020 target. The nominal cap is the new allowances introduced in a given year; emissions outcomes differ from it because banked allowances and offsets are available.[66][67]

CARB’s annual California allowance budgets were 334.2 million tCO2e allowances in 2020, 320.8 million in 2021, 307.5 million in 2022, 294.1 million in 2023 and 280.7 million in 2024. The budget fell by 53.5 million allowances, or 16.0%, from 2020 to 2024; it measures legal allowance supply, not avoided emissions.[114]

The linked California–Québec holding-limit calculation used a combined annual budget of 388.94 million units in 2020 and 332.25 million in 2024; those combined figures are not California’s program cap.[113]

### What total emissions reductions did China's national carbon market achieve

China’s national market used an intensity-based, output-linked allocation approach: the allowance total was aggregated from facility allocations and adjusted to actual production. It therefore did not impose a fixed, declining absolute cap; lower emissions per unit of output need not reduce total emissions as output grows.[40][37]

China’s national market initially covered approximately 4.5 billion metric tons of annual power-sector CO2 emissions; this coverage figure is not an estimate of avoided emissions in 2021–2024.[23][40] The estimated allocation total rose from about 4,500 MtCO2 in 2020 to about 8,000 MtCO2 in 2024. That increase indicates changed coverage and/or production-linked allocations, not an increase or decrease in aggregate emissions abatement.[40]

China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment reported national-market allowance allocations of 5.096 billion tonnes in 2021 and 5.104 billion tonnes in 2022, against verified emissions requiring surrender of 5.094 billion tonnes and 5.091 billion tonnes, respectively.[61]

For 2021, China issued 5.096 billion tCO2e of national-market allowances against 5.094 billion tCO2e of verified emissions requiring surrender; for 2022, the respective figures were 5.104 billion and 5.091 billion tCO2e.[61]

The Ministry reported allowance surpluses of 1.47 million tonnes in 2021 and 12.98 million tonnes in 2022, equal to 0.03% and 0.25% of allowances issued in those years, respectively.[61]

In China’s national market, 5.096 billion tonnes of allowances were issued against 5.094 billion tonnes of verified emissions in 2021, leaving a reported surplus of 1.47 million tonnes.[61]

In 2022, China issued 5.104 billion tonnes of allowances against 5.091 billion tonnes of verified emissions, leaving a reported surplus of 12.98 million tonnes.[61]

China’s economy-wide CO2 intensity was 48.4% below its 2005 level at end-2020, achieving the country’s 40–45% intensity objective. Preliminary official accounting then put 2021 carbon-emissions intensity 3.8% below 2020 and 50.8% below 2005. These are economy-wide intensity outcomes, not national-ETS covered-emissions reductions against a baseline.[41][43] Assessments of the first compliance cycle said the market had begun to promote emission reductions and low-carbon transition, while also stating that its overall emissions-reduction impact had not yet been fully assessed.[6][73] **China therefore has no comparable total 2020–2024 national-market abatement figure versus a no-market baseline.**

The Ministry reported that, by 2023, national thermal-power carbon intensity was 2.38% below its 2018 level, electricity carbon intensity was 8.78% below its 2018 level, and heat-supply carbon intensity had fallen by 2.5% per year; it also reported cumulative CO2 reductions of 250 million tonnes.[120]

### Synthesis —

The presented records do not produce a 2020–2024 programme-attributable reduction against a sector-matched no-policy projection for California’s Cap-and-Trade Program, the EU ETS, or China’s national carbon market. California’s quantified figures are planned Scoping Plan contributions rather than ex-post attribution; the EU supplies measured annual installation emissions but no matching baseline-projection series; and China’s production-adjusted intensity design makes allowance allocations unsuitable as total-emissions outcomes.[17][24][30][2][40][37]

The EU ETS’s observed installation-emissions decline was 157 MtCO2e, or about 12.5%, between the UK-excluded 2020 total and 2023. COVID-19 disruption, recovery, renewable deployment, and fuel switching all interacted with the carbon price. California’s cap provides an emissions constraint, whereas China’s intensity-based system principally measures improvement per unit of output rather than guaranteed absolute decline.[2][33][40][37]

### How much revenue did China's national carbon market generate during

China’s national ETS generated no national government auction revenue during 2020–2024 because the national market did not auction allowances.[44][45][41] This is not the same as saying that no carbon-market transactions occurred: trading between covered entities and transactions in offsets are private-market values, not receipts available to a public climate programme.[26][6]

China’s 2024 Interim Regulations signalled a gradual future transition toward a mixture of free and paid allocation, but did not specify either a timetable or a paid-allocation share.[18][14] The regulations therefore changed the prospective legal direction of allocation without creating a recorded auction-funded climate-programme budget in 2024.[18][14]

In the first compliance cycle, firms used more than 32 million tonnes of Chinese Certified Emission Reduction (CCER) credits for settlement, valued at more than CNY900 million.[6] CCERs are offsets—credits for qualifying reductions or removals used in place of a limited portion of compliance allowances—not government auction revenue. **China’s national market consequently imposed a carbon-compliance framework without the fiscal recycling mechanism that characterized California and the EU ETS.**

### How was California's Cap-and-Trade Program revenue allocated to climate programs

California directed revenue from auctions of state-owned allowances to the GGRF. The Legislature appropriated most GGRF revenue to climate-related programmes administered by state agencies; that programme portfolio is called California Climate Investments.[7] California law required at least 35% of GGRF money to benefit and be located in disadvantaged or low-income communities, or to benefit low-income households.[7]

Approximately 65% of annual GGRF revenue was continuously committed to selected transportation, transit, housing, and safe-and-affordable-drinking-water programmes, and US$200 million per fiscal year was committed to forest health and wildfire prevention.[7] Cumulatively as of November 2024, California Climate Investments had invested US$12.8 billion in 590,703 projects, with more than US$9.2 billion reaching disadvantaged and low-income communities.[9] These cumulative investment totals are distinct from gross auction receipts.[9][7]

California’s 2022 revenue-use analysis reported a dual allocation structure:

- **Climate investment and clean energy:** close to 70%, or US$4.254 billion, financed California Climate Investments and clean-energy and energy-efficiency programmes financed through the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC). [74]
- **Household ratepayer protection:** around 29%, or US$1.770 billion, financed Electric Climate Credits and Natural Gas Climate Credits for utility customers. [74]
- **Business assistance:** 3%, or US$177 million, financed the Small Business Electric Credit and Electric Industry Assistance for business utility customers. [74]

Utility allocations followed a separate route from state-owned allowance revenue. Investor-owned electric utilities received free allowances but had to consign all of them to quarterly auctions; the revenue returned to customers as California Climate Credits.[22][7] In 2023, natural-gas suppliers had to consign 65% of their allocation, while investor-owned electric utilities consigned 100%.[19] **California thus used carbon revenue both to finance emissions-reducing investment and to cushion electricity and gas customers from the policy’s energy-price effects.**

By November 2024, more than **71.9%** of California Climate Investments funding—over US$9.2 billion of US$12.8 billion—had reached disadvantaged and low-income communities.[9]

### How was EU ETS revenue allocated to climate programs during

EU ETS proceeds primarily flowed to Member-State budgets, with designated shares financing the Innovation Fund, the Modernisation Fund, and the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s REPowerEU component.[76] Before 5 June 2023, Member States were encouraged to use at least 50% of receipts for decarbonisation and energy-transition investment.

From June 2023, they were required to use 100% of collected revenue, or an equivalent financial value, for climate action and energy transformation, except amounts used to compensate electricity-intensive industries for indirect carbon costs.[2]

In 2023, €30.9 billion of Member-State receipts was allocated to climate action and energy transformation, while €2.1 billion financed indirect-carbon-cost aid.[2] Of the climate-and-energy amount, Member States reported €22.2 billion disbursed and €0.8 billion committed in 2023.[2] The disbursed amount comprised €9.7 billion for energy supply, grids and storage; €5.1 billion for public transport and mobility; €2.7 billion for social support and just transition; €2.3 billion for building energy efficiency, heating and cooling; €0.7 billion for industrial decarbonisation; and €1.4 billion for other purposes.[2]

From January 2021 to 30 June 2024, the Modernisation Fund confirmed €12.7 billion for 176 schemes and projects, mostly renewable electricity, energy efficiency, storage and energy networks.[2] The Innovation Fund’s first renewable hydrogen competitive auction ran from 23 November 2023 to 8 February 2024 and selected seven bids for €720 million in EU contributions.[2] **The 2023 revision converted climate-oriented revenue use from a minimum-share expectation into the governing purpose for relevant Member-State ETS receipts.**

### How was China's national carbon market revenue allocated to climate

China allocated no national ETS auction revenue to climate programmes during 2020–2024 because national allowances were free rather than sold by auction.[44][45][41] Free allocation can create a compliance incentive and a tradable asset, but it does not raise public revenue from the allocated allowances.[26]

The Interim Regulations effective 1 May 2024 indicated a gradual move toward combining free and paid allocation.[18] That policy direction could eventually create auction revenue, but the regulations neither set a paid share nor created a 2020–2024 national spending stream for climate programmes.[18][14] **China’s allocation model therefore left climate-programme funding to instruments outside national ETS auction revenue during this period.**

### Synthesis —

California and the EU ETS both translated allowance sales into climate-related public finance, but their fiscal architecture differed. California divided value between GGRF-funded California Climate Investments and utility credits that protected ratepayers; its 2022 allocation directed close to 70% to climate investments and related clean-energy programmes and around 29% to household credits.[74] The EU ETS generated much larger annual auction receipts and, after June 2023, bound relevant Member-State revenue to climate and energy purposes, while retaining an indirect-cost-aid exception for electricity-intensive industry.[2][76] China’s free-allocation national ETS created neither comparable auction receipts nor an auction-funded climate-programme allocation during 2020–2024.[44][45][41] The strongest fiscal comparison is therefore not total trading value, but whether allowance supply was auctioned and how the resulting public receipts were earmarked and spent.

## Compliance among covered entities

Compliance is the share of regulated participants—or, in some systems, the share of regulated emissions—that meets the duty to surrender allowances. An allowance is the tradable permit covering one tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent emissions (tCO2e). The headline rates are high in all three systems, but their denominators differ: companies in California, emissions in the EU’s principal reported measure, and both allowances and entities in China.

### What were compliance rates among entities covered by California's Cap-and-Trade

California Air Resources Board (CARB) reported **100% compliance for the 2018–2020 compliance period**: every covered business surrendered the required compliance instruments for its full three-year greenhouse-gas obligation.[11] A covered entity is a registered entity above the Regulation’s applicable emissions threshold, with an annual obligation determined from its covered emissions.[75] One allowance or eligible offset credit covers one metric tonne of CO2e.[4]

California uses an interim-and-final settlement structure. First, except in the year after a compliance period ends, an entity must surrender instruments equal to 30% of the preceding year’s verified emissions by the start of November. Second, it must surrender instruments for the remaining emissions by the start of November after the three-year period closes.[9] CARB described compliance at every compliance event through its November 2020 seventh event as near 100%.[21]

California’s automatic penalty is an untimely surrender obligation: an entity that misses the required amount must surrender the missing instruments plus three additional instruments for every missing one. CARB reported that only one entity had been subjected to this obligation and that it ultimately surrendered the required instruments.[21] **California therefore achieved universal company-level compliance in the period ending in 2020, while its subsequent published characterization indicates near-universal event compliance rather than a separate annual percentage for 2021–2024.**[11][21]

CARB’s programme-data page lists Compliance Reports for 2021, 2022, 2018–2020, and 2024; the 2021, 2022, and 2018–2020 reports were updated on 6 December 2024.[79] Covered entities report facility-level emissions annually, and a CARB-accredited independent third-party verifier verifies the submitted data.[80]

CARB also reported that the programme had achieved nearly 100% compliance at every compliance event to date.[64]

### What were compliance rates among entities covered by the EU

The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) requires each operator to submit an independently verified emissions report and surrender one allowance for every covered tCO2e. Operators report by 31 March in the following year; under the 2023 ETS revision, the surrender deadline moved from 30 April to 30 September from 2024 onward. An operator that does not surrender on time faces a EUR 100 penalty per uncovered tonne and must still surrender the missing allowances.[2]

The EU’s principal quantified outcome is emissions-weighted, not entity-weighted. During Phase 3, including 2020, operators responsible for more than 99% of stationary-installation and aviation emissions complied in most years, despite the COVID-19 crisis affecting the 2020 and 2021 compliance cycles.[12] The European Commission described the 2023 compliance cycle as very high.[2]

Enforcement data show that high aggregate compliance did not mean every operator complied. In 2023, 38 installations in 10 countries were fined for excess emissions, while nine aircraft operators in four countries received excess-emissions penalties.[2] Fourteen countries reported 75 infringements resulting in penalties, formal warnings, or formal notices; 43 reported fines totalled EUR 88.8 million, including seven aviation fines worth EUR 0.6 million.[2] Competent authorities also made conservative emissions estimates for 47 installations, approximately 0.6% of installations, in 2023.[2]

The Commission lists annual compliance-data files for 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023, published between May 2021 and October 2024.[5] **The EU ETS thus combined over-99% emissions-weighted compliance in its established Phase-3 measure with active enforcement against individual non-compliers; this is not the same measure as a percentage of covered entities.**[12][2]

From 1 January 2024, municipal-waste-incineration installations above 20 MW rated thermal output had to monitor and report emissions under EU ETS 1, but they had no allowance-surrender obligation. They therefore expanded the monitored population without adding entities to the surrender-compliance denominator.[81]

### What were compliance rates among entities covered by China's national

China’s national ETS began trading on 16 July 2021 and completed its first compliance cycle on 30 December 2021. That first cycle settled allowances for emissions in both 2019 and 2020 in one compliance period, with inter-year banking of allowances permitted; thus, 2020 was an emissions year in the first national obligation, while surrender was settled after trading began in 2021.[6] Covered firms must monitor and report CO2 emissions, with government-certified technical experts inspecting and verifying those reports; randomized enterprise–verifier matching was intended to reduce collusion.[82][37]

China’s first compliance cycle recorded a 99.5% compliance rate. The reported gap was 0.5% of verified allowances, equivalent to 43.4 million Chinese Emission Allowances (CEAs), so this measure is allowance-weighted rather than explicitly a share of firms.[6] The second compliance cycle had successfully concluded by July 2024.[62] For the 2023 compliance year, completed in December 2024, 99.98% of covered entities surrendered their compliance units.[40]

High reported surrender coexisted with integrity and enforcement problems. China’s market faced concerns over emissions-data accuracy, monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV), data fabrication, collusion with third-party verifiers, and late allowance surrender.[73][82] The national ETS Administrative Measures set fines of CNY 10,000–30,000 for non-compliance or falsified information.[41] **China’s quantified results show near-complete compliance in both its first-cycle allowance measure and its 2023 entity-surrender measure, but those two percentages use different denominators.**[6][40]

## COVID-19 disruption, recovery and market rules

Judgment — COVID-19 tested carbon markets in two ways. First, shutdowns reduced fuel use and industrial output, lowering emissions and the demand for allowances—the permits that covered entities must surrender for each tonne emitted. Second, recovery, energy-price shocks and policy reforms changed demand again. The key distinction is between an observed market outcome, such as an undersubscribed auction, and a rule change deliberately made in response to the pandemic.

### How did California's Cap-and-Trade Program experience the economic disruptions of

California’s March 2020 shutdown produced a severe statewide contraction: unemployment reached 16% and the economy lost 2.5 million jobs. By the second quarter of 2020, the state had 61,000 fewer businesses than at the end of 2019, a 5% decline.[83]

The contemporaneous cap-and-trade signal was exceptionally weak auction demand. CARB reported that pandemic-related uncertainty and reduced economic activity were reflected in the May and August 2020 auctions, in which 32 million allowances went unsold. In May, 21,161,000 of 57,540,731 current allowances and 1,763,000 of 8,672,250 advance allowances sold, at $16.68 per allowance.[66][84] This was an auction result, not a temporary reduction in the cap or a special COVID-19 supply intervention.

The May 2020 California–Québec joint auction settled at its reserve price of US$16.68 per current-vintage allowance.[84]

Economic recovery was relatively rapid in aggregate, although uneven across regions and service industries. The Public Policy Institute of California described the state as near pre-pandemic conditions by early 2022, while noting continuing weakness in leisure, hospitality and personal services.[83] Allowance prices later rose above $30 per metric tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent (tCO2e) in 2023, reached nearly $42 in February 2024, then fell to $37 in May and $32 in November 2024. The Congressional Research Service attributes the higher recent prices to recovery from recession and proposed rules that would increase program stringency.[7]

California’s utility-allocation system continued to cushion customer energy-cost effects during this period. Investor-owned electric and gas utilities received free allowances for ratepayer benefit, consigned them to quarterly auctions, and returned the proceeds through California Climate Credits—twice yearly on electricity bills and once yearly on gas bills.[22]

**The California evidence therefore shows a severe pandemic-era economic shock and unusually low May 2020 allowance demand, followed by a recovery-era price increase; it does not turn the 2020 auction outcome into a measured COVID effect on covered emissions or a pandemic-specific market intervention.**[83][24][7]

CARB also distinguished the pandemic shock from the 2016–17 period of political uncertainty: covered-sector emissions decreased in 2020, whereas they had not decreased in 2016–17 and allowance demand was therefore delayed rather than eliminated.[66]

The U.S. Energy Information Administration identified increased industrial activity during the post-pandemic recovery as a source of higher allowance demand. California Climate Investments funding also supported outreach plans and processes for underserved communities during social-distancing and stay-at-home orders.[85][86]

### How did the EU ETS experience the economic disruptions of

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) experienced a large but temporary COVID-era contraction. Verified emissions from stationary installations fell from 1,530 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (MtCO2e) in 2019 to 1,356 MtCO2e in 2020, a reported 11.4% decline. Aviation emissions fell 63% year on year in 2020.[2] The fall in aviation and industrial emissions was attributed to the pandemic’s temporary economic slowdown.[33]

European Union Allowance (EUA) prices also fell sharply as the outbreak emerged. They stopped declining by the end of March 2020, rose markedly from the end of May, and returned to their pre-pandemic level between June and July as restrictions were relaxed and economic activity revived.[54] A study of January 24–July 23, 2020 found that a 1% rise in confirmed cases was associated with a 0.0185% fall in EUA prices, while a 1% rise in deaths was associated with a 0.0323% fall; these are modelled associations rather than a complete estimate of the pandemic’s total price effect.[54]

Recovery raised emissions in 2021 but did not restore them fully to their pre-pandemic level. On a UK-excluded comparable basis, EU ETS-covered emissions increased 6.6% in 2021 relative to 2020 but remained 5.6% below 2019. Industrial-installation emissions rose 4.6%, while power-sector emissions rose 8.4%, partly because electricity demand recovered and high fossil-fuel prices increased coal use.[12] Aviation recovered more strongly: emissions rose 30% in 2021, 77% in 2022 and 9.5% in 2023, reaching 54.1 MtCO2e in 2023 and approaching the adjusted 2019 level.[2]

The later period was not simply a continuation of post-COVID recovery. The 2022 energy crisis sharply increased gas and electricity costs, reduced industrial production and weakened EUA demand through 2023.[58] In 2024, stationary-source emissions fell 5.7% year on year, largely alongside increased wind and solar generation and lower coal and gas use; emissions from installations were around 50% below 2005 levels at the start of 2025.[20]

The EU recovery plan also mattered to expectations. The European Commission proposed an approximately €750 billion fiscal recovery plan on May 27, 2020, framed as support for green transition and consistent with EU greenhouse-gas reduction and 2050 climate-neutrality goals.[54] The cited empirical study finds that EUA-price recovery accelerated after that date, though economic factors and energy prices also affected the market.[54]

**The EU ETS saw the clearest measured COVID interruption: emissions and EUA prices fell in early 2020, prices recovered by mid-year, emissions rebounded in 2021 without returning to the comparable 2019 level, and later energy and renewable-power developments became more important than pandemic recovery alone.**[2][54][12][58][20]

The European Commission recorded the early price shock more precisely: the EUA price reached €14.71 on March 23, 2020, compared with a €24.84 2019 average. A separate analysis recorded a fall from €24.07 per tonne to €15.25 per tonne in March and recovery to €22.10 per tonne by June 2020.[55][87]

Lower allowance demand during the 2020 COVID-19 disruption increased the EU-wide allowance surplus to 1.579 billion allowances.[55]

The Commission attributed the 2020 stationary-installation decline both to lower electricity consumption during the pandemic and to existing decarbonisation trends. Electricity-and-heat emissions fell by more than 15%; industrial-installation emissions fell 7%; and EU27 GDP fell 6%. It also identified coal-to-gas switching and replacement of fossil fuels with renewable energy as contributors: hard coal represented 16% of EU ETS emissions in both 2019 and 2020, compared with 29% in 2013, while natural gas represented 35% in 2020, compared with 24% in 2013.[55]

### How did China's national carbon market experience the economic disruptions

China’s national carbon market was not operating as a national trading market when COVID-19 first struck. The system, announced in 2017 and intended to begin operating in 2020 in the power sector, was delayed by COVID-19 alongside stakeholder bargaining and organisational restructuring; national trading began on July 16, 2021, and the first compliance cycle concluded on December 30, 2021.[38][41][6]

China’s economy-wide emissions fell sharply during the first pandemic quarter. Estimated national CO2 emissions were 257.7 Mt, or 11.0%, lower in the first quarter of 2020 than in the first quarter of 2019. Secondary industry accounted for 186.8 Mt of that decrease; power generation fell 8.4% and cement production 23.9%.[88] Ground-transport emissions across 31 provinces fell 65.1 Mt, or 32.7%, with freight recovering faster than passenger transport.[88]

Emissions rebounded as restrictions eased and stimulus was introduced. The cited study reports that emissions remained below the pre-pandemic mean in the first quarter but were above the stated mean in the second quarter of 2020.[88] Energy-consumption growth then more than doubled to 4% per year over 2020–2023, and China’s emissions increased by 565 Mt in 2023; one third of that increase was associated with lower hydropower output amid drought and floods.[14]

The market’s early trading pattern was heavily concentrated around compliance. November–December 2021 and November–December 2022 together accounted for 83% of reported trading volume.[50] That pattern is consistent with entities trading principally to settle compliance obligations, rather than with an active market response specifically driven by COVID-19.

**China’s pandemic-era emissions decline was primarily an economy-wide reduction in industrial, power and transport activity during a delayed market launch, so it cannot be credited as a national-ETS outcome; the subsequent rebound reflects recovery in the wider economy rather than a demonstrated carbon-market effect.**[38][88]

### Did California's Cap-and-Trade Program make temporary allowance adjustments in response

California did not make an identified temporary allowance-supply adjustment in response to COVID-19 or subsequent recovery. Its low-demand rules instead operated as standing program architecture: the state does not sell allowances below the annually rising auction floor, and unsold allowances can be removed from circulation. If demand does not exceed supply for 24 months, unsold auction allowances are permanently removed from auction and become available only through higher-priced reserve sales.[21]

California also retained high-demand protections. Reserve tiers can release allowances when demand is high, and a hard price ceiling established in 2021 permits the California Air Resources Board (CARB) to sell an unlimited number of additional compliance instruments if auction demand reaches the ceiling. The ceiling was $88.22 in 2024 and had not been reached by the reported date.[7][21]

The material program changes were structural rather than temporary pandemic relief. CARB states that program stringency doubled beginning in 2021, while the cap was scheduled to decline by about 13.4 MtCO2e annually during 2021–2030, averaging roughly 4% annually.[21][1] The 2021 hard ceiling limits price exposure, but if triggered could relax the effective emissions cap by creating additional compliance instruments.[7]

**COVID-19 coincided with weak California auction demand, but California relied on pre-existing allowance-withholding, reserve-tier and price-ceiling mechanisms rather than introducing temporary pandemic easing of its cap or allowance supply.**[24][21][7]

### Did the EU ETS make temporary allowance adjustments in response

The EU ETS did not adopt an identified temporary, COVID-specific allowance intervention. Instead, it relied on the Market Stability Reserve (MSR), a standing rule-based mechanism operating since 2019. The MSR adjusts auction supply according to the total number of allowances in circulation (TNAC): allowances are withdrawn from auctions and placed in the reserve when the surplus is high, or released and auctioned when it is low.[2]

Under the operative thresholds, a TNAC above 1,096 million allowances leads to withdrawal of 24% of the surplus volume over 12 months; a TNAC below 400 million releases 100 million allowances from the reserve.[20] The European Commission describes the MSR as improving resilience to sudden supply-demand shocks, including the 2020 COVID-19 downturn.[2]

The recovery-period application of the rule was substantial. The 2023 TNAC was 1.11 billion allowances, above the trigger threshold, producing a planned withdrawal of 267 million allowances from auctions between September 2024 and August 2025.[2] This was threshold-driven, not a discretionary COVID-recovery measure. The 2023 ETS revision strengthened the MSR and continued annual invalidation of reserve allowances above the applicable threshold.[2]

**The EU ETS used an automatic surplus-management rule rather than a temporary COVID allowance measure: the MSR buffered demand shocks but tightened auction supply when the allowance surplus triggered its pre-set thresholds.**[2]

The Commission reported the pandemic-era operation of the standing MSR: reduced 2020 emissions would produce an intake of 379 million allowances during 2021–22, and auction volumes in 2021 were reduced by nearly 40%, or close to 320 million allowances. These were automatic effects of the surplus rule rather than a new COVID-specific measure.[55]

The EU ETS also adjusted free allocation when industrial production changed significantly. Since 2021, a 15% increase or decrease in production triggered an adjustment, with subsequent 5% intervals. Actual free allocation was 545.7 million allowances in 2021, 542.4 million in 2022, 537.0 million in 2023 and 499.7 million in 2024, below initial allocations of 559.6 million, 558.9 million, 558.2 million and 557.5 million respectively. The Commission linked reduced production in some sectors primarily in 2022–23 to the pandemic and the energy crisis, with the two-year production average delaying the full allocation effect by two years.[68]

### Did China's national carbon market make temporary allowance adjustments in

China temporarily allowed allowance borrowing in 2021 and 2022. In the second compliance period, covering calendar years 2021 and 2022, regulated entities could borrow against their 2023 allowance budget to address financing hardships stemming from global fuel-supply disruptions. This was a compliance-timing flexibility, not a documented increase in national allowance supply.[40][60]

For China’s 2021–2022 compliance cycle, an entity whose calculated annual allocation was below 80% of verified emissions received allowances equal to 80% of those emissions; the Ministry of Ecology and Environment estimated that this would ease the burden for 306 enterprises in 2021. Entities with a shortfall of at least 10% and genuine operating difficulty could advance up to 50% of their shortage from their 2023 preliminary allocation.[121]

In 2021, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment announced possible market-stability tools: permit buybacks, auctioning and changes to China Certified Emission Reduction (CCER) offset rules.[18][14] CCERs are tradable credits from eligible emissions-reduction projects that covered entities may use within prescribed limits instead of surrendering all allowances.

The announced tools indicate that China was considering ways to cushion large price swings, but the stated measures are options rather than reported deployments.[18][14]

**China’s identifiable temporary flexibility was 2021–22 borrowing; its timing places it in the recovery period, but it is not documented as a COVID-caused allowance-supply intervention.**[40][18][14]

### Did California's Cap-and-Trade Program modify banking rules in response to

California continued to allow banking: covered entities may save unused allowances for future compliance, and allowances do not expire. Banking remained subject to holding limits that decline annually with the cap, while borrowing future-vintage allowances was prohibited.[7][9][89]

California’s holding limit applies to the allowances held by an entity or its corporately associated group.[115][113]

California’s entity-level allowance-holding limits were based on the combined California–Québec allowance budget.[113]

The distinction between banking and borrowing matters. Banking lets an entity carry a surplus forward; borrowing would let it use future compliance instruments for past emissions. California retained the former flexibility but prohibited the latter throughout the period.[9][89]

A 2023 policy brief proposed retiring unused banked allowances after three years or ending banking altogether, but these were reform proposals, not adopted 2020–2024 banking changes.[30]

**California preserved non-expiring banking with annually declining holding limits and no borrowing, rather than modifying banking rules in response to COVID-19 or recovery.**[7][9][30]

### Did the EU ETS modify banking rules in response to

The EU ETS retained longstanding banking. Companies could keep spare allowances for future use, banking had been permitted since 2008, and borrowing was not allowed.[3][20][8]

The Phase 3–Phase 4 transition did not erase existing holdings. Allowances issued before 2021 remained valid indefinitely, account holdings continued beyond January 1, 2021, and Phase 3 allowances could continue to be used without restriction.[5] A new Registry Regulation also allowed a positive compliance status—over-surrendered allowances—to carry into the next year and trading period rather than being deleted at the period transition.[5]

This registry change improved continuity of account compliance status, but it should not be confused with a pandemic-specific banking reform. The MSR instead governed aggregate auction supply, and one assessment argues that it was not well suited to shocks such as COVID-19, economic downturns or coal-plant closures.[32][33]

**The EU ETS preserved firms’ ability to bank allowances and prohibited borrowing; its relevant COVID-era flexibility was aggregate MSR supply management, not a temporary change in banking rights.**[20][8][32]

### Did China's national carbon market modify banking rules in response

China’s banking record during 2020–2024 is internally inconsistent across the supplied accounts. One account describes the national ETS as not allowing banking or borrowing, while stating that the Ministry of Ecology and Environment intended gradually to introduce banking.[18][14] Another reports that banking was unlimited during the first three compliance periods and that sector-specific limits began in 2024.[40]

Under the latter account, from 2024 power-sector entities could bank up to 10,000 tonnes plus 1.5 times net sales over 2019–2024. Steel, cement and aluminium-smelter entities could bank up to 100,000 tonnes plus 1.5 times net sales over the same period.[40] These limits were an ordinary-rule change from unlimited to capped banking, not a reported temporary COVID response.

China’s 2023–2024 banking scheme allowed restricted carry-forward into 2025, including a basic 10,000-tonne amount for entities with surplus allowances and an additional amount tied to net sales.[120]

**The reported accounts disagree on whether banking was available in China’s early national ETS, but they agree on no identified COVID-specific banking intervention; the detailed 2024 account describes a shift to sector-specific limits rather than pandemic relief.**[18][14][40]

### Synthesis

Judgment — The three systems handled COVID-19 differently because they entered the shock at different stages of institutional maturity. California’s mature market absorbed low auction demand through standing floor, reserve and holding rules, without temporary pandemic changes. The EU ETS experienced the most clearly measured emissions and price shock, then relied on the MSR’s automatic TNAC-based auction adjustments rather than ad hoc relief. China’s national market was delayed around its intended 2020 launch, making national emissions changes especially unsuitable as indicators of market performance; its identifiable temporary flexibility was 2021–22 borrowing.[24][21][2][38][40]

1. Treat pandemic-era emissions reductions as economic-shutdown effects unless a market-specific causal estimate identifies the carbon-pricing contribution.
2. Distinguish automatic reserve rules from discretionary emergency allowance changes when comparing market resilience.
3. Treat China’s early-period market outcomes separately from California and the EU ETS because its national system was still establishing operations during the initial COVID shock.

## Renewable energy mandates and coal phase-out schedules

Judgment — Renewable-energy mandates and coal-retirement policies change the demand for emissions allowances: they reduce fossil generation directly, while an emissions trading system (ETS) prices the remaining emissions. The resulting interaction matters because a hard cap can preserve the quantity limit, but complementary policies can shift where abatement occurs and can lower allowance demand and prices. During 2020–2024, no comprehensive California statewide coal-retirement timetable is established; the EU had no EU-wide coal-retirement date, although Germany’s 2020 agreement set a 2038 end date and Portugal completed its coal phase-out on 30 November 2021; and China had no dated full coal-power phase-out.[7][91][41][92] These coal-policy positions are distinct from prospective electricity-policy targets, including California’s 60% renewable-electricity target for 2030, the EU’s binding 42.5% renewable-energy target for 2030, and China’s 2030 renewable-capacity and non-fossil-energy targets.[22][93][18]

### How did renewable energy mandates interact with California's Cap-and-Trade Program

California’s Renewables Portfolio Standard (RPS) requires utilities to procure an increasing share of renewable electricity, while SB 100 set the 60% renewable-electricity target for 2030 and the 100% clean-electricity target for 2045. CARB’s 2024 regulatory analysis explicitly treated the cleaner electricity mix associated with the 2030 SB 100 target as an input to proposed electric-distribution-utility allowance-allocation assumptions.[21][22]

CARB also designs annual Cap-and-Trade caps using economy-wide modelling that incorporates expected reductions from the RPS, Low Carbon Fuel Standard, and Advanced Clean Cars program. The mandate therefore drives renewable procurement, while the carbon price is intended to influence dispatch—the order in which generators run—toward the lowest-carbon electricity.[21] **California consequently used the RPS/SB 100 and Cap-and-Trade as coordinated but distinct instruments: procurement rules change the generation mix and the cap anticipates those reductions.**

The interaction also has a price consequence. Successful complementary policies reduce covered emissions and therefore reduce demand for allowances, which can put downward pressure on allowance prices; California policy analysis has long described Cap-and-Trade as the economy-wide “backstop” that fills the remaining gap after direct regulations deliver much of the abatement.[48][7][17] SB 100 adds a regional safeguard: the transition to renewable and zero-carbon electricity must not increase greenhouse-gas emissions elsewhere on the western electricity grid.[94] **The cap preserves the statewide emissions constraint, but complementary electricity policies can reduce the carbon-price signal needed to meet it.**

Allowance allocation embedded the RPS in the program’s electricity-sector implementation. CARB allocates free allowances to electric utilities for ratepayer benefit according to anticipated compliance cost or cost burden, using each utility’s demand and supply forecast and assuming compliance with mandated RPS requirements. At its 5 October 2023 workshop, CARB staff evaluated an update to align the allocation calculation with the SB 100 RPS target, revised Integrated Resource Planning targets, and new demand and supply data; the regulation then specified utility allocations for vintages 2021–2030.[95]

### How did renewable energy mandates interact with the EU ETS

The EU ETS operated alongside Renewable Energy Directive policies, energy-efficiency rules, and the Fit for 55 and REPowerEU packages. In 2023, renewables supplied 44.7% of EU electricity; the European Commission reported that emissions from ETS power and industrial installations fell 16.5% from 2022, driven largely by accelerated renewable deployment in the power sector and facilitated by a sustained carbon-price signal.[2]

This is a complementary relationship: renewable deployment displaces fossil generation and cuts covered emissions, while the EU Allowance (EUA) price increases the operating cost of carbon-intensive generation and strengthens low-carbon investment incentives. Yet it is also a two-sided market interaction, because renewable and energy-efficiency policies reduce firms’ need for EUAs. Historical analysis attributes the largest share of 2005–2018 power-sector emissions reductions to renewable deployment, with the ETS contributing but not serving as the principal driver; national renewable and efficiency policies have also reduced allowance demand and depressed prices.[98][33][32] **The 2023 decline reflects a combined policy package, not an outcome that can be credited to the EU ETS alone.**

ETS revenue reinforced this relationship through dedicated investment mechanisms. The Modernisation Fund disbursed €12.7 billion for 176 schemes and projects from January 2021 through June 2024, mostly for renewable electricity, energy efficiency, storage, and electricity networks; the Innovation Fund’s first renewable-hydrogen pilot auction selected seven bids for €720 million in prospective EU contributions.[2] **Thus, the EU used both the EUA price and auction-funded support to accelerate renewable deployment and energy-system change.**

### How did renewable energy mandates interact with China's national carbon

China’s national carbon market was an output-based performance-standard system for power generation: allowances were allocated against emissions-intensity benchmarks, rather than under a declining absolute cap. This structure rewards lower emissions per unit of electricity generated, but it does not directly reward a generator for replacing coal output with solar, wind, or green hydrogen, and it contained no renewable-energy provision.[18][14]

Renewable deployment instead came mainly through the wider policy mix: renewable feed-in tariffs, subsidies and incentives, energy-performance standards, power-market reform, and renewable expansion targets. China added 297 GW of solar and wind capacity in 2023, a 63% increase, but the contemporary assessment identifies the national carbon market as playing a relatively limited role in mitigation compared with renewable expansion and other sectoral policies.[18] **China’s renewable build-out was therefore primarily driven by direct energy and industrial policy, while the national market primarily encouraged operational efficiency at fossil generators.**

Power-market reforms were intended to deepen that complementarity. Measures introduced from May 2023 included steps toward a unified national power market, capacity tariffs intended to raise renewable generation in non-peak periods relative to coal-priced peak loads, a peak-shaving market, and ancillary renewable-energy pricing arrangements for 2023–2026. Renewable portfolio standards were identified as a needed complement because they would fill the national market’s fuel-switching gap.[18][14] **Integrating a renewable portfolio standard with output-based benchmarks would add an incentive to replace fossil generation, rather than merely improving its emissions intensity.**

### How did coal phase-out schedules interact with California's Cap-and-Trade Program

Judgment — California did not operate a statewide coal phase-out schedule as a defined companion instrument to Cap-and-Trade during 2020–2024. That absence did not exclude out-of-state coal-fired generation serving California: imported electricity, including coal-generated imports, carried the allowance obligation at its first delivery onto California’s electricity grid, a design described as a state-level border carbon adjustment. California’s climate strategy instead treated Cap-and-Trade as an economy-wide backstop within the Scoping Plan, alongside sector-specific standards and incentives.[9][7]

The identifiable 2021 sectoral complement was SB 596, which required CARB to develop a cement strategy targeting greenhouse-gas-emissions intensity 40% below baseline by 2035 and net-zero emissions by 2045. California’s overlapping policies can lower allowance prices and redistribute abatement among covered sources, while the cap retains the aggregate emissions limit.[22][14] **California’s relevant policy interaction was therefore between an economy-wide cap and sectoral decarbonisation measures, not between Cap-and-Trade and a coal-retirement timetable.**

### How did coal phase-out schedules interact with the EU ETS

Coal exits within the EU ETS were principally national policies, not a single EU-wide power-plant closure schedule. Germany’s January 2020 agreement set an orderly coal exit by 2038, including approximately 2.8 GW of closures by end-2022 and 5.7 GW more by end-2029; Portugal completed its coal phase-out on 30 November 2021. Poland’s September 2020 strategy retained a substantially slower path, targeting a maximum 56% coal share in electricity in 2030.[91]

The EUA price reinforced these policies by raising coal’s operating cost relative to gas. By 2020, higher carbon prices were associated with coal-to-gas switching; Spain cited both stricter environmental rules and rising EU ETS certificate prices when seven of its remaining 15 coal plants closed in June 2020.[33][91] The recovery from COVID-19 showed that this price interaction remained contingent on energy-market conditions: power-sector emissions rose 8.4% in 2021 as electricity demand recovered and high fossil-fuel prices increased coal use, though emissions remained 8.1% below 2019 levels.[12] **The EUA price made coal less competitive, but it did not override fuel-price shocks or national energy-security choices.**

National closures can also create surplus EUAs by lowering demand for allowances. National coal phase-out plans were estimated to be capable of adding 2 billion EUAs to oversupply during 2021–2030, which is why Phase 4 permits Member States to cancel allowances from their auction shares after generation-capacity closures. Germany notified the Commission in December 2023 that it intended to cancel allowances associated with two plants closed in 2022; the quantity had not been determined, and the cancellations were planned for 2025–2030.[33][20][2] **The EU’s supply-management rule was designed to stop national coal closures from weakening the EU-wide scarcity signal by leaving unused allowances in the market.**

Germany’s 2020 agreement scheduled **8.5 GW** of coal-plant closures by the end of 2029: about 2.8 GW by end-2022 plus a further 5.7 GW by end-2029.[91]

### How did coal phase-out schedules interact with China's national carbon

China did not have a binding, dated full coal phase-out during 2020–2024. President Xi’s stated direction was for coal use to peak in 2025 and decline thereafter, while another account describes a commitment to reduce coal over 2026–2030 but no commitment to eliminate it entirely.[41][92]

The national carbon market consequently operated alongside continuing coal dependence and capacity additions. China added 38 GW of coal-fired capacity in 2020, while coal-power use was still rising overall as electricity demand increased and new plants continued to be approved and built.[91][92] Its output-based benchmarks could reward lower emissions intensity, and the Ministry of Ecology and Environment proposed significantly tighter benchmarks for the 2021–2022 compliance period, particularly for coal-fired plants.[14] **China’s market could improve coal-plant efficiency, but it did not provide a direct price incentive to retire coal generation or substitute renewable output.**

Coal-consumption caps, air-pollution policy, coal-capacity restrictions, renewable-performance standards, and closures of inefficient plants can shift generation toward lower-emitting resources. However, OECD analysis warns that such policies can create surplus allowances unless benchmark allocation reflects the resulting change in the generation mix.[99] **China’s key design challenge was to align benchmark tightening with coal and renewable policies so that complementary measures did not dilute carbon-market scarcity.**

### Synthesis

California, the EU, and China all paired carbon pricing with renewable-energy policy, but the market designs determined the interaction. California coordinated an RPS with a declining cap, making the cap a backstop while renewable procurement reduced allowance demand. The EU combined renewable targets, national coal exits, a stronger carbon-price signal, and auction-funded investment; its central risk was that coal closures could create EUA surplus unless allowances were cancelled. China’s output-based market rewarded efficiency but not fuel switching, so renewable deployment and coal management rested mainly on direct targets, subsidies, power-market reform, and administrative controls.[21][33][2][18]

Judgment — The strongest integrated design is one that aligns complementary-policy emissions reductions with allowance supply: otherwise clean-energy mandates and coal closures lower emissions but can unintentionally weaken the carbon price that is meant to sustain the transition.

## Carbon leakage, industrial relocation and border adjustments

Carbon leakage is the shifting of production and associated emissions from a regulated place to a less-regulated one, which can make local emissions fall without an equivalent global reduction. For California, the relevant distinction is between a safeguard’s coverage and an observed leakage outcome: the former can be quantified during 2020–2024; the latter cannot.

### What do industrial relocation data or border adjustment mechanisms quantify

California’s clearest border-related coverage measure was electricity imports. The program requires compliance for imported electricity, which the Congressional Research Service described as effectively a state-level border carbon adjustment; imported electricity accounted for 30% of California’s electricity supply. This 30% is a supply-share indicator, not a measure of either industrial relocation or emissions avoided, and the reported share is not tied to a specific year in 2020–2024.[7]

For industrial facilities, California relied principally on free, output-based allocation rather than a product-import border charge. CARB allocates free allowances using product-efficiency benchmarks, the declining allowance budget, and facility production, so higher output produces more allowances; its 2024 analysis says this approach is designed to minimize leakage. During 2020–2024, AB 398’s 100% industrial assistance factor through 2030 was California’s quantitative leakage-protection indicator, despite risk tiers based on emissions intensity and trade exposure, because the legislature judged industries to remain vulnerable to leakage.[1] The cap’s approximately 4% annual decline in 2021–2030 was expected to raise both allowance prices and potential leakage risk.[7][22][1]

CARB’s 2024 analysis also treated a product-level border carbon adjustment as prospective: AB 398 required a 2025 legislative report assessing leakage risk and whether imported products should bear a carbon liability comparable to that facing California production. California’s existing inclusion of electricity imports was described as effectively a state-level border carbon adjustment, whereas the prospective product measure would apply a carbon liability to imported products.[7][22] A December 2023 policy brief’s statement that the interstate commerce clause barred the described California product-border-pricing practice therefore does not contradict the electricity-import measure; the two mechanisms apply to different flows.[22][7][30]

A plant-level Difference-in-Difference study reported that regulated multi-state firms reduced emissions at their California plants by 33% relative to their plants in other states, while increasing emissions at out-of-state plants by 29% relative to firms without California plants; the two percentages use different comparison groups and are not a net leakage rate.[100] Accordingly, California’s 100% assistance factor and production-linked allocation quantified policy protection based on assessed vulnerability, not businesses or emissions proven to have moved out of state.[7][1] **California’s 100% industrial assistance factor was a quantified 2020–2024 leakage-protection measure, but California did not quantify industrial relocation or net emissions leakage during 2020–2024.**[49][1]

1. Treat the 30% imported-electricity share as border-policy coverage, not evidence that leakage was prevented.
2. Treat free allocation as a precautionary competitiveness measure; use measured facility closures, production shifts, and out-of-state emissions data before assigning a leakage rate.

On the criterion of breadth of sectors directly or upstream covered during 2020–2024, the conditional ranking is California first, the EU ETS second, and China third. California covered fuel-combustion emissions across mining, power, buildings, transport, industry, agriculture and forestry, with buildings, transport, and agricultural, forestry and fishing fuel use covered upstream at suppliers; the EU ETS covered electricity and heat generation, manufacturing installations and specified aviation through 2023, adding maritime transport on 1 January 2024; China’s national market covered power generation alone throughout 2021–2024.[8][2][46]

This ordering concerns sectoral breadth, not tonnes abated, compliance, or allowance prices. Confirmed national Chinese industrial coverage during 2020–2024, or a different decision criterion, would change the verdict.[46][8][2]

California’s Independent Emissions Market Advisory Committee (IEMAC) reported some evidence that resource shuffling may be occurring: a utility changes contracts to lower-carbon electricity while another buyer outside the program purchases the higher-carbon electricity. This is an electricity-accounting and trading concern, rather than evidence of industrial relocation.[101]

For the EU ETS, free allocation was the principal 2020–2024 industrial leakage safeguard. For 2021–2030, up to 57% of general allowances were to be auctioned and the remainder allocated free to mitigate leakage risk and encourage low-carbon technologies; free allocation used sector-specific performance benchmarks, and the third carbon-leakage list applies from 2021 to 2030.[102][103] The European Commission states that free allocation limits carbon costs for industries exposed to international competition while retaining the carbon-price signal and incentives to reduce emissions.[104]

The EU created a second, border-based safeguard through Regulation (EU) 2023/956 of 10 May 2023. Its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) transitional phase began in October 2023 and covered imports of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity, including some precursors and downstream products.[105] During the transition, which began in October 2023, importers submitted quarterly embedded-emissions reports without paying a financial adjustment; penalties could apply for missed reports.

A December 2024 assessment found no discernible changes in imports of CBAM goods in the first year after the October 2023 implementation.[105][106] The CBAM therefore quantified reported imports and their embedded emissions, not a carbon charge collected in 2023–2024.[105]

The EU’s border adjustment was paired with a scheduled change in domestic protection: free allocation for CBAM-covered sectors is to be phased out as CBAM phases in, while free allocation continues for other leakage-exposed sectors.[105][104] The Commission’s design thus shifts leakage protection from free allowances for EU producers toward comparable carbon-cost treatment of covered imports.[107]

This trade-flow finding is not an industrial-relocation count. Earlier EU ETS literature largely found that firm relocation had not materialised or that firms expanded their local asset base, although one study found increased outbound foreign direct investment in Italian manufacturing; most of that evidence concerned ETS phases through 2012.[108] A historical model-based analysis for 2000–2016 found positive carbon leakage from EU-regulated sectors toward unregulated countries, while stating that its output results could not distinguish production-process restructuring, outsourcing of high-carbon stages, and wholesale relocation.[108]

For China, no dated 2020–2024 quantitative indicator of industrial relocation or emissions leakage from the national power-sector market is established here. A January 2023 study stated that China’s carbon market had not introduced specific anti-carbon-leakage measures.[42] A December 2024 assessment described China as having the highest value and volume of CBAM exports to the EU, but supplied no numerical value, relocation measure, or Chinese border-adjustment coverage figure.[106][42]

China had no national carbon border adjustment or comparable domestic border carbon levy by 31 December 2024.[80][114][2][120]

China had no national carbon-border measure comparable to the EU’s CBAM by 31 December 2024. [136][102][61][114][117]

Verified national Chinese industrial coverage during 2020–2024, or a different decision criterion, would change the ordering.[8][2][46]

For the EU ETS, Phase 4 runs from 2021 to 2030. Auctioning accounts for up to 57% of the cap, while benchmark-based free allocation protects sectors exposed to carbon leakage. The Phase-4 carbon-leakage list applies from 2021 to 2030, includes fewer sectors classified as at risk than the preceding list, and ends free allocation for other sectors by 2030 except district heating.[81]

The EU ETS quantifies leakage exposure with a composite indicator: trade intensity multiplied by emissions intensity. A result above 0.2 meets the leakage-risk threshold; a result above 0.15 and below 0.2 receives qualitative assessment of abatement potential, market characteristics, and profit margins. Trade intensity is (imports + exports)/(imports + production); emissions intensity is [direct emissions + (electricity consumption × electricity emission factor)]/gross value added. These thresholds classify exposure to leakage risk rather than measuring industrial relocation.[81]

The 2023 EU ETS revision established the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) to address carbon-leakage risk. CBAM’s transitional data-collection phase began in October 2023: importers of electricity, iron and steel, cement, aluminium, fertilizers, and hydrogen had quarterly reporting obligations but no charges were due. The first report, covering imports in the fourth quarter of 2023, was due on January 31, 2024.[109][81][110]

CBAM therefore quantified covered import flows, their origin, and embedded emissions during 2023–2024; it did not impose a border carbon charge in that period. Free allocation for iron and steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, and hydrogen is scheduled to be phased out from 2026 to 2034 in parallel with CBAM’s phase-in. The CBAM factor for this reduction is 97.5% in 2026, 51.5% in 2030, and 14% in 2033.[111][81]
