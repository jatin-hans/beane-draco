# Columbus McKinnon’s Capital Allocation and Financial Risk Profile

## Executive summary

Columbus McKinnon’s capital allocation shows disciplined risk reduction alongside continued acquisition-led growth, but the sharp fall in its share price created a material employee-retention vulnerability. Its pension deficit improved by $4.044 million to $64.389 million at March 31, 2025, while $95.080 million of pension obligations and matching assets were removed through settlements, including an annuity-based U.S. plan termination that transferred substantial legacy pension risk.[1][2][3] Net debt fell by $57.420 million, from $395.836 million at March 31, 2022 to $338.416 million at March 31, 2023, indicating purposeful deleveraging after the debt-funded Dorner and Garvey acquisitions and before further acquisition financing.[4][5][6] At March 31, 2025, 95.85% of outstanding options were not in the money, following a 62.1% year-end share-price decline, which weakens the immediate retention value of option compensation even though options retain time value.[7][8][9] The remaining assessment of fiscal-2025 intangible-asset amortization determines whether the acquired customer relationships and technology assets support durable economic value or instead require a relatively rapid replenishment of acquisition-derived earnings.

## Pension management

Defined-benefit pensions are retirement promises for which the employer bears investment and longevity risk. Funded status measures the difference between the pension benefit obligation—the present value of promised benefits—and the fair value of plan assets set aside to pay them. A negative funded status is therefore a balance-sheet funding deficit, not necessarily a cash payment due immediately.

Columbus McKinnon measured its pension obligations and assets at March 31. The pension deficit narrowed during fiscal 2025, even though the remaining plans were still underfunded at year-end. Judgment — Funded status is a point-in-time economic measure, whereas annual cash contributions are governed by funding rules and management decisions. The improvement occurred alongside a substantial reduction in the size of pension plans through settlements. That distinction matters: a settlement can reduce pension obligations and the assets backing them by similar amounts, producing little direct change in aggregate funded status while materially reducing the company’s exposure to future benefit payments. This makes the year-end deficit comparison useful for remaining exposure, while the settlement analysis explains the strategic reduction in the gross pension footprint.[2][3]

### What does the settlement activity reveal about pension risk management

Columbus McKinnon’s fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025 actions followed a clear sequence: first, it terminated its two Canadian pension plans and began winding down one U.S. plan; second, eligible U.S. participants received lump-sum payments; third, it purchased annuity contracts on September 30, 2024 to settle the U.S. plan’s remaining liabilities.[3]

| Reduction in pension benefit obligation from settlements | 20.510 [8] | 95.080 [3] |

| U.S. terminated-plan asset surplus transferred out | Not applicable to this fiscal-2024 comparison | 6.974 [3] |

In fiscal 2024, the Canadian-plan terminations and U.S. lump-sum payments generated a $4.984 million settlement charge. The settlement reconciliation reduced both benefit obligations and plan assets by $20.510 million, showing that obligations were settled with their associated plan resources rather than merely remeasured.[3][8] The company’s December 2023 interim disclosure described the U.S. lump sums expressly as a step toward terminating the plan by transferring liabilities to a third party.[11]

In fiscal 2025, Columbus McKinnon completed the U.S. plan termination. The September 30, 2024 annuity-contract purchase settled the plan’s remaining liabilities and produced a $23.634 million non-cash settlement charge recorded in Other (income) expense, net.[3] The pension reconciliation records $95.080 million of settlements against both benefit obligations and plan assets, nearly 4.64 times the prior year’s $20.510 million settlement reduction.Derived — $95.080 million divided by $20.510 million equals 4.64.[3][8]

The $95.080 million fiscal-2025 settlement reduction represented approximately 87% of the $109.396 million annual decline in pension benefit obligations and 90% of the $105.352 million decline in plan assets.[2]

The accounting term settlement has a specific risk-management meaning. A pension settlement occurs when the employer’s action is irrevocable, it is relieved of primary responsibility for the obligation, and significant risk associated with the obligation and assets used to settle it is eliminated; lump sums and annuity contracts are standard settlement mechanisms.[12] Columbus McKinnon’s documented use of both mechanisms, followed by termination, therefore demonstrates actual risk transfer for the settled plans rather than only a change in investment mix.[11][3][12]

The fiscal-2025 charge was an accounting expense, and the company included the $23.634 million settlement amount as a non-cash adjustment in its operating-cash-flow reconciliation.[7] The charge should consequently be read as recognition of pension accounting effects associated with settlement, not as the cash price of the annuity contracts.

The termination also released pension-related amounts from accumulated other comprehensive loss, including $24.118 million before tax of unrecognized net pension amounts reclassified into pension cost.[7]

Columbus McKinnon reported an $8.379 million remaining surplus in its September 30, 2024 interim report, to be used for final benefit payments required under the annuity sales agreement and applicable regulations and to fund U.S. defined-contribution-plan obligations.[13] At termination, the company reported a $6.974 million pension-asset surplus, which was being used to fund certain U.S. defined-contribution-plan obligations; $6.342 million remained at March 31, 2025.[7][3][10] This treatment means the surplus supported other employee-benefit commitments rather than becoming unrestricted corporate cash.

The settlement program fits the company’s stated glide-path policy, under which it increases liability-hedging investments as pension funded status improves. Actual pension assets shifted from 8% equities and 92% fixed income in fiscal 2024 to 11% equities and 89% fixed income in fiscal 2025, despite a fiscal-2026 target of 0% equities and 100% fixed income.[7][1][14][2] The company expected its U.S. plan to be fully funded in fiscal 2026 and then to de-risk further.[7][1][14] Its funding policy was to contribute at least the annual ERISA minimum and potentially contribute more to minimize PBGC premiums.[3]

Columbus McKinnon described its domestic pension investment objective as maintaining growth and liquidity sufficient to support benefit payments.[2]

The December 2023 interim disclosure contains a narrow numerical inconsistency: its narrative reported a $4.599 million settlement charge for the quarter, while the pension-cost table reported $4.639 million for the quarter and $4.720 million for the nine-month period.[11] The subsequent fiscal-2024 annual disclosure reports the completed-year settlement charge as $4.984 million.[3]

The U.S. plan’s closure was the culmination of a multiyear exit from that defined-benefit arrangement. Columbus McKinnon froze new entrants in 2012 and new contributions in 2017; in May 2023 it announced that participants could elect a lump sum and that it would purchase annuities for remaining obligations. It purchased annuities for all remaining CMCO Pension Plan obligations in September 2024.[15][16] All of the company’s pension plans are closed to new entrants and frozen, so participants generally no longer accrue benefits, except for local statutory plans in Sweden and Japan; previously accrued benefits remain obligations and are generally based on retirement age and years of service.[17] Freezing most plans limits new benefit accruals, while minimum legal funding requirements and additional contributions that management considers appropriate address obligations already accrued.[17]

For the three months ended June 30, 2023, Columbus McKinnon reported a $41 thousand settlement component and $796 thousand of net periodic pension cost, compared with no settlement component and $545 thousand of net periodic pension cost for the three months ended June 30, 2022.[18] In the quarter ended December 31, 2023, certain U.S.-plan participants accepted lump sums; the company identified those payments as a step toward terminating the plan through third-party liability transfer.[15]

Columbus McKinnon’s September 30, 2024 interim report recorded a $23.201 million non-cash settlement charge from the annuity-contract purchase to settle the remaining liabilities of the terminated U.S. plan for the six months then ended, while the fiscal-2025 annual accounts recorded a $23.634 million non-cash settlement charge for the full year.[13][10]

The candidate’s cited interim disclosures also reported a $17.5 million pension settlement charge in the second quarter of fiscal 2025, compared with the $23.634 million charge reported for the full fiscal year.[2][35][42][43]

Columbus McKinnon’s September 30, 2024 disclosure expected approximately $5.461 million of pension contributions in fiscal 2025. This planned contribution is distinct from the non-cash settlement charge.[13]

The company stated that terminated-plan assets consisted of money-market funds, domestic corporate bonds, U.S. government securities and similar quoted fixed-income investments, all measured using Level 1 inputs.[10]

The fiscal-2025 termination also had accounting effects beyond the settlement charge: Columbus McKinnon reclassified $24.118 million before tax of previously unrecognized net pension amounts from accumulated other comprehensive loss into pension cost, producing an $18.182 million net-of-tax reclassification after a $5.936 million tax benefit.[10][7] The filing separately reports a stranded-tax-effect release of $7.050 million and another disclosure reports $7.605 million, each recorded as a decrease in fiscal-2025 income taxes as a result of the termination.[10]

Settlement activity increased reported pension expense: total net periodic pension cost was $27.030 million in fiscal 2025, compared with $7.945 million in fiscal 2024.[7] The $23.634 million fiscal-2025 annuity-settlement charge was recorded in Other (income) expense, net.[7][10]

Fiscal-2025 settlements equaled 35.47% of the $268.025 million opening pension benefit obligation, calculated as $95.080 million divided by $268.025 million.Derived — this measures the scale of settled obligations relative to the March 31, 2024 opening obligation.[1][3]

The termination illustrates a trade-off: Columbus McKinnon accepted a non-cash settlement charge that affected reported earnings while seeking to transfer pension obligations and reduce future pension-related volatility, including discount-rate and mortality-assumption risk. The report states that the settlement expense contributed to fiscal-2025 net loss of $5.1 million, compared with fiscal-2024 net income of $46.6 million; the proxy summary separately identifies a $22.1 million non-cash pension settlement in fiscal 2025 related to termination of a U.S. plan.[19][14][16]

The pension termination affected fiscal-2025 reported results through a 17-percentage-point reduction in the effective tax rate and through release of a $7.050 million stranded tax effect from accumulated other comprehensive loss, recorded as a decrease in income taxes in the consolidated statement of operations.[3][10]

The annuity settlement transferred the terminated U.S. plan’s settled obligations, while the glide path and the fixed-income-heavy allocation addressed market risk in pension exposure that remained. Columbus McKinnon’s policy was to increase liability-hedging investments as funded status improved; its fiscal-2025 actual allocation was 11% equities and 89% fixed income. Higher discount rates reduce measured pension liabilities, while interest-rate cuts can raise liabilities and bond-portfolio valuations, stabilizing funded status for sponsors using asset-liability matching strategies.[6][10][20]

In April 2025, Columbus McKinnon entered a Canadian bulk-annuity arrangement covering $42 million of $59 million in pension obligations; the remaining $17 million was scheduled for lump-sum settlement from plan assets.[43]

### Synthesis

**Columbus McKinnon’s pension actions show a deliberate shift from carrying defined-benefit exposure to settling, transferring, and hedging it.** The aggregate pension deficit narrowed by $4.044 million in fiscal 2025, while settlements removed $95.080 million each of obligations and associated assets from the plans and completed the U.S. plan’s annuity-based termination.[1][2][3] The much larger fiscal-2025 settlement charge relative to fiscal 2024 reflects the accounting cost of completing that risk transfer, not a deterioration in the reported funding deficit.[3][7] The remaining $64.389 million deficit and the company’s glide path mean pension risk was reduced rather than eliminated across the company’s pension arrangements.[2][7][1]

1. Treat the $64.389 million remaining deficit as an ongoing financial-risk item in the broader capital-allocation assessment.
2. Treat the $23.634 million fiscal-2025 settlement charge separately from cash pension funding and debt-service capacity.
3. View the plan terminations, annuity settlement, and fixed-income-oriented glide path as evidence that management prioritized reducing legacy pension volatility while retaining resources for the operating and acquisition strategy.

Judgment — Net debt is debt less cash: it is the amount that would remain if a company applied its available cash to repay borrowings. It is more informative than gross debt alone when assessing deleveraging, because a company can lower borrowings while also consuming cash, or increase cash while debt is broadly unchanged. For consistency here, net debt includes the balance-sheet current and non-current debt and finance-lease obligations, less cash and cash equivalents.

**Columbus McKinnon reduced balance-sheet net debt by $57.420 million—from $395.836 million at March 31, 2022 to $338.416 million at March 31, 2023.** The reduction had two components: a $39.634 million reduction in gross debt and finance-lease obligations and a $17.786 million increase in cash.[4] Thus, the improvement was not simply a cash-flow repayment measure; it reflects the company ending fiscal 2023 with both less debt and more cash.

The company’s separately reported Term Loan B principal declined from $502.560 million at March 31, 2022 to $462.560 million at March 31, 2023, exactly matching $40.000 million of principal payments made during fiscal 2023.[5] Its accounting debt carrying value—total debt after unamortized deferred financing costs—fell by $39.083 million, from $497.135 million to $458.052 million.[4] These figures corroborate the direction of deleveraging but answer a narrower question than net debt, because neither deducts cash.

The cash-flow statement likewise reported $40.550 million of fiscal-2023 debt repayments.[4] These are distinct reported measures: fiscal-2023 cash-flow debt repayments were $40.550 million, and fiscal-2023 Term Loan B principal payments were $40.000 million.[4] Conversely, it is less than the $57.420 million net-debt reduction because the latter also captures the year-end cash increase.[5] **The $57.420 million is therefore the appropriate answer to the requested net-debt question; $40.000 million, $39.083 million, and $40.550 million are related but non-interchangeable debt metrics.**[4][5]

At March 31, 2023, Columbus McKinnon had no borrowings outstanding under its New Revolving Credit Facility, although $15.104 million of letters of credit were issued against that facility.[4] The company’s later credit-agreement definition of net debt includes total debt plus standby letters of credit less cash and cash equivalents.[21] Applying that covenant definition would require consistently adding letters of credit at both March 2022 and March 2023; the balance-sheet calculation above instead answers the question with reported debt and lease obligations less reported cash. The balance-sheet basis establishes a clear deleveraging outcome across the requested dates.

On an alternative total-debt-less-cash basis using total debt carrying value after unamortized deferred financing costs, net debt fell from $381.745 million at March 31, 2022 ($497.135 million total debt less $115.390 million cash) to $324.876 million at March 31, 2023 ($458.052 million less $133.176 million), a $56.869 million, or approximately 14.9%, reduction.[14]

The $39.634 million reduction in gross debt accounted for 69.02% of the $57.420 million net-debt reduction, and the $17.786 million increase in cash accounted for 30.98%. Debt paydown was the larger, but not the only, source of improvement.[5][4]

### What does the net debt reduction pattern indicate about deleveraging

The March 2022 starting point already embodied acquisition-led financing. Columbus McKinnon completed Dorner on April 7, 2021 for $481.012 million, financing it through a $750 million First Lien Facility that included a $650 million bridge facility; the bridge proceeds also refinanced prior term-loan and revolving borrowings.[5] The company subsequently issued 4,312,500 shares for $207 million gross in May 2021 and used all net offering proceeds to repay part of the bridge borrowing before refinancing the remaining bridge balance with the Term Loan B. This sequence shows a mixed acquisition-financing model: debt initially funded the transaction, while equity reduced part of the acquired leverage.[4]

Finance-lease obligations were $14.084 million at March 31, 2022, principally associated with a Dorner manufacturing facility.[6]

For the Dorner acquisition, Columbus McKinnon targeted near-term net leverage below 4.0 times adjusted EBITDA and net debt to adjusted EBITDA below 2.0 times within two years, supported by expected combined cash generation and rapid debt repayment.[44][45]

Garvey extended the program. Columbus McKinnon completed the acquisition in fiscal 2022 and financed it with a $75 million draw under the Term Loan B Accordion feature, used for purchase consideration and related fees, expenses, and transaction costs.[4] The company stated that its indebtedness had increased significantly in connection with completing the Dorner and Garvey acquisitions: it incurred $450.000 million of Term Loan B debt for Dorner following its May 2021 $207.000 million equity offering, and $75.000 million of Term Loan B debt through the accordion feature for Garvey.[5] At March 31, 2023, approximately $100.000 million was available under the New Revolving Credit Facility before deducting $15.104 million of outstanding letters of credit; the company also stated that its potential fiscal-2024 montratec acquisition had increased indebtedness.[5] Dorner and Garvey formed the new Precision Conveyance group, adding high-precision conveying and production-accumulation capabilities that management presented as part of its shift toward intelligent motion.[4][5]

A separate acquisition disclosure put Garvey’s December 1, 2021 purchase price at $69.347 million, including $0.907 million of acquired cash; it also described a possible $2 million payment contingent on an EBITDA target and a specified executive remaining employed through at least March 31, 2023.[46][6][47]

Fiscal-2022 cash flows show the scale of the acquisition-and-financing phase: purchases of businesses, net of acquired cash, were $539.778 million; long-term-debt issuance generated $725.000 million; debt repayments consumed $477.846 million; and the equity offering generated $207.000 million.[5] Net cash provided by financing activities was $420.700 million in fiscal 2022.[5] Those figures should not be interpreted as simple incremental acquisition debt because the financing also refinanced prior borrowings and debt repayments were substantial, but they establish that M&A and capital-structure restructuring dominated fiscal 2022.[5]

Fiscal 2023 had a markedly different cash-allocation pattern. Business purchases, net of acquired cash, were $1.616 million, there were no proceeds from long-term-debt issuance, and debt repayments were $40.550 million, compared with fiscal-2022 business purchases of $539.778 million, long-term-debt proceeds of $725.000 million, and debt repayments of $477.846 million.[8] Net cash used for financing activities was $49.987 million in fiscal 2023, compared with net cash provided by financing activities of $420.700 million in fiscal 2022.[4] Net cash used in investing activities also fell from $554.311 million in fiscal 2022 to $13.932 million in fiscal 2023.[5] This shift gave the company room to reduce gross debt and accumulate additional cash.

Columbus McKinnon reported $12.632 million of capital expenditure in fiscal 2023 and described the $1.616 million Garvey-related investing outflow as a working-capital adjustment.[4]

The repayment was not merely contractual amortization. The Term Loan B required quarterly principal amortization of 0.25%.[22] The excess-cash-flow (ECF) percentage was 50%, stepping down to 25% when the Secured Leverage Ratio was no greater than 3.00:1.00 and to 0% when it was no greater than 2.50:1.00 for fiscal years ending on or after March 31, 2022.[5] During fiscal 2022, Columbus McKinnon made $22.440 million of Term Loan B principal payments.[6] At March 31, 2022, management stated that it planned approximately $40 million of total principal payments over the following 12 months, versus scheduled payments of $5.260 million plus any applicable excess-cash-flow requirement.[6] It then made $40 million of Term Loan B principal payments in fiscal 2023.[5] **The alignment of the stated plan and subsequent payment indicates deliberate debt paydown beyond the minimum scheduled installment, not passive balance-sheet runoff.**[6][5]

Columbus McKinnon also addressed the cost volatility of the acquisition-related debt. Its stated policy was to maintain 50–70% of long-term debt at fixed rates and 30–50% at variable rates.[4] The company entered into its third interest-rate swap in fiscal 2022 because of the additional Dorner and Garvey debt; the swaps exchange variable-rate receipts for fixed-rate payments and were designated as cash-flow hedges of variable interest expense on the senior secured term loan.

Their combined notional amount was $300.747 million at March 31, 2022 and $273.591 million at March 31, 2023, with maturities through February 28, 2025.[6] This was risk management of interest exposure rather than repayment, but it reduced uncertainty around the cash cost of the debt that remained outstanding.[4]

Interest and debt expense nonetheless increased to $27.942 million in fiscal 2023 from $20.126 million in fiscal 2022. Columbus McKinnon attributed the increase to higher interest rates and increased borrowing to finance Garvey.[5] The fact that net debt fell while interest expense rose highlights an important timing point: debt balances and interest expense need not move together when rates rise and when average debt during a year exceeds the closing balance. The deleveraging improved the year-end financial position, but it did not eliminate the acquisition financing burden during fiscal 2023.

Management’s use of debt reduction should be read as a balancing priority rather than evidence that acquisition-led growth had ended. The company describes acquisitions as a key part of its growth strategy and says its historical growth depended, and likely future growth would depend, on executing and integrating acquisitions successfully.[5] The company also identified the risks that an acquisition price could be too high, integration could cause management or employee turnover, transitions could increase costs and reduce profitability, and anticipated synergies, market opportunities, or integration from recent significant acquisitions could fail and materially harm the business, financial condition, and results.[5] Its credit agreement permitted acquisitions subject to conditions including no continuing default, a same-line-of-business requirement, target approval, and generally a pro forma Total Leverage Ratio no higher than 3.50:1.00; an exception allowed up to $50 million of acquisitions in a fiscal year above that threshold.[5] The financing structure therefore allowed expansion, but required leverage discipline as a condition of pursuing it.

That balance became especially relevant immediately after the comparison period. In April 2023, the company announced its agreement to acquire montratec, expected to close on May 31, 2023, and expanded its revolving facility by $75 million to finance it; it also planned approximately $50 million of accounts-receivable securitization debt to partially repay revolver borrowings.[5] The acquisition ultimately closed on May 31, 2023 for $115.721 million, initially financed by $117 million of revolver borrowing and later refinanced through $75 million of additional Term Loan B borrowing and $45 million of receivables-backed borrowing.[8] That later debt-funded acquisition does not alter the March 2022–March 2023 calculation, but it shows why the preceding deleveraging mattered: it restored capacity for the next strategic transaction.

Free cash flow—defined by Columbus McKinnon as net cash provided by operating activities less capital expenditures—increased from $35.777 million in fiscal 2022 to $71.004 million in fiscal 2023, a derived $35.227 million increase. The stronger fiscal-2023 internal cash generation was consistent with the company’s capacity to repay debt while acquisition cash demands had fallen.[15]

The First Lien Facilities also gave Columbus McKinnon the contractual ability to voluntarily prepay the Term Loan B or revolver in whole or in part at any time without premium or penalty, subject to lender-cost reimbursement, while requiring specified mandatory prepayments. The applicable Total Leverage Ratio ceiling was 4.50:1.00 from June 30, 2022 through before June 30, 2023 and 3.50:1.00 from June 30, 2023 onward when the Covenant Trigger applied. These terms made accelerated repayment feasible within a framework designed to constrain leverage.[10]

The post-period montratec financing demonstrates that the fiscal-2023 balance-sheet reset was followed by renewed acquisition leverage. On the company’s credit-agreement basis, reported net debt increased from $353.337 million at March 31, 2023 to $431.478 million at March 31, 2024, a derived $78.141 million increase, while net leverage rose from 2.15x to 2.40x. This covenant-based measure differs from the balance-sheet calculation above because the credit-agreement definition also includes standby letters of credit.[23][15]

Subsequent payment behavior supports the reading of debt reduction as an active capital-allocation priority. In the six months ended September 30, 2024, Columbus McKinnon paid $30.000 million of Term Loan B principal, of which only $2.488 million was required, and planned approximately $50.000 million of total principal payments over the following 12 months; it attributed the year-on-year decline in six-month interest and debt expense to reduced long-term debt resulting from accelerated principal payments.[13]

During the nine months ended December 31, 2022, Columbus McKinnon paid down $30.402 million of debt while also paying $6.006 million in dividends and spending $1.001 million to purchase treasury stock.[24] This allocation shows that debt reduction proceeded alongside shareholder distributions and treasury-stock purchases.[24]

In the first nine months of fiscal 2023, Columbus McKinnon repaid $30.402 million of debt, issued no long-term debt and used $37.639 million in financing activities.[24]

Columbus McKinnon stated that debt service requires a substantial portion of operating cash flow for principal and interest, reducing funds available for operations; that indebtedness may impair additional financing for working capital, capital expenditures, acquisitions, or other purposes; and that it may limit additional acquisitions or common-stock dividends.[25] These stated constraints explain why deleveraging supports both financial flexibility and acquisition capacity.[25]

Fiscal 2023 allocation combined debt repayment with shareholder distributions: Columbus McKinnon repaid $40.550 million of debt and paid $8.008 million of dividends during the fiscal year; during the nine months ended December 31, 2022, it also paid $6.006 million of dividends and purchased $1.001 million of treasury stock. Deleveraging was a priority, but not the only use of capital.[5][24]

Columbus McKinnon said its precision-conveyance acquisitions established a platform for organic growth and further acquisitions because the industry is highly fragmented and has complementary adjacencies.[8]

### Synthesis

The March 2022–March 2023 pattern is post-acquisition deleveraging: Columbus McKinnon reduced calculated net debt by $57.420 million through both lower gross debt and higher cash after the debt-intensive Dorner and Garvey transactions.[4] Fiscal 2023’s $40.550 million of debt repayments, no long-term-debt issuance, and sharply lower acquisition spending followed fiscal 2022’s $539.778 million of business purchases and $725.000 million of long-term-debt issuance.[8] The company also paid principal at a level it had planned well above scheduled amortization and hedged a substantial part of variable-rate exposure created by acquisition financing.[5][6] The strongest conclusion is that Columbus McKinnon treated deleveraging as a near-term financial-risk control following acquisition-led expansion, while preserving debt capacity and contractual flexibility to continue its acquisition strategy.[5][4][7]

The $57.420 million balance-sheet net-debt reduction represented **14.51%** of the March 31, 2022 calculated net-debt balance of $395.836 million. This puts the one-year post-acquisition deleveraging in proportion: it was a meaningful reduction in the opening net-debt position.[4]

Columbus McKinnon’s later capital-allocation statements make the strategic priority explicit: its lender presentation describes 《De-Leveraging Remains the Primary Focus of CMCO’s Capital Allocation Policy》 and pairs that priority with investment to drive sales growth and margin improvement and a continuing dividend record. ## Stock options and employee retention

Stock options are a form of equity compensation: they give an employee the right to buy shares at a fixed exercise price. Their retention value is strongest when the market price exceeds that exercise price, creating immediate economic value for the holder. This analysis measures how much of Columbus McKinnon’s option pool had lost that immediate value by March 31, 2025, and why that mattered for retaining skilled employees during a difficult period for the share price.

#### Pension funded status: improvement without full elimination of exposure

A defined-benefit pension plan promises specified retirement benefits. Its funded status is the fair value of plan assets less the present value of benefit obligations: a negative result is a deficit, meaning that assets do not fully cover the accounting value of promised benefits. Columbus McKinnon uses March 31 as its pension measurement date. The company’s pension deficit narrowed from $68.433 million at March 31, 2024 to $64.389 million at March 31, 2025. The $4.044 million improvement is derived as March 31, 2025 plan assets less benefit obligations of negative $64.389 million, minus March 31, 2024 plan assets less benefit obligations of negative $68.433 million.[1][2]

The balance-sheet direction was favorable, but the endpoint remains more important than the year-over-year movement for risk assessment. A $64.389 million deficit still exposes the company to investment returns, interest-rate changes, actuarial assumptions, future funding requirements and Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation premiums. Columbus McKinnon also reported $3.709 million of short-term pension liabilities and $62.675 million of accrued long-term pension cost at March 31, 2025, which demonstrates that pension obligations continued to affect the reported financial position after the termination activity.[1]

Columbus McKinnon’s pension funding policy establishes a minimum funding floor rather than a blanket commitment to eliminate deficits rapidly. The company states that it contributes at least the minimum annual amount required by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, or ERISA, and may contribute additional amounts to reduce Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, or PBGC, premiums. It planned approximately $3.778 million of contributions in fiscal 2026.[2] This policy preserves cash for operations, debt reduction and acquisitions, but it also means that the pace of closing any remaining accounting deficit is not determined by an announced full-funding objective.

The company’s investment policy shows a separate pension-risk control. Columbus McKinnon describes a “glide path” under which it increases liability-hedging investments as pension funded status improves. Its actual 2025 pension allocation was 11% equity securities and 89% fixed income, versus 8% equity and 92% fixed income in 2024; the fiscal-2026 target was 0% equity and 100% fixed income.[7][1] Fixed-income investments tend to move more closely with the interest-rate-sensitive value of pension liabilities than equities do, so this structure is designed to reduce funded-status volatility rather than maximize long-run return.

Judgment — The pension glide path is a liability-driven investment approach: it trades some expected equity-return upside for greater matching between plan assets and the liabilities that the assets are meant to fund. For a company allocating capital simultaneously to acquisitions and debt repayment, the strategic value is not merely lower pension volatility; it is also greater predictability of future funding demands.

The target shift to 100% fixed income should not be read as proof that all pension risk has vanished. The remaining deficit, required future contributions, and pension-related liabilities show that Columbus McKinnon still carried exposure at March 31, 2025. The policy instead indicates an intention to limit the risk that markets widen that exposure while management deploys capital elsewhere.[7][1][2]

#### Settlement activity: transfer of obligations, not merely accounting cleanup

The pension actions were more substantial than an asset-allocation change. In fiscal 2024 Columbus McKinnon terminated both Canadian pension plans and began the termination process for one U.S. pension plan. Eligible U.S. participants who selected lump sums were paid in the third and fourth quarters of fiscal 2024; the Canadian-plan terminations and U.S. lump-sum activity produced a $4.984 million settlement charge.[25]

In fiscal 2025 Columbus McKinnon completed the termination of that U.S. plan. On September 30, 2024, it purchased annuity contracts to settle the plan’s remaining liabilities, producing a $23.634 million non-cash settlement charge recorded in Other (income) expense, net.[7] An annuity purchase is significant because it moves the obligation to make the specified retirement payments from the company’s pension plan to the annuity provider, subject to the contract terms. The action therefore addresses the continuing liability itself rather than only reducing its accounting presentation.

The pension rollforward quantifies the scale of settlement. In fiscal 2025, the company recorded a $95.080 million settlement reduction in both the benefit obligation and plan assets, compared with matched $20.510 million reductions in fiscal 2024.[2] Equal reductions in assets and obligations are consistent with settling benefits using assets already held in the plans; they do not indicate that the accounting deficit itself fell by $95.080 million.

The $4.044 million funded-status improvement came from the combined effect of all annual changes, not from treating the settlement amount as an incremental surplus.[2]

The fiscal-2025 termination also left a $6.974 million pension-asset surplus. Columbus McKinnon stated that the surplus would fund certain obligations associated with U.S. defined-contribution plans, and that the remaining surplus was $6.342 million at March 31, 2025.[7] That use of surplus illustrates a broader employee-benefits allocation decision: excess assets from a closed defined-benefit arrangement can support other retirement obligations rather than become unrestricted corporate cash.

The reported settlement charge should be separated from the economic settlement transaction. Columbus McKinnon included the $23.634 million charge as a non-cash adjustment in its fiscal-2025 operating-cash-flow reconciliation.[7] Thus, the charge reduced reported earnings but was not cash paid in that same amount during the fiscal year.

The economics of the annuity purchase were still real—plan assets were used to secure the annuity contracts—but the financial statements distinguish that transaction from the non-cash acceleration of previously deferred pension losses and related accounting effects recognized in earnings.

The fiscal-2025 settlement cost was $18.650 million higher than fiscal 2024’s $4.984 million charge.Derived — $23.634 million − $4.984 million = $18.650 million.[2] This increase helps explain why Columbus McKinnon reported a $5.138 million net loss in fiscal 2025 despite operating income of $54.573 million; the company identified the U.S. pension-plan termination, plant consolidation and Kito acquisition-related expenses as contributors to that loss.[7][35]

The settlement activity reflects a deliberate willingness to accept an immediate earnings charge to reduce future defined-benefit exposure. The company had used a similar approach earlier: in fiscal 2021 it settled a U.S. plan with a combination of lump sums for electing participants and annuity contracts for those who did not elect lump sums.[4] The repeated use of lump sums, plan terminations and annuity contracts supports the conclusion that the fiscal-2024 and fiscal-2025 actions were part of a continuing de-risking pattern rather than an isolated transaction.

Judgment — For capital-allocation analysis, the relevant trade-off is between a one-time reduction in earnings and a permanent reduction in the volatility, administration and funding uncertainty of a terminated plan. Columbus McKinnon’s actions favor the latter. That is strategically coherent with an acquisition program because legacy pension variability can otherwise compete with debt capacity and acquisition integration for management attention and cash.

The pension program therefore makes three things possible: lower exposure to future defined-benefit obligations for terminated plans, more liability-oriented investment management for remaining plans, and redeployment of residual plan surplus to defined-contribution obligations. It does not eliminate pension risk across Columbus McKinnon because the aggregate plans still carried a $64.389 million deficit at March 31, 2025.[7][1][2]

The pension actions occurred alongside a broader fiscal-2025 effort to lower debt. The company made $60.670 million of debt repayments, paid $8.042 million in dividends and spent $10.000 million on treasury-stock purchases during fiscal 2025.[35][2] At the same time, it incurred $19.891 million of net cash used for investing activities, following $133.364 million in fiscal 2024, when montratec acquisition activity was a principal investing outflow.[7]

At March 31, 2025, Columbus McKinnon reported $53.683 million of cash and cash equivalents, $159.583 million of revolver availability and $26.889 million of accounts-receivable securitization availability, for reported liquidity of $240.155 million.[7] Liquidity is not cash alone: the disclosed amount includes borrowing capacity. Judgment — An interest-rate swap protects the cost of the hedged debt exposure, not the debt principal. Its presence therefore signals a risk-management response to acquisition-related leverage rather than deleveraging itself. Columbus McKinnon’s debt-repayment program and swaps should be read as complementary: repayments reduce the amount owed, while swaps reduce the uncertainty around the interest cost on amounts still owed.

The pension program and debt program have the same broad financial logic. Pension settlements removed or transferred long-duration benefit obligations; accelerated principal payments lowered borrowing balances and interest expense. Both actions reduce future financial uncertainty, although pension settlements created substantial near-term earnings charges and debt repayments consumed cash that could otherwise support acquisitions or shareholder distributions.[7][35][2]

This makes the pension actions strategically relevant to capital allocation rather than a stand-alone employee-benefit issue: pension de-risking protected financial flexibility while debt reduction preserved capacity for future investment, but both required management to accept immediate costs or cash uses.

The company acquired Dorner for $481.012 million on April 7, 2021 and said the acquisition would accelerate the shift to intelligent motion and expand advanced, higher-technology automation capabilities.[4] The financing combined a $750 million First Lien Facility—comprising a $100 million revolver and $650 million bridge facility—with a $207 million gross public equity offering; the equity proceeds were used to repay part of the bridge borrowings.[26]

Garvey extended the same platform. Columbus McKinnon described Garvey’s accumulation-system products as complementary to Dorner and said the acquisitions accelerated its move into intelligent motion and advanced automation.[22] The company borrowed $75 million through the Term Loan B accordion for Garvey’s purchase price and transaction costs.[5] The Dorner and Garvey transactions were thus not isolated purchases: management presented them as related building blocks in an automation platform.

Management also said Dorner and Garvey added approximately $5 billion to total addressable market, with specialty conveying estimated to grow at 6%–8% annually.[4] This was the company’s strategic market estimate, not a realized return measure.

The significance for capital allocation is that the company accepted acquisition leverage because it believed the assets would reposition its growth profile rather than merely add cyclical industrial volume.

montratec continued the Precision Conveyance program. Columbus McKinnon acquired montratec on May 31, 2023 for $115.721 million, including $7.576 million of acquired cash, a $540,000 working-capital settlement and a contingent payment recorded at $6.680 million.[7] The company described montratec as an automation-solutions business designing intelligent automation and transport systems for production and logistics, complementary to Dorner and Garvey.[8]

The transaction also marked a return to additional borrowing. Columbus McKinnon initially borrowed $117 million under the revolving facility to fund montratec, then replaced that borrowing through a further $120 million consisting of a $75 million Term Loan B accordion borrowing and $45 million under a receivables-secured credit agreement.[7] In fiscal 2024, it used $108.145 million of cash for the montratec purchase, received $120 million of gross long-term debt proceeds to fund it and nevertheless repaid $60.604 million of debt.[8]

This financing sequence reveals the limit of interpreting a single debt repayment figure as deleveraging. The revolver was repaid, but it was repaid with new Term Loan B and accounts-receivable securitization borrowing.[11] Repayment of one instrument can therefore be refinancing rather than a reduction in total debt. This is why the March 2022–March 2023 net-debt calculation must use total relevant obligations less cash, rather than focus on any one facility.

The higher leverage after montratec had a measurable earnings cost. Interest and debt expense increased to $37.957 million in fiscal 2024 from $27.942 million in fiscal 2023, which Columbus McKinnon attributed to higher rates and increased borrowing to finance montratec.[8] Reported total debt rose from $458.052 million at March 31, 2023 to $517.299 million at March 31, 2024.[25] Thus, the earlier $57.420 million net-debt reduction was not a continuous multiyear trend; it was a period of deleveraging followed by debt-funded acquisition expansion.

Columbus McKinnon subsequently resumed debt reduction. It made $60 million of Term Loan B principal payments in fiscal 2024 and $40 million in fiscal 2025; at March 31, 2025, it planned approximately $50 million of principal payments over the following 12 months, versus a stated $4.976 million required amount plus any applicable excess-cash-flow payment.[7] Paying more than scheduled principal indicates an active effort to reduce leverage after the acquisition funding, although the company also continued dividends, repurchases and operating investment.[7][35][2]

The company had $437.560 million of Term Loan B principal and $25 million of accounts-receivable securitization borrowings outstanding at March 31, 2025; there were no borrowings under the revolving facility.[7] The absence of revolver borrowings improves short-term liquidity flexibility, but it does not eliminate the sizable longer-term obligations concentrated in term debt and securitization financing.

#### Pending Kito: a larger capital-allocation test

The pending Kito transaction puts the earlier deleveraging and pension de-risking measures in a different perspective. On February 10, 2025, Columbus McKinnon announced an agreement to acquire Kito Crosby in an all-cash transaction valued at $2.7 billion, subject to customary adjustments and regulatory approval.[9] The company expected the acquisition to close during fiscal 2026 and said it would improve scale and geographic reach, expand lifting, securement and consumables offerings, and enhance its customer value proposition.[7]

The announced financing was large relative to Columbus McKinnon’s March 2025 debt position. The company intended to use $3.050 billion of committed debt financing, including a $500 million revolver, and $800 million of perpetual convertible preferred equity from CD&R. The preferred equity carried a 7% coupon payable in cash or payment-in-kind at the company’s option and a $37.68 conversion price; on an as-converted basis, CD&R would own approximately 43% of the company after issuance.[7]

The proposed preferred shares would rank ahead of common stock in specified rights and preferences, initially convert into 21.231 million common shares and entitle CD&R Investors to designate up to three directors, subject to ownership conditions.[7] The financing is therefore not a simple choice between debt and equity.

It substitutes part of the funding with securities that reduce immediate common-equity dilution only until conversion but impose a recurring preferred dividend and governance consequences.

The board’s materials cited an expected approximately 4.8x net leverage ratio at Kito closing and expected that leverage to decline substantially after closing through anticipated cash generation.[9] That forecast identifies the financial strategy: accept a sharp initial leverage increase to obtain scale and seek to deleverage through post-close operating cash flow and integration. It is an expected path, not a realized fiscal-2025 result.

Judgment — The Kito proposal changes the risk profile from moderate post-monstratec deleveraging to event-driven leverage. The investment case depends on the acquired business generating enough cash flow and synergies to reduce leverage faster than higher interest, preferred-dividend and integration demands consume financial flexibility.

The company had already identified its indebtedness as having increased significantly in connection with Precision Conveyance acquisitions.[7] Kito would extend this model from capability-building bolt-ons and mid-sized acquisitions toward a transformative transaction. The strategic rationale is consistent—greater scale, geographic reach and an expanded portfolio—but the financing scale materially raises refinancing, interest-cost, covenant, execution and shareholder-dilution risks.[7]

The planned transaction should not be treated as complete at March 31, 2025. It was announced and expected to close in fiscal 2026, subject to regulatory approval and other customary closing conditions.[7] Consequently, the announced $3.050 billion financing and $800 million preferred investment describe intended capital structure, not the company’s recorded debt or equity at the fiscal-2025 reporting date.

The conclusion from the acquisition and debt record is clear. Columbus McKinnon used deleveraging to restore capacity after the Dorner and Garvey financing cycle, then used that capacity for montratec and was preparing to use substantially more external capital for Kito. This is an acquisition-led capital-allocation model in which debt reduction is a recurring constraint-management tool, not a replacement for growth through acquisitions.[4][7]

#### Employee retention and the equity-incentive problem

Employee retention matters particularly in an acquisition-led industrial strategy because acquired customer relationships, technology, operating know-how and integration benefits depend on people as well as recorded assets. Columbus McKinnon employed 3,478 people globally at March 31, 2025; 3.7% were represented by one U.S. collective-bargaining agreement expiring in May 2027, and the company stated that it considered the risk of employee- or union-led production disruption remote.[7] That disclosure concerns production disruption, not the incentive or retention risk facing key salaried, technical and managerial employees.

Stock options are an employee incentive whose value depends on the market price exceeding the option exercise price. An option is in the money when the current share price is above its exercise price; it is underwater when the exercise price exceeds the market price. Columbus McKinnon had 1,253,626 stock options outstanding at March 31, 2025, with a weighted-average exercise price of $36.38 and a weighted-average remaining contractual life of 5.99 years.[7]

Columbus McKinnon’s closing common-stock price on March 31, 2025 was $16.70. The company reported that only 51,957 outstanding options were in the money at that date.[7] Subtracting those from the 1,253,626 options outstanding gives 1,201,669 options not in the money, or 95.85% of outstanding options.Derived — (1,253,626 − 51,957) ÷ 1,253,626 × 100 = 95.85%.[7]

The 95.85% figure is appropriately described as a proxy for underwater concentration rather than a perfectly strict count. The company’s disclosure identifies options that were in the money but does not identify whether any options had an exercise price exactly equal to $16.70. Options at exactly the market price are at the money, not underwater. The practical conclusion remains strong: almost all options were not delivering current intrinsic value at the fiscal-year-end price.[7]

The deterioration was substantial relative to the prior year. At March 31, 2024, Columbus McKinnon had 1,193,840 options outstanding, 1,048,000 of which were in the money, implying approximately 12.2% not in the money at a $44.63 closing price.[25] The March 2025 closing price of $16.70 was 62.6% below the March 2024 price.Derived — ($16.70 ÷ $44.63 − 1) × 100 = −62.6%.[7][25]

The option book therefore moved from one in which most options carried intrinsic value to one in which almost all did not. The weighted-average $36.38 exercise price was more than twice the $16.70 market price.Derived — $36.38 ÷ $16.70 = 2.18 times.[7] The aggregate intrinsic value of the outstanding option population was only $80,000 at March 31, 2025.[7]

Columbus McKinnon’s options generally vest over three years at 33% per year beginning one year after grant, and their exercise price is at least 100% of the grant-date fair market value.[7] These terms make options a retention tool when employees expect the company’s share price to rise over the vesting period.

When a large option population is underwater, the same terms can lose immediate motivational value because continued service does not create realizable intrinsic value unless the share price recovers.

Judgment — A 95.85% not-in-the-money option concentration is a retention risk rather than proof of employee departures. It weakens the economic “golden handcuff” effect of options for employees who hold grants at substantially higher exercise prices, especially where competitors can offer new grants, cash compensation or restricted stock with immediate or lower-price value.

The company continued to use equity compensation despite the weak stock performance. In fiscal 2025 it granted 188,089 options at a weighted-average exercise price of $45.34, recorded 12,648 option exercises and 115,655 cancellations.[3] The cancellation count cannot be equated with employee turnover because options can be cancelled for several reasons, but the magnitude is relevant in a year when the share price ended far below the weighted-average exercise price.[7][3]

Columbus McKinnon also expanded the 2016 Long Term Incentive Plan in July 2024 by 2.8 million shares.[3] That expanded capacity demonstrates that management retained the ability to issue new equity awards. It does not show that underwater legacy options were repriced or that awards were specifically redesigned to address retention risk.

Restricted stock units, or RSUs, partly mitigate the option-value problem because their value does not require the share price to exceed an exercise price. The company issued 128,000 vested RSU shares during fiscal 2025.[37] Performance shares also remained outstanding: 254,856 were unvested at March 31, 2025, with a weighted-average grant-date fair value of $33.90; 123,401 were granted, 19,960 vested and 27,485 were forfeited during fiscal 2025.[1] These awards broaden the compensation mix beyond options, though performance awards have their own performance-condition risk.

Stock-based compensation expense fell to $6.256 million in fiscal 2025 from $12.039 million in fiscal 2024 and $10.425 million in fiscal 2023.[7] At March 31, 2025, the company had $2.615 million of unrecognized expense for non-vested options to recognize over approximately 1.7 years.[7] Lower accounting expense does not itself show lower retention risk; it can reflect award mix, grant levels, forfeitures, vesting schedules or changed performance expectations.

The company also incurred direct employee-related severance and retention costs in its footprint simplification program. The Charlotte manufacturing relocation to Monterrey generated $1.093 million of employee-related severance and retention costs during fiscal 2025.[1] The planned relocation of a U.S. Precision Conveyance factory to Hartland and consolidation of Latin American Precision Conveyance operations into Hartland and Monterrey generated a further $1.069 million of employee-related severance and retention costs.[1]

Those two programs produced $2.162 million of recorded employee-related severance and retention costs.Derived — $1.093 million + $1.069 million = $2.162 million.[1] The costs show that management was spending to manage workforce transition during operational consolidation. They should not be treated as proof that underwater options caused those costs; the disclosed purpose was relocation and consolidation.

The broader operational context amplifies the retention issue. Columbus McKinnon described fiscal-2025 priorities as profitable growth, margin expansion, footprint simplification, improved customer experience and factory-performance improvement.[38] These initiatives require technical, commercial and operating talent while the company is relocating operations and preparing a transformative acquisition.

Weak equity incentives raise the risk that the company must rely more heavily on cash compensation, refreshed awards, retention payments or other arrangements to keep key personnel.

Employee retention is therefore a financial-risk issue, not only a human-resources issue. Loss of experienced personnel can delay integration, impair customer continuity, reduce the realization of operating improvements and increase replacement costs. In Columbus McKinnon’s case, that risk is heightened by the coexistence of a substantially underwater option book, active facility consolidation and an acquisition strategy that relies on technology and customer relationships embedded in acquired businesses.[7][1][4][8]

The implication is not that the company lacks employee-incentive capacity. It has option, RSU and performance-share programs, and it increased LTIP capacity in July 2024.[3][37][1] The implication is that option-heavy incentives granted at much higher share prices are unlikely to be sufficient on their own to provide retention value at the March 2025 price.

#### Customer relationships and technology: what the fiscal-2025 amortization rate says

Identifiable intangible assets are nonphysical assets acquired in business combinations that can be separately valued, such as customer relationships, technology, trademarks, patents and trade names. Finite-lived intangible assets are amortized: their recorded cost is recognized as expense over their estimated useful lives. Goodwill is different; it represents the excess of purchase consideration over the fair value of identifiable net assets and is tested for impairment rather than amortized.

At March 31, 2025, Columbus McKinnon reported $356.562 million of identifiable intangible assets, net. Customer relationships had a $226.379 million net carrying amount, derived from $355.845 million gross carrying amount less $129.466 million accumulated amortization. Acquired technology had a $69.927 million net carrying amount, derived from $112.507 million gross carrying amount less $42.580 million accumulated amortization.[3]

Customer relationships and acquired technology together represented $296.306 million, or 83.10%, of fiscal-2025 net identifiable intangible assets.Derived — ($226.379 million + $69.927 million) ÷ $356.562 million × 100 = 83.10%.[3] This concentration is strategically meaningful: most of the recognized finite-lived acquisition value was attributed to customer access and technical capability rather than to brands, other intangibles or residual categories.

Columbus McKinnon reported $29.946 million of fiscal-2025 intangible amortization expense.[7] Dividing that annual expense by the March 31, 2025 net identifiable-intangible balance of $356.562 million produces an 8.40% year-end carrying-value amortization rate.Derived — $29.946 million ÷ $356.562 million × 100 = 8.40%.[7][3]

The 6.23% result is a gross-balance-weighted proxy for annual straight-line amortization across finite-lived categories, using the reported category-specific weighted-average periods. It is not a company-published weighted-average amortization rate or an asset-class-specific realized expense rate.[7]

An approximate inverse of the 6.23% rate is 16.1 years.Derived — 1 ÷ 6.23% = 16.1 years. This blended period aligns directionally with management’s multiyear useful-life assignments for acquired customer relationships and technology.[7][11]

The company’s disclosed asset-specific useful lives provide evidence of the intended durability of selected acquisitions. At March 31, 2025, Columbus McKinnon reported weighted-average amortization periods of 17 years for customer relationships, 15 years for acquired technology, 13 years for finite-lived trademarks and six years for other assets, with 16 years overall for finite-lived intangibles.[7] These lives show that management expected customer relationships and acquired technology to contribute to cash flows over substantially longer periods than the other finite-lived category.

Dorner illustrates the strategic composition of those acquisition values. The company assigned $137 million of customer relationships, $45 million of technology and $8 million of trade names to the Dorner acquisition, with a 15-year weighted-average life for acquired identifiable intangibles subject to amortization.[4] Customer relationships and technology accounted for $182 million, or 95.8%, of Dorner’s $190 million identified intangible assets.Derived — ($137 million + $45 million) ÷ $190 million × 100 = 95.8%.[4]

Garvey’s acquired intangible base also contained customer and technical assets: customer relationships of $8.2 million, engineered drawings of $4.67 million, trademarks of $3.61 million, patents of $2.44 million, backlog of $2.1 million and a non-compete agreement of $0.33 million. The weighted-average life of Garvey’s amortizable identifiable intangible assets was estimated at 10 years.[4] The shorter average life compared with Dorner reflects Garvey’s mix, including backlog and non-compete assets that normally have shorter expected periods of benefit.

montratec added $33.470 million of customer relationships, $16.196 million of technology and $2.915 million of trade name assets. Columbus McKinnon estimated a 14-year weighted-average life for montratec’s acquired identifiable intangible assets subject to amortization.[14] Its preliminary valuation used a 10.0% customer-relationship attrition rate and a 5.0% technology royalty rate, with a 12.5% weighted-average cost of capital.[11] The attrition rate is a valuation assumption that recognizes customers will not all remain indefinitely; it is not an observed measure of subsequent customer losses.

The customer-relationship and technology values support the stated strategic rationale for the Precision Conveyance acquisitions. Dorner was positioned as a platform for advanced automation, Garvey as a complementary automation and accumulation-systems business, and montratec as an intelligent automation and transport-systems provider complementary to both.[4][8] Accounting allocations to customer relationships and technology are consistent with paying for customer access, engineering capability and automation know-how rather than simply acquiring manufacturing capacity.

Judgment — A long amortization life supports management’s view that acquired customer relationships and technology should provide multiyear benefits, but it does not prove that they will. Amortization is an accounting allocation of acquisition-date value; it is not evidence of future revenue, margins, customer retention, technology competitiveness or return on invested capital.

The fiscal-2025 amortization rate therefore has two implications. First, a 6.05% blended annual expense rate means that a meaningful portion of the gross finite-lived acquired-intangible base is recognized through earnings over time. That recurring non-cash charge is distinct from operating cash generation when assessing acquisition economics. Second, the rate is consistent with multiyear expected benefits, particularly because customer relationships and technology have historically been assigned 17-year and 15-year average lives, respectively.[7][14][11]

The result does not establish that acquisitions are economically durable. The key test is whether the acquired businesses produce cash flows sufficient to support their carrying values, debt service, integration costs and the return expected on acquisition capital. Columbus McKinnon’s impairment testing shows both positive evidence and a caution.

At March 31, 2025, Columbus McKinnon reported $710.807 million of goodwill, including goodwill from montratec within the Precision Conveyance reporting unit.[7] The company’s earlier disclosure said its quantitative test found no Precision Conveyance goodwill impairment, while its later proxy disclosure states that the sustained reduction in stock price and market capitalization led to a $200.0 million partial goodwill impairment for that reporting unit in the fiscal-2025 annual analysis.[7][33]

The available headroom was limited. Precision Conveyance’s fair value exceeded its book value by only 2.6%, and management performed the quantitative test because recently acquired businesses created a relatively small difference between book and fair value.[7] Management’s model assumed approximately 9.7% compound annual revenue growth over the first seven years, a 3.5% terminal growth rate and a 12.0% discount rate.[7] A one-percentage-point reduction in projected seven-year revenue growth would reduce estimated fair value by $42.7 million; a 50-basis-point increase in the discount rate would reduce fair value by $35.6 million; and a 25-basis-point terminal-growth reduction would reduce fair value by $15.386 million.[7]

The impairment result and limited headroom point in opposite directions only superficially. The unit passed the test, so no impairment was recorded. But the 2.6% excess means that the valuation is sensitive to weaker growth, lower margins, higher discount rates or adverse market assumptions. In capital-allocation terms, this makes successful integration and performance of the acquired customer relationships and technology more important because the company has relatively little valuation cushion in the reporting unit that contains them.[7]

montratec supplied some initial operating evidence. Columbus McKinnon reported that the acquisition contributed $32.6 million, or 3.5%, of fiscal-2024 sales growth.[21] In the six months ended September 30, 2023, montratec contributed $12.194 million of revenue and $6.292 million to the increase in gross profit.[39] These contributions demonstrate that the acquired business entered reported revenue and gross profit; they do not establish whether the acquisition will meet its full cost of capital or sustain the projected growth used in impairment testing.

The durability conclusion is therefore balanced. Customer relationships and technology constituted 83.10% of March 2025 net identifiable intangibles, and the blended 8.40% annual amortization rate plus historical 14- to 17-year useful lives are consistent with management expecting multiyear benefits.[3][7][11] However, the narrow 2.6% goodwill-test headroom makes those expected benefits execution-sensitive rather than demonstrated economic durability.[7]

#### Integrated capital-allocation implications

The four dated measures show an acquisition-led strategy accompanied by targeted risk containment. Dorner, Garvey and montratec built the Precision Conveyance automation platform, while the pending Kito transaction was intended to add scale, geographic reach and broader lifting offerings.[7]

The company’s financial-risk management has sought to make that strategy financeable. It reduced net debt by $57.420 million between March 2022 and March 2023, used interest-rate swaps to limit variable-rate exposure, accelerated principal payments in later years and reduced legacy pension exposure through terminations, lump sums, annuity contracts and a fixed-income glide path.[4][7][1] These actions all preserve capacity and reduce uncertainty, but they consume cash, create earnings charges or reduce upside from risk assets.

The measured residual exposures were the $64.389 million pension deficit, $417.292 million of derived March 31, 2025 debt less cash, 95.85% of options not in the money, and 2.6% Precision Conveyance goodwill-test headroom.[2][36][7] The option concentration followed a decline in the closing share price from $44.63 at March 31, 2024 to $16.70 at March 31, 2025 and weakens the immediate intrinsic-value component of option-based retention; retention remains strategically important because acquisition integration entails management and employee-turnover risk.[7][8]

The pending Kito financing—$3.050 billion of committed debt financing and $800 million of perpetual convertible preferred equity—would intensify the need for cash generation, integration execution, debt service and employee retention if the transaction closes.[7]

For the client, the practical implication is to assess Columbus McKinnon less as a company choosing between growth and conservatism than as a company trying to make both work simultaneously. The correct question is whether cash generation and acquired-business performance can fund debt reduction, pension obligations, integration and employee retention without impairing the strategic assets for which it paid.

The retention issue is directly connected to Columbus McKinnon’s strategic and governance framework. The company states that effective human-capital management is key to execution of its long-term strategy, and its Human Resources Framework focuses on attracting, developing, engaging and rewarding talent. Its Board oversees human-capital management, including culture, talent management, succession planning, compensation, benefits and employee-engagement feedback.[15]

Columbus McKinnon’s May 20, 2024 annual grant process issued 188,596 stock options and 74,745 time-based RSUs to certain employees, including named executive officers. The company sought an additional 2.8 million shares under the 2016 Long Term Incentive Plan because the 339,019 shares then available were insufficient for anticipated new-hire and annual grant needs; it stated that failure to approve the amendment would leave it at a significant competitive disadvantage in attracting and retaining talent.[15]

The 2016 Plan prohibits repricing or cash buyouts of underwater options without shareholder approval and generally requires at least one year of vesting. For ordinary termination, unvested time-vested options are forfeited and vested unexercised options generally remain exercisable for 30 days. These terms make the recovery of option value and continued employment central to the retention value of awards.[15]

For fiscal 2025, Columbus McKinnon changed the named-executive-officer long-term-incentive mix to 50% performance stock units, 25% time-based RSUs and 25% non-qualified stock options. This reduced the option share of the target mix and increased performance-based equity, while preserving RSUs that do not require a share price above an exercise price to have value.[15]

The legacy ESOP was not an ongoing equity-retention mechanism at March 31, 2025: it had been closed to new participants since January 1, 2012, made its final allocation in fiscal 2015, had no further share contributions planned, and all accounts were fully vested. It nevertheless held 142,867 Columbus McKinnon shares with a $2.419 million fair value at March 31, 2025; the plan reported $3.704 million of investment depreciation and a $4.412 million reduction in net assets available for benefits during fiscal 2025.[40]

The company’s incentive architecture links the human-capital dimension to the same financial priorities. Its long-term incentive program is intended to align key employees with business strategy and shareholder interests and to attract and retain executive talent; the Compensation Committee also considers acquisitions and their integration among strategic achievements when making compensation recommendations.[16] The Board specifically considered the effect of the pending Kito announcement on its ability to attract and retain key personnel, and identified employee attrition as a possible consequence if the transaction were delayed or not completed.[16] Accordingly, the underwater-option concentration compounds an already identified transaction-execution risk, while the company’s RSUs, PSUs, annual cash-flow incentives and potential future equity-award capacity are the mechanisms available to address it.[16]

Columbus McKinnon identifies the ability to attract, develop, engage and retain qualified employees, retain key management, successfully integrate acquisitions, manage indebtedness and debt-covenant restrictions, and manage changing interest rates as risk factors.[18] These stated risks connect employee retention directly to acquisition integration and connect workforce execution to the company’s leveraged growth model.[18]

It states that employees are its most significant assets and are critical to delivering its transformation and strategic progress.[7][16]

### Synthesis —

Columbus McKinnon paired strategic expansion with measured risk controls: it reduced net debt by $57.420 million from March 2022 to March 2023 and narrowed the pension deficit by $4.044 million from March 2024 to March 2025 while terminating plans and purchasing annuities for the terminated U.S. plan’s remaining liabilities.[4][2] The company nevertheless retained a $64.389 million pension deficit and $417.292 million of derived March 31, 2025 debt less cash.[2][36]

The principal tension is execution. Customer relationships and technology dominate the identifiable-intangible base and support the acquisition thesis, but the Precision Conveyance unit had only 2.6% valuation headroom.[3][7] Meanwhile, 95.85% of options were not in the money, weakening the retention value of legacy option awards during consolidation and planned expansion.[7][1] The pending Kito financing would make successful integration, employee retention and sustained cash generation decisive to whether the strategy strengthens or strains the balance sheet.[7][9]

The termination also accelerated pension amounts previously held in accumulated other comprehensive loss, or AOCL, into earnings. Columbus McKinnon reclassified $24.118 million before tax of previously unrecognized net pension amounts from AOCL in fiscal 2025; after a $5.936 million tax benefit, the amount was $18.182 million net of tax and was included in net periodic pension cost.[10] The filing separately reports that the pension termination released stranded tax effects from AOCL and reduced income-tax expense by $7.050 million and, in a separate disclosure, by $7.605 million.[10] These accounting effects show that plan termination changed both pension expense recognition and reported tax expense, in addition to transferring the terminated plan’s remaining liabilities through annuity contracts.[10]

The financing framework permitted Columbus McKinnon to voluntarily prepay the Term Loan B facility or revolver, subject to specified lender-cost reimbursement, and required certain mandatory prepayments. The revolving-credit covenant was triggered when qualifying revolving-credit extensions were outstanding and capped the Total Leverage Ratio at 4.50:1.00 from June 30, 2022 to before June 30, 2023, then at 3.50:1.00 from June 30, 2023 onward.[10] These terms gave management both a mechanism to retire debt and a financial constraint on borrowing during the acquisition period.[10]

Restricted stock units provide a second service-based retention mechanism. Columbus McKinnon granted 136,870 restricted stock units in fiscal 2025 at a weighted-average grant-date fair value of $39.71 per share, and 247,636 units remained unvested at March 31, 2025 at a weighted-average grant-date fair value of $36.38 per share. The employee restricted stock units granted in fiscal 2024 through fiscal 2026 vest in equal one-third installments after one, two and three years of service.[10] Performance shares issued in fiscal 2025 were also tied to revenue and EBITDA-margin growth across fiscal 2025 through fiscal 2028, linking part of the equity-incentive program to operating execution.[10]

Columbus McKinnon previously settled another U.S. pension plan in fiscal 2021 through lump-sum payments and annuity contracts: the lump sums paid in the quarter ended June 30, 2020 produced a $2.722 million settlement charge, and annuity contracts purchased in the quarter ended September 30, 2020 produced a total $19.038 million settlement charge for the fiscal year ended March 31, 2021.[4]

Columbus McKinnon completed the Garvey acquisition on November 30, 2021 and used an additional $75 million Term Loan B accordion borrowing to finance the purchase price, fees, expenses and transaction costs.[5]

The company’s fiscal-2023 repurchase of 31,000 common shares for $1.001 million was made under a program authorizing up to $20 million of repurchases; it made no repurchases in fiscal 2022.[4]

Columbus McKinnon stated that indebtedness increased significantly in connection with the Precision Conveyance acquisitions and increased Term Loan B principal by $75 million in each of fiscal 2022 and fiscal 2024.[7]

For montratec financing, Columbus McKinnon increased the revolving-credit facility by $75 million to $175 million and initially borrowed under that facility in May 2023. It subsequently borrowed $75 million through the Term Loan B accordion and $45 million through the accounts-receivable securitization facility, using the latter two borrowings to fully repay revolving-facility borrowings before June 30, 2023.[11]

Reported total debt increased by $59.247 million, from $458.052 million at March 31, 2023 to $517.299 million at March 31, 2024.Derived — $517.299 million − $458.052 million = $59.247 million.[25]

Columbus McKinnon announced the definitive agreement to acquire Kito Crosby Limited on February 10, 2025 and anticipated closing during fiscal 2026.[7]

At Kito closing, Columbus McKinnon expected at least $500 million of additional liquidity, including cash and availability under its revolving credit facility and accounts-receivable securitization facility.[9]

For the Charlotte, North Carolina-to-Monterrey, Mexico relocation of North American linear-motion operations, Columbus McKinnon also recorded $3.567 million of fixed-asset impairment and inventory-obsolescence costs and $3.268 million of right-of-use lease-asset impairment costs during the twelve months ended March 31, 2025.[1]

For the U.S. Precision Conveyance factory relocation to Hartland, Wisconsin and the consolidation of the Latin American Precision Conveyance business into Hartland and Monterrey, Columbus McKinnon also recorded $2.115 million of fixed-asset impairment and inventory-obsolescence costs, $643,000 of right-of-use lease-asset impairment costs, and a $544,000 reserve on other current assets during the twelve months ended March 31, 2025.[1]

At March 31, 2025, Columbus McKinnon reported $494.990 million of gross finite-lived intangibles: $22.770 million of trademarks, $355.845 million of customer relationships, $112.507 million of acquired technology and $3.868 million of other intangibles. It also reported $46.294 million of indefinite-lived trademarks, which are not amortized.[14]

Columbus McKinnon reported fiscal-2024 intangible amortization expense of $29.396 million, compared with $29.946 million in fiscal 2025, and attributed the fluctuation to foreign-currency translation.[7]

At December 31, 2023, Columbus McKinnon reported $46.672 million of trademarks with indefinite useful lives that were not amortized. It reported weighted-average amortization periods of 13 years for trademarks, 17 years for customer relationships, 15 years for acquired technology, five years for other intangibles and 16 years in total for finite-lived intangible assets.[11]

The U.S. pension-plan termination produced a $23.634 million non-cash settlement charge in fiscal 2025, compared with a $4.984 million settlement charge from the fiscal-2024 Canadian-plan terminations and lump-sum payments. The terminated U.S. plan had a $6.974 million pension-asset surplus at termination, of which $6.342 million remained at March 31, 2025 after use to fund certain U.S. defined-contribution-plan obligations.[2]

The proposed preferred shares would initially convert into 21.231 million common shares and give CD&R Investors approximately 43% of Columbus McKinnon’s common stock on an as-converted basis, as well as the right to designate up to three directors subject to ownership requirements.[7]

In fiscal 2024, Columbus McKinnon used $108.145 million of cash to purchase montratec, funded in part by $120 million of gross long-term-debt proceeds; cash uses also included $60.604 million of debt repayments, $8.044 million of dividends and $2.859 million of fees for the new borrowings.[8]

At March 31, 2024 and March 31, 2025, reported total debt was $517.299 million and $458.708 million, respectively. The debt table included Term Loan B principal of $477.560 million and $437.560 million and accounts-receivable securitization borrowings of $45 million and $25 million, respectively.[7]

Columbus McKinnon stated that its near-term capital-allocation priority was debt repayment and deleveraging while maintaining its dividend, and that it expected significant free cash flow over time to advance its Intelligent Motion strategy.[41]

Columbus McKinnon stated that its people enable growth and that its fiscal-2025 objectives included driving a people-first culture through engagement, training and development opportunities.[7]

Columbus McKinnon stated that it expects to incur substantial additional indebtedness in connection with the Kito Acquisition.[7]

### Open questions

The weighted-average fiscal-2025 intangible-asset amortization rate, including its asset-class weighting and the implications of customer-relationship and technology assets for acquisition durability, remains to be established. The relevant reported gross or net carrying amounts and annual amortization expense by intangible-asset class would settle that calculation and permit a bounded assessment of the assets’ economic longevity.
