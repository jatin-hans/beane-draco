# Memristor Neuromorphic Claims, Crossbar Challenges, and CMOS Benchmarks

## Executive summary

Commercial resistive-switching memories demonstrably provide nonvolatile, history-dependent conductance for artificial weights, but the evidence does not establish them as Chua’s strict ideal fourth circuit element because their state is generally governed by ionic, thermal, filamentary, and other material variables rather than net charge alone.[1][2] HP’s 2008 TiO2 device established a useful memristor–synapse analogy in the engineering sense: programmed conductance can store a synaptic weight, not that the device reproduces a biological synapse or settles the charge–flux question.[3][4][5] Vongehr and Meng’s objection therefore stands for the narrow historical claim, while it does not negate the practical value of oxide and electrochemical switching memories.[6] For neuromorphic design, the actionable conclusion is to benchmark such devices as imperfect analogue memory elements against CMOS synapses on write energy, retention, update linearity, variation, array overhead, and measured task accuracy, rather than treating the memristor label itself as evidence of superior plasticity.[7][8]

## Chua’s fourth element and the memristor debate

This debate turns on a distinction that is easy to lose in ordinary engineering language. A resistive-switching memory can be a valuable nonvolatile, analogue device for storing a synaptic weight without necessarily being the ideal fourth circuit element that Leon Chua proposed in 1971. The distinction matters because the word *memristor* is used both narrowly, for a particular charge–flux circuit law, and broadly, for practical two-terminal memories whose resistance changes with electrical history.

### What properties did Chua’s 1971 framework predict for the theoretical

Leon Chua’s 1971 paper, 《Memristor—The Missing Circuit Element》, introduced the memristor as the fourth basic two-terminal circuit element alongside the resistor, capacitor, and inductor. His symmetry argument used four circuit variables: current, voltage, charge, and flux linkage. The proposed missing relation was between charge, q(t), and flux linkage, φ(t), where charge is the time integral of current and flux linkage is the time integral of voltage.[9][10][11][3]

For a charge-controlled memristor, the defining constitutive relation is φ as a function of q. Its incremental memristance, M(q), is the derivative of flux linkage with respect to charge: M(q) = dφ/dq. Because dφ/dt is voltage and dq/dt is current, the instantaneous electrical relation is voltage equals M(q) times current. Memristance has units of ohms, like ordinary resistance, but its value depends on accumulated charge rather than being fixed.[12][9][6]

Chua’s central prediction was therefore not simply “a resistor that can switch.” It was a two-terminal element whose present resistance depends on the complete prior history of current through it, or equivalently whose conductance depends on the prior history of voltage across it. If no current flows, accumulated charge does not change, so the memristance remains at its previous value. This is the formal circuit-theory basis for the familiar description “memory resistor.”[9][13]

A linear φ–q relation produces constant memristance. In that special case, the memristor reduces to an ordinary linear time-invariant resistor. Chua’s fourth-element proposal is therefore meaningful only in its nonlinear, history-dependent form: a charge-dependent resistance distinct from a fixed resistor.[9][7][14]

Chua also set a passivity condition. For a differentiable charge-controlled φ–q curve, a memristor is passive if and only if its incremental memristance is non-negative. He further stated that a device without an internal power supply requires a monotonically increasing φ–q curve. In ordinary terms, the device may dissipate electrical energy like a resistor, but it cannot create energy from nowhere.[9]

The often-cited *pinched hysteresis loop* is a current–voltage curve that passes through the origin and encloses a loop when a device is driven by a bipolar periodic signal. Chua associated nonlinear memristive behavior with frequency-dependent hysteresis: as excitation frequency rises, the loop contracts, and at the high-frequency limit the device approaches a single-valued resistive response.[9][2][15]

That current–voltage signature is useful but secondary. A pinched loop is a response in the voltage–current plane; the ideal memristor is defined in the charge–flux plane. Chua’s original theory therefore cannot be reduced to the observation that a device has hysteresis, retains a resistance state, or switches between high- and low-resistance states.[2]

The 1971 paper explicitly separated a theoretical element from a physical discovery. Chua wrote that no passive physical memristor device had then been found, although active-circuit laboratory models had been built using mutators—active circuits constructed to imitate the proposed element’s behavior.[9][11]

There is a genuine interpretive disagreement over the meaning of flux in this framework. One account treats flux as the circuit quantity defined by integrating voltage over time, which need not imply a literal magnetic-flux mechanism in a material device.[16] Vongehr and Meng instead read Chua’s original electromagnetic discussion as predicting a real physical element based on interaction between electric and magnetic fields.[6] That disagreement drives much of the later controversy.

**Chua’s fourth-element claim is specifically a charge–flux-linkage law with resistance determined by net accumulated charge; it is not the broader observation that a two-terminal device can switch between resistance states.**[11][16][2]

This evaluation makes three separate decisions: whether a device satisfies Chua’s ideal fourth-element definition, whether a crossbar is engineered to manage array non-idealities, and whether device physics delivers superior synaptic plasticity relative to CMOS. The benchmark-publication window is 2020–2024. In that comparison, measured hardware inference means that the inference workload is executed on the hardware system; physical-weight measurement with software inference means that hardware conductances are read and software performs forward propagation and calculates accuracy; and device-informed simulation means that measured device conductance behavior supplies parameters to a software neural-network simulation.[11][17][18][19]

Chua’s later identification criteria made the current–voltage signature more specific: under any bipolar periodic voltage or current drive and irrespective of initial conditions, the loop is pinched at the origin; its lobes shrink as frequency rises; and in the infinite-frequency limit it becomes a straight line through the origin.[20] Chua treated that signature as sufficient to identify a memristor, but redox-based resistive-switch models have been shown to expose a modeling deficiency in that criterion.[21]

A later account of the same history likewise describes Chua’s proposal as a symmetry-based postulate of a fourth two-terminal element whose resistance depends on the integral of its terminal input.[22]

A historical account states that, in 1971, Chua postulated through symmetry arguments a missing fourth two-terminal element, a memristor or memory resistor, whose resistance depends on the integral of the input applied to its terminals.[22]

In the quasi-static expansion of Maxwell’s equations reviewed by Gale, Chua’s rationale for a fourth basic circuit device was the case in which both first-order electric and first-order magnetic fields are non-negligible. Chua argued that this possibility provides a hint towards the existence a fourth basic circuit device.[15]

Chua’s framework also has a flux-controlled dual: charge is a single-valued function of flux linkage, q = g(φ), and the memductance is W(φ) = dq/dφ, giving i = W(φ)v. The charge-controlled and flux-controlled descriptions express the same defining requirement: terminal behavior must reduce to a constitutive relation between q and φ, rather than merely to a resistance state with hysteresis.

### What memristor–synapse analogy did Williams’ HP Labs team advance in

On 1 May 2008, Dmitri Strukov, Gregory Snider, Duncan Stewart, and R. Stanley Williams published 《The missing memristor found》 in *Nature*. The paper connected nanoscale two-terminal resistance switching to the memristor concept and proposed applications in ultradense semi-nonvolatile memory and learning networks requiring a synapse-like function.[23][3][24]

The HP device was a metal/oxide/metal cross-point switch with a critical oxide layer approximately 5 nm thick. It contained an insulating titanium-dioxide region, TiO2, and an oxygen-poor, more conductive titanium-dioxide region, TiO2−x. Oxygen vacancies act as mobile positively charged dopants. Under applied electric field, vacancy drift shifts the boundary between the regions, changing the total resistance of the device.[3][4][25]

HP modeled the device as two series regions with limiting resistances R_ON for the doped region and R_OFF for the undoped region. Under its linear-drift approximation, the boundary width w changes with charge passed through the device, yielding a charge-dependent resistance.[3][136]

In HP’s scaling argument, the memristive term varies as D⁻² and becomes 1,000,000 times larger as the characteristic dimension falls from the micrometre scale to the nanometre scale.[3][137]

HP’s 2008 moving-boundary model predicted that the memristive effect would become more important as devices were scaled down to nanoscale dimensions.[24][3][136]

In the HP account, a positive voltage pushes oxygen vacancies into the stoichiometric TiO2 region, making more of the film conductive; reverse bias moves the vacancies back and raises resistance. The device’s conductance therefore depends on the polarity, magnitude, and duration of prior electrical stimulation.[4][23]

That is the basis of the synapse analogy. A biological synapse changes its efficacy—its ability to transmit a signal—according to prior stimulation. A resistive memory device changes conductance according to prior stimulation. The device conductance can therefore represent a *synaptic weight*, meaning the adjustable numerical strength assigned to a connection between an artificial presynaptic neuron and postsynaptic neuron. Williams described synaptic connections as becoming stronger or weaker according to the polarity, strength, and duration of chemical or electrical signals, in functional parallel with memristive resistance change.[4]

The analogy is hardware-functional rather than biological-identity based. In a crossbar, a programmable conductance at each wire intersection can encode a connection weight. Applying voltages on row wires and summing currents on column wires implements a vector–matrix multiplication, the dominant arithmetic operation in many neural networks. This is why the field treats a physical conductance state as a candidate artificial synapse.[26][27]

The 2008 *Nature* paper itself made a narrower claim than much later neuromorphic literature sometimes suggests. It named learning networks that require a synapse-like function as an application, but it did not present a detailed model of neurotransmitter release, calcium dynamics, short-term plasticity, long-term plasticity, or spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP).[3]

Later literature extended the analogy in more biologically specific directions. The proposed physical parallel is that ionic species move in inorganic barriers while neurotransmitters and ions shape biological synaptic dynamics. Memristive devices have been used to emulate short-term plasticity, long-term potentiation, and STDP because electrical pulses can change conductance gradually, temporarily, or persistently.[28][29][5] Those later developments do not retroactively make the 2008 TiO2 demonstration a complete biological synapse.

Williams also reported state retention: sufficiently small read voltages left the resistance unchanged, and early working devices reportedly showed no significant resistance change over years of periodic testing.[4] Such nonvolatile retention is important for a synapse implementation because it permits weights to remain stored without continuous refresh power.

**The HP team’s memristor–synapse claim was a functional hardware analogy: persistent, stimulus-history-dependent conductance can store an adjustable connection weight; it was not a claim that the TiO2 device reproduced all mechanisms of a chemical synapse.**[3][4][5]

An account of the 2008 HP Labs device specifies a titanium-oxide slab between platinum electrodes: the doped region is highly conductive, the undoped region highly resistive, and applied terminal voltage moves their boundary and changes total resistance.[25]

Williams dated his derivation of the current–voltage and vacancy-motion equations to 20 August 2006; he described pinched-hysteresis I–V loops and resistance change that increased with the duration of positive voltage until a minimum resistance was reached.[4]

HP’s illustrative simulations used a 10 nm device, oxygen-vacancy mobility of 10⁻¹⁰ cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹, 1 V and 10 mA units, and a 10 ms characteristic time; alternating voltage produced hysteresis that collapsed toward a line at high frequency.[138][3]

An experimental HP-device current–voltage hysteresis loop was obtained with a 0.5 V, 0.68 Hz sinusoid.[138][3]

A review describes HP’s May 2008 *Nature* paper as announcing the first experimental realization of the memristor: positive voltage drives positive oxygen deficiencies from conducting TiO2−x into insulating TiO2, moves the boundary, and increases conductivity; after the voltage is removed, the oxygen bubbles and boundary remain frozen in place.[23]

In the proposed hybrid crossbar, a two-terminal memristor at every crosspoint connects CMOS-based pre-neurons and post-neurons with unique synaptic weights; a 100 nm-pitch crossbar could potentially provide a synaptic density of 10¹⁰/cm².[26]

### What objections did Vongehr and Meng raise in 2015 about

In their 25 June 2015 *Scientific Reports* article, 《The Missing Memristor Has Not Been Found》, Sascha Vongehr and Xiangkang Meng argued that the 2008 HP claim conflated three different concepts: the ideal circuit element, the real physical fourth device they believed Chua’s 1971 electromagnetic argument implied, and the broader class of resistance-switching memory devices later called memristive systems.[30][31][6]

Their first objection was definitional. An ideal charge-controlled memristor has resistance M(Q) determined only by the net charge Q that has passed through it. A broader *memristive system*, as formulated by Chua and Kang in 1976, has an internal state that can depend on other quantities, including current, voltage, time, temperature, ion configuration, or additional state variables. In a memristive system, flux need not be uniquely determined by charge.[31][3][16]

This distinction matters for physical switching memories. A filamentary ReRAM or electrochemical metallization cell changes state because ions, vacancies, atoms, interfaces, local temperature, and electric fields evolve. Such mechanisms can produce history-dependent resistance. They do not automatically produce a single-valued charge–flux relation in which net charge alone determines device state.[32][33]

Vongehr and Meng’s second objection was physical. They read Chua’s 1971 proposal as grounded in electromagnetic symmetry: the real missing fourth device would require an instantaneous interaction between first-order electric and magnetic fields. In their interpretation, the missing real memristor should be magnetic in a physical sense, much as a real inductor requires magnetic flux.[6][34]

The HP TiO2 device did not use magnetism as its switching mechanism. Its resistance changed through ionic drift and material-state evolution. The 2008 paper explicitly stated that magnetic field did not play an explicit role in its memristance mechanism and argued that mathematics required only a nonlinear relation between the integrals of current and voltage.[31][4][35]

Vongehr and Meng accepted that the HP devices were analogue memory devices. Their objection was that an analogue memory device that works without magnetism does not establish discovery of the particular magnetism-grounded physical fourth device they considered implicit in Chua’s 1971 argument. They argued that accepting a magnetism-free memristor on those original grounds would be analogous to accepting an inductor that works without magnetism.[31][7][8]

Their third objection concerned novelty and historical framing. They stated that TiO2 films between metals had been known since the 1960s and that memristive behavior in thin films existed before 1971. They characterized the 2008 advance as showing that known films could be described as resistors with memory, rather than discovering a previously unknown physical element.[31][6][36]

The earlier polarity-dependent thin-film memory reports identified in this critique include publications from 1967 and 1995.[31][6][34]

Their fourth objection concerned the idealization of the HP model. The original HP model represents the device as two serial resistance regions separated by a movable boundary. The boundary position acts like the slider of a potentiometer or rheostat: vacancy migration changes how much of the device occupies the low-resistance and high-resistance phases.[7][15] Vongehr argued that the device can be modeled by memristance only while the doped-region width remains between zero and the film thickness, and that boundary conditions must be enforced as those limits are approached.[34]

The critics did not deny that resistance switching occurred. They denied that switching and hysteresis alone proved the stronger historical claim that Chua’s missing fourth physical device had been discovered. Their conclusion was that the magnetism-grounded device remained unverified and was likely impossible, while still allowing that a real device might one day be found, perhaps in electro-optics.[6][31]

The debate therefore has two levels. At the broad engineering level, a device with useful history-dependent conductance can be called a memristor or memristive device. At the narrow fourth-element level, Vongehr and Meng reserve the name for a passive physical realization of a particular charge–flux and electromagnetic proposition.[6]

**Vongehr and Meng did not refute TiO2 resistive switching; they argued that resistive switching and analogue memory do not prove discovery of the magnetism-grounded fourth physical element that they read into Chua’s 1971 framework.**[6]

Vongehr and Meng also treated the four basic two-terminal circuit elements as circuit-theory models rather than automatically as physical devices. They argued that the proposed elements are mutually independent: a memristor cannot be constructed merely by connecting ordinary resistors, capacitors, and inductors, or by adding battery-like components.[37]

They reinforced their magnetic-induction argument with a mechanical analogue. Vongehr defined an ideal mechanical memristance as M = dp/dx, with p denoting momentum, and argued that a physically symmetry-derived mechanical realization must crucially involve inert mass; by analogy, he argued that the 1971 electrical realization must crucially involve magnetism.[38][20]

Vongehr and Meng recorded that Chua accepted the 2008 discovery claim, but rejected that acceptance as resolving the dispute. They noted Chua’s later statements that flux can be the time integral of voltage without a magnetic-flux interpretation and that resistance-switching memories are memristors; the critics regarded those statements as a broadened definition rather than validation of their proposed physical fourth element.[39][20]

Vongehr and Meng’s *Scientific Reports* article was published on 25 June 2015 in volume 5 as article 11657.[40]

RRAM is a two-terminal memory device with reversible transitions between a high-resistance state (HRS) and low-resistance state (LRS) when voltage polarity changes; formation, growth, and disruption of a conductive filament through an insulating layer can produce those transitions.[33]

Vongehr and colleagues identified the absence of magnetism in the 2008 HP model as its primary disqualifier, arguing that accepting a memristor without magnetism would also mean accepting an inductor that works without magnetism; they also objected to mixing magnetic flux with computed flux.[7][8]

Vongehr stated that the thin-film sandwiches involve no magnetic fields, although their resistance changes according to charge already passed; they can be modeled by memristance only while the doped-region width w lies between zero and the thin-film thickness D.[34]

The 2008 claim held that magnetism need not explicitly participate because mathematics requires a nonlinear relationship between the integrals of current and voltage; Vongehr and Meng answered that this is valid for a perfect memristor and mathematics alone, but not for the hypothesized real device.[6]

Vongehr and Meng wrote that the 2008 claim was not discovery of the missing memristor implied by the 1971 prediction of a fourth missing real device. They regarded that real device as likely impossible, while stating that it might still be discovered, possibly in electro-optics.[31]

Vongehr and Meng contrasted the 1971 hypothesized device, which they said needs magnetism but has no material memory, with available devices that constitute analogue memory and would work in a world without magnetism.[6]

### How did Strukov subsequently respond to the fourth-element objections?

Before Vongehr and Meng’s 2015 critique, Strukov, Snider, Stewart, and Williams’ 2008 *Nature* paper distinguished an ideal current-controlled memristor, v = R(w)i and dw/dt = i, for which the state variable w is charge, from a broader current-controlled, time-invariant, one-port memristive system, v = R(w,i)i and dw/dt = f(w,i), for which flux is not uniquely defined by charge.[3]

Its ideal model used voltage equal to state-dependent resistance times current, with state derivative equal to current. The authors stated that the state variable in this ideal case is charge and acknowledged that no physical model had been proposed that fully satisfies those simple equations.[3]

The same paper described memristive systems using a state-dependent resistance and a more general state equation. In this broader class, the internal state can evolve as a function of state and current, and flux is not uniquely defined by charge.[3] This is an important point in the later debate: the HP paper itself contained a conceptual distinction that critics used to argue that practical switching devices often fit the broader class better than the charge-only ideal.

The broader Chua–Kang memristive-system model can explicitly include time dependence: v = R(x,i,t)i and dx/dt = f(x,i,t). Its ideal charge-controlled special case has dq/dt = i and R = R(q).[16][139][140]

A later model clarification addressed electric-field assumptions in the original phenomenological treatment. In correspondence reported by Gale, the corresponding author stated that the field was not uniform across the device; it had a discontinuity at the moving boundary between doped and undoped regions. The voltage drop within the doped region limited boundary motion.[35] Gale argued that the published derivation required a discontinuity and that a midpoint approximation across a smoothed boundary was a better one-point approximation.[35]

A 2009 Williams-group drift–diffusion model produced pinched hysteresis without a single integrated-voltage versus integrated-current-density curve; it was consequently described as a memristive device rather than an ideal charge-controlled memristor.[137]

That clarification concerns how to model vacancy transport and field distribution. It does not answer Vongehr and Meng’s historical and physical objection about whether a magnetism-free oxide switch realizes the 1971 missing fourth device.

### Do commercial memristors truly exhibit the theoretical fourth-circuit-element properties predicted

Commercial devices marketed as memristors demonstrably exhibit useful resistance-switching and memory behavior. For example, Knowm describes its self-directed-channel devices as electrochemical metallization cells in which Ag+ motion changes resistance through changes in silver concentration at agglomeration sites along conductive channels.[43] Such devices are real physical memories, and their switching can be engineered for nonvolatile storage, analogue weights, and adaptive circuits.

The strict ideal question is whether resistance is determined only by net charge through a single-valued charge–flux constitutive relation. Tests of the commercial Knowm BS-AF-W and M+SDC Cr electrochemical-metallization cells found that their resistance states were not determined only by net transported charge.[1]

The relevant study tested Knowm BS-AF-W and M+SDC Cr electrochemical-metallization cells. These devices use Ag atoms moving through chalcogenide layers, with a Ge2Se3 layer doped by W in BS-AF-W devices or Cr in M+SDC Cr devices.[1] The authors applied more than 30 tests to each sample, using triangular and rectangular voltage pulses of both polarities.[1]

The test used a capacitor in series with the device. If the capacitor returns to its initial charge, an ideal charge-controlled memristor must return to its initial resistance because its state is determined only by net transported charge.[1][44] The tested Knowm cells did not do so: their final states differed from their initial states across multiple tests and operating conditions. The authors concluded that the resistance-switching memories tested could not be described by a memristance depending only on charge.[1]

Pershin and Di Ventra’s capacitor-based return-to-state test classified Cu–SiO₂ switching memories, as well as commercial Knowm devices, as non-ideal memristors.[141]

That result is device-specific but consequential. It rejects the ideal charge-only classification for the tested commercial Knowm samples; it does not say that every commercial ReRAM, phase-change memory, magnetic device, or electrochemical memory has been tested.[1][44]

The same line of critique shows why a pinched hysteresis loop cannot settle the issue. A pinched loop can arise in devices with internal memory variables other than charge, including ionic configuration, spin polarization, thermal state, atomic position, or defect distribution.[1][32][16] A current–voltage loop is therefore evidence of memory-dependent nonlinear dynamics, but not proof of the ideal M(q) law.

Finite response delays can make the integrated-voltage versus integrated-current relation of a physical nonlinear element multivalued even when its current–voltage curve crosses the origin.[142]

Commercial resistance-switching physics also creates reasons to expect departure from the ideal relation. ReRAM switching is stochastic because conductive-filament formation and rupture depend on thermally activated ion motion, local defects, and random microscopic paths. A threshold may be crossed at unpredictable times, so the final resistance state need not have a one-to-one relation with earlier voltage or charge history.[45][13][46]

The phenomenology of commercial devices also includes threshold behavior, state saturation, relaxation, read disturbance, retention loss, stochastic conductance change, and sometimes electrochemical nanobattery effects. These are not minor details: they are the physical mechanisms that make a switching memory work, but they place the device in the broader category of stateful nonlinear dynamical systems rather than proving a charge-only ideal element.[13][47][48]

Chua’s later broader position differs. He argued in 2011 that resistance-switching memories are memristors.[31][41] Under that inclusive engineering definition, commercial ReRAM, PCM, MRAM, and related two-terminal nonvolatile resistive memories may be called memristors if their periodic response meets the relevant memristive criteria.[13]

Vongehr and Meng, as well as later critics, use the word more narrowly. They reserve *ideal memristor* for the charge-only relation and reserve the historical “missing fourth device” claim for a physically grounded realization of the original charge–flux proposition.[31][6] Under this definition, commercial switching memories are generally memristive systems or resistance-switching memories, not demonstrated ideal fourth elements.

A 2024 review reaches the same practical distinction. It states that all reported memristors are resistive-switching devices and a special class of memristive systems, while an ideal memristor remains defined axiomatically by the charge–flux constitutive relationship.[2] Another 2024 paper describes a magnetic charge–flux interaction as progress toward an ideal device but states that a fully functioning ideal memristor with multiple or infinitely many stable states and no parasitic inductance remains to be achieved.[49]

**The evidence-qualified judgment is that commercial resistance-switching memories are demonstrated memristive devices or memristive systems, but they are not demonstrated realizations of Chua’s strict ideal fourth circuit element.** This conclusion rests on the distinction between charge-only state dependence and material-state-dependent switching, on direct negative capacitor-test results for specified commercial Knowm cells, and on continuing efforts to build a physical charge–flux device rather than merely a resistance-switching memory.[1][2][49]

This verdict does not diminish the engineering value of commercial devices. A device can be a useful nonvolatile analogue weight, a dense crosspoint memory, or a low-energy in-memory-computing element without being a fundamental passive circuit element. Abraham makes this point explicitly: disputing fundamentality does not diminish potential computing value.[7][8]

The broad engineering category covers several distinct physical mechanisms: structural phase change; thermally or electrically induced changes in electronic conduction; electrochemical filament growth; and spin-torque resistance change from spin alignment at an interface between differently polarized magnetic materials.[21] These mechanisms supply internal material state variables that can create history-dependent resistance and nonvolatile memory without establishing the charge-only ideal relation.[21]

Interface-barrier devices are another memristive technology category: they store a conductance state through barrier modulation rather than solely through filament formation or vacancy redistribution.[143][144][145]

Redox-based ReRAM also exhibits a nanobattery effect that conflicts with Chua’s memristor model; Valov and colleagues’ 2013 report accordingly concluded that memristor theory requires extension for those resistive switches.[20]

Chua’s original 1971 definition made the memristor the fourth fundamental circuit element with a nonlinear relation between electric charge and magnetic flux linkage; in 2011 he argued for a broader definition including all two-terminal nonvolatile memory devices based on resistance switching.[20]

Vongehr and Meng reported that Chua, originator of the 1971 hypothesis, defended the purported 2008 discovery and wrote in 2011, “Resistance switching memories are memristors.”[31]

The broader hardware landscape includes thermistors with temperature-dependent internal state; molecules whose resistance changes with atomic configuration; spintronic devices whose resistance varies with spin polarization; TiO2 thin films in which oxygen-vacancy ionic motion changes resistance; and VO2 thin films whose memory mechanism is an insulator-to-metal transition.[16]

RRAM exhibits statistical distributions in HRS resistance, LRS resistance, and switching voltage: fluctuations in conductive-filament radius affect LRS resistance, tunnelling-gap-distance fluctuations affect HRS resistance, and stochastic ion-migration barriers vary switching voltage.[45]

In oxide-based RRAM, the stochastic nature of conductive filaments produces resistance fluctuations that can defeat program-verify through fluctuation-induced false reading and produce a large resistance-distribution tail immediately after programming.[46]

Establishing the strict claim for any particular commercial device requires terminal measurements that reconstruct one reproducible φ(q) relation over a specified operating range, show that M = dφ/dq predicts behavior for different waveforms and histories except through q, and exclude additional state variables such as thermal, capacitive, ionic, or electrochemical dynamics. A successful demonstration would apply to that defined operating regime and device, not automatically to every product marketed as a memristor.

### The history of Chua’s 1971 theoretical memristor and Williams’ HP

The historical sequence explains why two groups can look at the same TiO2 switch and reach different conclusions. Chua’s 1971 proposal was a circuit-theory construct: a fourth two-terminal passive element defined by a charge–flux-linkage relation, with an electromagnetic-field interpretation and no passive physical realization then known.[11][9]

Williams, Strukov, Snider, and Stewart’s 2008 paper connected that framework to a nanoscale oxide switch whose resistance changes because charged defects move under bias. Their central contribution was to make a compact physical device whose ionic-electronic dynamics could be represented by a memristive mathematical model and could retain a programmed conductance state.[3][24]

The HP result therefore established an important link between circuit theory and resistive-switching device physics. It did not settle every interpretation of the word *memristor*. The HP group treated the integral of voltage as a measure of dopant motion and resistance change, while explicitly saying magnetism did not play an explicit role.[4]

Vongehr and Meng treated the absence of magnetism as decisive for the narrower historical claim. Their objection was not that the device lacked memory, but that it had the wrong kind of memory: material memory in an oxide through ionic rearrangement rather than the magnetic-field-grounded physical relation they inferred from Chua’s original symmetry argument.[6]

The two positions are therefore not directly contradictory at the device level. HP showed useful coupled ionic-electronic resistance switching. Vongehr and Meng disputed the circuit-theory label when it is used to claim discovery of a fundamental fourth physical element. The former is a claim about observable device operation; the latter is a claim about what that operation proves.

For neuromorphic hardware, the practical implication is straightforward. Designers should specify whether “memristor” means a broad resistance-switching synaptic device, a memristive system with internal state variables, or a strict charge-controlled ideal memristor. That terminology determines what is being claimed about device physics, not whether the hardware can store weights or perform useful computation.

The HP thin-film mechanism has finite state bounds: memristance is displayed while both doped and depleted regions contribute to resistance. When ionic motion reaches a boundary, the device holds its state and behaves as a constant resistor until the current is reversed.[20] This bounded ionic state is consistent with a useful switching-memory model but is distinct from demonstrating unrestricted charge-controlled ideal-element behavior.[20][37]

Vongehr and Meng reproduced Chua’s 1971 statement that the physical mechanism characterizing a memristor device must arise from the instantaneous, memoryless interaction between first-order electric and first-order magnetic fields, while stating that the 2008 claim treated magnetic flux as irrelevant.[6]

### Synthesis —

Chua’s 1971 memristor is a charge–flux-linkage circuit element whose resistance depends on accumulated charge; passivity requires non-negative memristance and a monotonically increasing φ–q curve.[9][11] HP’s 2008 TiO2 device demonstrated real, nonvolatile ionic-electronic resistive switching and supplied a compelling functional synapse analogy because conductance can store an adjustable connection weight.[3][4] Direct capacitor-based tests found that specified commercial Knowm electrochemical memory cells did not satisfy the charge-only ideal-memristor criterion.[1] The practical consensus is thus narrower than the marketing label: resistance-switching devices are important memristive hardware, while the strict ideal fourth-element claim remains unsettled and unproven for commercial devices.[2][49]

## Crossbar arrays: sneak paths and device variability

Judgment — A crossbar array places two-terminal memory devices where perpendicular metal lines intersect. It is attractive because the physics of current summation can perform analogue multiply–accumulate operations in place. Its central engineering problems are sneak-path current—unwanted current through unselected cells—and device variability—differences between cells and between switching cycles. Both can corrupt a stored conductance, which is used as a synaptic weight.

KAIST describes RRAM as storing information by forming or breaking a conductive filament in a thin insulating layer. KAIST also pursued a distinct, algorithmic route. KAIST’s approaches are complementary: self-rectification suppresses unwanted conduction, reliability-aware decoding corrects its read-error consequences, and electrode engineering aims to make conductive-filament formation less random. ### How do Knowm’s AHaH nodes address the sneak path problem?

Knowm calls an AHaH node a set of \(n\) memristive synapses that share a common output electrode. It maps each synapse to a biological synapse and the node to a biological neuron; the node implements a linear summation because physical currents add at the shared electrode.[50][65] Its synaptic weight is represented by a differential conductance: two competing conductive paths encode positive or negative weight.[65][58]

Knowm identifies sparse input activation as a source of sneak paths because driven lines can send current through electrically floating rows or columns. It states that this error worsens as the crossbar grows.[58] Its response is a unit-crossbar architecture: row and column bidirectional analogue multiplexers select one device in a small \(N \times M\) crossbar at a time. An 8 × 8 unit thus selects one of 64 devices, or no device.[58]

First, Knowm keeps individual crossbars small. It states that arrays of 32 × 32 or less permit individual reads and writes without sneak currents becoming overwhelming, and gives 16 × 16 as an example of relatively clean single-device access without a transistor at every junction.[73] Second, it combines many unit crossbars at a higher level, recovering parallelism outside the individual passive array.[73] Third, the proposed meta-crossbar shares unit-crossbar selection lines and combines row and column enable lines with a NAND gate to control unit-crossbar enables, so that elements can be isolated from active rows and columns similarly to a selector device.[58]

This is a mitigation strategy, not a claim that passive-array leakage vanishes. Knowm explicitly says that keeping arrays small reduces the effect, whereas complete elimination requires selector devices or driving the whole crossbar.[58] In its described build, non-selected rows and columns are left floating; Knowm says this produces sneak paths and cross-talk.[73] In programming experiments, one column of an 8 × 8 array was relatively straightforward to tune, but programming across the full crossbar had not achieved perfect isolation.[58]

**Knowm therefore addresses sneak paths by time-multiplexed selection and architectural partitioning, not by claiming that every crosspoint is independently isolated.** This preserves the compact passive cell but trades fine-grained parallel access for multiplexers, enable logic, and composition across many small arrays.[58][73]

Knowm addresses variability on several levels. Its self-directed-channel (SDC) devices are electrochemical-metallization devices, a different class from HP’s oxygen-ion-based valence-change-memory devices.[74] Knowm states that CBRAM variability arises because stochastic filament creation makes filament shape and contact points inconsistent over switching cycles, and that SDC devices overcome these challenges through a self-organized channel that constrains and defines conductive pathways and contact points.[75] Knowm reports a 300–500 Å acceptable active-layer-thickness range and typical SDC functional-device wafer yields above 90%; it contrasts this process tolerance with CBRAM, for which Ag incorporation, glass thickness, and Ag thickness must be tightly controlled because too little Ag switches poorly and too much produces saturation.[75]

The devices remain stochastic. Knowm’s generalized metastable-switch model treats a memristor as many switches that change state probabilistically with voltage and temperature.[50] Laguna-Sanchez, Lopez-Guerrero and Barron-Fernandez’s 2023 threshold study characterizes Knowm SDC set and reset thresholds and incorporates hysteresis-cycle variability into the Generalized Mean Metastable Switch (GMMS) memristor model, enabling simulations that reproduce this variability.[52] Alpha-stable random variables represent skewed and heavy-tailed threshold variability.[52] Experimental/model work also identifies cycle-to-cycle SET and RESET variation and adds a chaotic generator to represent threshold fluctuations.[74]

Characterization of commercial Knowm self-directed-channel cells reported low-current behavior and cycle variability absent from a common mean-metastable-switch model.[51][146]

A 2022 model of Knowm devices added low-current effects and cycle-to-cycle variation absent from the mean-metastable-switch model; a 2023 study modeled commercial SDC variability with a memdiode model.[146][51]

At circuit level, Knowm uses a differential pair for a synapse, reducing common-mode noise including decay.[58] It calibrated the sensing path with known resistors from roughly 1 kΩ to 499 kΩ because component variation and parasitic capacitance distorted the nominal current–voltage relationship.[58] Its iterative Flip-Flop programming algorithm reached within approximately 5 kΩ of the target resistance in 2–100 pulses; high-resistance measurement noise limited the result.[58] Because non-selected rows and columns float, the arrangement is sensitive to a stuck-on or very-low-resistance device; the devices begin in a GΩ high-resistance state and were operated effectively in the 100 kΩ–500 kΩ range, within a stated 100 kΩ–1 MΩ operating range without elevated forming voltages.[58]

A 2022 Knowm-device identification study used a 46.25 kΩ series resistor, close to a 50 kΩ current-limiting recommendation, to limit device stress and snapback current; the resistor was not an array selector.[146]

At algorithm level, Knowm presents AHaH plasticity as a fault-tolerance mechanism: its reported exercise began with a support-vector-machine-trained network, then applied unsupervised AHaH adaptation under faults and errors. The network reportedly self-repaired when redundancy remained and most synapses followed the AHaH rule.[76] **The resulting design logic is to tolerate and adapt around variation, alongside device tuning and calibration, rather than to assume uniform switching.**[52][58][76]

For generic selector-based comparison, the stated 8F² 1T1M cell occupies **2.0×** the stated 4F² 1S1M cell area. This quantifies the density trade-off when a transistor is used for strong cell isolation rather than a stacked nonlinear selector; 1T1M gate-line compliance currents also assist linear, symmetric conductance increase and decrease with minimal cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variation.[27]

Sneak-path current depends on array size, resistance values, input voltage, and the input/output switch vector; parasitic resistance and wire resistance also constrain crossbar scaling.[77][78]

Jeon, Ryu, Im and colleagues (2024) demonstrated the direct physical alternative to an added selector in a 32 × 32, 1 kb self-rectifying-memristor array: 1/3-biasing placed 1/3 of the operating voltage on unselected upper lines and 2/3 on unselected lower lines. The array had self-rectifying selectivity of approximately 10⁴ under that scheme and suppressed sneak currents; an 8 × 8 control array using a memristor without selection functionality showed inaccurate programming and readout from sneak-current interference.[79]

In the kT-RAM implementation, standard RAM addresses the two-dimensional synapse space, an H-tree forms the shared AHaH-node electrical summation point, and temporal partitioning assigns different array spaces to different AHaH nodes. Switches connect selected memristor-pair synapses to the H-tree for read and write cycles, while other synapses remain floating.[80][69]

The 2022 *Nature Communications* authors reported a transistor-free Pt/gradual-oxygen-concentration-TiOₓ/Ti memristor with a rectification ratio of 10⁴ and an on/off ratio above 2,000. They fabricated a selector-less 20 × 20 crossbar containing 400 devices and reported that self-rectification prevented sneak-path disturbance during readout.[60][81]

Comparison and ranked verdict — The ranking criterion is quantified fabricated-array isolation and variation. First is KAIST’s gradual-TiOₓ implementation: its transistor-free, selector-less 20 × 20 array of 400 devices used self-rectification with a rectifying ratio of 10⁴; the authors reported that readout was not disturbed by sneak paths, 1.39% temporal variation over 125 consecutive DC cycles, 3.87% spatial variation across 30 randomly selected devices, and approximately 100% array yield.[60] Second is Knowm: small multiplexed unit crossbars limit access to one selected device, but its floating-line arrangement retained sneak paths and its 8 × 8 programming work had not achieved perfect whole-array isolation; its variation response includes threshold modelling, calibration, and iterative programming.[58][73][52] Third are the distinct Michigan systems: the 2009 crossbar reported fabrication and parametric yield and proposed a PN diode for crosstalk mitigation, while the 2010 graded Ag/Si synapse reported forming-free continuous-front analogue switching.[59][26]

Knowm addresses sneak paths through small multiplexed unit crossbars and time-multiplexed access rather than a dedicated transistor at every crosspoint. The AHaH self-repair result concerns software network adaptation under injected faults rather than a physical-array sneak-path measurement.[58][73][22][76]

Self-rectifying resistive memory avoids an extra rectifying device; a reported five-layer three-dimensional crossbar of 100 nm memristors used built-in diodes and experimentally suppressed both intralayer and interlayer sneak-path currents.[62]

AHaH means Anti-Hebbian and Hebbian. It names Knowm’s learning rule and node architecture, rather than a material-based selector or an intrinsic sneak-path-blocking device property.

kT-RAM means Thermodynamic RAM, Knowm’s proposed hardware architecture for implementing AHaH nodes with adaptive memristive synapses and a shared electrical summing node.

### How do Knowm’s AHaH nodes address device variability?

The Michigan Ag/Si device is an amorphous-silicon memristor with a co-sputtered silver/silicon active layer. A deliberately engineered Ag/Si composition gradient forms an Ag-rich, high-conductivity region and an Ag-poor, low-conductivity region.[23][26] Its defining physics is continuous motion of a conduction front under bias, replacing abrupt, localized conductive-filament creation.[26]

The device eliminates the electroforming step. The two variation types are device-to-device (D2D) variation, in which random electroforming creates a different filament structure in each filamentary device, and cycle-to-cycle (C2C) variation, which comes from unstable channels or random formation of new channels.[26][5] Jo and colleagues state that their graded structure produces reliable analogue switching.[26]

**The Michigan graded Ag/Si synapse addresses variability at the switching-mechanism level by avoiding forming and localized filament creation.** This is well matched to analogue synaptic weights, because gradual conductance change is easier to use than abrupt binary switching.[26] The separate 1 kb Ag/a-Si/p-Si crossbar reported 98% fabrication yield; among 400 tested crosspoints, 92% met an ON/OFF-ratio-greater-than-10³ parametric-yield criterion and 80% had ON resistance of 50–150 kΩ.

Abrupt filament formation can be useful for digital switching, even though it usually hinders multilevel analogue learning.[145][147]

The authors also reported uniformity in the first 100 measured bits.[59] The Ag/a-Si/p-Si crossbar’s switching was attributed to conductive filaments comprising a series of Ag particles, with ON-state resistance dominated by tunnelling between the final Ag particle and the bottom electrode.[59]

Judgment — A passive Ag/Si crossbar needs an array-access mechanism to control sneak paths. Jo, Kim and Lu’s proposed PN diode is such a crosspoint-level mechanism for crosstalk mitigation.[59] In generic RRAM crossbars, a 1T1R cell uses a series MOS transistor for current control and to minimize leakage from half-selected and unselected devices, while a 1S1R cell adds a nonlinear selector in series with the RRAM device: the selected cell receives the selection voltage, whereas all other cells receive a smaller voltage and consequently exponentially lower current.[82]

For a generic 1S1R access cell, preserving crosspoint density depends on a selector with high nonlinearity, low leakage and compatible endurance.[147]

The reported Ag/Si design makes a clear engineering trade: it attacks one root cause of analogue-weight variation, but crossbar-scale reliability still depends on access circuitry, line resistance, programming procedures, and the distribution of cell conductances. Conductance drift and device-to-device or cycle-to-cycle variation can degrade learning accuracy even when a device displays analogue switching.[83] **Michigan is thus the clearest of the three examples as a materials-level anti-filament strategy, while Knowm and KAIST supply more explicit array-level mitigation concepts.**[26][58][55]

In Jeon, Ryu, Im and colleagues’ 2024 self-rectifying-array study, the 1 kb, 32 × 32 array had HRS and LRS read-current coefficients of variation of 0.060 and 0.057, respectively, an operating distribution below 6%; incremental-pulse SET and RESET mean amplitudes were 4.3 V and −4.9 V, each with 0.1 V standard deviation, yielding voltage coefficients of variation of 0.023 and 0.020, approximately 2%.[79] The authors attributed the narrow distribution to non-filamentary interface switching and exclusion of electroforming.[79] All 1,024 cells were reported to operate without failure and the fabrication yield was 100%.[79]

The 2014 AHaH design encodes each signed synaptic weight with a same-polarity differential pair of memristors, rather than with one device. In the absence of Hebbian feedback, the authors state that the pair evolves toward a symmetric state that alleviates device inhomogeneity; their functional model also includes Gaussian noise and decay variables for stochastic device behavior.[22]

For the gradual-TiOₓ implementation, the 2022 *Nature Communications* authors measured 1.39% temporal variation, expressed as σ/μ, across 125 consecutive DC cycles without current compliance and 3.87% spatial variation across 30 randomly selected devices. They reported approximately 100% array yield, endurance above 10⁶ cycles, 10 μs switching speed, and uniform operation across all 400 cells of the 20 × 20 array.[60]

Ageing is loss of a conductance state after prolonged ON/OFF switching; changes, combination, or disappearance of multiple conductance states introduce crossbar analogue-dot-product errors and make analogue-network programming unreliable.[84]

A memristor AI chip uses peripheral circuitry for on-chip error correction linked to a digital controller that runs AI algorithms in the analogue domain by self-calibration, without compensation operations or pretraining.[85]

The same SDC study performed 18 sinusoidal-excitation tests per memristor, each containing 100 periods and 1,000 points per period. It found that optimized metastable-memristor-model parameters change drastically with excitation-signal parameters and must be adjusted for the intended signal; the parameters do not generalize device dynamics across all operating conditions.[86]

A reported rapid-pulsing experiment found that the KNOWM memristor had comparatively weaker and less stable pinched hysteresis loops, limiting the magnitude and repeatability of its state change under rapid pulses.[87]

The distinction between yield and switching uniformity is material to Knowm’s approach. Device-to-device variation arises from spatial fabrication differences such as line-edge roughness and film-thickness irregularity, while temporal randomness during operation remains the more difficult variation mode in memristive devices.[88] Knowm’s probabilistic-threshold model, closed-loop programming, differential encoding and AHaH adaptation are therefore complementary responses to distinct spatial and temporal error mechanisms.[52][58][76]

A measurement-and-modeling study tested each Knowm memristor with 18 sinusoidal-excitation experiments, each containing 100 periods and 1,000 points per period, and found that optimized metastable-memristor-model parameters change drastically with excitation-signal parameters.[86]

In one Knowm array, the reported device yield remained 100% after multiple years on the shelf, although one array element had a programming error.[58]

In 10,000 trials on a reset Knowm device at fixed voltage, wider pulses generally produced larger resistance changes, but the conductance changes were stochastic and measurement noise was considerable.[58]

Iterative write-verify programming applies SET or RESET pulses until measured conductance reaches a target range, and a dual-threshold write-verify circuit checks whether resistance lies within its target window.[89]

Closed-loop calibration adjusts operating voltages or pulse widths to compensate non-idealities, while redundancy and algorithm-level error tolerance accommodate device variation.[71]

Program-verify can reduce conductance variation and improve programming precision, but it can require many pulses and substantial overhead.[82]

Across the three examples, there is no single universal remedy. Knowm combines small multiplexed arrays, calibration, iterative programming, and adaptive learning; this is the broadest documented response, but its own floating-line implementation retains sneak paths and imperfect whole-array programming isolation.[58] Michigan’s graded Ag/Si device directly addresses the physics of abrupt filamentary variability through electroforming-free analogue switching, but it is not an access-device architecture.[26][5] KAIST spans physical self-rectification, decoder-based error correction, and proposed filament localization, showing that RRAM mitigation often crosses device, circuit, and algorithm layers.[57][55][61]

**The engineering conclusion is that scalable crossbars require co-design: conductance uniformity alone does not suppress sneak paths, and a selector or decoder alone does not make synaptic weights uniform.**[27][45]

Available-evidence comparison and conditional ranking — On the named criterion of directly documented treatment of both sneak paths and device variability, Knowm is first because it documents multiplexed unit-crossbar selection, residual floating-line leakage, threshold-variation modelling, calibration and closed-loop programming; KAIST is second because its separately documented self-rectifying-array and decoder approaches address sneak-current consequences while its electrode engineering targets filament consistency; Michigan is third because it documents forming-free analogue switching but no Ag/Si-specific array-access treatment.[58][52][57][55][61][26]

This ordering would change with matched measurements of array size, read/write biasing, sneak current or read margin, read-error rate, device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle conductance distributions, programming yield, energy and accuracy.[27]

Jo, Kim and Lu (2009) described a 32 × 32, 1 kb crossbar comprising boron-doped polysilicon-nanowire bottom electrodes, an a-Si active layer, and Ag-nanowire top electrodes; every crosspoint was a two-terminal Ag/a-Si/p-Si switch.[59] The p-Si nanowire resistance could reach 30 kΩ and limit fully automated operation, so the authors proposed a metal or silicide layer next to p-Si to reduce series resistance.[59] For Ag/a-Si/p-Si devices, changing a-Si deposition temperature controlled ON resistance from approximately 100 MΩ to 10 kΩ.[59] The Ag/a-Si/p-Si configuration had typical endurance above 10⁵ cycles, whereas tested Ag/a-Si/Ni devices had approximately 1 kΩ ON resistance, approximately 1 mA programming current, and endurance of only a few hundred cycles.[59]

Ranked verdict — On the criterion of a documented array-level mechanism for sneak-path control together with quantified device-variation measurements, KAIST ranks first: its gradual-TiOₓ 20 × 20 passive array combines self-rectification with reported 1.39% temporal and 3.87% spatial variation. Knowm ranks second because it documents multiplexed partitioning, programming and adaptive mitigation but reports residual floating-line sneak paths and imperfect full-array isolation. ## Synaptic plasticity and 2020–2024 benchmarks

Neuromorphic hardware calls an adjustable connection between artificial neurons a **synapse**. The central engineering question is not whether a resistive-memory device can change conductance—many can—but whether its physical change provides a better way to learn, retain, and use weights than transistor circuits and CMOS memories. This requires separating device-level plasticity, where a component changes state under pulses, from system-level learning and inference, where an entire network achieves an accuracy, energy, latency, and retention result.

Judgment — “Memristor” is a broad implementation label, not a single synapse technology. It includes devices whose state changes through ionic motion, filament formation, phase transformation, ferroelectric polarization, or magnetic effects. A fair comparison therefore asks which device, array cell, programming protocol, peripheral CMOS, neural-network model, and benchmark conditions were used.

### What relevant device and CMOS synapse implementations form the hardware

A **memristive synapse** normally represents a neural-network weight as electrical conductance: high conductance means a stronger connection and low conductance a weaker one. In a crossbar array, voltages encode input activations on rows, conductances encode weights at crosspoints, and summed column currents implement vector–matrix multiplication (VMM), the multiply-and-accumulate operation that dominates neural-network inference. The field groups two-terminal synaptic devices into resistive random-access memory (RRAM), phase-change memory (PCM), conductive-bridge memory, and spin-based memory; a cell can represent either a binary potentiated/depressed state or an analog weight.[94][28]

RRAM typically changes resistance by forming, rupturing, or modifying a conductive filament in an insulating switching layer. Its conductance can be programmed to multiple levels, making it useful for multilevel weight storage, although the state changes can be nonlinear, asymmetric, and stochastic.[95][96] PCM stores a weight through the resistance difference between amorphous and crystalline material states; PCM arrays are often used with one or more access transistors and differential pairs of devices for signed weights.[18] Conductive-bridge and diffusive devices use metal-ion or nanoparticle migration, often silver, to form and relax a conductive connection. That relaxation can itself implement short-lived synaptic state.[94][95]

A signed neural-network weight needs both positive and negative values, while a single conductance is nonnegative. A common solution is a **differential pair**, two devices whose conductance difference represents the signed value. Knowm’s kT-bit uses this approach: the synaptic weight is the difference between two memristor conductances.[73] Differential weights also appear in PCM systems, including the ResNet-32 experiment in which each weight was represented by two PCM devices.[18]

Knowm’s AHaH—Anti-Hebbian and Hebbian—implementation is a particular memristor-synapse architecture, not a generic description of all memristors. Its AHaH Node is a perceptron-like neuron operating an AHaH plasticity rule, and its physical synapses use differential pairs of metal-doped self-directed-channel (SDC) memristors. Knowm describes circuit variants called 1-2, 2-1, 1-2i, and 2-1i; each requires a distinct drive pattern to increment, decrement, or decay the stored synaptic state.[97][58][73][98] In its architectural description, analog weights are stored, read, and modified locally while communication is digital.[58]

The University of Michigan Ag/Si synapse is materially and operationally different. Its active layer is co-sputtered silver and silicon with an intentional composition gradient that forms Ag-rich and Ag-poor regions. The authors report that continuous motion of a conduction front replaces abrupt localized-filament formation, avoiding a forming step and providing analog switching.[26] Its demonstrated neuromorphic circuit used CMOS integrate-and-fire neurons connected through a 100 nm × 100 nm memristor.

The CMOS timing circuit converted the interval between pre- and postsynaptic spikes into a programming-pulse width: a presynaptic spike before a postsynaptic spike potentiated the device, while reverse ordering depressed it.[26]

The practical hardware landscape is dominated by hybrids rather than pure alternatives. In a hybrid memristor–CMOS synapse, the resistive device commonly stores the long-lived weight while CMOS provides pulse shaping, neuron dynamics, sensing, conversion, addressing, calibration, activation functions, and learning control. A proposed DPI–memristor synapse illustrates the division of labor: the **differential-pair integrator (DPI)**, an analog CMOS current-mode synapse circuit, supplies shared temporal dynamics, while memristor conductance scales the synaptic output current.[28] Similarly, NeuRRAM integrates 3 million RRAM devices with CMOS circuitry in 48 compute-in-memory cores; each cell is a one-transistor–one-resistor (1T1R) structure.[17]

CMOS-only synapses have several forms:

- **SRAM synapses:** static random-access memory stores binary or multibit weights in transistor cells. A conventional SRAM bitcell has at least six transistors, is volatile, and is mature in CMOS manufacturing. SRAM can also support in-memory multiplication and accumulation through current-, charge-, or digital-domain circuits. [99][96][100]

- **Floating-gate and Flash synapses:** charge trapped on a floating gate shifts transistor threshold voltage, which changes the synaptic current. In subthreshold operation, the input signal depends on gate voltage while the stored threshold shift supplies a multiplicative weight term. [95] NOR Flash arrays have been adapted for selective programming and erasing for synaptic applications, at a reported 2.3-times cell-area increase for one rerouted architecture. [95]

- **Analog CMOS synapses:** capacitors, transconductors, current mirrors, and spike-trace circuits implement time-dependent synaptic dynamics. The DPI circuit can create biologically plausible time constants and nonlinear short-term plasticity. [28] Capacitor-based weights are volatile and must be refreshed or repeatedly charged, however. [29][99]

- **Digital neuromorphic synapses:** systems such as Intel Loihi store and update digital synaptic state in SRAM-backed CMOS logic. Loihi’s learning engine supports programmable learning rules rather than relying on a device’s intrinsic analog state transition. [101]

**The key hardware distinction is that a memristive synapse can colocate conductance-based multiplication and nonvolatile weight storage in a small two-terminal element, whereas a CMOS synapse realizes the same functions through transistors, capacitors, and memory cells that can be more controllable but consume more circuit resources.** In deployed systems, however, the two are usually complementary: the Michigan device requires CMOS neurons and pulse logic, and major RRAM or PCM accelerators rely extensively on CMOS periphery.[26][28][17][96]

Memristive behavior has several distinct physical bases. A review classifies them as structural phase changes such as PCM; thermally or electrically excited carrier transport; electrochemical filament growth; and spin-torque resistance changes.[21] Ferroelectric devices provide another weight-control route: gradual nucleation and growth of oppositely polarized domains evolves the domain configuration and tunes intermediate resistance values.[20] Magnetic tunnel junctions can show memristive behavior through spin-transfer torque, in which current-flow duration determines the required current and charge is the key variable.[20]

A 2021 IEEE Transactions on Electron Devices paper illustrates an additional RRAM implementation boundary: it proposed and simulated a passive-RRAM-crossbar long short-term memory (LSTM) network, rather than reporting an experimentally realized array. Its framework performed sampling, forward pass, loss calculation and backpropagation digitally, while modeled passive arrays performed vector–matrix multiplication and conductance updates using an experimentally calibrated compact model.[102] The proposed update used one 100 ns, 0.8 V set pulse for potentiation or one 100 ns, −0.8 V reset pulse for depression according to the sign of the desired weight change.[102]

Additional 2024 device demonstrations broaden the implementation landscape. A fabricated hybrid CMOS/memristor binarized neural-network system places TiN/HfOₓ/Ti/TiN memristors, with a 10-nm HfOₓ active layer, above a 130-nm CMOS process. It stores binary +1/−1 synaptic weights and neuron thresholds nonvolatily; a dedicated XNOR precharge sense amplifier reads the two devices representing a weight, and digital population-count circuits accumulate the results.[103] The same system performs binarization and training off chip, uses a microcontroller between layers, and has no input buffer or dedicated convolution circuitry.[103]

A 2024 analogue Ag/InP₃S₆/graphene-or-PtTe₂ memristor uses silver-ion intercalation and de-intercalation to obtain analogue switching. The authors reported an on/off ratio up to 10⁸, more than 8-bit conductance states, and attojoule-level power consumption.[104] These implementations reinforce that a memristive synapse can be binary and digitally read, or multilevel and analogue; neither format eliminates the CMOS circuits that perform sensing, accumulation, control, and system integration.[103][104][105]

A PCM prototype used doped Ge2Sb2Te5 PCM devices integrated in 90-nm CMOS. Its transistor-accessed experimental array contained one million devices arranged as 512 word lines by 2,048 bit lines.[18]

PICO-RAM is a CMOS SRAM compute-in-memory implementation in which each cluster contains nine standard 6T SRAM cells that store weights and a separate thin-cell multiply-accumulate unit that performs multi-bit charge-domain multiplication and accumulation.[100]

RRAM is nonvolatile memory used as a synapse that realizes ANN weights in mixed-signal CMOS neuromorphic hardware; RRAM crossbar analogue-compute arrays perform analogue vector–matrix multiplication and can support localized on-chip learning.[105]

The 2024 hardware review identifies memristive crossbars and 1T1R structures as components of memristor-based neural-network hardware, alongside CMOS digital and analogue circuitry for sensing and activation functions.[96]

A 2023 HfOₓ-bilayer memristor study by Sahu, Park, Chung, Han, and Yoon specifically targeted 《Linear and Symmetric Synaptic Weight Update Characteristics by Controlling Filament Geometry》. It identifies filament-geometry control as a device-design route for linear and symmetric weight updates.[106]

Lithium-ion three-terminal electrochemical random-access memory (ECRAM) is another device alternative. A review reports that Li-ion ECRAM has less stochasticity, good reproducibility, and improved switching symmetry and linearity compared with existing RRAM devices.[93]

NeuRRAM illustrates the physical cost of a selected-cell RRAM implementation. Its 1T1R cell places each RRAM device in series with a selector NMOS transistor; the selector cuts off sneak paths and supplies current compliance during programming and reading. The current array density is limited by RRAM write-current and write-voltage requirements, and the chip uses large thick-oxide I/O transistors to withstand forming voltages above 4 V and provide sufficient write current.[107]

Sagar et al. (2022) demonstrated solution-processed organic memtransistors using an unconventional redox-gating mechanism.[108] The devices use a conjugated-polymer thin film and a redox-active solid electrolyte as gate dielectric, operate below −1.5 V gate voltage, have subthreshold swing below 120 mV/dec, and have ON/OFF current ratio above 10⁸.[108]

RRAM crossbar options include a passive one-resistor (1R) cell, a one-selector/one-resistor (1S1R) cell, a one-transistor/one-resistor (1T1R) cell, and a one-capacitor (1C) array.[82]

Wang et al. (2017, 'Memristors with diffusive dynamics as synaptic emulators for neuromorphic computing') (2019) reported an artificial synapse that integrates a memristor and selector transistor into a multiterminal device using monolayer polycrystalline MoS₂ grown by chemical-vapor deposition.[60][109] Its switching mechanism does not require a filament-forming process, unlike the filament-formation variability identified for two-terminal memristor artificial synapses.[109] Gate tunability enables bipolar resistive-state switching up to four orders of magnitude and high cycling endurance, while the third gate terminal provides an additional degree of freedom for tuning synaptic plasticity.[109]

In kT-RAM, a synapse consists of two serially connected memristors used as a voltage divider, and its output voltage represents the synaptic weight.[110]

A kT-RAM read pulse both returns the weight and drives the synapse toward a forgetful state, while a subsequent write pulse across one memristor performs learning.[110]

The proposed Thermodynamic-RAM co-processor combines a two-dimensional array of differential memristor-pair synapses with CMOS RAM circuitry that digitally addresses which pairs are coupled together as neurons.[110]

### What synaptic-plasticity mechanisms does memristor physics enable?

**Synaptic plasticity** is the change in connection strength caused by activity. In a memristive device, it is usually expressed as a conductance update: **potentiation** increases conductance and therefore strengthens the effective synaptic weight; **depression** decreases conductance and weakens it. Voltage-pulse sequences produce these changes by modifying physical state variables such as atomic positions, oxygen-vacancy distributions, filament geometry, material phase, ferroelectric polarization, or spin configuration.[96]

In oxide RRAM, a preliminary forming operation may create a conductive filament made of oxygen vacancies or metallic impurities. Subsequent pulses adjust the filament and hence resistance. The ability to tune the resistance analogically supports multilevel weight storage, with the cited review describing at least 3-bit operation.[95] In the early HP titanium-oxide picture discussed elsewhere in this report, ionic motion shifts a boundary between more-conductive and less-conductive regions; more generally, this type of history-dependent ionic redistribution gives a device both memory and conductance modulation.[2]

Memristive devices can also offer tunable intermediate conductance states under low-stress operation.[143]

Pulse-driven updates can approximate the two basic learning operations. In AIST-based devices, 50 potentiation pulses at −0.6 V for 5 μs and 50 depression pulses at 0.6 V for 5 μs changed conductance. In PCMO-based devices, 50 potentiation pulses at −3 V for 1 ms and 50 depression pulses at 3 V for 1 ms changed memristance. In TaOx-based devices, 100 potentiation pulses at −1 V for 10 μs and 100 depression pulses at 1.15 V for 10 μs changed conductance.[12] RRAM can similarly represent weights with multiple conductance states adjusted by wordline and bitline pulses.[111] These mechanisms are physically local: the state is stored where the multiplication occurs, unlike a conventional processor that normally moves data between memory and arithmetic units.

A central biologically inspired rule is **spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)**. In STDP, the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes decides whether a weight strengthens or weakens. In a common overlap implementation, shaped pre- and postsynaptic pulses are individually below switching threshold but their overlap exceeds the threshold. If the pre-spike arrives first, the overlap produces a set transition and potentiation; if the post-spike arrives first, a reset transition produces depression.[95] The cited 1T1R RRAM experiment allowed updates only when the timing difference was between −10 ms and +10 ms.[95]

The Michigan Ag/Si hybrid circuit experimentally demonstrated this timing rule. The CMOS neurons generated programming pulses whose widths decayed exponentially with the timing difference, and the resulting weight change was fitted by exponential functions resembling biological STDP curves.[26] This is an important result, but it is a hybrid result: the memristor provides the adjustable conductance while CMOS creates the timing representation and pulse protocol.

Memristive devices can also encode short-lived state. A **diffusive memristor** with Ag nanoclusters uses pulse-driven growth and relaxation of conductive nanostructures. The cited implementation emulated paired-pulse facilitation and paired-pulse depression, both forms of **short-term plasticity (STP)** in which recent activity transiently changes the response to a later pulse.[94] An Ag/MgO/Pt device was reported to emulate paired-pulse facilitation, augmentation, post-tetanic potentiation, early long-term potentiation, and late long-term potentiation, with responses spanning milliseconds to days; the mechanism was formation and spontaneous rupture of silver nanofilaments.[29]

A second route uses **second-order memristors**, in which another internal variable retains short-term history. The cited review gives temperature as an example: after one spike, elevated local temperature persists for about a 500 ns thermal time constant, so a second non-overlapping spike can trigger a larger conductance change. This allows sequential spikes rather than pulse overlap to implement timing- and rate-dependent learning.[95] An atomic-switch RRAM with a silver bottom electrode, Ag2S solid electrolyte, and a metal top electrode separated from Ag2S by a nanogap uses incomplete versus complete Ag bridges to distinguish short- from long-term potentiation. Spontaneous bridge relaxation supports short-term plasticity, while pulse frequency determines spike-rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP) potentiation or depression.[95]

The same physical features that enable compact local learning also create nonideal updates. RRAM and PCM conductance changes are often nonlinear, asymmetric between potentiation and depression, and stochastic; these properties complicate precise in-situ gradient-based training because an intended numerical update may not equal the actual device update. ECRAM and ionic floating-gate concepts have been investigated for more linear, symmetric, analogue conductance behavior. The number of available conductance states and the linearity of potentiation and depression determine the weight-update procedure and the basic requirements for the peripheral CMOS circuitry that performs it.[95][96] Cycle-to-cycle RRAM resistance variation occurs even below the switching threshold, and measured distributions after training can contain tail bits that indicate training failures.[45]

Judgment — Memristor physics does not intrinsically implement a complete biological learning rule. It supplies state-dependent conductance changes and, in selected devices, volatile internal dynamics. A usable STDP, rate rule, reinforcement rule, or backpropagation update also requires pulse encoding, signed-weight representation, selection, readout, and error-management circuits.

Knowm’s 2014 AHaH Computing paper connects the metastable-switch (MSS) device model, Anti-Hebbian and Hebbian (AHaH) plasticity rules, and nodes built from differential memristor pairs. Its differential memristor pairs encode a signed state, while the AHaH rule combines Hebbian strengthening with anti-Hebbian weakening through circuit-specific drive patterns.[51][73][98] That is a real synaptic-computing implementation, but it is a defined architecture built around particular devices and programming instructions, not evidence that every commercial or experimental memristor naturally performs AHaH learning.

Memristor physics therefore makes local, nonvolatile and potentially analog weight updates possible, including pulse-rate, timing-dependent, short-term, and long-term plasticity; it does not remove the need to engineer update linearity, repeatability, retention, and learning circuitry. The decisive engineering issue is not whether plasticity occurs, but whether a given device’s physical update curve is sufficiently predictable for the network and learning algorithm assigned to it.[95][96]

A 2023 organic two-terminal lateral memristive device provides another device-level STP implementation. Applied potential redistributes mobile ions in an insulating layer, changing the electric field and the conductivity of its semiconductor channel; potentiating pulses increased, and depressing pulses decreased, conductance. The authors attribute its temporal response to competition between field-driven ion migration or displacement and spontaneous relaxation.[112]

The 2018 review describes AHaH as a proposed memristor architecture combining Anti-Hebbian and Hebbian learning for logic gates and machine learning, and states that the AHaH and Universal Memory Machines solutions discussed there had not been experimentally tested.[21]

PCM supplies a distinct material-dependent mechanism: after amorphization, its resistance rises spontaneously through structural relaxation, approximately following a power law, whereas crystalline-state resistance is almost constant.[113] Lim et al.

(2021) used this drift in spontaneous sparse learning (SSL), in which high-resistance weights remain reinforced unless gradient-based updates switch them low. Their 784-256-10, 203,264-weight MNIST multilayer perceptron increased testing accuracy from 89.6% with conventional binary weights to 93.2% with SSL; the system used a central backpropagation unit with full-precision weights, binarized the weights before writing PCM cells, and derived each modeled cell’s set, reset, and drift behavior from measurements of more than 100 cells.[113] The study reports that memristor conductance changes are nonlinear, asymmetric, precision-limited, and stochastic through device-to-device and pulse-to-pulse variation.[113]

On-chip learning can also use bidirectional analogue switching directly. The STELLAR scheme described for a 2T2R differential weight unit has Forward, SET, and RESET stages and updates only one memristor in each pair during a cycle.[114] In this configuration, the two cells share a source line and subtract currents in the current domain, unlike conventional 1T1R differential pairs using separate source lines and digital subtraction; the reported purpose is lower source-line current and reduced IR drop.[114]

The PCM spontaneous-sparse-learning study extracted resistance distributions and deviations from more than 100 cells. For each cell, it measured set and reset resistance at more than 100 data points and measured resistance drift at 1,000 data points, using one read operation per second.[113]

For analogue RRAM, **linearity** means the relationship between conductance and the number of programming pulses, while **symmetry** means how uniformly conductance changes under potentiating and depressing pulses. Nonlinear or asymmetric updates require iterative write-and-verify cycles and thereby increase computational overhead.[115]

Although voltage- or current-controlled conductance modulation is theoretically continuous, practical analogue-RRAM synapses discretize conductance into distinguishable states because of inherent device variability and other nonideal characteristics.[115]

Ag nanoclusters doped into TiO₂ can form quantum-dot arrays whose tunnelling-gap width produces progressive conductance modulation. The reported device consumed 26 pJ per switch and transitioned from short-term memory to long-term memory as pulse amplitude, width, and interval were controlled.[115]

The Tiki-Taka device-aware training method uses a coupled dynamical system to minimize both the original objective function and the unintended cost caused by device asymmetry. Its reported simulation training accuracy was comparable with that of ideal symmetric devices.[82]

### What synaptic-plasticity mechanisms do CMOS-based alternatives provide?

CMOS does not lack synaptic plasticity. It implements plasticity algorithmically, with circuit state and programmable logic rather than relying on ionic or phase-change dynamics. A CMOS system can store weights digitally in SRAM or DRAM, as analog charge on capacitors, or nonvolatily through floating-gate threshold shifts. Digital systems encode membrane states and weights as binary bits; analog systems commonly use subthreshold transistor currents and capacitors to emulate continuous dynamics.[29][95]

A CMOS DPI synapse can generate temporal filtering and nonlinear short-term effects. Additional circuits that adjust its bias voltage or current can implement STP, STDP, and homeostatic adaptation. **Homeostatic adaptation** means a rule that stabilizes activity—for example, preventing all synapses from becoming permanently strong or weak. Long-term weight storage, however, requires extra circuit resources such as floating-gate cells or local analog-to-digital converters plus SRAM.[28]

CMOS STDP circuits generally generate decaying traces from pre- and postsynaptic spikes, compare their timing, and modify a stored state. In one CMOS memristor-emulator circuit, the pre- and post-spikes are transformed into exponential voltage traces; a pre-spike before a post-spike increases the synaptic state and conductance, while the reverse sequence decreases it.[116] A 2022 standard-CMOS SNN study likewise defines STDP as strengthening a connection when the presynaptic neuron fires earlier and weakening it when it fires later; its circuit used pre/post exponential traces, an operational transconductance amplifier, a capacitor state, and a transistor whose conductance represents the weight.[117]

CMOS can go beyond pairwise STDP. The same 2022 study tuned its STDP circuit to implement **Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro (BCM) learning**, a rate-based rule in which lower postsynaptic firing rates cause depression and higher rates cause potentiation.[117] Earlier CMOS work also demonstrated floating-gate synapses with STDP, a floating-gate MOS learning array with locally computed updates, and NOR Flash arrays used for STDP-based unsupervised learning.[95]

Intel’s Loihi provides an especially clear counterexample to the claim that complex learning rules require memristor physics. Its digital learning engine supports pairwise STDP, triplet STDP, reinforcement learning with synaptic tags, and rules using both rate-averaged and spike-timing traces.[101] Configurable trace time constants allow short traces to capture precise timing and longer traces to capture spike rate; multiple traces support differential Hebbian learning and BCM-type triplet-STDP rules.[101] Loihi also gives each synapse two additional state variables, which can serve as reinforcement-learning tags, and supplies signed reward traces representing reward or punishment.[101]

The trade-off is physical implementation cost. Capacitor-based analog weights leak and require refresh or repeated charge operations.[29][99] Floating-gate storage provides nonvolatile analog weights, fine granularity through bidirectional threshold-voltage tuning, low subthreshold current, and mature CMOS fabrication.[95] It still needs high-voltage program/erase infrastructure and must manage program noise and random telegraph noise, which are threshold-voltage instabilities caused by capture and release of charge in oxide defects.[95]

SRAM provides reliable and fast digital weight storage, but it is volatile and transistor-intensive. A 6T SRAM cell contains six transistors; CMOS artificial-synapse systems using SRAM consequently face area constraints, and peripheral circuits such as ADCs also consume energy.[99][96] Yet SRAM compute-in-memory can be highly efficient: one 65 nm 4 kb 6T-SRAM system reported 55.8 TOPS/W for binary synaptic connections.[96] More broadly, SRAM is valued for mature integration, high endurance, and reliability relative to nonvolatile-memory approaches, though it does not match nonvolatile memory density.[118]

CMOS alternatives also have measurable process, voltage, and temperature constraints. A current-domain SRAM CIM design reported that PVT—process, voltage, and temperature—variation produces transfer-function variation; charge-domain operation is described as more PVT robust and more linear than current-domain accumulation.[119] A 7T-SRAM design reported a 2.08 mV maximum read-bitline standard deviation across 400 Monte Carlo runs at 27 °C, under 1% of full range, and associated a 1% variation with a 0.1% MNIST accuracy reduction in software inference.[119]

**CMOS is therefore not limited to fixed, binary, or nonplastic synapses: it can implement timing-, rate-, homeostasis-, and reward-dependent learning rules, with the principal trade-off being circuit resources and state-retention architecture rather than the absence of plasticity.** This rules out treating STDP or multivariable learning as unique proof of a memristor advantage.[28][101][117]

SRAM compute-in-memory is a further CMOS alternative for weight use rather than a device-physics plasticity mechanism. Analog SRAM-CIM accumulates cell currents, charges or time-domain signals, whereas digital SRAM-CIM performs bitwise AND/XOR operations in memory and combines partial sums digitally. Hybrid designs can assign high-order bits to digital computation and low-order bits to analog accumulators.[120]

CMOS also supplies the circuitry that makes resistive-device updates usable at array scale. In a CMOS–RRAM accelerator, spike-coded activations mitigate RRAM-cell nonlinearity and can eliminate DACs and ADCs; its asynchronous multi-bit leaky-integrate-and-fire CMOS neurons encode more than one bit through multiple thresholds and can be adapted for on-chip learning.[105] The reported 65-nm 2T2R/op-amp design has a 5 fJ per spike per synapse energy figure of merit.[105]

A fabricated floating-gate CMOS example comprised 24 STDP synapses connected to a spiking neuron and was fabricated in 0.35-µm, four-metal, two-poly CMOS technology. In that circuit, tunnelling and injection activated only when integrated sampled presynaptic or postsynaptic activity exceeded a threshold; tunnelling was local rather than global or continuously enabled across all synapses.[121]

Loihi’s reported pairwise-STDP figures at 0.75 V were 120 pJ per synaptic update and 6.1 ns per synaptic update.[101]

RRAM provides long-term plasticity by nonvolatile storage of a parameter such as a weight or membrane potential. Pure-CMOS circuits require relatively large capacitors to realize long time constants, increasing neuromorphic-chip cost.[82]

NeuRRAM is the strongest directly measured resistive-memory MNIST result in this set. It reported 99.0% accuracy on a seven-layer CNN and placed all CNN parameters—including convolutional, fully connected, and batch-normalization parameters—on a single chip.[17] Its result is still a system result, not proof that bare RRAM devices alone performed every operation: partial sums and average pooling were implemented on a board-level FPGA.[17]

The 2021 CFB-CNN paper reported nominal accuracies near 98.2%, but its robustness numbers show why device variation must remain part of the comparison. Under 26% simulated resistance variation, reported accuracies ranged from 89.68% to 94.3% depending on weight representation and neuron mode. Under 64% simulated yield, reported values ranged from about 87.32% to 91.28%.[122] The nominal score and the variation-stressed score measure different things: one measures idealized model performance; the other measures modeled tolerance to defective or variable devices.

The 2023 ITO/SiN/TaN study gives a further example of why a device-informed MNIST number should not be treated as an end-to-end hardware benchmark. Its 97.22% result came from a Python/Google Colab simulation that translated measured conductance behavior into a neural-network model.[19] It demonstrates that incremental pulse behavior can support a good simulated weight-update curve; it does not measure a fabricated 28 × 28 crossbar executing all MNIST inferences.

The CMOS results also cannot be reduced to one headline ranking. The 2024 single-polysilicon chip’s 83% accuracy was measured on low-resolution MNIST and 8,000 test images, not the standard 10,000-image MNIST test configuration used in many other studies.[123] The SRAM results are higher, but the cited work explicitly identifies the accuracy evaluation as software inference using simulated variation.[119] Thus, comparing NeuRRAM’s 99.0% to the 83% low-resolution CMOS-chip result would confound network architecture, input resolution, precision, and measurement boundary.

A useful CMOS MNIST point is that compensation can materially affect results. The SRAM temperature-compensation design reports a 16.54 percentage-point improvement at 120 °C—from 80.81% without compensation to 96.14% with it—and a 16.54 percentage-point improvement at −40 °C—from 79.6% to 96.14%.Derived — These differences are calculated as 96.14 minus the reported uncompensated accuracies in E1346. The result demonstrates that CMOS analog CIM, like analog resistive memory, depends on device-aware calibration rather than delivering ideal arithmetic automatically.

**MNIST evidence from 2020–2024 shows that measured RRAM-CIM hardware can achieve 99.0% on a seven-layer CNN and that CMOS implementations can achieve high accuracy under their own conditions, but it does not isolate an intrinsic synaptic-plasticity winner because the reported networks, image formats, temperature ranges, and hardware boundaries differ.** The appropriate conclusion is that both technologies can support MNIST-class inference; the more discriminating question is how they compare under matched online-learning, retention, energy, and variability conditions.[17][123][119]

Yao et al. (2020) reported a fully hardware-implemented five-layer memristor CNN using eight 2,048-cell arrays, PCB and FPGA support, and more than 96% MNIST accuracy.[125][129] The authors report a 32-level conductance distribution across a full 2,048-cell array and attribute a small number of programming errors to device variation; hybrid training reduced training-set error after weight transfer from 4.82% to 2.89% for G1, 6.43% to 4.22% for G2, and 5.85% to 3.40% for G3.[125]

Yao et al. (2020) also reported a five-layer TiN/TaOₓ/HfOₓ/TiN 1T1M memristor CNN built from eight 2,048-cell arrays with PCB and FPGA support, achieving more than 96% MNIST accuracy. The authors tracked conductance-weight changes for 30 days after transfer and reported that conductance drift deteriorated recognition accuracy after hybrid training.[27][129][125] They reported system energy efficiency more than two orders of magnitude above state-of-the-art GPUs.[125][130]

A review reports that Yao et al.’s fully hardware-implemented CNN used eight HfO₂-based arrays with 2,048 cells each, achieved more than 96% MNIST accuracy, and consumed 29 mW.[71]

The 2020 PCM study is an important high-accuracy result, but its boundary must be stated precisely. It physically programmed every ResNet-32 weight onto PCM devices, using 723,444 devices because each signed weight used a differential pair.[18] The conductance values were then read individually using an on-chip ADC and passed to TensorFlow software for forward propagation and accuracy calculation.[18] It is therefore a measured hardware-weight-retention experiment combined with software inference, not a fully integrated chip running the entire network in the crossbar.

Its time-dependent behavior is also central. At 25 seconds after programming, accuracy was 93.75%; without drift compensation it fell to random-guessing performance, 10%, within approximately 1,000 seconds.[18] Global drift compensation retained accuracy above 92.6% for one day.

Periodic correction of batch-normalization parameters to correct activation distributions retained CIFAR-10 accuracy up to 93.5% on hardware, compared with simple global scaling of layer outputs, and required additional digital computations during calibration.[18] Thus, the high result is evidence for a calibrated PCM-plus-algorithm system, not for drift-free PCM physics.

NeuRRAM differs in both memory technology and system boundary. Its primary 2022 paper reports 83.67% measured multi-core ResNet-20 CIFAR-10 accuracy, compared with 87.03% for its 4-bit software model.[17] The same paper reports that training with noise injection was crucial: a cited CIFAR-10 accuracy rose from 25.34% without noise injection to 85.99% with noise injection.[17] Its best model under 10% test-time weight noise used 20% training-time noise injection.[17]

NeuRRAM’s abstract reports 85.7% CIFAR-10 accuracy, and its detailed 14.34% error result corresponds to 85.66% accuracy by subtraction; the same paper separately identifies 83.67% as the measured multi-core ResNet-20 result.[17] These are separate reported configurations. The reported metric is specific to ResNet-20, its quantization, noise-aware training, and board-level support for some operations.[17]

The 64-core PCM chip provides a closer full-chip inference example. It has 64 cores, each with a 256 × 256 PCM crossbar, and uses CMOS digital logic for activation functions and other ResNet processing.[131] Its ResNet-9 CIFAR-10 hardware result was 92.81%, only 0.86 percentage points below the 93.67% software baseline.Derived — The 0.86-point gap is 93.67 minus 92.81 from E1296.

It achieved this through hardware-aware software training that modeled 256 × 256 tiling, experimentally derived PCM programming noise, additive output noise, and 8-bit DAC/ADC precision.[131]

The PICO-RAM result is a relevant CMOS comparison because it uses the same broad ResNet-20 family as NeuRRAM. PICO-RAM reported 90.7% on a 4-bit quantized ResNet-20, only 0.3 percentage points below its software emulation without analog nonidealities.[100] NeuRRAM reported 85.7% on ResNet-20, but the studies do not use a matched training procedure, identical quantization scheme, same peripheral architecture, or common voltage and temperature protocol.[17][100] The results demonstrate that both RRAM-CIM and SRAM-CIM can execute CIFAR-10-scale quantized convolutional inference; they do not demonstrate a device-physics ranking.

The VGG-like 8T-SRAM result of 91.47% is similarly not directly comparable to ResNet results. It uses 4-bit input, 4-bit weight, and 8-bit output precision and reports a 2.21-point loss from a 93.68% floating-point reference.[119] Its macro and energy claims are tied to simulation-verified designs rather than a stated measured prototype CIFAR-10 evaluation.[119]

**CIFAR-10 evidence shows that high resistive-memory accuracy is achievable, but only with device-aware training, differential mapping, iterative programming, calibration, and CMOS support; CMOS SRAM-CIM also reaches around 90% under quantized conditions.** The benchmark record therefore supports co-design, not a claim that memristor physics alone gives superior learning or inference.[17][131][100][119]

For NeuRRAM’s reported CIFAR-10 experiment, training-time noise injection increased accuracy from 25.34% to 85.99%, a **60.65-percentage-point** gain. This quantifies the reported importance of noise-aware training to that RRAM system’s measured-noise tolerance.[17]

In the PCM ResNet-32 weight-transfer experiment, CIFAR-10 accuracy declined by **83.75 percentage points**—from 93.75% at 25 s after programming to 10% by approximately 1,000 s—without drift compensation.[18]

A 2023 PRIVE study adds measured-RRAM-programming evidence rather than full on-chip inference. It programmed quantized weights onto HfO₂-RRAM prototype chips monolithically integrated in a commercial CMOS process, retrieved measured conductances, and evaluated pretrained models using those values. At a 6 kΩ low-resistance-state target, VGG-7 CIFAR-10 accuracy was 92.53% for the baseline, 90.78% with conventional write-verify, and 91.91% with PRIVE; ResNet-18 CIFAR-10 values were 93.16%, 91.56%, and 92.61%, respectively.[133]

The 2024 HfOₓ near-memory system gives a power-supply-tolerance result rather than an online-plasticity result. Its memristors stored binary weights before inference; the authors reported that MNIST accuracy declined by 0.7 percentage points at 0.08 suns and that CIFAR-10 accuracy declined from 86.6% in software to 73.4% under the same illumination.[103] The fabricated circuit measured 2.9 TOPS/W at 10 MHz and 0.7 V; its clock-gated, optimized-read estimate was 22.5 TOPS/W, and a 28-nm scaled-design estimate was 397 TOPS/W.[103]

A published comparison table identifies the 4-phase-read PCM configuration as **92.81%** CIFAR-10 accuracy, ISSCC ’22 PCM as **91.89%**, Nature ’22 RRAM as **85.66%**, Nature Electronics ’21 RRAM as **92.01%**, and JSSC ’22 SRAM as **91.51%**; it reports no CIFAR-10 value for its 1-phase-read configuration or the listed ISSCC ’22 NOR-Flash entry.[131]

Yao and co-workers’ 2020 study, *Fully hardware-implemented memristor convolutional neural network*, reported 91.3% CIFAR-10 classification accuracy. This is a substantial experimental RRAM-CNN result, but programmed conductances used for inference do not by themselves demonstrate online synaptic learning.

### Does the evidence support a current consensus that memristor physics

The answer is no: there is no current consensus that memristor physics is generally superior to CMOS-based alternatives for synaptic plasticity. There is consensus on a narrower proposition: resistive-memory devices can provide compact local analog or multilevel state, nonvolatile weight storage, dense crossbar organization, and device-level plasticity behaviors that may reduce the circuitry required per synapse.[28][29][96] That is a technological opportunity, not a demonstrated universal advantage in trained-network performance.

The strongest argument for memristive synapses is physical consolidation. A single device can potentially represent weight storage and conductance-based multiplication, and some device classes also display STP, long-term potentiation, STDP, and rate-dependent effects.[29][95] A review contrasts approximate standard-CMOS synaptic events at the nanojoule level with reported memristive artificial synapses at several picojoules or several hundred femtojoules.[134] However, those are broad device-level ranges, not matched measurements of complete networks including ADCs, drivers, selectors, calibration, and data conversion.

System-level overhead is decisive. A 2024 review notes that ADCs can consume up to 70–90% of crossbar-computation-unit area and 80–88% of energy in some architectures.[96] It also reports that advanced hybrid RRAM/CMOS ANN architectures can achieve throughput similar to fully CMOS versions while sometimes remaining limited by ADC area.[96] Consequently, a small switching-energy figure for an individual memristor does not directly establish a lower energy per trained example, per synaptic update, or per classified image.

Variability remains the principal resistive-memory limitation for learning. RRAM and PCM updates can be nonlinear, asymmetric, and stochastic.[95] NeuRRAM measured conductance relaxation and used repeated program–verify iterations; after three iterations, the conductance standard deviation fell to about 2 μS, a 29% reduction from its initial value, and network inference began at least 30 minutes after programming.[17] PCM exhibits power-law drift, with device-to-device variation in drift exponent increasing matrix-vector-multiplication error over time.[131] These are solvable engineering problems in specific systems, but they mean the physical state is not automatically a precise analog weight.

CMOS alternatives have their own nonidealities, including capacitor leakage, SRAM area, floating-gate program noise, random telegraph noise, and PVT sensitivity.[29][95][99][119] Their countervailing advantages are mature fabrication, deterministic digital logic, high endurance, well-developed design tools, and predictable scaling.

SRAM is explicitly described as robust, reliable, and highly integrated in CMOS, although less dense than nonvolatile-memory alternatives.[119][118] Floating-gate synapses offer analog and bidirectional threshold tuning with mature CMOS fabrication.[95]

The benchmark evidence reinforces the conditional conclusion. NeuRRAM measured 99.0% MNIST and 85.7% CIFAR-10 with RRAM-CIM, but its models used quantization, noise-aware training, and FPGA support for selected operations.[17] The PCM ResNet-9 chip measured 92.81% CIFAR-10 after hardware-aware software training and two-device programming.[131] The PCM ResNet-32 experiment reached 93.75% shortly after programming and above 93.5% after one day with adaptive calibration, but the network forward propagation remained in software.[18] These are strong demonstrations of resistive-memory-assisted inference, not matched demonstrations of superior online plasticity.

CMOS results are also conditional rather than definitive. The 2024 PICO-RAM prototype reported 90.7% CIFAR-10 on 4-bit ResNet-20 and tested supply and temperature sensitivity.[100] The 2024 analog CMOS chip measured 83% on low-resolution MNIST versus 85% software.[123] SRAM simulation studies reported 96.14% compensated MNIST accuracy over −40 °C to 120 °C and 97.5% for a hybrid-domain design.[119] None is a same-network, same-precision, same-training, same-array-size, same-operating-condition comparison with a memristive alternative.

Some sources make stronger claims. A hybrid bi-memristor synapse paper concludes that its design surpasses available CMOS and memristive synaptic devices in area and plasticity strength.[135] That paper’s array evidence includes Cadence simulation of a 3 × 3 crossbar with CMOS neurons and CMOS–memristor synapses, rather than a benchmarked large hardware comparison.[135] A BFO-memristor review says its devices have the potential to provide superior outcomes to conventional CMOS electronics.[134] Those claims identify promising directions, but they do not overturn the system-level conclusion because their comparison basis differs from a replicated end-to-end benchmark.

Judgment — The deciding criterion should be a like-for-like end-to-end experiment: identical dataset split, model architecture, quantization, learning rule, weight precision, update count, temperature and voltage range, and measured reporting of accuracy, energy per update and inference, area, latency, endurance, retention, yield, and variation. A technology is superior only if it wins across the criteria relevant to the intended application, not if it has a lower isolated switching energy or a higher headline accuracy on a different network.

The current practical consensus is consequently conditional. Memristive devices are most compelling where nonvolatile dense weights, analog VMM, and local update physics outweigh calibration and variability costs. CMOS is most compelling where repeatability, programmable learning rules, integration maturity, and high endurance dominate. Hybrid systems often offer the strongest near-term path because they assign each technology the task it performs best: resistive memory for weight storage and VMM, CMOS for control, timing, conversion, calibration, and neuron or learning logic.[28][17][96]

**The evidence through 2024 supports memristor physics as a potentially more compact physical substrate for some synaptic functions, but it supports CMOS as a fully capable plastic-synapse platform and does not establish general superiority for either technology across MNIST, CIFAR-10, or online learning.** The remaining contest is not memristor versus CMOS in isolation; it is between complete, application-specific hardware systems and their co-designed algorithms.[28][101][95][96]

A cross-layer review frames robustness as a device, array, circuit, data-conversion and system problem, and identifies algorithm–hardware co-design as an effective mitigation strategy. Its nonideality-aware training model includes read nonlinearity, mismatch, variation and noise.[89] This supports the system-level judgment: neither a resistive-memory state variable nor a CMOS memory cell alone determines plasticity or network robustness.[89]

Recent demonstrations sharpen the conditional verdict. The 2024 passive self-rectifying crossbar reported 100% yield across all 1,024 cells, high selectivity, low device-to-device variation, robust nonvolatility, gradual SET and RESET conductance programming, and 100% hardware classification across 1,500 three-class MNIST inferences.[79] Its authors attribute the narrow distribution to non-filamentary switching and exclusion of the initial electroforming process.[79] This is a concrete route to more uniform analogue weight programming, while its software-trained three-class task remains a different workload from ten-class MNIST and CIFAR-10 CNN benchmarks.[79]

Nonvolatility is a genuine system-level differentiator in the HfOₓ binary system: its authors state that SRAM loses stored information on power loss whereas memristors retain it, and they observed no read disturb in their experiments.[103] They also state that analogue memristor in-memory computing faces high device variability, analogue-CMOS imperfections, voltage drops, and supply-voltage-specific peripheral circuits.[103] Thus, the evidence supports nonvolatile local state and array VMM as the distinctive opportunity, while predictable plasticity and efficient operation remain properties of the complete CMOS–memory implementation.[103][114]

The 2024 hardware review identifies up to approximately 100 and approximately 1,000 nonvolatile states, approximately 10 fJ per state transition with zero static consumption when idle, crossbar and 3D-stacking integration, and 85-ps switching as memristive-device attributes relevant to ANN hardware.[96]

### Synthesis

Memristive synapses store weights as local conductance and can express potentiation, depression, STP, STDP, and rate-dependent effects through ionic, filamentary, thermal, phase, or polarization dynamics. CMOS circuits can implement the same named learning rules through programmable digital engines or analog spike-trace circuits, while using SRAM, floating-gate, Flash, or capacitor state for weights.[28][101][95]

The 2020–2024 benchmarks demonstrate substantial resistive-memory inference: NeuRRAM reported 99.0% MNIST and 83.67% measured multi-core CIFAR-10 accuracy, while a PCM chip reported 92.81% CIFAR-10. They also demonstrate competitive CMOS-CIM inference, including 90.7% CIFAR-10 in a 65 nm SRAM prototype.[17][131][100] The performance gap cannot be assigned to synapse physics because network architecture, precision, training, calibration, and hardware/software boundary differ. Variability, drift, and nonlinear updates remain central memristive costs; CMOS pays in area, volatility, or peripheral circuitry.[95][17][131][99]

**Decision 1 — commercial ideal-element status.** Commercial resistive-switching devices do not have established status as Chua’s ideal fourth circuit element. This decision would change with measurements on a commercial device that demonstrate the defining charge–flux constitutive relation and the associated ideal-memristor behavior over its stated operating range, rather than resistive switching alone.[1]
2.

Compare MNIST and CIFAR-10 scores only when model, precision, test set, and hardware boundary match.

3. Prefer hybrid CMOS–memristor systems where analog VMM and nonvolatile density matter, but require measured retention, variation, and calibration overhead.
4. Do not treat STDP, rate learning, or multi-factor plasticity as evidence unique to memristors; CMOS implements all of these mechanisms.

### Open questions

Whether any physical commercial device can satisfy a single-valued charge–flux relation with state determined solely by net charge remains open. It would be settled by reproducible device-level tests that exclude hidden state variables and show return to the same resistance whenever net transported charge returns to the same value, across relevant operating conditions.
