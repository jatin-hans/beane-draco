This framework is not feasible as a routine $200–$300-per-week plan while maintaining the recommended $14 maximum loss per trade on a $700 account. The target requires $200–$300, or 28.6%–42.9% of the account in one week before trading costs, and equals 14.29–21.43 times the $14 risk unit.[1][2][3] The recommended route is to use only broker-approved, defined-risk positions whose complete loss including estimated costs is no more than $14, which generally excludes credit spreads and most standard debit spreads and makes no trade the correct outcome when no qualifying contract exists.[4][5] Single-leg long calls or puts and narrowly priced debit spreads are permitted only when their full paid premium or debit fits that cap, while naked short options are excluded because their losses can be unlimited or substantial.[4][3] The $200–$300 aspiration can be used as a stress-test benchmark for payoff, win rate, trade frequency, and costs, but not as an expected return or income target.

## Position-sizing rules and maximum risk per trade

This framework is **educational only, not personalized financial advice, a recommendation, or a solicitation to trade**. It explains how a very small options account can set loss limits and test a return aspiration mathematically. It does not imply that any individual should trade, that a broker will approve any strategy, or that the hypothetical outcomes are representative of results to expect. FINRA describes options as complex instruments whose risks vary by how they are traded, and says leverage can magnify gains and losses because one contract equates to 100 shares of the underlying asset. Charles Schwab’s August 26, 2025 article describes its material as general educational information, not individualized investment advice, and says its examples are illustrative rather than reflective of results an investor should expect.[6][4][2][7][8]

The central constraint is stark. A gain of $200–$300 in one week on a $700 account is a gross return of 28.6%–42.9% in that week, before commissions, bid-ask execution costs, regulatory fees, taxes, or losses on other positions. That is a speculative target to examine, not a routine income expectation. Options examples are expressly illustrative and not reflective of results an investor should expect.[1][2][3]

Broker approval is also a real constraint. A brokerage firm approves an account for specified kinds of options transactions, and not every customer will be approved for multi-leg spreads or short-option strategies. Firms must consider the customer’s investment objectives, financial position, experience, transaction size and frequency, concentration, and trading results.[4][9]

### Position-sizing rules

An option is a contract giving its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying interest at a specified exercise price, also called the strike price, before expiration. A call gives the right to buy; a put gives the right to sell. The option premium is the price paid by the buyer and received by the writer.[6][10]

For most equity options, one standard contract represents 100 shares. Thus, an option quoted at $0.35 costs $35 per contract before fees; an option quoted at $1.00 costs $100; and one quoted at $2.50 costs $250. Contract multipliers can differ for some products or adjusted contracts, so the actual contract specification must always be checked before placing an order.[6][10][11][12]

Judgment — **Adopt a $35 all-in maximum loss per trade for this educational $700-account framework.** This equals 5% of current account equity: $700 × 0.05 = $35. Recalculate the dollar limit after material changes in account equity. The 5% figure is a deliberately loose small-account ceiling, not a routine risk target.[13][14][5] It is a conservative design choice within published small-account guidance.

Under an alternative, more aggressive 10%-per-trade illustration for a $700 account, the routine risk budget is $35–$50 per trade and the hard all-in ceiling is $70 per trade, including estimated commissions, exchange fees, and likely closing costs.[167][168][169]

One source calls 1%–2% of account equity a standard per-trade range and says beginners should lean toward 1% or less; for $700, those levels are $7 and $14. A separate drawdown-based rule sets 1.5% risk per trade when the maximum acceptable drawdown is 15% and the expected worst losing streak is 10 trades; applied to $700, that is $10.50. Guidance that beginners keep maximum drawdown below 20% likewise identifies 1%–2% per-trade risk as typical.[5][15][16]

A $14 cap is not an assertion that $14 is universally suitable. Position-size guidance differs because it is tied to risk tolerance, strategy, volatility, account size, and contract granularity:

| Published guideline | Stated basis | Dollar implication for a $700 account | Framework treatment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Beginners should lean toward 1% or less; common range is 1%–2% per trade. [5] | Account equity | Derived — $7 at 1%; $14 at 2% | $14 is the hard maximum; $7 is the preferred risk where a viable contract exists. |
| General rule of thumb: risk no more than 1%–3% on one trade; options may warrant even 0.5%. [17] | Capital | Derived — $7–$21; 0.5% is $3.50 | Supports conservatism but illustrates why standard contracts can be unaffordable. |
| Maximum loss of 5% of trading capital as an upper limit. [14] | Trading capital | Derived — $35 | Treat as an outer ceiling for comparative stress testing, not normal risk. |
| $500–$1,000 accounts may risk up to 5% per trade and hold one to two positions. [13] | Account tier | Derived — $35 | Selected operating cap; costs must fit inside this $35 limit. |
| Small accounts may use roughly 2%–3% because option contracts are indivisible. [5] | Account equity | Derived — $14–$21 | Supports a $14 cap when a full contract can fit. |

The disagreement has a practical explanation. Options trade in whole contracts, not fractional contracts. A $700 account may have a $7–$14 risk budget, while even a low-priced $0.50 option costs $50 per contract. The correct calculation is not to round up. If the formula gives fewer than one contract, the position size is zero and the trade is skipped or redesigned.[5]

The sizing rule is:

1. **First, calculate current account equity.** For the opening illustration, equity is $700. Recalculate the cap after material gains or losses rather than treating $700 as permanently fixed.

2. **Second, set maximum dollar risk.** Use 5% of current equity. At $700, the all-in maximum loss, including estimated costs, is $35. [13][14][10]

3. **Third, calculate maximum loss per contract.** Use the complete defined loss of the chosen strategy, including estimated entry and exit costs.

4. **Fourth, calculate quantity.** The permitted quantity is the lesser whole-number quantity allowed by (a) $35 divided by all-in maximum loss per contract, (b) the remaining $70 aggregate-risk capacity divided by all-in maximum loss per contract, and (c) broker buying power. Round down; no fractional equity-option contracts exist. [5][4][10]

5. **Fifth, trade only if one contract fits all three limits and the broker permits the strategy.** Otherwise, the permitted size is zero and the order is skipped or redesigned with a cheaper defined-risk structure. [4][5]

The formula in ordinary notation is:

| Item | Calculation | $700-account framework result |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum trade risk | Current account equity × 2% | $700 × 5% = **$35**. [13][14] |
| Contracts | Maximum trade risk ÷ maximum loss per contract | Round down to zero or a whole number. For long calls or puts, maximum loss is limited to the upfront investment; for defined-risk vertical spreads, use the spread’s defined maximum loss per contract. [18][19] |
| Long call or put maximum loss | Premium per share × 100 + costs | Full paid premium is at risk |
| Debit-spread maximum loss | Net debit per share × 100 + costs | Full net debit is at risk |
| Defined-risk credit-spread maximum loss | Strike width minus net credit, then × 100 + costs | Credit received is not the amount at risk |

The long-option rule is direct. A purchased call or put can lose the full premium paid. Its all-in maximum loss is premium per share × 100 plus estimated entry, exit, and execution costs, and it fits only when that total is $35 or less.[5][10]

For a debit spread, the net debit is the maximum loss. A bull call spread, for example, buys a lower-strike call and sells a higher-strike call with the same expiration. The debit paid for that paired position is the maximum loss, plus transaction costs.[3][20][21]

For a defined-risk credit spread, maximum loss is not the credit received. It is the difference between the strikes, minus the credit received, multiplied by 100, plus costs. If a $1-wide credit spread brings in $0.30, its maximum loss is $70 per contract before costs; it does not fit a $14 loss cap.[5][22][3]

**The $35 cap still makes “no trade” the required answer whenever one contract fails the all-in-loss, aggregate-risk, or broker-buying-power test.** Whole-contract sizing must be rounded down rather than upward.[5][4][10]

A planned stop-loss order does not reduce the contractual maximum loss used for sizing. A stop is an exit intention, not a guarantee of an exit price. Options can gap overnight; implied volatility can decline; a stop may not fill at the intended price; and a purchased option can lose its entire premium through time decay, even if the underlying later moves in the anticipated direction.[5]

Judgment — **The framework should size every purchased option and debit spread as though its full premium or debit can be lost.** A favorable earlier exit is helpful, but it is not allowed to justify a larger opening position.

Transaction costs matter disproportionately at this account size. Options costs can include commissions on opening and closing, exchange and regulatory fees, and, for some positions, margin and interest costs. Multi-leg spreads incur costs on each component. One broker’s stated charge is $0.65 per contract, with a $1 minimum per options order; another cited broker charges $0.04 in regulatory and clearing fees for each option contract traded.[10][25][26]

The economic cost is broader than posted commissions. Buying at the ask and later selling at the bid means crossing the bid-ask spread twice. In one liquidity illustration, a $2.00 bid and $2.05 ask created a $0.05 spread, a $2.025 midpoint, and a 2.5% spread relative to midpoint; buying at the ask and later selling at the bid cost $5 per contract. In a thinner-market illustration, a $0.60 bid and $0.80 ask created a $0.20 spread and $0.70 midpoint; paying the $0.80 ask is $0.10 above midpoint, and later selling at the bid gives up another $0.10, so the position needs to gain about $20 per contract just to cover the spread. On a $14 risk budget, even a few dollars of execution cost is material.[27]

Judgment — **Include a contemporaneous estimate of commissions, fees, and bid-ask execution cost in the $35 maximum-loss calculation before entry.** The cost allowance is part of the cap, not an amount added after position size is selected. Multi-leg spreads have costs on multiple option purchases and sales.[10][28][21]

Account-level exposure needs a separate rule. A per-trade limit does not prevent several positions from losing at once, especially when they all depend on the same market direction. One source suggests total open risk of roughly 5%–8% of the account, or $35–$56 on $700; another permits up to 15%, or $105, while warning that correlated positions can be hit together.[24][13]

A separate general trading guideline places maximum daily loss at 3%–5% of account equity and total open risk, sometimes called portfolio heat, at 6%–10%.[168]

Judgment — **Cap total simultaneously open all-in defined risk at $70, or 10% of $700, and do not normally open more than two positions.** Treat positions with the same bullish, bearish, or sector exposure as one shared risk theme. Correlated positions can lose together, so separate contracts do not create independent exposure.[13][10]

The $56 account-level cap also shows why a $35-per-trade rule is materially looser. Two positions each risking $35 would create $70 of open risk, exceeding the selected aggregate limit. After one full-$35 position, only $21 remains under a $56 total-risk cap.[24]

A separate cash-commitment limit matters for cash accounts. Long options and debit spreads require payment of their debit. Credit spreads may require collateral or buying-power reduction equal to their defined maximum loss, subject to broker rules. Brokers can have differing approval and margin requirements, and a broker can require liquid funds or liquidate positions if requirements are not met.[10][29][3]

Judgment — **Do not commit more than $350 of the $700 account to paid debits or spread collateral, even if the risk calculation theoretically allows it.** This is a cash-management reserve, not a claim that $350 is the maximum possible loss. It preserves cash for settlement, closing transactions, fees, and unexpected operational needs. The $56 open-risk limit remains the stricter economic risk rule.

Some strategy types are incompatible with any fixed $14 maximum-loss rule. An uncovered or naked call has theoretically unlimited loss if the underlying rises. An uncovered put may require buying 100 shares at the strike and can incur substantial losses if the stock falls. Uncovered writers may also face additional margin calls after adverse price moves.[4][30][10]

If the underlying falls to zero, an uncovered short put can lose an amount approaching its strike price multiplied by 100 shares per standard contract, less the premium received.[4]

A spread is not automatically safe merely because it has a long leg. A spread must be properly paired: same underlying, appropriate strikes, and the same expiration where the defined-risk payoff calculation assumes it. A long option with an earlier expiration than a short option can leave significant uncovered risk.[6][10]

The sizing rule permits only positions whose full contractual loss plus estimated costs is at or below $35; it rules out naked writing and every long option or vertical spread that fails that all-in test.[5][3][10]

Using the cited $0.05 and $0.20 round-trip bid-ask examples, execution friction alone equals 35.7% and 142.9%, respectively, of this framework’s $14 maximum loss. That arithmetic shows why a $20 round-trip spread cost cannot fit a one-contract trade designed around a $14 loss budget.[27][5]

At the selected $14 maximum loss, the cited $5 and $20 round-trip bid-ask-cost illustrations consume 35.7% and 142.9%, respectively, of the loss budget. This quantifies why a $20 execution-cost burden cannot coexist with a one-contract trade constrained to $14 of total risk.[27][5]

A 1.5% drawdown-based alternative would limit a $700 account to $10.50 per trade: $700 × 0.015 = $10.50. The selected $14 limit is the upper end of the separate 1%–2% beginner range, rather than a claim that the drawdown-based rule selects $14.[16][5]

Its maximum loss is the net debit plus commissions and fees, and its maximum profit is strike width less entry cost. The short call reduces entry cost but caps gain at the higher strike.[31][32]

### maximum risk per trade

Maximum risk is the worst loss that the trade structure can produce under its contractual payoff rules, before any special operational complications. For an option buyer, the premium paid is the maximum loss. For a debit spread, the debit is the maximum loss. For a defined-risk credit spread, the maximum loss is strike width less credit.[4][5][3]

The phrase maximum loss needs careful use. A debit spread can have a defined expiration payoff, yet holding it through expiration can create assignment or exercise issues. If one leg is exercised or assigned while the other expires worthless, the account can be left with long or short stock. That stock position can move before it is closed, making operational loss larger than the simple spread diagram implies.[3][41][42][43]

A bull put spread illustrates this distinction. Its standard options payoff is capped by the strike width minus the credit. Fidelity states that maximum risk equals the difference between strikes minus net credit, including commissions: in its JKL example, a 5.00 strike difference less 1.90 net credit equals 3.10 per share, less commissions, and that maximum risk occurs if the stock is at or below the long-put strike at expiration. Yet the short put can be assigned early, particularly around an ex-dividend date when an in-the-money put has time value below the dividend. If held through expiration near the short strike, assignment remains uncertain until the following Monday.[44][45]

A similar expiration issue applies to call spreads. Holding a bull call spread through expiration can leave a long-stock position when the stock closes between the strikes, while holding a bear put spread can create short stock between the strikes. The strategy payoff diagram is useful, but it is not a substitute for closing-risk management.[20][46][47][21]

Judgment — **For a $700 account, close all multi-leg stock-option spreads and single-leg equity options before expiration rather than allowing them to become exercise or assignment problems.** Standard equity options that finish in the money are generally exercised automatically; one $100-strike call can create a purchase of 100 shares costing $10,000. In a hypothetical expiration example, a call bought for $150 and closing $0.03 in the money had a −$147 option result at the official close, but exercise followed by an after-hours mark of $94 produced a combined −$750 economic result before fees.[48][49]

The full-risk rule also applies to single-leg long calls and puts. A long option buyer needs the underlying to move in the expected direction by enough, and soon enough, to overcome the premium paid. Being right about direction eventually is not enough if the move arrives after expiration or if time decay and volatility changes reduce the option’s value first.[12][10][50]

Time decay, measured by Theta, is the erosion of option value as expiration approaches. It accelerates in the final 30 days. In an example of a 30-day at-the-money call priced at $3.00 with Theta of negative $0.05 per day, the premium falls by about $0.05 per day and reaches $1.50 after 30 days if stock price and volatility do not change.[51][52]

This is why a planned stop is not a substitute for maximum-loss sizing. The option may fall because of elapsed time or lower implied volatility, not only because the stock crosses a chart level. A loss-control plan can still be useful, but it should be viewed as an effort to lose less than the defined maximum, not as permission to risk more than the account can bear.[5][24]

The standard formulas are worth stating plainly because they are the denominator for position size:

| Strategy type | Maximum loss per one standard contract, before costs | Maximum profit at expiration, before costs | Source basis |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Long call or long put | Premium paid × 100 | Call: potentially unlimited; put: limited by a fall toward zero | [4][53][5] |
| Bull call or bear put debit spread | Net debit × 100 | Strike width minus debit, then × 100 | [3][20][46] |
| Bull put or bear call credit spread | Strike width minus net credit, then × 100 | Net credit × 100 | [3][44][45] |
| Naked short call | Theoretically unlimited | Premium received | [4][53][10] |
| Naked short put | Substantial downside risk; assignment can require 100 shares at strike | Premium received | [4][10][54] |

The terms premium, debit, and credit describe cash flow at entry. A debit means the trader pays net money to open the position. A credit means the trader receives net money at entry. Cash received is not the same as risk; a credit spread may receive $30 and still risk $70.[22][5]

The choice between a $14 framework cap and a looser $35 ceiling needs an explicit conclusion. At $14, one maximum loss equals 2% of account equity. At $35, it equals 5%. A $35 loss is five times the dollar risk of a 1% rule and 2.5 times the selected $14 cap. It can make some whole-contract trades possible, but it makes drawdowns arrive faster.[5][13][55]

In a cited model of a $10,000 account with equal-sized wins and losses, increasing risk per trade from 2% to 10% sharply increased modeled ruin risk at both 55% and 52% win probabilities; those results are not a ruin estimate for this framework’s unequal-payoff trades.[172]

For illustration, six consecutive full $14 losses would reduce a $700 account by $84, or 12%. Six consecutive full $35 losses would reduce it by $210, or 30%. These are arithmetic outcomes, not probability forecasts. The likelihood of streaks depends on the true win rate and dependence among trades; even a strategy with a 60% win rate can have losing streaks.[5][56]

For independent trades with equal-sized wins and losses, a classic eventual-ruin expression is (q/p)^N when win probability p exceeds loss probability q and N is the number of account risk units; if p is no greater than q, continued trading indefinitely results in eventual ruin under that model.[172]

Judgment — **The 5%/$35 guideline is the operating limit for position sizing, accepted trades, and the illustrative weekly-target calculation.** The 1%–2%/$7–$14 range is a more conservative alternative that would reject the worked $35-risk trades.[5][13][14]

A $700 account must also distinguish buying power from loss capacity. A broker may allow a position only if account approval, settlement, cash, and margin rules are met. Conversely, a position that fits buying power may still be too large for the framework’s loss limit. The risk rule governs whether the trade belongs in the plan; broker buying power governs whether the order can be placed.[4][9][10][29]

A cash account adds settlement constraints. Options transactions typically settle on the next business day, and a trader who buys using unsettled proceeds and sells before funding settles can create a good-faith or free-riding violation. Thus, a small cash account cannot assume that the same $700 is available for repeated independent purchases throughout a day.[24][57]

**The maximum-risk rule decides the trade before the chart, strike, or premium is allowed to make it attractive: if all-in loss is above $35, remaining aggregate-risk capacity is insufficient, or broker buying power is unavailable, the framework rejects it.**[4][10][5]

### Testing the $200–$300 weekly target

A weekly target must be separated from **expectancy**, the average profit or loss implied by assumed win probability, average win, average loss, and trading costs across many trades. A positive expectancy does not mean a profit in any particular week, and a high win rate does not by itself make a strategy profitable.[66][67][68]

The core expectancy calculation is:

> Expected profit per trade = win probability × average win − loss probability × average loss − average cost per trade.

For a two-outcome model, let:

- **n** = number of completed trades in a week;
- **p** = probability that any one trade is a win;
- **W** = average gross dollar win per winning trade;
- **L** = average gross dollar loss per losing trade;
- **c** = average round-trip cost per trade, including commissions, fees, and execution slippage.

Then weekly expected P/L is:

> Weekly expectancy = n × [p × W − (1 − p) × L − c].

This model is a simplification. It assumes each trade has the same win probability and average payoff, and that trades are independent. Those assumptions fail when trades are correlated, when market regimes change, when a trader varies execution, or when different setups have different quality.[69][67][13]

The win rate needed merely to break even, excluding costs, depends on reward-to-risk. If average win equals average loss, break-even is 50%. If average win is twice average loss, break-even is 33.33%. If average win is half average loss, break-even is 66.67%. Swingfolio gives the formula as average loss divided by the sum of average win and average loss; its $1,100 average-loss and $900 average-win example has a 55% break-even win rate.[70][71]

Costs raise the required win rate. In one example, a strategy with a $200 average win and $100 average loss has a 33.33% break-even win rate without costs. Adding $10 per round trip changes the net average win to $190 and net average loss to $110, raising break-even to 36.67%.[66]

In a separate payoff illustration, reducing a $300 average winner to $275 and increasing a $100 average loss to $125 through costs raises the break-even win rate from 25% to 31.25%.[173]

This is especially important for a $700 account. A few dollars may seem minor, but if gross expected profit is only $5–$10 per trade, commissions and slippage can consume most or all of the apparent edge. One illustration shows a strategy with $60 average wins and losses and a 55% win rate producing $6 gross expectancy per trade, but only $2 after $4 costs and negative $1 after $7 costs.[67]

| Upper target | $300 | 21.43R | Derived — $300 ÷ $14 |

The selected $14 cap makes the return target more demanding, not less. Earning $200 in one week requires net gains equal to 14.29 full-risk units. Earning $300 requires 21.43R. A framework with a small fixed loss limit can preserve capital, but it cannot make an unusually large weekly dollar target routine. The separate 5%/$35 small-account guideline is a published comparison, not an operating limit or an input to this framework’s weekly calculations.[5][13][72][1]

To state the required win rate mathematically, rearrange the expectancy equation. If costs are included in net payoff amounts, the per-trade win probability needed to make weekly expected P/L exactly equal a target T is:

> Required win probability = [T ÷ n + L] ÷ [W + L].

If costs are stated separately rather than incorporated into W and L, the expression becomes:

> Required win probability = [T ÷ n + L + c] ÷ [W + L].

A result above 1.00, or 100%, means that target is mathematically impossible under the assumed trade count and payoff limits. This calculation gives the probability needed for the *average* week to equal the target; it is not the probability of actually achieving that amount next week.[66][67]

A probability distribution requires another step. Under the simplified model, the number of winners X in n independent trades follows a **binomial distribution**: X has n opportunities to win, each with constant win probability p, and each trade is classified as either a win or a loss. The statistics text illustrates a binomial experiment with 20 independent games, a 55% probability of winning each game, and a 45% probability of losing each game, with those probabilities remaining unchanged from game to game.[69]

If every winner earns W and every loser loses L, before costs, then weekly P/L when there are x wins is:

> Weekly P/L = x × W − (n − x) × L.

The minimum number of wins needed to reach target T is:

> Required wins = round up [T + n × L] ÷ [W + L].

The probability of reaching that threshold is the sum of the binomial probabilities for every qualifying win count. This is a conditional model only; it does not assert that real options outcomes are independent, binary, or equal-sized.[69][33][67]

Because vertical spreads can have partial gains or losses when the underlying finishes between their strikes, the probabilities of maximum profit and maximum loss need not sum to 100%.[174]

A transparent stress test shows why the target should not be underwritten as expected income. Use assumptions deliberately favorable to simplicity, not realism:

- 10 independent trades per week;
- every winning trade earns $35;
- every losing trade loses $35;
- no commissions, fees, or slippage;
- maximum loss per trade is the looser $35, or 5% of the account;
- per-trade win probability is 60%.

Under these assumptions, each week’s P/L equals:

> (2 × number of wins − 10) × $35.

These figures are not claims about an achievable options strategy. They demonstrate the win rate that the stated assumptions would require. The model is already aggressive: it assumes ten completed trades each week, identical $35 gains and losses, no costs, and independence. Real small-account options trading faces bid-ask spreads, position-specific premiums, different payoff shapes, changing volatility, and limited availability of contracts that fit the risk cap.[6][10][27][67]

The same test under the recommended $14 maximum loss shows the scale mismatch more clearly. If each trade can only win or lose $14 and there are ten trades, the best possible week is $140. Therefore, neither $200 nor $300 is mathematically reachable in that ten-trade symmetric-payoff model.[5]

More generally, a $14 maximum loss does not mean maximum profit is $14. A debit spread or long option can have a larger potential reward than risk. But higher theoretical reward usually comes with a lower probability of reaching it, and comparing reward-to-risk does not reveal likelihood. A $10-wide debit spread bought for $2 can theoretically make $800 against $200 at risk, but that return-on-risk figure does not show that the spread is likely to pay.[74][75][68]

This relationship between probability and payoff matters. A 70% win rate with average $50 wins and $100 losses produced only $50 total profit across 10 illustrative trades. A 40% win rate with average $300 wins and $100 losses produced $600 across 10 trades. Win rate alone is not the performance metric; the size of wins, losses, and costs jointly determines expectancy.[70]

An illustrative trade with a 75% win probability, a $100 average win, and a $400 average loss has an expected loss of $25 per trade.[175][173]

A high claimed win rate should therefore not be used to justify oversized positions. A strategy can win 90% of the time while risking $9 to make $1; one loss wipes out nine wins. Conversely, a lower-win-rate directional strategy can be profitable if average winners are sufficiently larger than average losers.[70][68][75]

The probability distribution also cannot be read from option delta alone. Delta measures how sensitive an option’s premium is expected to be to a $1 underlying move, and a 0.70 delta is often interpreted as roughly a 70% probability of expiring in the money. But expiring in the money is not the same as being profitable after premium, costs, and spread structure.[76][77][53]

For example, treating a short call’s 0.30 delta as approximately a 70% probability of profit for its seller is only a model-based shortcut, not a guarantee of a net realized win after fills, fees, and early exits.[175]

A robust process would measure actual results separately for each permitted setup: average winner, average loser, costs, holding period, drawdown, and the frequency of wins. A 60% observed win rate from six winners in 10 trades has a very wide 95% Wilson interval of 31.27%–83.18%; the same 60% rate from 60 winners in 100 trades has a narrower 50.20%–69.06% interval.[66]

Judgment — **Do not treat a small sample of trades, a chart signal, or an option delta as evidence that the framework has the 78.57%–92.86% win probability required by the illustrative $35 stress test.** A verifiable trade record, with setup definitions and net results after costs, would be needed to estimate a strategy-specific distribution.[78][66]

An analysis of approximately 130,000 trade ideas across 155 symbols with 1–30 days to expiration assumed cash settlement at expiration and excluded position management, assignment, early exercise, and transaction costs; it therefore does not establish a weekly $200 or $300 attainment probability for a $700 account.[176]

The requested $200–$300 target also conflicts with published small-account return benchmarks. One source describes a $500–$1,000 tier as having a 2%–5% realistic monthly return range, or $10–$50. That benchmark is itself only a source’s stated range, not a guarantee, but it is far below $200–$300 every week on a $700 account.[13]

Short-term trading does not solve the arithmetic. FINRA notes that short-term profit-seeking is generally less reliable than long-term investing and that prices may not move as expected. Weekly options can have high gamma, meaning a small underlying move can magnify both gains and losses; frequent trading also brings higher costs that can erode returns.[57][7]

**The evidence supports defined-risk education and strict sizing; it does not support presenting $200–$300 per week as a plausible routine return from a $700 account under the selected limits.**

### What the $700 account can and cannot support

The framework can support a disciplined learning process if it treats preservation of capital as the first objective. It can identify whether a contract fits a fixed loss budget, reject trades that do not fit, and record results in a way that eventually permits an evidence-based estimate of expectancy. Defined-risk long options and properly constructed vertical spreads provide calculable maximum-loss inputs for this process.[5][19]

It cannot simultaneously promise strict 1%–2% risk and frequent $200–$300 weekly income. At $14 maximum loss, most standard single contracts and standard vertical spreads exceed the loss budget. At $35 maximum loss, a few more structures may fit, but the account is risking 5% per position and still needs unusually large net weekly gains.[5][13]

The following decision sequence keeps the trade target from overriding account risk:

1. **First, determine whether the account is approved for the contemplated strategy.** The broker’s approval category and operational requirements are binding. [4][9]

2. **Second, calculate the full defined maximum loss plus estimated costs.** Do not use a hoped-for chart stop as the risk amount. [5]

3. **Third, apply the $14 cap and round contract quantity down.** If quantity is zero, reject the order. [5]

4. **Fourth, check aggregate open risk against the $56 cap and treat correlated trades as shared exposure.** [24][13]

5. **Fifth, ensure the position can be closed before expiration if it contains short options.** Assignment and exercise can create stock exposure that a $700 account may not be able to carry. [3][41][20]

6. **Sixth, record net—not gross—results.** The record should include contract count, premium, commissions, trade and settlement dates, realized P/L, and whether the position was closed before expiration. [9][10]

7. **Seventh, evaluate the weekly target only after a sufficiently large record exists.** Use actual average win, average loss, costs, and trade frequency to calculate expectancy and a conditional probability distribution. [22][66][67]

8. **Eighth, perform an observable implementation check before and after every position.** Compare contemporaneous quotes for each leg and for the net spread, confirm the broker’s approval and buying-power treatment, use a limit order when an absolute price limit is required, and keep a dated record of net fills, commissions, trade and settlement dates, and realized outcomes against the assumptions used in the weekly calculation. [4][36][9][10][66]

A framework built this way may often produce no eligible trade. That outcome is preferable to converting an account-size limitation into an oversized risk decision. Published small-account guidance explicitly says that when whole-contract sizing produces zero, the trader should skip the trade or find a cheaper structure rather than round upward.[5]

Five completed trades per week requires separate scrutiny when intended holds last 3–21 days and no more than two full-risk positions may be open. Before relying on any fitted win rate, paper-record qualifying spreads, executable entries and exits, all fees, holding times, simultaneous exposure, assignments, and net weekly outcomes. Assess win rate and drawdowns across market conditions, and do not infer a reliable rate from a small sample.[13][22][3][78][66]

## Permitted option strategies

An option is a contract: a call gives its holder the right to buy, and a put gives its holder the right to sell, the underlying security at the strike price by expiration. A buyer pays a premium; a writer, also called a seller, takes on an obligation if assigned. Standard U.S. equity-option quotes are per share, and one contract normally represents 100 shares, so a quoted $0.60 debit is $60 per contract.[6][2]

**This educational framework permits only positions whose worst-case option payoff, including estimated transaction costs, can be calculated before entry and is no more than the $35 maximum dollar-risk cap set in Position-sizing rules and maximum risk per trade.** Position size is the whole number of contracts obtained by dividing the dollar-risk cap by maximum loss per contract and rounding down.[3][4] Prefer affordable, same-expiration bull-call or bear-put debit spreads.

Conditionally allow bull-put and bear-call credit spreads only when width less credit, all-in costs, collateral, broker approval, and assignment management satisfy the limits. Conditionally allow each purchased call and purchased put only when its entire premium and costs fit the limit. Exclude uncovered short options. It is educational only, not personalized financial advice.[3][4][41]

### debit spreads

A **debit spread**, also called a vertical debit spread, is a same-underlying, same-expiration position in which the trader buys the more expensive option and sells a cheaper option farther out of the money. The debit is the net amount paid to open it. A **bull call spread** buys the lower-strike call and sells the higher-strike call; a **bear put spread** buys the higher-strike put and sells the lower-strike put. Both are 1:1 directional positions: bull calls are moderately bullish and bear puts moderately bearish.[19][42][47]

For a debit spread, maximum loss is the net debit paid times 100, excluding commissions and fees; maximum profit at expiration is the strike width minus that debit, times 100. Selling the second option reduces cost versus a standalone long option, but it also caps upside. Fidelity’s illustrative bull-call example—long 50 call at $2.90 and short 55 call at $1.20—has a $1.70 debit, $170 maximum loss, $330 maximum profit, and a $51.70 break-even point before commissions.[51][28][83]

Schwab illustrates a $3-wide debit spread opened for a $1.20-per-share debit: one standard contract has $120 of theoretical maximum loss and $180 of maximum expiration profit before transaction costs.[3]

A bear put debit spread’s expiration break-even stock price is its long-put strike minus the net debit per share.[170][62][19]

A purchased single-leg call or put may also be permitted, but only if its entire premium fits the framework cap. A **long call** is the bullish single-leg position; a **long put** is the bearish one. In either case, the maximum loss is the premium paid, and the option can expire worthless, losing 100% of that premium.[72][84][2][85]

Judgment — One contract is permitted when a $1-wide call or put debit spread has a net debit of no more than $0.35 per share. A bull call spread is permitted for a moderate rise toward a defined resistance target, and a bear put spread is permitted for a moderate decline toward a defined support target. The technical definitions and entry conditions belong in Stock selection criteria based on technicals and Strike and expiry selection rules.[2][5][19][47]

The debit-spread choice does not erase time risk. Time decay, called **theta**, erodes option value as expiration approaches; it generally hurts a bull call spread, though less than a plain long call. For a bear put spread, its effect depends on where the stock is relative to its strikes.[20][47] This makes debit spreads suitable only where the anticipated move has enough time to occur, rather than as an automatic income trade.

A vertical spread uses two options of the same type on the same underlying, with the same expiration and different strikes; one option is bought and the other sold. Its expiration payoff caps both maximum profit and maximum loss.[58][91]

A permitted vertical debit spread must use matching underlying, expiration, deliverable, and quantity for its two legs. A long call covers a short call only when its strike is the same or lower and its expiration is the same or later; a long put covers a short put only when its strike is the same or higher and its expiration is the same or later.[38][81]

Fidelity’s bear-put-spread analysis states that the position has net negative delta: it tends to rise as the stock falls and decline as it rises. Its delta changes little as stock price changes with time to expiration unchanged, which Fidelity calls “near-zero gamma.”[47]

Fidelity also calls the bear put spread’s volatility sensitivity “near-zero vega”: because it contains one long and one short put, its price changes very little when volatility changes while stock price and time to expiration are held constant. Volatility measures the percentage fluctuation in stock price, and option prices tend to rise as volatility rises when other factors are unchanged.[47]

For a bull call spread, early assignment of the short call is possible at any time and generally occurs when the stock goes ex-dividend. ### credit spreads

A **credit spread** sells the higher-premium option and buys a lower-premium protective option on the same underlying, normally with the same expiration. It produces a net credit at entry. A **bull put spread** sells the higher-strike put and buys the lower-strike put; it is bullish to neutral.[22][44]

For a correctly constructed vertical credit spread, maximum profit is the credit received, while maximum loss is spread width minus credit received, multiplied by 100. The collected credit is not the amount at risk. FINRA’s definition of a limited-risk spread requires matching underlying, exercise style, listing status, and aggregate underlying contract value; the short option must expire on or before the long option.[51][5][29]

Judgment — Permit one-contract bull put and bear call verticals only if all of the following are true: the protective long leg is present at entry; the contracts are equal quantity, same underlying, and same expiration; the calculated width-minus-credit loss, plus estimated fees, is below the framework cap; and the broker has specifically approved spread trading. Treat the trade as one multi-leg order rather than entering legs separately, because a spread order reduces legging risk.[87][4][96]

Credit spreads can suit a thesis that price will remain beyond the short strike, rather than needing to reach a target. Their payoff trades a wider potential profit zone for a less favorable reward-to-risk relationship: the maximum gain is the credit, while loss commonly exceeds it. They also benefit from time decay and may be selected when implied volatility is relatively elevated, but neither condition establishes a winning probability.[42][51]

A defined-risk structure does not override the $35 hard risk rule: a credit spread is rejected when its width-minus-credit loss plus all-in costs exceeds $35.[3][6]

The short put creates operational risk before expiration. U.S. stock options may be exercised on any business day, so the short put can be assigned early; an assigned put writer must purchase shares at its strike. At expiration, a vertical spread can also finish between strikes, leaving one leg exercised or assigned and potentially creating a stock position.[47][10][42] Thus, the permitted credit-spread rule requires closing or actively managing a spread before expiration rather than assuming its theoretical graph alone controls realized risk.

For basic two-legged spreads, the debit spread’s net debit must be paid in full. For a credit spread, cash or cash equivalents equal to maximum risk less the net credit received must be deposited and held in the account; this funding requirement applies in addition to the framework’s maximum-loss test.[98]

An assigned short equity put requires the seller to buy 100 shares at the strike price. American-style short options can be assigned at any time through expiration, so the protective long option defines the expiration payoff but does not remove the operational need to handle an assignment before expiration.[99][92][100]

A **bear call spread** is the bearish-to-neutral credit vertical: sell the lower-strike call and buy the higher-strike call on the same underlying with the same expiration. Fidelity’s example sells one XYZ 100 call at 3.30 and buys one XYZ 105 call at 1.50, producing a 1.80 net credit.[101][62]

E*TRADE states, “Margin approval is required for Levels 3 and 4.” Matt Danadel states that most brokers require a margin account before permitting spreads or option selling and gives $2,000 as FINRA’s minimum equity requirement for a margin account.[96][102]

Fidelity states that spread trading in a nonretirement account requires a margin agreement, Tier 2 approval, and at least $10,000 in equity; it also states a $10,000 retirement-account minimum while holding a spread.[171]

### single-leg calls/puts

Single-leg long calls and puts have the simplest defined loss: the premium. They need no short option, so they avoid short-leg assignment risk, but they require a sufficiently large move in the correct direction before time value decays. A long call’s breakeven at expiration is strike plus premium; a long put’s is strike minus premium.[28][84][85]

Judgment — Conditionally permit a purchased single-leg call for a bullish setup and a purchased single-leg put for a bearish setup only when the full contract premium, including estimated costs, is no more than $35 and the trade has a clear price target that makes the needed move plausible. Prefer it over a debit spread only when the technical thesis calls for a move beyond the spread’s capped-profit strike; otherwise, a debit spread can reduce the cash cost while capping profit.[4][72][2][3][6]

Price alone is not a reason to select a single leg: a purchased option can expire worthless, losing its premium.[103] One option normally controls 100 shares, and delta—the estimate of an option-price change for a stock-price change—can make even a low-premium option react like a much larger stock position.[5][2] The permitted rule is therefore premium-capped, one contract at a time, with the directional exposure reviewed before entry.

The two accepted hypothetical trades, their calendar entry, expiration and intended-exit dates, complete-spread bid and ask, intended limit fills, leg-by-leg liquidity results, estimated costs, planned exit prices, and separate expiration payoff tables belong after the position-sizing, technical, strike-and-expiry, and liquidity rules. A limit order sets the execution limit but may not fill; multi-leg positions are opened and closed as a single order; and costs apply to each component of a spread.[36][104][87][6][10]

FINRA requires a brokerage firm to approve or disapprove a customer for options trading before accepting an options order, and the approval may be limited to particular transaction types. FINRA lists purchases of puts and calls only, covered-call writing, uncovered put and call writing, and options spread transactions as examples of transaction types.[105]

A purchased call or put can also lose value when implied volatility declines, even if the underlying moves broadly in the anticipated direction.

Pure Power Picks characterizes directional long calls and puts as having wins “often 35% to 50%,” a 1:2 or 1:3 reward-to-risk payoff, premium-paid defined risk, and wins from a few large moves. It characterizes premium selling as having wins “often 65% to 80%,” risk often two to four times the reward, defined risk with spreads but large risk when naked, and losses that are rare but can be large.[106]

Pure Power Picks states that credit spreads, cash-secured puts, iron condors, and other premium-selling approaches can produce repeated small wins while a tail event—a gap on earnings or macro surprise—creates one catastrophic loss that overwhelms them.[106]

### Excluded strategies and operational boundaries

Uncovered, or **naked**, calls and puts are excluded. An uncovered call has theoretically unlimited loss; an uncovered put can impose substantial losses when the underlying declines and can require the writer to buy shares at the strike. Cash-secured puts are also excluded: securing one requires cash equal to the exercise price, while stock-loss risk remains.[4][10][6]

Covered calls, protective puts, collars, short straddles, short strangles, ratio spreads, iron condors, calendars, and diagonals are not part of this beginner $700 route. Covered calls and collars require 100 shares; multi-leg combinations add per-leg transaction costs and execution risk; ratio call spreads can include an uncovered call with unlimited risk.[2][6][107] A four-leg iron condor can be defined risk, but it combines two credit spreads. Multi-leg strategies tend to have wider bid-ask markets as legs are added, so the two-leg alternatives are the permitted route.[108][13][109]

Avoid cash-settled American-style index spreads in this framework. Assignment timing can allow the index to move between short-leg assignment and action on the long leg, leaving the long leg’s cash proceeds insufficient for the short-leg obligation.[6][10] The permitted universe is deliberately narrower: long options and properly matched vertical spreads on broker-approved products.

Long straddles and long strangles are also excluded. A long straddle buys a call and put at the same strike and expiration; a long strangle buys a call and put at different, typically out-of-the-money strikes with the same expiration. Both are two-legged premium-buying volatility strategies rather than the framework’s single-directional call, put, or vertical-spread positions.[97]

Calendars and diagonals remain excluded because their multiple expiration dates can make early assignment particularly disruptive.[110]

An **iron butterfly** uses four contracts: a long call, long put, short call, and short put; unlike an iron condor, both short contracts are typically at the money at the same strike, and the strategy is intended for low volatility.[111]

The recommended route is long calls or puts and one-contract, same-expiration vertical spreads: bull calls or bull puts for bullish views, and bear puts or bear calls for bearish views. Long options limit loss to premium; debit spreads limit loss to debit; protected credit spreads limit loss to width minus credit. Those payoff limits make them compatible with a fixed dollar-risk framework, unlike naked calls, naked puts, and collateral-heavy cash-secured puts.[84][51][4][10]

Debit spreads are the better starting structure because the maximum loss is paid upfront and no short-option credit is mistaken for risk control. Credit spreads remain permitted only when their hedge, approval, buying-power requirement, assignment plan, and full width-minus-credit loss all satisfy the framework.[5][29][4][10]

This narrow strategy list is a framework choice, not a statement that other defined-risk structures do not exist: long and short condors, iron condors, butterflies, calendars, straddles, and strangles are multi-leg strategies. The framework excludes them in favor of one-leg long options and 1:1 verticals, whose entry debit or width-minus-credit loss can be calculated directly before entry.[97][40]

Broker approval is a gate, not a recommendation: Cboe states that broker-dealers assess options accounts and assign tiers that determine the complexity of strategies available. FINRA requires firms to record the transaction types approved, including covered writing, uncovered writing, and spreads.[112][113]

A purchased call or put can be paid in full in a cash account, with the whole premium at risk; buying options in a cash account has no legal minimum. This access rule does not make a premium affordable under the framework’s dollar-risk cap.[102]

The strategy sequence is conditional on account access: use a purchased long call or put when it satisfies the premium cap and is approved; use a vertical only when the broker has approved spreads and the account meets the broker’s margin and buying-power requirements. Broker approval means the strategy is allowed, not recommended.[114][115][102]

## Stock selection criteria based on technicals

Judgment — Technical screening is a way to decide which underlying stocks deserve attention before an option is chosen. It does not predict a weekly income result. For a small account, the screen should favor stocks that trade actively, have a visible price structure, and show momentum that agrees with the proposed direction; the Position-sizing rules and maximum risk per trade section determines whether a qualifying idea is affordable.

A candidate passes this screen only when three elements agree: participation shown by volume, a defined support or resistance zone that frames the trade, and momentum confirmation. A chart signal is not a substitute for a predefined loss limit or for the Liquidity filters section’s option-chain checks.

### volume

Volume is the number of shares traded during a stated period, usually one trading day for stocks. Price is the primary chart input, while volume supplies secondary confirmation of the price move.[85] A liquid stock has enough buyers and sellers that it can be bought or sold in the needed quantity without much price impact; heavily traded stocks are easier to trade quickly, while thinly traded stocks may require a materially worse entry or exit price.[116][117]

**Framework judgment — Volume Gate:** Use daily stock data. A candidate passes only when its 20-day average daily share volume exceeds 5 million shares; an upside setup has rising price, above-average volume, and rising OBV, while a downside setup has falling price, above-average volume, and falling OBV. A breakout through a support or resistance zone additionally requires volume of at least twice that 20-day average, or RVOL of at least 2.[118][119] Reject the candidate when these conditions are not all met.[56][85][120][119][118]

A daily-volume floor of more than 5 million shares is a practical watchlist rule used for Dow Jones and S&P 500 stocks in small-account options trading.[56] High stock-trading volume typically indicates that the options market is liquid, and frequently traded large-cap stocks and major exchange-traded funds tend to have tighter spreads than less actively traded securities.[121][86] The most liquid names cited for small accounts include SPY, QQQ, AAPL, MSFT, and IWM; SPY is described as having penny-wide option spreads.[13]

The 5-million-share floor is a stock-selection condition, not proof that every strike and expiry is tradeable. OCC says that option volume alone does not establish liquidity, because activity can be concentrated in one at-the-money strike rather than distributed across strikes.[122] OCC also notes that high trading volume can coexist with wide bid-ask spreads or with options activity while the underlying stock itself is not active.[122] The Liquidity filters section therefore applies its contract-level open-interest and bid-ask-spread tests after this underlying-stock gate.

Judgment — Use a 20-day average rather than a single day’s volume because a one-day headline, rebalance, earnings reaction, or unusual event can distort the reading. Reject a stock that meets the 20-day floor solely because of an isolated spike if the current price action lacks sustained participation.

Volume should agree with the direction of the trend. In an uptrend, volume should be heavier on rallies and lighter on downside corrections; in a downtrend, volume should be heavier on selloffs and lighter on bounces.[85] Rising price with rising volume is described as an engaged uptrend, while rising price with falling volume indicates weakening conviction; falling price with rising volume is distribution, whereas falling price with falling volume can be a lower-pressure pullback within an uptrend.[119]

For an upside breakout, require volume of at least twice the stock’s average daily volume on the breakout day or during the breakout sequence. A breakout on two or three times average daily volume is described as evidence of institutional participation, while a breakout on thin volume is more likely to fail.[119] More generally, heavier volume when price breaks a trendline, support, or resistance gives the price action greater weight.[85] This relative-volume test decides whether a setup has enough participation to trade; otherwise defer it rather than force an options position.

On-Balance Volume, or OBV, is a cumulative volume indicator that adds volume when the stock closes higher than the prior close and subtracts volume when it closes lower.[120] A rising OBV line indicates that upside volume exceeds downside volume, while falling OBV indicates the reverse.[85] For a bullish candidate, require price to rise with OBV rising; if price rises while OBV is flat or falling, that divergence warns that volume may not support higher prices.[85]

For a bearish candidate, falling price with falling OBV supports downside pressure. A bullish OBV divergence occurs when price makes a lower low while OBV makes a higher low; a bearish divergence occurs when price makes a higher high while OBV makes a lower high.[120] OBV should be assessed from closing prices, and a volume spike can cause a sharp move in the indicator that may require time to settle.[120] Thus, OBV is a confirmation tool rather than a stand-alone entry trigger.[120]

The Volume Gate makes it possible to distinguish a tradeable, participated-in move from a chart move occurring on weak participation. It rules out low-activity stocks and unsupported breakouts, while leaving final option-contract liquidity to the Liquidity filters section.[56][119][122]

News- or event-driven volatility can widen bid-ask spreads even when volume spikes, and unexpected volume spikes can cause otherwise valid RSI/MACD signals to fail.[123][124]

Relative Volume (RVOL) compares the volume of each price bar with average volume over a specified number of preceding bars; RVOL above 1 means above-average volume and RVOL below 1 means below-average volume.

Relative volume can compare current trading volume with a stock’s normal volume at the same point in the session, rather than with the volume of a completed trading day.[177]

Relative Volume StDev compares a price bar’s volume with its simple moving average in standard-deviation units; a reading above 2.0 standard deviations can identify unusually high volume and a potential Defended Price Line (DPL), a horizontal level based on that bar.[178]

For a volume-spike bar, the DPL normally uses the bar’s closing price; overlapping bars can use the prior close, while a gapping bar requires a reference price based on the gap’s size.[178]

FatTail gives a less demanding day-trading screen of at least 1 million underlying shares per day and at least 10,000 option contracts per day, and names SPY, QQQ, AAPL, TSLA, AMZN, NVDA, META, AMD, and MSFT as popular choices.[125] Cboe’s August 2024 table of the five single stocks with the highest current average daily options volume lists NVDA, TSLA, AAPL, AMD, and AMZN.[112]

Contract activity can add a secondary clue after the stock and chart pass their tests: TradeAlgo states that call-option volume exceeding open interest by three times or more at one strike can indicate a significant new position, and combines that observation with a technical breakout pattern for directional screening. Volume is contracts traded in the current session, whereas open interest is the number of outstanding contracts.[126]

For an income-oriented screen, TradeAlgo uses an average true range (ATR) below 5% of stock price, market capitalization of at least $5 billion, and minimum open interest of 1,000; ATR measures the stock’s trading range, so the ATR condition excludes highly volatile names.[126]

OptionsPilot advises avoiding individual stocks below $20 billion market capitalization, meme stocks, and exchange-traded funds with low options volume; it associates meme stocks with wide spreads and unpredictable gaps.[13]

For an intraday breakout, breakdown, bounce, or rejection, require volume to be running at least 1.2 times the normal pace for that time of day, or require the completed signal day to close above its 20-day average volume. Exclude a candidate whose apparent signal is solely an opening gap that cannot be entered at a realistic price.

### support/resistance

Support is a price area below the current market where demand is thought strong enough to halt or slow a decline. Resistance is a price area above the current market where selling pressure is thought strong enough to halt or slow a rally.[127] Previous reaction lows are a method for identifying support, and previous reaction highs are a method for identifying resistance.[127]

Support and resistance are zones, not exact prices. Prices can briefly trade below support or above resistance, so a framework should not treat a line drawn at one quoted price as a guaranteed boundary.[127]Judgment — Call this the Zone Rule: identify a range around prior price action, then place the trade’s invalidation point beyond the zone rather than exactly at its center or edge.

A validated zone needs repeated, time-separated price reactions. Three price-action zones well spread across time are best; two are the minimum fallback.[116] Another statement of the same practical test is three or four connected price-action points, with levels tested more frequently receiving greater weight.[116] More recent levels also exert more influence on subsequent price action than older levels.[85]

**Framework judgment — Zone Rule:** Use daily underlying-stock spot-price data. Mark a support or resistance zone only when at least three time-separated price-action reactions occur near the same area; use the nearest opposing zone as the price objective. For a bullish candidate, price must be at or near validated support and show absorption or a bullish reversal; for a bearish candidate, price must be at or near validated resistance and show rejection or a bearish reversal. Place the invalidation point beyond the relevant zone.[116]

For a bullish setup, select the stock only when price is at or near a validated support zone and shows a bullish response: absorption of selling, a higher low, a reversal candle, or a confirmed rebound. Support may be viewed as a potential buying opportunity while the range remains valid, and a price-action signal at a key level can provide confirmation that price may move away from that level.[127][128] A long-trade checklist places the pattern low near support and the stop-loss around support.[116]

For a bearish setup, require price at or near validated resistance and evidence of rejection, such as failure to sustain a move through the zone, a bearish reversal, or a confirmed downside break. Resistance may be viewed as a possible selling opportunity, and the short-trade checklist places the pattern high near resistance.[127][116] A stop for a support-based long is placed slightly below support to leave room for ordinary volatility; the equivalent principle for a bearish trade is to place invalidation slightly above resistance.[116]

The immediate opposing zone defines the underlying-price objective. For a bullish trade, the next resistance is the prospective price target; for a bearish trade, the next support is the prospective target.[116] The source describes a 3–4% distance between support and resistance as sufficient for a short-term trade, but it is not an options-specific universal threshold.[116]Judgment — Take the options trade only if the room from entry to that opposing zone is enough to reach the planned option-profit objective after allowing for the zone’s width, time decay, transaction costs, and the possibility of an early exit.

The framework should differentiate range trades from breakout trades. A trading range is a period in which price moves within a relatively tight band because supply and demand are balanced; a break above the range signals demand winning, and a break below signals supply winning.[127] A rectangle pattern requires both horizontal support and resistance to be touched at least twice, and it ends when price breaks one of those lines.[129]

For a range setup, the directional path is support-to-resistance for a bullish idea and resistance-to-support for a bearish idea. For a breakout setup, wait for confirmation such as a firm close beyond the zone or a retest of broken resistance as support, or broken support as resistance.[15]Judgment — Do not buy a call merely because price touches support, or buy a put merely because price touches resistance; require the price response and volume confirmation already defined by the Volume Gate.

A break changes the chart structure. After price decisively breaks support, that level can become resistance; after price breaks resistance, that level can become support.[127] A breakout through a range is more meaningful when it occurs with heavier volume.[85] The trader should therefore redraw the immediate zones after a decisive break instead of assuming that the old range still governs the next 3–21 days.

Trendlines and moving averages can supply dynamic support or resistance when horizontal levels are less useful. An uptrend consists of rising peaks and troughs, while a downtrend consists of declining peaks and troughs.[85] A valid trendline can be drawn from two points, but a third touch identifies it as valid; its significance rises with duration and repeated tests.[85] The 20-day moving average is used by short-term traders to spot short-term trend changes, while the 50-day moving average can act as support in an uptrend.[85]

At an all-time high, there is no prior price history above the current price from which to draw conventional resistance; at an all-time low, there is no comparable historical support below price.[116]Judgment — Exclude all-time-high and all-time-low breakouts from this small-account framework unless a separate, preplanned trend-following method supplies both an underlying-price invalidation point and a realistic target. The ordinary support-to-resistance or resistance-to-support framework needs both sides of the trade map.

Support and resistance provides the price map: it specifies the directional premise, the point at which that premise fails, and the available room before the next opposing zone. It does not convert a chart level into certainty, so confirmation and position risk control remain necessary.[116]

Framework judgment — worked-trade validation: Each hypothetical bullish trade must state its entry stock price, validated support zone, opposing resistance zone, and stock-price objective at that resistance; each hypothetical bearish trade must state its entry stock price, validated resistance zone, opposing support zone, and stock-price objective at that support. For a bull call debit spread with long-call strike \(K_L\), short-call strike \(K_S\), and debit \(D\) per share, calculate expiration P/L at the stated objective \(S_T\) as \([\min(\max(S_T-K_L,0),K_S-K_L)-D]\times100\). For a bear put debit spread with long-put strike \(K_L\), short-put strike \(K_S\), and debit \(D\) per share, calculate expiration P/L at the stated objective \(S_T\) as \([\min(\max(K_L-S_T,0),K_L-K_S)-D]\times100\). Include a trade only when the calculated expiration P/L at its opposing-zone objective reaches or exceeds the planned profit target.[116][51][46]

A moving average is the average of closing prices over a chosen period; a simple moving average weights each closing price equally, whereas an exponential moving average gives more weight to recent prices.[130] Investing.com identifies the 50-day and 200-day moving averages as useful references and describes the 200-day moving average as dynamic support in an uptrend and dynamic resistance in a downtrend.[130]Judgment — Use the 200-day moving average as a secondary trend-context check: favor bullish zone setups when price is above it and bearish zone setups when price is below it; horizontal reaction zones remain the primary entry, invalidation, and target map.[130][116]

For a bullish call-debit-spread candidate, a recent breakout to fresh highs on strong volume, a continuing rise, or a bullish reversal are favorable directional contexts; a pullback into previous support within a larger uptrend is another such context.[32] A bull call spread fits a moderately bullish outlook in which the underlying is expected to increase modestly over the options’ life.[131]

A reaction high is an intermittent peak and a reaction low an intermittent trough as a security fluctuates; the minimum time interval and price movement used to define either one are part of the analyst’s criteria. These features supply the prior highs and lows used to draw the zones.[118][127]

A retracement reverses part of a preceding advance or decline. ChartSchool states that retracements typically cover one-third to two-thirds of the prior move, while one exceeding two-thirds typically signals a trend reversal. This provides an additional way to assess whether a pullback still fits the existing trend before treating a support or resistance response as a continuation setup.[118]

Relative-strength ratio analysis compares two entities. Dividing a stock by the S&P 500 Index shows whether that stock is outperforming or underperforming the market, and a rising ratio means the numerator is outperforming; the framework may use this as an additional alignment check for a bullish candidate.[118]

When marking price-action zones, prioritize zones followed by significant price action rather than ordinary small reactions, and plot no more than three points.[116]

A stock approaching long-term resistance with sharply rising put volume can signal an impending reversal.[126]

Also mark prior consolidation ranges as potential support or resistance zones, alongside reaction highs and lows. For example, a bullish trade that needs a move from $50 to $53 to meet its profit target fails the screen if the next material resistance is near $51.

### momentum indicators

A momentum indicator measures the speed, direction, or strength of price movement. This framework uses two daily-chart indicators: the 14-day Relative Strength Index, or RSI, and the Moving Average Convergence/Divergence indicator, or MACD. RSI measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes on a 0–100 scale, while MACD combines moving averages into a momentum oscillator.[119][132][133]

The standard RSI setting uses 14 periods. Readings above 70 are traditionally described as overbought and readings below 30 as oversold.[132][134] These labels describe the speed and extent of the move, not an automatic reversal: RSI can remain overbought or oversold for extended periods during a strong trend.[132][134]

MACD is normally calculated as the 12-day exponential moving average, or EMA, minus the 26-day EMA. Its signal line is a 9-day EMA of the MACD line.[133] MACD above zero means the 12-day EMA is above the 26-day EMA and indicates increasing upside momentum; MACD below zero indicates increasing downside momentum.[133] A bullish crossover occurs when MACD crosses above its signal line, and a bearish crossover occurs when it crosses below.[133]

The 14-period Average Directional Index (ADX) measures trend strength rather than direction: a reading below 20 is generally nontrending, while a rising reading above 20—and especially one above 25—indicates a stronger trend.[8]

**Framework judgment — Momentum Confirmation Rule:** Apply this rule only after the Volume Gate and Zone Rule pass. Use daily 14-day RSI and daily MACD with 12, 26, and 9 settings. A bullish candidate passes only when price is above its 200-day moving average, RSI is from 50 through 70, MACD is above its signal line, and MACD is below zero. A bearish candidate passes only when price is below its 200-day moving average, RSI is from 30 through 50, MACD is below its signal line, and MACD is above zero. Reject all other readings.[132][133][135]

StockCharts demonstrates its RSI In Bullish Range 50–70 scan by restricting it to S&P 100 stocks with a StockCharts Technical Rank (SCTR) above 60; SCTR is StockCharts’ named technical-rank measure.[135] Another StockCharts bullish scan requires the stock to be above its 200-day moving average, MACD above its signal line, and MACD below zero, describing an upturn after a pullback.[133] Together, those conditions identify a stock with broader upward trend context and improving shorter-term momentum.

For a bearish candidate, a StockCharts scan uses price below the 200-day moving average, MACD below its signal line, and MACD above zero, describing a downturn after a bounce.[133] A separate RSI scan uses price below the 200-day moving average and RSI at or above 70 to identify a downtrend with an overbought RSI condition.[132] In an Apple example, RSI falling below 40 put the uptrend in doubt, and RSI dropping below 30 confirmed a downtrend; the same example describes RSI rebounding above 65 as a possible reversal sign.[135]

The small-account screen should reject or defer a bullish momentum entry when RSI is below 40, because the cited Apple example treats that as a warning that the bullish trend is in doubt.[135] It should also defer a bullish entry when price makes a new high while RSI makes a lower high, because that is a bearish RSI divergence showing weakening rally momentum.[119][132] A bearish divergence can persist in a strong uptrend, so it is a warning that needs price-structure confirmation rather than a standalone short signal.[132]

MACD is primarily a trend-trading momentum oscillator rather than an overbought-or-oversold indicator.[136][133] In a trading range, MACD can whipsaw as its line repeatedly crosses the signal line, and users commonly avoid trading that condition or close positions to reduce portfolio volatility.[136]Judgment — If MACD has crossed its signal line repeatedly during the recent range, do not use a MACD crossover as the reason for a 3–21 day options trade; wait for a confirmed range break with the Volume Gate satisfied.

MACD can lag a price move because it is calculated from moving averages, so its confirmation may arrive after part of the move has occurred.[179][180]

RSI and MACD should corroborate price action, not replace it. RSI is intended to be used with other tools and indicators to confirm signals and reduce false alarms.[132] Technical analysis recommends confirmation across volume, moving averages, oscillators, and related stocks in the same group.[85] A top-down process first reviews the broad market, then stronger or weaker sectors and industry groups, then individual stocks.[85][117]

Judgment — For a bullish candidate, prefer a stock whose broader market, sector, price structure, volume, RSI, and MACD all point upward; for a bearish candidate, seek the corresponding downside alignment. Do not add indicators simply to make a checklist longer: use RSI and MACD consistently, then record outcomes over a sufficiently large series of trades before claiming that either setup has an expected win rate.

Momentum indicators make it possible to distinguish a support bounce or resistance rejection with directional follow-through from one that lacks trend confirmation. They rule out chasing a stretched move on RSI alone and reduce reliance on MACD crossovers inside a range.[132][136][8]

Use RSI calculated from closing prices, and keep the chart-history setting consistent because RSI smoothing means that the same 14-day RSI can differ slightly when calculated from different total lengths of price history.[137]

RSI interpretation can also be trend-relative. The CMT Association describes an uptrend RSI range with lows near 40–50 and highs between 80–90, and a downtrend range with lows around 20–30 and highs limited to 55–65.[138] Thus, an RSI above 70 during a strong uptrend can indicate momentum rather than exhaustion, while an RSI reading alone is not an automatic entry or exit signal.[138]

StockCharts provides a more restrictive bullish MACD confirmation example: with MACD settings of 12, 26, and 9, the MACD line crosses above the signal line while both are below zero and the MACD histogram has risen for at least three days.[139]Judgment — A trader who wants fewer, more selective bullish candidates may require this three-day rising-histogram condition in addition to the framework’s bullish RSI and MACD requirements.[139][130]

The MACD-Histogram is MACD minus its 9-day EMA signal line: it is positive when MACD is above the signal line and negative when MACD is below it. An expanding histogram shows MACD moving farther from its signal line, while a contracting histogram shows convergence and is the first step toward a signal-line crossover.[140]

The histogram can flag a developing change before a MACD crossover. A peak-trough bullish divergence has MACD at a lower low while the histogram makes a higher low; the bearish counterpart has MACD at a new high while the histogram makes a lower high. Longer, more pronounced divergences are more robust than short, shallow ones, and the signal-line crossover is the confirmation.[140]

The daily 14-period RSI is appropriate to this framework because a period on a daily chart represents one trading day; the RSI formula is 100 − [100 / (1 + RS)], where RS is average gain divided by average loss over the selected period.[141][142] RSI’s 50 centerline is a directional reference: readings above 50 indicate that gains are outpacing losses, and readings below 50 that losses are outpacing gains.[141]

In a strong uptrend, OANDA describes RSI as typically remaining between 40 and 90, with 40–50 acting as RSI support; in a strong downtrend, it describes RSI as ranging from 10 to 60, with 50–60 as RSI resistance. RSI trendlines can also identify a potential indicator breakout when RSI breaks the drawn line.[141]

StockCharts’ daily bullish MACD-Histogram scan requires price above its 200-day moving average, the histogram to move from negative to positive, and the MACD line to remain negative; its bearish counterpart requires price below the 200-day moving average, a positive-to-negative histogram move, and a positive MACD line. These conditions frame a resumption after a pullback in the prevailing longer-term trend.[140]

Other StockCharts sample scan conditions illustrate additional momentum and trend screens: a positive or negative 20-period slope of the 12,26,9 MACD-Histogram; a 10-period positive OBV slope; a 12-period ROC with a five-period slope above 10; and a 14-period RSI whose minimum remains above 40 for 60 periods.[139]

For swing-trading context, Investing.com describes RSI between 40 and 60 as consolidation and moves above 70 or below 30 as possible breakouts; it also states that price and RSI moving in the same direction confirms trend strength. RSI divergence—price lower lows with RSI higher lows, or price higher highs with RSI lower highs—signals weakening momentum and a possible reversal.[142]

Within the required bullish RSI range, prefer RSI that is rising; within the required bearish RSI range, prefer RSI that is falling. A rising MACD histogram strengthens a bullish confirmation, while a declining histogram strengthens a bearish confirmation.

The technical screen is one reproducible sequence. (1) Volume Gate: daily 20-day average volume exceeds 5 million shares; direction and OBV agree; and any breakout has RVOL of at least 2. (2) Zone Rule: daily spot-price support or resistance has at least three time-separated reactions; bullish setups occur at support with absorption or reversal and target the next resistance, while bearish setups occur at resistance with rejection or reversal and target the next support. (3) Momentum Confirmation Rule: bullish setups require price above the 200-day moving average, RSI 50–70, MACD above its signal line and below zero; bearish setups require price below the 200-day moving average, RSI 30–50, MACD below its signal line and above zero.[56][85][120][119][116][132][133][135]

Judgment — The recommended route is to trade only candidates that pass all three tests and the separate Liquidity filters. This is feasible as a disciplined selection process, but it is not a basis for treating $200–300 per week as an expected return: volume, chart structure, and momentum identify a defined trade premise, while the probability distribution and required win rate belong to the target-feasibility analysis.

The added technical evidence supports a confluence approach: moving averages supply trend direction, RSI measures price-move speed, MACD measures trend momentum, and volume confirms conviction.[130] Require agreement between direction and momentum indicators rather than using either indicator alone.[130][124]

An options screener is software that searches optionable securities using user-defined criteria. TradeAlgo describes the workflow as using the screener to generate a watchlist, then using chart analysis to time candidates; its directional screen uses price above or below the 50-day moving average, a volume spike of at least twice average daily volume, and IV percentile below 50. IV percentile places current implied volatility within its preceding 52-week range.[126]

The numerical volume and momentum cutoffs are proposed screening judgments, not measured evidence that selected option trades will win.[143]

## Strike and expiry selection rules

For a 3–21-day directional trade, the strike and expiry are not interchangeable choices. The **strike price** (also called the exercise price) determines where the option can be exercised, while the **expiration date** is the contract’s fixed final valid date. A sound small-account plan selects both from a defined price target and time horizon, then plans to exit by sale rather than drift into expiration.[4][77]

### strike

**Moneyness** describes the strike relative to the current stock price. A call is at the money (ATM) when stock price equals strike, in the money (ITM) when stock price is above strike, and out of the money (OTM) when the strike is above stock price; the put relationships reverse.[6] An ITM option generally costs more than an OTM option, while a farther-OTM purchase requires a larger favorable move before it can be profitable.[76][6]

An option’s intrinsic value is the amount by which it is in the money: a call has intrinsic value when the stock is above its strike, and a put has intrinsic value when the stock is below its strike.[76]

At-the-money (ATM) options contain the most time value; out-of-the-money options are generally cheaper but less likely to finish in the money.[77][181]

Judgment — Use the **Target-Strike Rule** for a 3–21-day directional setup: first set the technical price target from the support/resistance and momentum analysis; second select the spread’s short strike at or just inside that target; third select the long strike ATM or slightly ITM, subject to the maximum loss in Position-sizing rules. For a bullish debit spread, buy the lower-strike call and sell the higher-strike call.

A bull call spread consists of a long call at a lower strike and a short call at a higher strike, and both calls use the same expiration. A bear put spread similarly buys the higher-strike put and sells the lower-strike put.[21][46] Placing the short strike near the forecasted move allows a debit spread to approach its full expiration value if the forecast is reached, rather than requiring an exceptional move beyond the technical target.[144]

For a bull call debit spread, the expiration break-even stock price is the long-call strike plus the net debit per share.[20][3][170]

The debit spread’s net debit is the amount paid to enter it, and its strike width is the dollar distance between its strikes.Judgment — Reject the trade if one spread’s maximum loss—the net debit multiplied by the standard 100-share contract multiplier—exceeds the pre-set dollar cap in Position-sizing rules; do not widen the spread or add contracts merely to pursue a larger weekly outcome. Fidelity notes that strike and expiry largely determine each contract’s gain-and-loss potential, and contract count must reflect both that potential and the trader’s loss tolerance.[4][53]

For a single-leg long call or put, use ATM or slightly ITM strikes only when the entire premium paid fits that same maximum-loss cap. ATM options are commonly used for directional speculation; ITM options have higher delta, meaning their value tends to respond more to a stock move, but they cost more. Far-OTM options may look affordable, yet they are described as highly speculative because they require a very large directional move.[145][76] This rules out cheap, far-OTM “lottery ticket” contracts unless the technical thesis specifically calls for an unusually large move within the available time.

The strike decision therefore converts a chart target into a defined payoff: the long strike supplies directional exposure, the short strike marks the target at which a vertical spread can realize most or all of its capped value, and the debit determines whether the idea fits the loss cap.[144][53]

For options with the same expiry, Fidelity states that in-the-money strikes carry higher total premiums and have a greater likelihood of intrinsic value at expiration, whereas at-the-money and out-of-the-money strikes are more aggressive buyer choices and are less likely to have intrinsic value at expiration.[146]

Judgment — Do not open a debit spread with both legs in the money. Robinhood identifies an in-the-money call debit spread as one in which both strikes are below the underlying price and an in-the-money put debit spread as one in which both strikes are above it; it associates these structures with short-option early-assignment risk. Robinhood also gives a general debit-spread guideline of paying no less than one-quarter and no more than one-half of the spread width.[97]

Strike width is also a payoff choice. Longbridge states that wider vertical spreads can deliver larger returns with more risk, while narrower spreads can have a higher probability of success with lower rewards. The framework therefore compares the actual debit, breakeven, maximum profit, and maximum loss before selecting width; it does not select a wider spread merely because its maximum payout is larger.[147][61][38]

Delta measures an option’s sensitivity to a $1 underlying-price change. Fidelity states that a 0.70 delta implies an anticipated $0.70 option-price move for a $1 stock move and a 70% probability of finishing in the money at expiration; this is not a probability of a profitable trade. Higher delta also corresponds to a higher implied probability of finishing in the money and a more expensive option.[148]

Delta is not the probability of reaching a profit target during a shorter 3–21-day hold: it does not account for changes in implied volatility, bid-ask execution, the planned early-exit price, or a vertical spread’s capped payoff.[76][77]

Strike width is also a payoff choice. TradingBlock states that a narrower bull call spread has lower cost, lower maximum profit, and a higher probability of maximum profit, whereas a wider spread has higher cost, higher maximum profit, and a lower probability of maximum profit.[115]Judgment — Within the pre-set loss cap, prefer the narrowest liquid spread whose short strike remains at or just inside the technical target; this prevents increasing the required move merely to enlarge the payoff.[115][144][53]

As a secondary chain check rather than a probability guarantee, TradingBlock’s typical bull-call-spread convention uses a 0.50–0.60 delta long call, a 0.20–0.35 delta short call, and a 0.30–0.40 delta difference between the legs. Delta measures the option’s expected change in value for a $1 change in the underlying, all else equal.[115] This convention is consistent with using an ATM or slightly ITM long call and a farther-upside short call, but the technical target and the actual debit remain the framework’s deciding rules.[115][144][53]

A published directional guideline uses approximately 0.50–0.60 delta for the purchased leg and 0.15 delta for the short leg of a debit spread.[145]

A numerical bull-call-spread illustration shows the trade-off directly: buying one 50 call for $210 had a $52.10 breakeven, while adding a short 55 call reduced cost by $80 and lowered breakeven by $0.80, but capped profit at $370 above $55.[38] The short strike therefore exchanges unlimited long-call upside for lower entry cost and a defined maximum payoff.[38]

Fidelity says some traders use options statistics, including implied volatility and historical volatility, when choosing a strike.[76]

For a put debit spread, Robinhood says to avoid buying an in-the-money put spread, meaning both strikes are above the underlying price. It also says that paying no less than one-quarter and no more than one-half of spread width roughly translates to a 25–50% theoretical chance of success.[97]

For a call credit spread, Robinhood says credit spreads are typically constructed with out-of-the-money options; the common call selection uses strikes above the underlying price and collects roughly one-third of spread width as premium.[97]

The Montréal Exchange guide describes selling credit vertical spreads as a way to cap the risk of selling a naked call or put and specifies selling a 0.50-delta, at-the-money option and buying a 0.25-delta, far-out-of-the-money option.[145] The same guide describes short puts as an income and stock-acquisition strategy and specifies a 0.40-delta, slightly out-of-the-money strike.[145]

TradingBlock’s stated bullish-call-spread rule of thumb is to buy a call 3–5% out of the money and sell the short call about 7% out of the money.[115]

For this $700-account framework, one contract’s net debit multiplied by 100 must be no more than $35; the same $35 ceiling applies to the full premium of a single-leg long call or put. Select a spread width only if its maximum value minus the net debit can support the trade’s stated target profit while still meeting that loss ceiling.

### expiry

The **expiration date** is the contract’s last valid day; an option not sold or exercised by expiration ceases to exist and has no value.[6][4] **Days to expiration (DTE)** means the calendar days remaining from entry to that fixed date. A planned 3–21-day hold is therefore not an instruction to choose a contract expiring in 3–21 days.

Judgment — Apply the **Seven-Day Buffer Rule**: choose an expiry with intended holding days plus at least seven calendar days remaining. This produces a **10–28 DTE range of minimum requirements**, not a maximum-DTE range: a planned 3-day hold requires at least 10 DTE, while a planned 21-day hold requires at least 28 DTE; a later expiry remains permissible if its premium fits the loss cap.[6][4][77] The planned action is sale to close before expiration, not exercise.

This buffer gives the technical thesis time to work after the intended holding window. A longer-dated contract generally costs more but gives the underlying more time to move profitably; a shorter-dated one is cheaper but leaves less time for the required move.[77][76] The time-value portion of an option premium usually declines as expiration approaches, and that decay—called **theta**—accelerates as time becomes short.[6][4][145]

Option time-decay models often reflect seven calendar days of decay across five trading days, although model conventions differ.[182]

SPY’s following-session trading range was $699.01–$718.29, illustrating the size of an underlying-price move that a near-expiration position may encounter.[183]

The risk is especially acute for long OTM options. The OCC states that the further OTM an option is and the less time it has to expiration, the greater the risk that the holder loses all or part of the investment; an option buyer must be right about both direction and timing.[6][10] A longer expiry does not ensure a profit, but it reduces dependence on an immediate, unusually large move.

Judgment — Do not deliberately hold a small-account position into expiration week. Close or roll—close the current position and, if still justified, open a later-dated replacement—by 7 DTE. This is an operational rule because time decay accelerates toward expiration, late moves can sharply change short-dated-spread values, and closing before expiration avoids pin risk.[63][39][91]

Gamma measures how quickly an option’s delta changes as the underlying price moves. Near expiration, an at-the-money option can have rapidly changing directional exposure while losing time value quickly.[184]

Expiration also creates exercise and assignment issues unrelated to the original chart thesis. Unless the Options Clearing Corporation waives the Exercise-by-Exception procedure, standardized equity options that are in the money by specified amounts are automatically exercised absent contrary instructions; brokerage firms can impose their own exercise cut-off procedures.[9][10][149] For spreads, closing before expiration avoids the risk that one leg is exercised or assigned while the other creates an unintended stock position.[41][46]

The expiry decision therefore separates the trade thesis from contract mechanics: choose enough DTE to cover the planned 3–21-day hold plus the buffer, then exit before expiration converts a remaining options position into exercise, assignment, or lapse risk.[6][9]

The 10–28-DTE framework range is shorter than the 30–60-DTE textbook approach Robinhood gives for call and put debit spreads, and shorter than Longbridge’s stated 30–60-DTE range for debit spreads. The framework uses the shorter range only because the stated holding period is 3–21 days and retains a seven-calendar-day buffer after that planned holding period.[147][97]

A separate published guideline proposes 45–60 or more days to expiration for directional long calls, puts, and debit spreads, versus 30–45 days to expiration for credit verticals; these are remaining contract lives, not intended holding periods.[145]

The OCC identifies exercise price, time until expiration, underlying value, and the underlying’s volatility as factors affecting option value. Expiry selection must therefore be assessed together with the selected strike and the expected price movement, not as a standalone calendar choice.[150]

FINRA states that options lose value over time and that an option expiring OTM is worthless.[151] This reinforces the framework’s requirement to select an expiry after the planned exit and to sell to close rather than depend on last-day value.[151][152]

Exercise style and settlement must be checked before entry. Cboe states that all single-stock and ETF options are American-style, so they may be exercised or assigned at any point on or before expiration; FINRA likewise identifies stock and ETF options as American-style in U.S. markets.[80][153] Standard equity options are physically settled, so exercise or assignment delivers securities rather than simply ending in cash.[151] In contrast, Cboe identifies Mini-SPX (XSP) as European-style, exercisable or assignable only at expiration, and the SPX as European-style and cash-settled.[80][49]

For every proposed short option leg, record the product’s settlement type, exercise style, last trading time, resulting shares and cash exposure under exercise or assignment, broker instruction deadline and customer threshold, and the account’s cash, margin, buying-power, and concentration capacity. Then test the resulting stock position above and below the strike after normal options trading ends, and remove an unwanted outcome while a market is available.[49]

FINRA states that a holder’s final exercise decision is due by 5:30 p.m. ET on the business day of expiration; when a standardized equity option expires on a non-business day, the deadline is the immediately preceding business day. Member firms may impose an earlier customer-instruction deadline.[151] FINRA found that firms used varying early cut-off times and did not communicate or apply them consistently.[151] Most stock options stop trading at 4:00 p.m. ET, while many stocks trade after hours until 8:00 p.m. ET, so after-hours price movement can affect an expiration-Friday exercise decision.[92]

At expiration, in-the-money options are commonly exercised automatically; Interactive Brokers states that OCC uses a $0.01 in-the-money threshold, while the clearing or brokerage firm may use a different threshold.[154][99] A clearing member may also submit contrary instructions to exercise an option below the threshold or not exercise one that meets it, so no closing price guarantees that a short equity option will avoid assignment.[49] Closing a short option during trading hours generally removes later assignment risk on that closed position, provided the order fills and assignment has not already occurred.[49]

A roll is not an automatic response to a losing position. Reassess the technical thesis, remaining risk, liquidity, and the new contract’s cost before opening any replacement position.

Option-chain comparison includes the event window, neighboring strikes for premium and directional exposure, and expirations for term structure.[155]

### 3–21 day holds

The intended holding period should be driven by the expected time for price to reach the technical target, not by the availability of a cheap weekly contract. Fidelity advises estimating how long the trade is likely to take to become profitable before selecting expiration.[77] Weekly options are short-term contracts, commonly expiring on Friday for single stocks, though weekly expirations are not available for every stock.[4][77]

Judgment — Before entry, write three dates in the trade plan: the expected target date, the latest acceptable exit date within the 3–21-day window, and the mandatory 7-DTE closure date. Enter only if the target date occurs before both exit deadlines. This prevents an attractive strike from overriding the time required for the setup.

Theta descriptions should not be treated as a universal ranking of ATM versus OTM contracts. The Montréal Exchange guide characterizes ATM long options as high-gamma and moderate-priced and OTM options as cheap and low-gamma, while IBKR describes both ATM and OTM options as susceptible to rapid time decay near expiration.[145][52] The practical rule is simpler: compare the actual premium, delta, target distance, DTE, and bid-ask spread for the specific chain rather than assuming a cheap strike has low risk.

This holding-period rule makes timing testable: if the target cannot plausibly be reached before the planned exit and before 7 DTE, the trade is rejected rather than extended into expiration pressure.[77][10]

Before entry, assess the chance of reaching the technical target by the intended target date. Fidelity states that its probability calculator can estimate the likelihood that an underlying will trade above, below, or between targets on a specified date; it permits adjustment of the price target, target date, volatility parameters, and strike, including use of different strikes to determine the probability of a successful option strategy. **Implied volatility** is the volatility reflected in an option’s market price and the market’s forecast of future stock-price volatility.[148][63]

The Rule of 16 estimates a one-standard-deviation daily stock-price move by dividing annualized implied volatility by 16 and applying the result to the stock price; it estimates move magnitude, not direction.[184]

For a vertical spread, both legs must have the same expiration date and differ in strike price. A bull call spread buys the lower-strike call and sells the higher-strike call; a bear put spread buys the higher-strike put and sells the lower-strike put.[31][88][156][91]

Use the yearly Expiration Calendars to verify the selected equity, index, interest-rate, or currency-option expiration date, expiration-month code, strike-price code, and exchange holiday before entering the position.[157]

**Application to the hypothetical examples —** Each hypothetical trade uses an entry date of November 20, 2026, and a December 18, 2026 expiration: 28 calendar days remain to expiration. The planned sale is no later than December 11, 2026, which permits a hold of up to 21 calendar days while retaining the framework’s seven-calendar-day buffer. For each example, state the technical objective first, place the short strike at or just inside that objective, then calculate the actual net debit times the standard 100-share contract multiplier and reject the position if its maximum loss exceeds the pre-set loss ceiling.[6][4][77][144][53]

The framework favors ATM or slightly ITM long strikes and uses the short strike of a defined-risk vertical spread at or just inside the technical target. It then requires DTE of at least the intended 3–21-day hold plus seven calendar days—a 10–28 DTE range of minimum requirements—preserving a seven-day post-hold buffer and requiring a sale-to-close by 7 DTE. More time costs more, but it reduces dependence on an immediate move; nearer expiries may be cheaper, but theta decay and expiration mechanics become more consequential.[6][4][77][145][144][9]

1. Set the technical target first, then choose the spread’s short strike at or just inside it.
2. Use ATM or slightly ITM long strikes; reject cheap far-OTM contracts without an exceptional-move thesis.
3. Select DTE of at least the 3–21-day planned hold plus seven days; 10–28 DTE is the range of minimum requirements, not a maximum. [6][4][77]
4. Close or roll by 7 DTE; do not rely on expiration handling for the exit.

The framework’s target-based spread construction is consistent with Fidelity’s instruction to match the expiration date to the expected stock-price timing and to choose offsetting strikes that match the forecast. It is also consistent with the common bull-call approach of buying a strike at or near the current price and selling a higher strike near the price target.[94][39][93]

Check both the listed expiration calendar and the broker’s current procedures, because holiday schedules or unusual market conditions can alter expiration timetables.[49][157]

## Liquidity filters

Open interest and bid-ask spread are the two required contract-level liquidity checks in this framework. Open interest counts outstanding option contracts for a specific call or put, strike, and expiration; the bid-ask spread is the gap between the best displayed buying and selling prices. They matter because a small account can lose a meaningful part of a position’s value to poor entry or exit prices even when the price outlook proves correct.[4][87][86]

### minimum open interest

Set the framework’s minimum open-interest threshold at **500 contracts for every option series traded**.[158] An option series is one specific option type, strike, and expiration. Thus, a single-leg call or put must meet 500 contracts, and both the long and short options in a debit spread or credit spread must independently meet 500 contracts.[158] This is a deliberately conservative screen.

Tradingfives treats at least 500 open contracts as a good-liquidity screen; TradeAlgo sets minimum daily volume at 100 contracts for individual stocks and 500 for ETFs, and minimum open interest at 500 for stocks and 2,000 for ETFs.[158][126]

A separate liquidity guideline prefers more than 10,000 open contracts at the selected SPY strike and expiration, or more than 1,000 open contracts for a liquid individual stock. It also prefers current daily volume above 100 contracts and considers fewer than 10 contracts generally too thin.[185]

Open interest is not the same as volume. Open interest is the cumulative count still open after the prior clearing cycle, while volume is the number of contracts traded during the current session. Open interest is calculated at the end of the trading day, so the displayed figure is not a live count; check current volume as a separate confirmation of activity.[159][160]

SPY’s aggregate option open interest was 18.72 million contracts, comprising 6.79 million calls and 11.93 million puts; that aggregate does not establish the liquidity of a particular strike and expiration.[186][187][188]

Higher open interest is generally one indicator of more activity and liquidity, but it is not sufficient on its own. OCC describes a liquid chain as having high open interest across multiple strikes and tight spreads, while also warning that volume alone is inadequate. Applying the 500-contract per-leg rule avoids pairing a liquid-looking long leg with a short leg that may be hard to close or adjust.[160][122][158]

The 500-contract floor is consistent with guidance to seek at least hundreds of open contracts, ideally thousands, and with a 500-contract illustrative liquidity screen.[121][158] It can exclude newly listed weekly options that have little or even zero displayed open interest but may execute well when the underlying is liquid. **The framework accepts that missed opportunity in exchange for a simple, conservative execution rule.**[121][158][87]

Open interest below roughly 50–100 contracts is a warning sign, but even substantial open interest does not establish that executable size is available at the displayed bid or ask.[189][121]

TradeAlgo attributes to Options Clearing Corporation research the finding that contracts with open interest above 1,000 have bid-ask spreads 62% tighter than contracts below 500 open interest.[126]

Check the exact underlying, strike, and expiry because each combination is a separate contract with its own spread and activity.[161]

Use visible size at both the bid and ask, recurring trading volume, and quotes that remain displayed through small underlying-price movements as additional signs of liquidity. No single liquidity measure is sufficient.[161]

TradeAlgo states that a daily-volume-to-open-interest ratio above 2.0 often indicates significant new interest in a strike. It also states that call volume exceeding open interest by 3x or more can indicate a significant new position; combined with a technical breakout pattern, this is a directional screening setup.[126]

### maximum bid-ask spread

Set two concurrent discretionary leg screens: each leg’s ask-minus-bid width must be **no more than $0.05 per share** and **no more than 20% of its midpoint**.[24][27][86] The midpoint is the average of bid and ask, and the percentage spread is ask minus bid divided by midpoint.[87][86] For example, a $2.00 bid and $2.04 ask has a $2.02 midpoint and a 1.98% spread, so it qualifies; a $1.00 bid and $1.08 ask has a $1.04 midpoint and a 7.69% spread, so it fails.

Both limits must be met; the $0.05 cap controls the absolute quoted cost and the 20%-of-midpoint cap controls that cost relative to the option premium.[24][27][86][87][162]

A separate illustrative per-leg liquidity rule sets different concurrent limits: open interest of at least 500 contracts and a bid-ask spread no wider than both $0.10 per share and 10% of the quoted midpoint.[161][190]

Options Hawk illustrates why spread width must be assessed relative to premium: a $1 spread on a $50 option is 2% of its price, while 2% of a $5 option is a $0.10 spread.[163] FlashAlpha’s under-3% figure is an at-the-money average-spread symbol screen for strategy execution, distinct from this framework’s individual-leg maximum.[162]

For option positions held 1–5 days, one practical liquidity guideline uses an approximate maximum bid-ask spread of 3%–5% of midpoint.[185]

In sampled weeks during 1999–2000, historical SEC data showed average quoted spreads of $0.28–$0.35 for options priced below $20.[121][191]

For debit and credit spreads, apply both leg screens to each leg, including any out-of-the-money wing, and require the complete vertical’s displayed ask-minus-bid width to be **no more than $0.10 per share**.[24][27][162][58] An at-the-money chain can appear liquid while a farther-out strike has a much wider spread; cited guidance notes that a 5% at-the-money spread can coincide with 20% or more at a 10-delta wing.

Submit a limit order for the complete spread, with entry computed at the displayed natural prices: buy the long leg at its ask and sell the short leg at its bid. A limit order sets the worst acceptable price but may not fill.[36][104][155][162]

Before entry, refresh each leg’s bid, ask, displayed size, and quote timestamp: market-data fields can reflect different observation times, and a snapshot does not guarantee a real-time executable price.[190]

Spreads change with volatility, news, the opening and closing periods, and underlying-market liquidity. Chase states that spreads are typically wider at the open and close because of higher price volatility and unpredictable order flow, tend to be tighter midday as trading stabilizes and volume becomes more predictable, and have exceptions.[86] A qualifying quote at entry does not assure the same exit quote, an orderly market, or profitability; options markets are not guaranteed to provide a secondary market at all times.[122][164][104][10][6]

The spread is the best ask minus the best bid. Buying at the ask and immediately selling at the bid realizes that difference as a loss; spreads reduce profits and increase losses even where commissions are zero.[123]

For a standard 100-share-multiplier contract, a $0.50 quoted spread represents $50 per contract, and a $0.10 spread on a $1.00 option produces $0.20 of entry-and-exit spread cost per share, or 20% of the option’s value, when crossed on both sides. Price the planned round trip before entry.[165][125]

One liquidity illustration gives a $0.02 spread around a $2.01 option as less than 1% of midpoint and $2 per standard contract to cross once; another gives a $0.50 spread around a $1.75 midpoint as about 29% and $50 per contract to cross once.[185]

For a two-leg vertical, each leg has its own bid-ask spread; calculate the displayed package bid from selling the long leg at its bid and buying the short leg at its ask, and calculate the displayed package ask from buying the long leg at its ask and selling the short leg at its bid. The package ask-minus-bid width must not exceed $0.10 per share.[58][104][155] Multi-leg positions therefore require the leg-by-leg tests already specified and a package limit order.[58][39]

A midpoint is a calculated center of bid and ask, not a promised fill, and the separate leg midpoints of a multi-leg position can imply a package price that was never simultaneously available. Cboe states that electronic auctions guarantee an order a price no worse than the national best bid and offer (NBBO) while providing an opportunity for further price improvement; retail executions often occur inside the NBBO and near the midpoint.[161][112]

The National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO) is the best displayed bid and offer across relevant market venues.[190]

Effective spread measures actual execution cost relative to the contemporaneous midpoint, rather than only the displayed quoted width.[191] Market-depth slippage occurs when an order exceeds the quantity available at the best price and fills across several worse price levels.[192]

Dense weekly-expiration listings are a practical preliminary liquidity sign; products with only a few monthly expirations tend to have wider spreads. Recheck the actual contract before entry and exit because liquidity concentrated around an earnings report can dry up on subsequent days.[109]

Thin volume, high volatility, and proximity to an event widen option spreads; far out-of-the-money strikes, distant expirations, and low-interest contracts tend to have the widest spreads. News- or event-driven volatility can widen spreads quickly.[165][123]

For a debit spread, place the limit order at or below the planned debit; for a credit spread, place it at or above the planned credit. If the complete position cannot fill within the stated limits, skip it or reprice only within those limits rather than automatically accepting wider execution friction.

FlashAlpha states that a tight at-the-money spread with only 50 contracts showing may not hold for larger orders; market participants told Traders Magazine that narrower spreads can distribute liquidity across multiple price levels and reduce liquidity at the best bid and offer.[166][162]

Displayed volume, open interest, and spreads may not show all available liquidity. Cboe states that more market participants can be present than appear on a trading screen, while a newer product can display less liquidity and a wider bid-ask spread than an established product.[36]

OptionGeometry reports that Bryzgalova, Pavlova, and Sikorskaya (2023) found retail investors favored cheaper weekly options with an average bid-ask spread of 12.6% and lost money on average.[113]

FlashAlpha states that wing average-spread percentage matters for strategies using out-of-the-money strikes. When wing spreads are 5–8 times at-the-money spreads, the chain has a liquidity cliff at the wings and multi-leg strategies with out-of-the-money legs become expensive to execute.[162]

The framework retains the separate **$0.10-per-share package-width maximum** for a vertical spread. Omen illustrates that, when each leg is $0.10 wide, crossing both legs at entry and exit gives up about $0.20 per share relative to midpoint execution, or $20 per standard 100-share-multiplier contract round trip.[39]

InsiderFinance illustrates natural-price spread entry with a 50/55 spread: paying the ask for the long option and selling the short option at the bid produces a $1.75 debit; a limit closer to the midpoints can save money if filled. Use the live quote and displayed size to plan the order, then use the executed price to establish position cost.[155]

Read together, the discretionary filters require at least 500 open contracts for every traded series, an individual-leg width no greater than $0.05 per share and no greater than 20% of midpoint, and, for a vertical, a displayed package ask-minus-bid width no greater than $0.10 per share.[158][24][27][86][58] Open interest measures established outstanding participation, while current volume, displayed size, and spread width show different aspects of current tradability.

The 1,000-contract floor is a participation screen, while the percentage and package-width limits are execution-friction screens; all must be checked against contemporaneous leg and package quotes.[24][87][162][155] Neither metric guarantees execution, because liquidity and quotes can change rapidly.[121][158][159][160][27][162][164]

1. First, reject any single-leg option or either spread leg below 1,000 open contracts.
2. Second, calculate each leg’s live ask-minus-bid width divided by its midpoint and reject it if it exceeds 20%. [27][86]
3. Third, for a vertical, calculate the displayed package ask-minus-bid width from the natural leg prices and reject it if it exceeds $0.10 per share.
4. Fourth, use a limit order for the complete position; compute entry from buying at the ask and selling at the bid, and do not treat an unfilled order as permission to cross a wider spread. [24][87][162][36][104][155]

A 500-contract floor is the framework’s participation screen, while the $0.05-per-share, 20%-of-midpoint, and $0.10-per-share package-width limits are its execution-friction screens.[158][27][86][58]

The 1,000-contract open-interest floor is twice the cited 500-contract screen, while the 3%-of-midpoint spread cap is 40.0% lower than the cited 5% rough comfort guide. These comparisons quantify that the framework’s proposed liquidity filters are materially stricter on both participation and displayed spread friction.[158][27][162][24]

The actual probability of achieving $200 or $300 in a week remains open because it depends on the realized win rate, average win and loss, number of qualifying trades, execution costs, and correlation among positions. A recorded series of trades using the exact entry, exit, sizing, and liquidity rules would establish those inputs for this framework.
