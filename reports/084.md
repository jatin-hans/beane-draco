# Technical Analysis of Competing Digital Identity Approaches

## Executive summary

No single approach is the best digital identity system because the five solve different parts of the problem. For banking, use a legally recognised identity or trusted attribute issuer for onboarding, FIDO/WebAuthn for phishing-resistant account access, and privacy-preserving VC presentation where reusable evidence is useful.

For healthcare, prefer minimal, purpose-bound attribute sharing and strong authentication, with DIDs/VCs valuable only where governance, issuer trust, revocation, and recovery are in place. For humanitarian aid to stateless populations, do not make access depend on Aadhaar, eIDAS, e-Residency, or any national legal identity, because those systems are population- and jurisdiction-bound; locally accountable proofing plus portable, selectively disclosable credentials is the more suitable direction.

DIDs/VCs can reduce unnecessary disclosure and support portability, but neither creates legal identity, universal interoperability, safe recovery, nor protection from correlation by itself. Aadhaar provides powerful centralised identification and authentication for Indian residents, while eIDAS and e-Residency provide state-backed recognition in their defined scopes, at the cost of greater institutional dependence and potential transaction visibility.

## Digital identity functions and system types

Digital identity is not one capability. A system may need to establish who someone is, accept evidence about a particular attribute, confirm that the same person is returning, or permit an action after those checks. Keeping those functions separate is essential before comparing technical standards with public identity programmes.

### What is identification within digital identity?

Identification is the process of establishing which person or entity is represented by claimed identity attributes—such as a name, date of birth, address, organisational role, or identifier. In identity proofing, the formal process of establishing an identity for a particular context, an identity service provider first collects attributes and evidence; second, validates the evidence by checking that it is genuine and accurate against a reliable source; third, verifies that the evidence belongs to the applicant; and, where required, resolves the records to one unique person. The resolution step is often called de-duplication.[1]

An identifier is not identification. An identifier is information that refers to an entity within a context—an account name, national number, or DID—whereas identification is the process of deciding whom or what that information represents. The W3C describes identification as recognising an entity through information it provides; similarly, eIDAS defines electronic identification as use of electronic person-identification data uniquely representing a natural or legal person.[2][3] A DID is particularly clear on this point: it can refer to any digital, physical, abstract, or concrete resource, and normally does not reveal its subject from the identifier alone.[4]

Identity proofing does not invariably mean obtaining complete legal identity. At Identity Assurance Level 1 (IAL1), the NIST framework requires no link to a real-world identity; at IAL2, it requires high confidence that evidence is genuine, its attributes accurate, and that it relates to the applicant. Humanitarian services may appropriately ask first what information is necessary for protection or aid, rather than automatically demanding full identification. For refugees, assurance can be developed through in-person interviews, local knowledge, and regular validation over time.[1][5]

**Identification establishes the person-to-attributes relationship; it does not merely record a number, display a document, or confirm that someone can operate a device.** This determines what a service can safely rely on: a strong authenticator cannot repair weak or absent identity proofing.[1]

### What is credential presentation within digital identity?

Credential presentation is the runtime act in which a holder shows a digital credential, or cryptographic proof derived from it, to a verifier online or in person. A verifiable digital credential is a cryptographically verifiable digital representation of a credential or attributes—for example, a driving licence, diploma, insurance coverage, or proof of being over 21. The verifier checks the credential’s authenticity and integrity, then assesses whether its claims meet the relying party’s requirements.[6][7]

Presentation follows issuance. An issuer creates and signs a credential; a holder receives and stores it, often in a digital wallet; and a verifier requests and checks it. A W3C Verifiable Presentation (VP) is a holder-created expression of claims that can draw on multiple Verifiable Credentials (VCs). The VC Data Model permits a holder to create and share a VP to demonstrate possession of credentials with particular characteristics.[8][9][10] The W3C VC specifications define the credential and presentation data model, but deliberately do not define the transport protocol by which credentials or presentations move between parties.[9]

Presentation is not the original proofing of the person, and it is not necessarily authentication. Proofing asks whether evidence is genuine and belongs to the applicant. Presentation carries an issuer-backed claim to a verifier. Authentication asks whether the current presenter controls the authenticator or key bound to the credential, rather than being a thief or impostor.[11][1] A verifier therefore still has to assess four things: whether the issuer is trusted for the claim; whether the credential is authentic, current, and not revoked; whether the presenter has the required connection to it; and whether the disclosed information meets the transaction’s need.[11]

Selective disclosure is the presentation of only the claim needed for a transaction—for example, age eligibility rather than a date of birth and home address. It can reduce unnecessary data transfer, but it is not the same as anonymity. An issuer signature, a holder-binding public key, and expiry or revocation metadata can each act as a persistent or correlatable identifier across presentations. Anonymous-credential designs using CL- or BBS-based zero-knowledge proofs can avoid disclosing the issuer signature and holder public key directly.[12][13]

Presentation makes portable identity possible: an unrelated service can accept previously proofed identity evidence without recollecting documents and repeating all checks. It does not compel acceptance; portability depends on a verifier trusting the issuer, format, assurance level, and status information.[1][11]

### What is authentication within digital identity?

Authentication is the process of establishing whether a claimant seeking access possesses and controls an authenticator bound to an account or identity; that account or identity need not have been proofed against a specific real-world person.[1][14] In plain terms, it asks whether the current claimant can use the authenticator previously bound to the account or identity, not whether the claimant’s real-world identity has been established.[1][14] It does this by checking possession and control of an authenticator bound to an account or identity.[1]

An authenticator is the controlled means used to make that proof. It may be a cryptographic key or hardware token—something possessed; a password or PIN—something known; or a biometric—something inherent to the user. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) combines independent factors from at least two categories. Authentication assurance is separate from identity assurance: Authentication Assurance Level concerns confidence in the authentication process, while Identity Assurance Level concerns confidence in the earlier proofing process.[1]

FIDO/WebAuthn illustrates the distinction. WebAuthn defines a web API for creating and using scoped, public-key credentials to strongly authenticate a user. At registration, an authenticator creates a public/private key pair, retains the private key, and supplies the public key to the relying party for association with that service account. At sign-in, the authenticator signs the relying party’s challenge; the relying party verifies the assertion with the stored public key.[14] WebAuthn’s local user verification may use a PIN, password, or biometric, but it does not give the relying party a concrete identification of the user.[14]

WebAuthn credentials are account- and relying-party-specific. They prove control of a private key for a particular service; they do not normally convey a reusable bundle of civil-identity attributes to another service. The browser also checks the relationship between the web origin and the relying-party identifier, which binds use of the credential to the legitimate service domain.[15][16]

**Authentication proves continuing control of an account-bound or identity-bound authenticator, not the truth of the original biographical identity claim.** A bank, hospital, or aid provider may require identity proofing, credential presentation, and authentication together, but each answers a different question.[1]

The technical objective of authentication is resistance to impersonation, including phishing and replay attacks; the policy objective is to select assurance proportionate to the harm if an account is taken over.

### What are standards in this comparison?

A technical standard is a shared specification for making components work consistently: it can define data structures, cryptographic checks, interfaces, protocols, and assurance requirements. It is not itself an institution that enrols people, issues official identity, or decides which issuers are trusted. Digital-ID assurance frameworks likewise assess the reliability of identity proofing, authentication, and federation; they do not select one technology as inherently preferable.[1]

FATF distinguishes proofing and enrolment from authentication and from lifecycle management for loss, theft, expiry and revocation; it treats portability as optional.[112]

W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials are standards building blocks for identifiers and signed claims. DID Core became a W3C Recommendation on 19 July 2022 and recommends wide Web deployment. It specifies DIDs and DID Documents, which can contain public verification methods, authentication relationships, and service endpoints.[17] The VC family provides a data model for issuer claims about a subject and for holder-created presentations; as of May 2025, all but two documents in the VC family were W3C Recommendations.[18][9]

FIDO/WebAuthn is a different standards family. WebAuthn became a W3C standard on 4 March 2019 and specifies public-key authentication by web applications; FIDO Alliance specifications include FIDO2 Client to Authenticator Protocol (CTAP), which connects clients to external authenticators. FIDO is therefore principally an authentication and authenticator-certification ecosystem, not an official-identity issuer.[19][15][1]

Standards can support interoperability, but publication alone does not produce it. The VC Data Model requires compatible transport and trust arrangements outside the model. Likewise, implementations of newer credential-presentation standards may differ in cryptographic options, device binding, and cross-device flows, requiring versioning, feature negotiation, and testing.[9][6]

DID Core permits multiple DID methods: the method determines how a DID is created, resolved, updated, and governed, so privacy and interoperability can differ substantially between implementations. A DID does not require a blockchain, and the VC Data Model neither designates trusted issuers nor guarantees selective disclosure in every credential format.

### What are government-issued digital ID systems in this comparison?

Government-issued digital ID systems are public-authority-backed arrangements for issuing or recognising electronic evidence of official identity. FATF defines official identity as a unique natural person specified by attributes or identifiers that establish uniqueness and are recognised by the state for official purposes. Such systems can combine registries, credentials, biometrics, APIs, and authentication services.[1]

eIDAS is not a single EU-issued identity card. It is the European legal and institutional framework for electronic identification and trust services, intended to support secure cross-border transactions. Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, published on 30 April 2024 and in force from 20 May 2024, established the European Digital Identity Framework and requires every Member State to provide at least one European Digital Identity Wallet for natural and legal persons’ access to public- and private-sector electronic services.[20][21] The wallet can hold person-identification data and electronic attestations of attributes, present them to relying parties, authenticate online and where appropriate offline, and support qualified signatures or seals.[3]

Aadhaar is India’s foundational resident-identity programme. The Aadhaar Act 2016 provides the legal framework for assigning unique identity numbers to people residing in India for targeted financial assistance and other services; the programme began in 2009. Aadhaar establishes identity but does not establish nationality, citizenship, or an entitlement to benefits.[22][23] Its authentication service compares an Aadhaar number plus demographic data, one-time password, biometrics, or combinations of them against the Central Identities Data Repository (CIDR), returning a yes/no result; e-KYC can, with authorised authentication, return identity attributes such as address, date of birth, and gender.[24][25] UIDAI assigns Aadhaar numbers, maintains residents’ biometric and demographic data in the centrally managed CIDR, and provides identification and authentication services.

Because Aadhaar authentication submits the Aadhaar number and demographic attributes, biometrics, or OTP to UIDAI’s CIDR for matching, UIDAI can observe authentication requests even though CIDR’s response to the service provider is only yes or no and returns no personal identity information.[24] Mandatory Aadhaar enrolment for many social-protection schemes has created access barriers, cuts in food-ration access, and starvation deaths linked to those cuts.[26]

Estonia’s e-Residency is a narrower government-issued digital-identity programme for non-Estonians. Introduced in December 2014, it provides remote digital access to services including company formation, banking, payment processing, and taxation. E-residents receive a digital ID card for secure online authentication, digital signatures, encryption, and document transmission.[27] The e-resident’s digital ID is usable only for identification and digital signatures in an electronic environment; it is not a travel document or citizenship status.[28]

These programmes use standards, but they are more than standards: they assign institutional responsibility for proofing, issuance, lifecycle management, legal recognition, and service access. eIDAS coordinates recognition among Member States; Aadhaar is a national foundational identity system for residents; and e-Residency is Estonia’s defined-access digital identity for foreign participants in its digital business environment.[1][22][27]

eIDAS also provides for cross-border recognition, under specified conditions, of national electronic-identification schemes that Member States notify; the Member States, notified schemes, wallet providers, relying parties, and trust-service providers remain distinct actors.

Aadhaar is administered by the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) and assigns a 12-digit number following demographic and biometric enrolment.

e-Residency does not guarantee access to banking, which remains a decision for financial institutions subject to their customer due-diligence obligations.

Under eIDAS, an electronic identification means is a material or immaterial unit containing person-identification data and used to authenticate a person for an online service or, where appropriate, an offline service. An electronic identification scheme is the system under which those means are issued to natural or legal persons, or to natural persons representing them.[3]

An electronic identification scheme is eligible for notification when its electronic-identification means are issued by the notifying Member State, under its mandate, or independently but recognised by it; can access at least one of that state’s public-sector services requiring electronic identification; and meet at least one assurance level. The notifying Member State must ensure that unique person-identification data are attributed to the natural or legal person when the means are issued.[3]

The eIDAS framework has three European assurance levels—low, substantial, and high—whose requirements differ in the reliability, security, and quality of enrolment, management of electronic-identification means, authentication, management, and organisation.[29]

The eIDAS Regulation sets requirements for assurance levels without prescribing the specific technologies national electronic-identification schemes must use.[30]

For an electronic attestation of attributes issued by, or for, a public-sector body responsible for an authentic source, eIDAS requires a qualified certificate supporting the body’s qualified electronic signature or seal. The certificate identifies the body as responsible for the authentic source or designated to act for it, unambiguously represents that source, and identifies the applicable Union or national law; the Member State must ensure that issuing bodies meet reliability and trustworthiness equivalent to qualified trust service providers.[3]

At substantial assurance, two-factor authentication uses two different factors, such as possession of a mobile device and knowledge of a PIN. High assurance adds protection against duplication and tampering, including multi-factor authentication, tamper-resistant hardware tokens for private data or keys, and cryptographic protection of personally identifying information.[31]

UIDAI issues Aadhaar’s 12-digit unique identification number and retains enrollees’ demographic and biometric information, including iris scans, in the national, centralised Central Identities Repository.[32]

Estonia defines an e-resident as an alien for whom it creates a digital identity based on the identity of the person’s country of nationality and issues an e-resident’s digital ID card.[28]

E-residents receive a digital ID card with two PIN numbers for secure digital authentication and digital signatures.[33]

The distinction between the eID means and the eID scheme matters in this comparison: the means is the credential or unit containing person-identification data used for authentication, while the scheme is the issuing system.[3][30]

### Synthesis

The five approaches occupy different layers of the identity landscape. Identification and identity proofing establish the relationship between a person and attributes; credential presentation conveys selected evidence of those attributes; authentication proves continuing control of an account- or credential-bound authenticator.[1] DIDs/VCs standardise identifiers and cryptographically verifiable claims, while FIDO/WebAuthn standardises strong account authentication.[17][18][14] eIDAS, Aadhaar, and e-Residency add public authority, defined populations, issuance processes, and legal or service consequences.[21][22][27]

The resulting design rule is straightforward: use an identity programme or trusted issuer to establish claims where official identity is required; use credential presentation to disclose only the claim a transaction needs; and use strong authentication to protect the account, wallet, or credential at the moment of use. Selective disclosure reduces data transfer, but correlation risks remain unless the presentation design also limits linkable signatures, keys, and status checks.[12][13]

## Technical approaches

Digital identity is not one technology. It is a set of functions: establishing who or what an entity is, issuing evidence about it, proving control of an account or cryptographic key, and deciding whether that evidence is good enough for a particular transaction. The five approaches considered here sit at different points in that chain. Their design histories explain why they should be combined in some settings rather than treated as substitutes.

**Functionally, the approaches are distinct.** W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) provide portable identifiers and signed claims; FIDO/WebAuthn proves control of an account authenticator; eIDAS is an EU legal and technical framework for recognised electronic identity and trust services; Aadhaar establishes and authenticates a resident identity against a central biometric system; and Estonia’s e-Residency gives approved non-residents a state-issued credential for selected Estonian digital services.[17][9][19][34][22][27]

### What do W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials do

A **Decentralized Identifier (DID)** is a Uniform Resource Identifier (URI), meaning a machine-readable identifier, for a subject such as a person, organisation, device, dataset, or abstract entity. It is not inherently a legal name, passport number, or state-recognised identity. DID Core says that a DID identifies a subject determined by its controller and enables that controller to prove control without another party’s permission.[17] The W3C published DID Core v1.0 as a Recommendation—a W3C Web Standard—on 19 July 2022.[17]

A DID has the general structure `did:method:method-specific-identifier`. The **DID method** is the method-specific rulebook that defines how the identifier is created, resolved, updated, and deactivated. Consequently, “DID” does not name one registry, ledger, wallet, or authentication service. It names a common identifier architecture whose operating characteristics depend on the selected method.[35][17] The DID Core specification expressly does not require a particular cryptographic system, blockchain, or registry.

DID methods include `did:key`, `did:web` and registry-backed methods; their privacy and recovery properties differ.[113][114][115]

It can bridge centralised, federated, and decentralised identifier systems; for example, a DID may be created from an identifier already registered with a conventional identity provider.[17][4]

**DID resolution** is the process of turning a DID into a **DID document**: a machine-readable record associated with that identifier. A DID document can express public cryptographic material, verification methods, authentication relationships, key-agreement relationships, and optional service endpoints. In ordinary terms, it provides the information another system may need to check control of an identifier, validate a signature, encrypt to the subject, or locate a related service.[17] A DID document does not itself establish that the controller is the natural person named in a government record. It establishes, at most, control of the keys or methods specified by the DID method.[17][4]

DID Core separates the **DID subject**—the entity being identified—from the **DID controller**—the party authorised to change the DID document. They are often the same, but need not be. A parent can control a child’s DID, a corporation can control a subsidiary’s DID, and a manufacturer can control a DID for a product or device.[17][4] A DID document may also have multiple controllers, either independently authorised or required to act together using multiple signatures or a threshold number of signatures.[17][4] This flexibility is useful for organisations, guardianship, delegated authority, and shared-control arrangements; it also means that a DID alone does not prove that the identified subject personally controls it.

A **Verifiable Credential (VC)** is the claim-bearing layer. The W3C VC Data Model v2.0 defines it as a set of tamper-evident claims and metadata that cryptographically prove who issued it. A credential might say that a person holds a professional licence, is insured, has completed a qualification, or is above an age threshold.[10][9] The W3C VC 2.0 family became a Recommendation on 15 May 2025.[36] A VC must identify an issuer, which can be represented by a DID or a JSON Web Key (JWK), but VCs do not require DIDs and DIDs do not require VCs. They are complementary standards, not an inseparable stack.[10]

The usual VC ecosystem has three principal roles:

- **Issuer:** the organisation that makes and signs claims. It is commonly an authoritative source, such as a government agency, bank, university, employer, healthcare provider, or professional body. The issuer often identity-proofs the recipient before issuing a credential. [7][37]

- **Holder:** the entity controlling the credential, normally through wallet software. The holder may be the credential’s subject, but this is not universal: a guardian or legal representative may hold a credential concerning another person or an organisation. [18][38]

- **Verifier:** the organisation or service that receives a credential or proof and decides whether to rely on it. It checks the cryptographic proof and the credential’s status, but also applies its own business, legal, and risk rules. [7][9][10]

A **Verifiable Presentation (VP)** is what the holder sends to a verifier. It can contain one or more credentials, a subset of credential claims, or a derived cryptographic proof.[18][38] Presentation is therefore different from issuing a credential and different again from deciding whether to grant access.

In a typical flow, first the issuer authenticates or otherwise verifies the holder for issuance; second, the holder requests a service; third, the verifier requests proof; fourth, the holder sends a presentation; and fifth, the verifier verifies it and may grant access.[2]

This architecture distinguishes **identification**, **credential presentation**, and **authentication**. A VC can identify a credential subject through claims and metadata. Presentation delivers those claims or proofs to a verifier. Authentication proves that the presenter controls an authentication key or credential-bound key. The VC model notes that a verifier may authenticate a holder with a signature in a presentation only where the holder is also the credential subject and relevant verification metadata is available.[10] A credential can thus contain a claim about Alice, while another person, device, or legal representative presents it under authorised rules.

A cryptographic proof establishes origin and integrity: it can show that the credential was issued by the claimed issuer and has not been modified. It does not establish that the issuer’s claims are true, that the issuer was authoritative to make them, or that a service must accept them. A verifier must decide whether it trusts the issuer’s identity-proofing process, the credential’s current status, the presenter’s connection to the credential, and the sufficiency of the disclosed claims for the transaction.[11][10] The VC Data Model therefore does not create transitive trust by default; a verifier directly trusts an issuer or it does not.[10]

DIDs can identify issuers, holders, verifiers, and credential subjects. An issuer’s DID can lead to verification material used to validate its VC signatures; a holder’s DID can help prove control during presentation; and a verifier can use a DID for trusted interactions.[17][39] A government authority can identity-proof a person and issue a credential binding that legal identity to a DID. That binding is what gives a self-created identifier legal relevance in a scheme that requires legal identity; creating a DID alone does not do so.[22][40]

The technical standards emerged from an attempt to make identifiers and digital attestations portable across systems. Early DID work arose from mid-2010s collaboration involving the Telehash project and the W3C Web Payments Community Group, was refined through Rebooting the Web of Trust workshops, incubated in the W3C Credentials Community Group, and moved to the W3C Decentralized Identifiers Working Group for standardisation.[4] The W3C’s current DID v1.1 text was published as a Candidate Recommendation Snapshot on 5 March 2026, seeking implementation experience; it is not a final Recommendation.[4]

The standards’ division of labour matters. DID Core standardises syntax, a common data model, core properties, serialised representations, DID operations, and the broad process of resolution.[41] The VC family standardises how claims, metadata, proofs, presentations, and selected status information can be represented.[9][10] Neither standard by itself defines a complete public identity service, a universal wallet, a common trust registry, or a common onboarding process.

The VC data model does not mandate a particular signature scheme or revocation protocol; deployments must choose these separately.[10][116]

Interoperability is an intended outcome, but it is layered. DID Core expects implementations of the same DID method to interoperate, while conforming software need not implement every method. A conforming resolver need only support at least one DID method and one conforming representation.[17][4] VC v2.0 provides a canonical data model and supports mappings from multiple syntaxes, but applications must deterministically translate their selected syntaxes back into that model for validation and comparison.[10] In practice, different DID methods, proof formats, credential types, status mechanisms, and presentation protocols create integration work.

The DID/VC survey identifies competing DID methods, varying cryptographic proofs, different VC formats, and the lack of a single standardised verification protocol as continuing interoperability challenges.[8]

UNTP narrows interoperability choices through a profile covering the VC Data Model, proofs, rendering, vocabularies and DID methods; EBSI adds schemas, accreditation, and DID and issuer registries, with different trust models for VC Data Model versions 1.1 and 2.0.[114][117]

OpenID4VC illustrates the additional protocol layer that often makes credentials usable by applications. It provides protocols for issuing and presenting credentials and can support formats including ISO/IEC 18013-5 mobile driving licences, IETF SD-JWT, and W3C VCs.[42] This is important because a common data model alone does not tell a browser, wallet, bank app, or hospital portal how to request, transmit, secure, and validate a presentation.

DIDs and VCs therefore provide reusable identifier and claim infrastructure, not a substitute for issuer trust, legal recognition, or a complete authentication service. They make it possible to separate the issuer’s signed assertion from later presentation and verification, but the receiving service still decides whether the issuer and evidence meet its requirements.[7][9][10]

DID Core does not prescribe a wallet, private-key backup, or key-recovery process. Recovery consequently depends on the DID method’s controller and update rules and, for credentials, may require an issuer to bind a replacement key and reissue the credential.

### What does FIDO/WebAuthn do within the digital identity landscape?

**FIDO/WebAuthn** is an authentication standard stack. It addresses the question: can this person or device prove control of the cryptographic authenticator registered to this service account? It does not, by itself, answer: what is this person’s civil identity, are they legally resident, are they licensed, or has a government issued a particular attribute.

Web Authentication (WebAuthn) is the W3C browser and platform API for accessing public-key credentials. WebAuthn Level 1 became a W3C standard on 4 March 2019, Level 2 is dated 8 April 2021, and WebAuthn Level 3 became a W3C Recommendation on 25 August 2026. Level 3 remains compatible with Levels 1 and 2.[19][15][43] The broader FIDO family began with the December 2014 Universal Authentication Framework (UAF) and Universal 2nd Factor (U2F).

U2F standardised physical security keys as an additional factor, while UAF supported passwordless or multi-factor authentication on a registered device using local user verification such as a fingerprint, face, voice, or PIN.[44]

In a WebAuthn registration, the relying party—the website or application being accessed—asks an authenticator to create a public-key credential associated with that relying party and the account it controls. The authenticator retains the private key, while the relying party stores the corresponding public key and a credential identifier. At sign-in, the service sends a fresh cryptographic challenge. The authenticator signs a response after the required local interaction, and the relying party validates that signature using its stored public key. This replaces the password’s shared secret with a proof generated by a private key that should not leave the authenticator.

Judgment — The important security property is relying-party binding: a credential registered for `bank.example` cannot produce a valid assertion for a deceptive site at another origin. A phishing page can collect a password and a one-time code, but it cannot turn its own interaction into a valid WebAuthn response for the legitimate site. This is why properly implemented FIDO/WebAuthn is called phishing-resistant authentication rather than merely “strong login.”[45]

CISA places FIDO/WebAuthn in the strongest category of its MFA comparison, identifying resistance to phishing, push bombing, SS7 compromise and SIM swapping.[118][119]

The local biometric or PIN does not normally travel to the relying party as identity evidence. It authorises use of the local authenticator. The website receives a public-key assertion and treats it as proof that the person or process using the account has control of a credential previously registered to that account. Thus, FIDO/WebAuthn authenticates the account relationship defined by the relying party; it does not independently identity-proof the account holder.

FIDO2 combines WebAuthn with the FIDO Client-to-Authenticator Protocol (CTAP), which enables a browser or platform to communicate with an external authenticator such as a roaming security key.[15] The word **passkey** is now commonly used for FIDO credentials.

Apple, Google, and Microsoft announced support for a passwordless FIDO implementation under that name in 2022; the FIDO Alliance expanded the term in 2023 to include both synchronised passkeys and device-bound passkeys.[44] A synchronised passkey can be available across devices through a platform provider’s synchronisation service, while a device-bound passkey remains associated with a particular authenticator. That difference affects recovery, portability, and enterprise control, topics considered in the later recovery section.

WebAuthn is deliberately narrow. It defines interoperable public-key authentication mechanics, including public-key-algorithm requirements intended to promote consistent implementation. For example, it fixes curves for ES256, ES384, ES512, and EdDSA credentials.[14] It does not define civil registration, credential semantics, eligibility rules, a universal identity provider, or legal recognition of an authenticated account across unrelated services.

This narrowness is an advantage in account security. A bank may use WebAuthn to authenticate access to an existing online-banking account without disclosing the customer’s attributes to a third party. A hospital may use it to authenticate an already enrolled clinician or patient portal user. A social-service portal may use it to make a beneficiary’s account harder to take over. In all three cases, the service must separately decide how the account was initially opened and what evidence is needed to associate it with a real person.

The FIDO model also differs from an identity wallet. A VC presentation conveys issuer-signed claims such as age, qualifications, or address. A WebAuthn assertion proves control of an authenticator registered to one relying party. The former can be designed for cross-service presentation; the latter is intentionally scoped to a relying party account. A user may use a FIDO authenticator to unlock a VC wallet or to authenticate to a wallet provider, but that combination joins two layers rather than making them the same technology.

Deployment depends on service support and policy. An application must accept the intended authenticator, enforce it for the relevant accounts, and avoid weaker recovery or fallback paths that nullify the security benefit. Controlled enrolment and secure account recovery remain required.[45] Broad browser and platform support helps: WebAuthn is reported as supported by most browsers and major websites, though the cited estimate that 85% of devices can use WebAuthn or FIDO2 has no stated measurement date or method.[46]

The FIDO model can incorporate privacy-protective choices. Authenticators may use attestation certificates shared across large batches of devices rather than a unique device certificate. WebAuthn cites UAF guidance that at least 100,000 devices share an attestation certificate to produce sufficiently large groups.[14] This limits device-specific attestation as an identifier, but privacy outcomes depend on authenticator configuration and relying-party implementation.

FIDO/WebAuthn therefore solves secure authentication of a returning service account, not portable identity proofing or credential presentation. It is complementary to eIDAS, Aadhaar, e-Residency, and VC systems because each of those can establish or carry identity evidence that a relying party may then protect with FIDO authentication.[1][47]

Authenticator attestation is a manufacturer-signed statement about an authenticator’s provenance or characteristics, not proof of the human user’s identity. It is optional, and relying parties must decide whether the device information it supplies is necessary for their risk policy.

### What does EU eIDAS do within the digital identity landscape?

**eIDAS** means electronic Identification, Authentication and Trust Services. It is an EU legal framework for electronic identification and trust services in electronic transactions, not one EU-wide credential, one wallet application, or one login protocol. Regulation (EU) No 910/2014 has provided the legal foundation since 2014.[48][34] It connects national electronic-identification schemes and establishes legal conditions for services such as electronic signatures and other trust services across the internal market.

The framework was substantially expanded by Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 of 11 April 2024, which amended Regulation 910/2014 to establish the European Digital Identity Framework. It was published on 30 April 2024 and entered into force on 20 May 2024.[49][21] The amendment requires every Member State to provide at least one European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI Wallet) for natural and legal persons to access a range of public- and private-sector electronic services.[21] The European Commission states that Member States must provide wallets by the end of 2026.[50]

An **electronic identification scheme** under eIDAS is the organised system through which a Member State or recognised provider issues electronic-identification means and enables their use. It can include different national designs: smart cards, mobile identities, bank-backed credentials, or other national arrangements. This is why eIDAS is better understood as a recognition and assurance framework above national systems than as a replacement for all national identity infrastructure.

Its initial technical mechanism for cross-border identity recognition was the **eIDAS node**, also called an eIDAS bridge. Nodes connect national infrastructures so authentication points in one Member State can recognise and verify electronic identity proofs from another.[40] The 2019 European Commission evaluation cited in the comparative literature found that only 14% of key public services were available cross-border through notified eIDAS solutions.[22][40] The number shows the difference between a legal interoperability framework and real service deployment: mutual-recognition rules do not instantly make every public service technically integrated.

eIDAS manages that diversity through notification, assurance levels, peer review, and cross-border recognition. In 2022, there were 24 notified eID schemes and 40 electronic-identification means operating across Member States. The cited analysis expected national schemes to continue after wallet deployment, requiring service providers to support several eIDs, including the EUDI Wallet.[48] A 2022 survey found that 29 of 40 notified eID means used at least one single-sign-on standard—SAML, OpenID Connect, or FIDO—while nine used direct authentication; 62% used SAML. eIDAS therefore accommodates different authentication technologies rather than mandating WebAuthn, DIDs, or any single credential format.[30]

The EUDI Wallet adds a holder-facing credential and presentation layer. It is intended to store, manage, and share digital credentials such as person-identification data, identity documents, professional certificates, and business licences.[51] The technical architecture specifies formats and protocols rather than one exclusive credential representation.

The Architecture and Reference Framework identifies ISO/IEC 18013-5 mobile driving licences, W3C VC Data Model v1.1, and IETF SD-JWT-based VCs as suitable formats for person-identification data and electronic attestations of attributes; ICAO Digital Travel Credentials can also be used as attestations.[47]

OpenID4VC is a central protocol choice. It supplies issuance and presentation protocols and allows a wallet ecosystem to support W3C VCs, SD-JWT credentials, and ISO mobile driving-licence formats.[42] The eIDAS wallet process can use OpenID Connect extensions for credential issuance and presentation, including OpenID Connect for Credential Issuance and OpenID Connect for Credential Presentation.[48] This makes eIDAS an example of legal and technical layering: the Regulation sets rights, obligations, assurance, and recognition; national authorities and wallet providers implement credentials; and technical standards carry them between wallets and relying parties.

The relationship between eIDAS and DIDs requires precision. The EUDI Wallet can handle W3C VC-formatted credentials, and some analyses describe wallet ecosystems associating wallets with DIDs.[13][37] However, one technical mapping concludes that eIDAS does not support DIDs as part of its described stack.[48] In May 2026, the European Commission was discussing a CEN specification that would build on W3C DID work for use in the eIDAS ecosystem, but had reached no conclusion on reference in an Implementing Act.[52] Accordingly, W3C VCs are already one recognised technical option in the wallet architecture, while DID integration remains a more unsettled technical and regulatory question.

eIDAS is also distinct from a conventional federation. In a federation, an identity provider authenticates a user to relying parties. In the wallet model, the user can hold credentials issued by multiple issuers and present selected evidence to a verifier. The legal framework supplies common rules around identity assurance and trust services, but it does not make every credential issuer automatically trusted for every claim. A bank, healthcare provider, or public authority still applies its own sector-specific legal and operational rules.

eIDAS therefore coordinates nationally rooted identities and trust services across borders; the EUDI Wallet extends that framework into portable, user-mediated credential presentation. Its purpose is not to create a single EU civil registry or displace every national eID, but to make recognised national identity evidence and qualified attestations usable across European services.[21][48][47]

eIDAS electronic-identification schemes authenticate users to services at three assurance levels: low, substantial, and high.[48][1]

Its core trust services are electronic signatures, electronic seals, electronic time stamps, electronic registered delivery services, and website authentication.[22]

Its three electronic-identification assurance levels are “low, substantial and high.”[40]

### What does India’s Aadhaar do within the digital identity landscape?

**Aadhaar** is India’s state-administered foundational identity programme for residents. It began as a project in 2009 and gained a statutory framework through the Aadhaar Act in 2016. The Act’s stated purpose is targeted delivery of financial assistance and other services to individuals residing in India through assignment of unique identity numbers.[22] The Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) administers the programme. Aadhaar assigns a unique 12-digit number linked to demographic information and biometric information including fingerprints and iris scans.[53]

Aadhaar’s primary technical task is **deduplication**: determining whether an enrollee is already represented in the system, so the programme can issue one unique identifier per resident. It is therefore a foundational identity system: it seeks to establish an authoritative, population-scale identity record that other public and private services can query or use. This is different from WebAuthn, which protects a service account, and from a VC, which carries an issuer’s claim for later presentation.

At enrolment, Aadhaar binds biographic and biometric information to the issued number. At later use, **Aadhaar authentication** compares submitted demographic data, biometric data, or other specified factors against the Central Identities Data Repository. The system’s function is normally to respond whether the presented information matches the central record. This is authentication against a central identity repository, not a portable credential presentation model in which a holder independently gives an issuer-signed attribute to many verifiers.

The scale of the programme is central to its role. As of 2016, a source reported that Aadhaar had surpassed one billion enrollees, including 97% of adult Indians and 67% of children.[32] A government press note reports Aadhaar authentication in more than 98% of public food-grain distribution and use across more than 3,100 Direct Benefit Transfer schemes and over 360 public services.[54] Cumulative Aadhaar e-KYC transactions reached 2,393 crore as of 30 April 2025, according to the same government source.[54]

Aadhaar is not proof of citizenship. Its statutory and operational focus is resident identity. Nor does successful Aadhaar authentication itself establish that someone qualifies for a benefit, may open a particular financial product, is entitled to health treatment, or meets a service provider’s other legal conditions. It supplies a foundational identity and an authentication result that another programme can use in its own eligibility or customer-due-diligence process.

Aadhaar also sits within a wider Indian digital-public-infrastructure ecosystem. Digital signatures and payments were built on the Aadhaar foundation as part of India Stack.[55] Document-sharing services may use Aadhaar-linked identity proofing, but they should not be conflated with Aadhaar’s core centralised enrolment, uniqueness, and authentication functions. In other words, Aadhaar provides a shared population identity layer; services above it may provide documents, payments, signatures, benefits, or sector-specific authorisation.

The system was designed partly to reduce conventional-document barriers. A survey cited in reporting found that Aadhaar was the first identity document for 8% of adults, 15% of homeless respondents, and 14% of trans respondents.[56] This shows that a state foundational-ID programme can reach some people who previously had no formal document.

Yet access remains conditional on the state’s enrolment processes and the ability to establish a unique record. In-person enrolment, data quality, connectivity, operator practices, and biometric matching are therefore part of the identity architecture, not incidental implementation details.

The historical legal position on Aadhaar and banking also needs careful separation from its technical capabilities. The Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment, as described in FATF guidance, left Aadhaar mandatory for tax purposes and certain publicly funded benefits but removed mandatory Aadhaar use for opening a bank account or obtaining a mobile number. For customer due diligence, use became voluntary and subject to informed consent.[1] Aadhaar can therefore be used in financial-service workflows where law and provider policy permit, but it is not a universal legal requirement for private-sector banking.

Aadhaar’s distinctive contribution is state-run, biometric-backed foundational identification at population scale, followed by centralised authentication of a resident record. It can lower identity-document barriers for some residents, but it remains a state-recognition and enrolment system rather than a generic authentication standard or holder-controlled credential protocol.[22][53][56]

Aadhaar supports multiple online and offline verification routes that disclose different information to a requesting party. Online authentication can use biometric matching or a one-time password (OTP), while the m-Aadhaar application generates a Virtual ID linked to, but different from, the Aadhaar number; either identifier can be authenticated online against the Aadhaar database or offline using a QR code. Requesting entities must obtain the user’s consent and explain the authentication purpose and the resulting service or information, and basic biometric data cannot be shared with another party.[1][53][22]

Aadhaar biometric requests must originate from registered devices and be encrypted; requesting entities may not store, share, publish or retain core biometric data.[120][121]

Aadhaar’s offline-verification regulations require notice, consent and notice of alternatives, and prohibit denial solely because someone refuses or cannot authenticate when a viable alternative exists.[120]

Aadhaar has five authentication types: matching an Aadhaar number with demographic attributes such as name, address, and date of birth; an OTP sent to the registered mobile number or email address in CIDR; a fingerprint or iris biometric; OTP combined with fingerprint or iris; and OTP, fingerprint, and iris together.[24][25]

In Aadhaar authentication, the Aadhaar number and demographic attributes, biometrics, or OTP are submitted to CIDR for matching.[24] CIDR responds Yes or No and returns no personal identity information as part of that response.[24]

UIDAI’s authentication interface can submit a Virtual ID or UID Token instead of the Aadhaar number, together with demographic information, an OTP, biometrics or combined factors; its yes-or-no response is normally digitally signed.[121][122]

The Aadhaar authentication result is normally a digitally signed yes-or-no response.[121][122]

Every Aadhaar authentication mode requires the Aadhaar number and specified UIDAI input parameters, reducing authentication to a 1:1 match against the claimed record.[57]

### What does Estonia’s e-Residency do within the digital identity landscape?

Estonia’s **e-Residency** is a government-issued, transnational digital-identity programme for non-residents. It launched in 2014.[58][33] It gives foreign nationals an opportunity to apply for an e-resident digital identity card that provides secure access to selected electronic services supplied by Estonian public- and private-sector organisations.[58] Its central purpose is remote participation in Estonia’s digital business and administrative environment, rather than creation of a new nationality, immigration status, or universal international identity.

The programme gives non-Estonians remote digital access to services including company formation, banking-related services, payment processing, and taxation. E-residents receive a digital ID card that can be used to authenticate online and digitally sign, encrypt, and send documents.[27] For operating a company, the official programme describes signing documents digitally, filing reports online, outsourcing administration, and engaging with its e-resident business community.[59] Since December 2014, the credential has allowed foreigners to establish and manage an Estonian company while living abroad.[60]

e-Residency is built on Estonia’s established public-key infrastructure (PKI). In **public-key infrastructure**, a private key—kept under the user’s control—creates digital signatures, while the matching public key lets other parties verify them. Estonia’s national e-ID infrastructure uses a private key stored on the electronic chip of an identity card, with public-key information retrievable through public directories.[22] Its resident e-ID card and DigiDoc software support online authentication using PIN1, digital signatures using PIN2, encryption, data exchange, and access to public- and private-sector services.[22] E-residency applies this established technical model to an approved non-resident population.

The e-resident credential therefore performs several functions together:

- **Identification:** Estonia issues a state-backed digital identity credential to an approved foreign applicant. The programme’s process combines online application with identity checks and physical collection. From May 2015, applicants could apply online but, after approval, had to attend one face-to-face meeting to receive the card. [61]

- **Authentication:** the card supports secure online authentication to services that accept it. This is stronger than merely uploading an identity-document image because the transaction can require proof using the credential’s cryptographic keys and PINs. [27][22]

- **Electronic signature:** the credential supports legally significant digital signing, encryption, and document transmission. Estonia’s established e-signature regime treats qualified electronic signatures as equivalent to handwritten signatures in document management across public and private sectors. [62]

The programme does not confer residence, citizenship, a passport, visa rights, travel-document rights, or tax residence. A 2018 report stated that e-residency provides a government-issued digital ID and access to Estonian banking and virtual-business activity, but not legal residency or citizenship.[63] This boundary is essential: e-Residency is digital access to an Estonian service environment, not membership in the Estonian polity.

Its entry requirements also define who can use it. A historical description of the process states that applicants identify themselves with a passport, provide biometric data, pass a background check, and collect the credential at a foreign embassy.[64] This makes e-Residency unsuitable as a direct identity route for people who lack a recognised passport or cannot reach a collection point. Its technical portability across borders does not eliminate the programme’s state-document and vetting prerequisites.

The programme has grown beyond a narrow pilot. Its official site reported 140,000-plus e-residents as at May 2025; a January 2026 account reported more than 134,000 e-residents and more than 39,000 companies established by them.[27][65] The different counts reflect different reporting dates and source bases, rather than a single fixed current total.

The official homepage also reported 142,332 e-residents from more than 170 countries and 43,024 companies founded by e-residents, without a stated measurement date.[59]

E-residency’s practical value depends on service acceptance. The programme enables a credentialed person to authenticate and sign, but banks, payment firms, registries, and other private providers retain their own customer-acceptance, anti-money-laundering, and risk rules. A Reuters report from 2019 found that some e-resident-created companies held accounts with LHV, while only a small number of e-residents opened personal Estonian bank accounts; it also noted no public count of e-residents banking in Estonia.[66] The distinction reinforces that digital identity access and financial-service acceptance are separate decisions.

**e-Residency is therefore a state-issued non-resident credential for remote participation in Estonia’s digital and business ecosystem, combining identification, authentication, and electronic signing.** Its value lies in access to connected Estonian services, while its boundaries—passport-based application, background checking, physical credential collection, and provider discretion—mean it is not a borderless substitute for legal residence or civil identity.[27][64][63]

### What in the development and current design of W3C Decentralized

The systems differ because they begin from different problems.

DID and VC work emerged from web-standardisation and decentralised-identity efforts. Their starting problem was how to create portable identifiers and cryptographically verifiable claims without requiring every interaction to run through one identity provider.[17][9][10] Their model separates issuer, holder, and verifier. Trust is distributed: issuers attest to claims; holders store and present them; verifiers check proofs and decide which issuers to trust.[7][10]

FIDO/WebAuthn emerged from the password and phishing problem. Its design centres on a public-key credential bound to one relying party and account. It is deliberately not a civil-identity registry or claims format, because its security objective is to ensure that a sign-in assertion cannot be replayed at a different service or captured as a reusable shared secret.[44][14][45]

eIDAS emerged from the EU internal-market problem: national identity systems, electronic signatures, and trust services needed legal recognition across Member States without forcing all states to abandon their existing schemes. Its solution is a regulatory architecture that combines national issuance, notified schemes, assurance levels, technical bridges, and mutual recognition.[34][40][48] The EUDI Wallet expands that architecture to user-mediated credential storage and presentation, but retains state and regulated trust infrastructure.[21][47]

Aadhaar emerged from the population-scale foundational-ID and service-delivery problem. Its design uses central enrolment, demographic and biometric data, deduplication, a unique number, and central authentication.[22][53] Its core trust relationship is therefore between the state identity authority, the central repository, enrolment operators, authentication users, and the resident.

This is structurally different from a DID/VC ecosystem, where a verifier may validate a credential without contacting the original issuer at presentation time.[8][2]

e-Residency emerged from Estonia’s attempt to extend its pre-existing digital-state and business infrastructure to approved non-residents. It uses state issuance, PKI-backed authentication, and digital signatures, but does not try to establish a new global civil registry.[58][27][22] Its trust arrangement remains state-mediated: Estonia decides who receives the credential and connected services decide whether to accept it.

For people without state-recognised identity, this division of functions is decisive. A person may create a DID or hold a self-asserted credential without possessing a passport, but a high-assurance bank, government, or healthcare service may require claims from an issuer it recognises. The VC standard itself permits unsigned self-asserted credentials, but they are not verifiable because authorship is unknown or cannot be trusted.[10] A FIDO authenticator can secure an account once one exists, but does not create the official identity evidence required to open a regulated account.

An eIDAS wallet or e-Residency credential requires issuance within a state-recognised framework. Aadhaar can reduce document barriers for eligible residents, but it still depends on programme enrolment and uniqueness determination.[56][22]

Judgment — Stateless-population design must therefore start with identity proofing and recognition policy, not with a choice of wallet or authenticator. A humanitarian organisation, government, or mandated international body must decide what alternative evidence, community attestation, interview process, or continuing validation it will recognise. DIDs, VCs, and FIDO can make resulting identities and accounts more portable or secure; they cannot independently confer the institutional recognition that a regulated or public service demands.

### Synthesis

The five approaches solve different layers of the identity problem. DIDs and VCs separate portable identifiers and issuer-backed claims from the service that later verifies them; FIDO/WebAuthn secures account access with relying-party-specific public-key authentication; eIDAS creates legal and technical recognition across national European systems; Aadhaar creates and authenticates a central resident identity record; and e-Residency extends Estonia’s established PKI-backed e-government model to approved non-residents.[9][45][21][22][27]

The pattern is clear: standards can make credentials, identifiers, and authenticators interoperable, but state recognition and issuer trust remain institutional decisions. This makes the systems complementary. A high-assurance service may combine state or regulated proofing, VC presentation, and FIDO authentication; none of those layers alone supplies all three functions.[7][9][47][10]

## Privacy

Privacy in digital identity has two distinct parts: **data minimisation**, meaning disclose only what a transaction needs, and **unlinkability**, meaning prevent separate transactions from being connected to the same person. A system can do well on the first while failing on the second. It can also protect the contents of a credential while still exposing who used it, where, and when.

Judgment — Privacy is therefore not a property of an identity label such as decentralized, biometric, or government-issued. It is an outcome of identifier design, credential format, network and status checks, wallet or authenticator software, relying-party data practices, and applicable law.

### What is the approach to privacy of W3C Decentralized Identifiers

W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) are designed to let an entity control how much information it reveals. DID Core names privacy as a design goal, including minimal, selective, and progressive disclosure. A DID and its DID document do not inherently contain personal data, and the specification strongly advises non-public entities not to publish personal data in DID documents. For public registries, it instead envisages private, peer-to-peer exchange of personal data through channels secured using DID-document verification methods.[17]

Verifiable Credentials (VCs) extend that approach from an identifier to claims issued by an issuer and held by a person or organisation. A holder can create a **verifiable presentation**—a proof tailored to a verifier’s request—and disclose a selected claim rather than an entire credential. Selective disclosure can show, for example, that a person meets an age threshold without revealing a birth date; a **zero-knowledge proof** (ZKP) can prove the condition without revealing the underlying value at all. W3C VC Data Model 2.0 encourages issuers to use abstract claims such as `ageOver`, atomise information into smaller claims, and use securing mechanisms that support selective disclosure.[22][2][10]

A VC can be verified without contacting its issuer at presentation time, which can stop an issuer learning where its credential has been used. W3C’s model nevertheless makes this conditional: credential-status checking must not “phone home” to the issuer for each presentation, because that would notify the issuer of verifier interest in a particular holder. Status lists that bundle many credentials are intended to provide more group privacy than a separate status URL for every credential.[8][10][9]

An online credential-status check that contacts the issuer can reveal a verifier’s interest in a holder. Accumulators, holder-held witnesses, updates and validity credentials are alternative status designs that can reduce that observation, while assigning additional work to issuers, wallets or verifiers.[123]

DIDs are not inherently anonymous. A globally unambiguous DID can be used to correlate a person’s transactions. DID Core’s principal mitigation is the **pairwise DID**: a different pseudonymous DID for each relationship. That protection fails if supposedly separate DID documents reuse keys, distinctive service endpoints, or other metadata. Public DID documents, immutable registry histories, and endpoint correlation can also support traffic analysis and inference about relationships.[17]

The same limitation applies to VCs. Selective disclosure reduces what a verifier receives, but does not stop a verifier requesting unnecessary information, a deceptive portal obtaining a presentation, or a recipient repurposing disclosed data. Long-lived identifiers, signatures, holder-binding keys, revocation metadata, IP addresses, browser fingerprints, and location data can all reintroduce correlation. W3C explicitly notes that modern cryptography cannot by itself defeat long-lived identifiers and browser tracking; the remaining control is often policy and enforcement.[47][10]

**The DID/VC approach is privacy-capable rather than privacy-guaranteeing:** it can minimize disclosure and issuer observation, but practical privacy depends on pairwise or rotating identifiers, unlinkable proof formats, privacy-preserving status checks, minimal DID documents, and wallet software that does not collect telemetry.[17][18][10]

### What is the approach to privacy of FIDO/WebAuthn?

FIDO/WebAuthn protects privacy principally by limiting authentication to a particular **relying party** (RP), meaning the website or application to which a person signs in. A WebAuthn credential is scoped to that RP’s identifier—normally its domain—and cannot be used by another RP. The standard says RPs cannot detect the properties, or even the existence, of credentials created for other RPs.[14]

The authentication event proves control of a service-specific private key. The private key is bound to the authenticator and is not exposed to the server or even normally to the authenticator owner. Where biometric user verification is used, the biometric comparison occurs locally; the RP receives a signed assertion that verification succeeded, not the fingerprint or face template.[14]

This design prevents a single shared login secret from becoming a cross-service tracking identifier. WebAuthn specifies that credential IDs and public keys for different RPs should not be correlatable as belonging to the same user. Two colluding services therefore need additional information—such as a reused email address or username—to link the accounts. The user handle provided to the authenticator must be an opaque value and must not contain a username, email address, or an unsalted hash of either.[14]

**Attestation**—the signed information about an authenticator at registration—is the principal privacy exception. With the default `none` attestation preference, potentially identifying attestation information is replaced with non-identifying versions or an empty attestation statement. Direct attestation reveals authenticator information, while enterprise attestation may include uniquely identifying information for managed organisational deployments.[14]

WebAuthn’s anti-correlation design has implementation limits. The standard recommends per-credential signature counters because a counter shared across RPs may become a correlation handle. It also warns that an RP can leak whether a named person has an account through different responses or through exposed credential IDs in an `allowCredentials` list.[14]

A 2022 study of eight selected FIDO Level 1-certified hardware authenticators found two vulnerable to a timing attack that could link accounts across services: HyperFIDO Titanium Pro showed an average timing difference of 10 milliseconds and Feitian K26 showed 2 milliseconds. The study found that browser-side mitigation—deduplicating and limiting credential-handle lists—mitigated the tested Chrome attack in the canary release tested after 5 October 2021.[72]

FIDO/WebAuthn therefore minimizes authentication-data disclosure, not observation of a service’s own login events: per-RP keys and local biometrics are strong protections against cross-service linkage, while reused account identifiers, identifying attestation, global counters, and faulty RP or authenticator implementations can reopen correlation paths.[14][72]

### What is the approach to privacy of EU eIDAS?

eIDAS is an EU legal and interoperability framework, not one central identity database. Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, adopted on 11 April 2024, amended the original eIDAS Regulation to establish the European Digital Identity Framework and the European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDIW). Personal-data processing under the framework remains subject to EU data-protection law, including data minimisation, purpose limitation, and data protection by design and by default.[49][3][13]

The EUDIW model gives a user control to request, obtain, select, combine, store, delete, share, and present person-identification data and electronic attestations of attributes. It must support selective disclosure, including for online and, where appropriate, offline authentication. Relying parties are expected to state what data they request and why, including safeguards relevant to special-category data such as health data.[3]

The amended framework permits user-chosen pseudonyms where Union or national law does not require legal identification. It requires wallet providers not to collect wallet-use information unnecessary for providing wallet services, not to combine wallet data with other services’ data without the user’s express request, and to keep wallet-related personal data logically separate. Users must receive a dashboard and transaction log showing relying parties, requested and shared data, dates, and counterparts; they can request erasure by an RP and report suspicious requests to a data-protection authority.[3]

The wallet’s user-facing transaction log is exportable and records relying parties, shared attributes and data types; the Spanish data-protection authority notes that access to such records by providers or outsiders could make the log a surveillance asset.[124]

The legal rules also seek to restrict transaction visibility. Wallet providers should achieve **unobservability**, meaning they do not collect data or gain insight into user transactions. Attribute-attestation providers must not obtain information about use of their attestations, and intermediaries acting for RPs must not store transaction content.[3]

A wallet’s transaction log can help a user make a complaint about a relying party’s request, but the log itself can become a surveillance asset if exposed.[124]

In the Spanish data-protection authority’s interpretation, wallet providers should neither collect transaction data nor see transaction details.[124]

The original eIDAS approach was criticised because it required disclosure of the full mandatory set of identification attributes even where only a subset was necessary. EUDIW’s selective-disclosure requirement is a material improvement, but disclosure minimisation is not the same as unlinkability. Salted hashes, issuer signatures, holder-binding public keys, expiry information, and revocation metadata can remain persistent fingerprints that colluding RPs or attribute providers use to link presentations.[13]

The Regulation says Member States should integrate ZKPs, which can establish that a statement is true without disclosing the underlying identity data. Their use is not mandatory in the cited eIDAS architecture and reference framework, however. Anonymous-credential approaches based on CL or BBS cryptography can hide signature and holder-key identifiers, but the cited analysis identifies a current trade-off: these schemes are not implemented in the dedicated cryptographic hardware used by consumer phones for high-assurance wallet binding.[3][13]

**eIDAS 2.0 provides the strongest explicit legal privacy controls in this comparison, but not anonymity by default:** it reduces routine provider observation and full-record disclosure, while technical metadata and cross-party collusion remain capable of producing linkability unless implementations adopt stronger unlinkable presentations.[3][13][73]

### What is the approach to privacy of India’s Aadhaar?

Aadhaar is a state-run foundational identity system for Indian residents, created for delivery of financial assistance, subsidies, benefits, and other services through a unique identity number. It began as a project in 2009 and received its statutory framework in the Aadhaar Act 2016. UIDAI limited collection to core demographic information—name, gender, age, and address—with mobile number and email optional; it resisted collecting fields such as disability and blood group that it did not regard as necessary to the central identity purpose.[22][25]

Its privacy architecture is nevertheless structurally centralised. Aadhaar uses a single, persistent identifier and a Central Identities Data Repository (CIDR). A comparative analysis identifies the resulting risk plainly: use of one global identifier across online transactions permits CIDR to correlate activity and maintain a history of who accessed what. Centralisation also concentrates breach risk; Privacy International listed almost 40 isolated Aadhaar-related breaches between February 2017 and May 2018, involving demographic data and, in some cases, pregnancy, religion, caste, or bank details.[22][26]

UIDAI introduced offline verification in 2018 through a digitally signed demographic-data QR code. This permits local verification without connecting to the central database, reducing disclosure created by an online CIDR query in that transaction. It is an alternative verification channel, not a replacement for Aadhaar’s central identity infrastructure.[23]

A holder-generated, digitally signed offline Aadhaar e-KYC XML file provides another offline verification route.[121][122]

Legal limits matter. The Supreme Court’s 2018 ruling upheld Aadhaar for government subsidies and welfare programmes but rejected mandatory use for private services including mobile connections and banking. The July 2019 amendment kept Aadhaar mandatory for tax purposes and Consolidated Fund-financed benefits, subsidies, and services, while making use for customer due diligence voluntary and dependent on informed consent.[74][1]

**Aadhaar’s privacy trade-off is efficient, broad identity verification against a central correlation capability:** data minimisation at enrolment and offline QR verification reduce some collection and query exposure, but a universal identifier and central repository make cross-domain linkage a built-in risk rather than an exceptional implementation error.[25][22][23]

### What is the approach to privacy of Estonia’s e-Residency?

Judgment — Estonia’s e-Residency is a government-issued digital identity programme for non-residents, built around a state-recognised identity and public-key credentials rather than anonymous or pairwise identifiers. Its privacy model is consequently governed identification: it prioritises accountable access to Estonian digital services over hiding the user’s legal identity from those services.

Estonia’s broader e-ID design uses smart-card chips containing private keys for online identification, authentication, and digital signatures; Estonia treats electronic identity documents as accepted on the same basis as physical documents. This makes the private key a protected authenticator rather than a centrally shared password, but it does not turn the legal identity into a pseudonym.[22]

Privacy in this model depends on operational controls as well as cryptographically authenticated identification.[22] In 2011, Estonia distributed 120,000 faulty ID cards that could be used by anyone physically holding them without a PIN; the affected cards were replaced.[27] A separate incident exposed more than 280,000 identity photos, names, and personal ID codes, although those data alone were insufficient to access e-state services.[75]

**e-Residency is thus privacy-protective mainly through secure authentication, access control, and regulated handling of a durable state identity, rather than through technical unlinkability:** a persistent government identity supports reliable use across services, while its reuse creates a correlation risk that pairwise DID or per-RP WebAuthn designs are specifically intended to reduce.

The My e-Residency portal processes the e-resident’s ID code and email address for authentication and login.[76]

### Synthesis

The five approaches solve different privacy problems. DID/VC systems offer the widest potential for minimisation and unlinkability, but only when issuers, wallets, identifiers, status checks, and verifiers use privacy-preserving designs. FIDO/WebAuthn offers strong privacy for authentication through per-service keys and local biometrics, but it does not minimise the identity data an application has already collected. eIDAS 2.0 combines wallet controls with legally binding provider duties and selective disclosure, yet presentation metadata may still be linkable. Aadhaar and e-Residency prioritise durable, state-recognised identity; Aadhaar’s central repository creates the greatest structural correlation risk, while Estonia’s model relies more on secure credential operation and governance than pseudonymity.[17][14][3][13][22]

## Interoperability

Interoperability is the ability of different identity systems to exchange and correctly use identification or authentication information. It has technical layers—formats, protocols, cryptographic keys and software interfaces—but also legal and organisational layers: a service must recognise the issuer and accept the assurance behind a credential. A shared format alone therefore does not make a credential usable everywhere.

### What is the approach to interoperability of W3C Decentralized Identifiers

W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) take an open-standards approach. DID Core specifies shared identifier syntax, a common data model, core properties, serialised representations, operations and DID resolution—the process of obtaining the technical document associated with a DID. It permits JSON and JSON-LD representations and allows other representations if they express the common model; conversion is intended to be deterministic, bi-directional and lossless.[41][17]

A DID Document is the machine-readable record reached through resolution. It can express public verification keys, authentication relationships and service endpoints. The DID method—the middle component in an identifier such as `did:example:abc`—defines the method-specific rules for creating, resolving, updating and deactivating that DID. DID Core deliberately does not prescribe a ledger, database, cryptographic system or central operator. It can therefore bridge identifiers rooted in centralised, federated or decentralised systems.[17][4]

That flexibility sets a real boundary. A conforming resolver need implement only one DID method, and the W3C expects interoperability among implementations of the *same* method, not automatic interoperability among all methods. At the 2022 DID Core Recommendation’s publication, there were 103 experimental DID-method specifications, 32 experimental method-driver implementations and 46 implementations submitted to the conformance test suite.[17] Unregistered extensions also make interoperability with the wider ecosystem less reliable even where two parties have privately agreed to use them.[4]

VCs standardise the credential-data layer: an issuer signs claims, a holder stores them and presents them, and a verifier checks signature, issuer key, status and the suitability of claims. The VC Data Model uses shared vocabularies, JSON-LD contexts and schemas to support syntactic and semantic interoperability—both exchanging fields and interpreting terms consistently. It allows multiple securing mechanisms and other serialisations, but each must be mapped deterministically to the canonical data model for processing.[2][9][10]

**DIDs and VCs provide a shared technical vocabulary, not a single universal identity network.** End-to-end portability depends on compatible DID methods, credential formats, cryptographic proof suites, wallet capabilities and issuance/presentation protocols such as OpenID4VCI and OpenID4VP.[8][2]

A verifier also needs a trust framework: it must obtain the issuer’s public key, decide whether that issuer is trusted for the claimed fact, confirm the credential is current and unrevoked, establish the presenter’s connection to it where required, and decide whether the disclosed claims meet its own requirements.[7][11] Thus, DID/VC interoperability makes cross-system credential exchange possible; it does not compel a bank, hospital or government agency to accept a credential.

### What is the approach to interoperability of FIDO/WebAuthn?

FIDO/WebAuthn standardises authentication, not portable civil identity or reusable attribute credentials. FIDO2 combines Web Authentication (WebAuthn), the W3C web and application programming interface used by browsers and applications, with the Client-to-Authenticator Protocol (CTAP). WebAuthn defines how a relying party—the website or application being accessed—asks for a public-key authentication ceremony. CTAP defines how an external or roaming authenticator, such as a security key or smartphone, communicates with browsers and operating systems over USB, NFC or Bluetooth Low Energy.[43][77][78]

This creates interoperability at the authenticator–client–relying-party layer. A person can independently obtain a conforming authenticator and use it with multiple relying parties; the relying party need not issue its own hardware. FIDO’s specifications, certification programme and browser/platform support are designed to enable cross-vendor operation.[14][1][44] WebAuthn also fixes details that help implementations interpret cryptographic keys consistently, including specified elliptic curves for ES256, ES384, ES512 and EdDSA credential keys.[14]

The boundary is deliberate: WebAuthn credentials are scoped to a relying-party identifier (RP ID), normally the web origin’s effective domain. The authenticator verifies that the RP ID for an authentication request exactly matches the RP ID to which the credential is scoped.[14] A passkey used for one unrelated website is therefore not a portable login credential for another site, even if the same authenticator is used for both. Each service retains its own account registration, account-recovery policy and identity-proofing decision.

There are controlled exceptions and compatibility limits. Related Origin Requests allow a passkey for one RP ID to work across a small declared set of related domains. CTAP1 supports legacy U2F security keys in FIDO2-enabled environments. Yet WebAuthn Level 2 features can fail with older user agents, and optional extensions may be ignored by clients or authenticators; relying parties must detect capabilities and handle that outcome.[43][14] Passkey portability across credential-manager ecosystems has also been a limitation. FIDO’s Credential Exchange Format and Credential Exchange Protocol address it, but both were labelled Working Draft in the supplied specification overview.[79][80]

**FIDO/WebAuthn achieves substantial interoperability for phishing-resistant authentication, not federated identity or reusable identity claims.** It enables one authenticator to serve many services, while origin binding intentionally blocks reuse at unrelated services and protects that security boundary.[14][43]

### What is the approach to interoperability of EU eIDAS?

eIDAS is a European Union legal and technical framework, rather than one EU-issued identity credential. The original 2014 Regulation enabled mutual recognition of notified national electronic-identification schemes and trust services across the Union. Its technical mechanism included eIDAS nodes, also called bridges, that connect national infrastructures so authentication points can recognise and verify electronic identity proofs or identity-related transactions from another Member State.[81][40]

The framework’s interoperability model is federation with legal recognition. National schemes remain distinct, but notification, assurance rules, peer review and cross-border nodes provide a route for one country’s scheme to be recognised in another. A 2022 survey of 40 notified eID means found that 29 used at least one single-sign-on standard—SAML, OpenID Connect or FIDO—while nine used direct authentication; 62% used SAML. This shows that eIDAS interoperates across diverse national technical stacks rather than imposing one login protocol.[3][30]

The revised European Digital Identity Framework, established by Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, extends this approach through European Digital Identity Wallets (EUDI Wallets). Member States must provide wallets by the end of 2026. The Regulation requires common protocols and interfaces for issuance, relying-party requests and validation, and online and, where appropriate, offline presentation of person-identification data and electronic attestations of attributes.[50][82] It also requires a common Union technical architecture, reference framework, standards and technical references.[3]

The EUDI wallet architecture supports ISO/IEC 18013-5, ISO/IEC 23220-2 and SD-JWT VCs, with W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 as an optional format; interoperable use still requires common profiles and conformance testing.[116][125][126]

Member State registration and access or registration certificates structure trust for EUDI providers and relying parties. Person-identification data, qualified attestations and public-body attestations use X.509 public-key infrastructure, while non-qualified attestations may use other trust models.[127]

The wallet framework deliberately supports several credential formats rather than one exclusive format: ISO/IEC 18013-5 mobile driving licences, W3C VC Data Model v1.1 and SD-JWT-based VCs are specified as suitable formats for person-identification data or electronic attestations of attributes. OpenID4VC provides the protocol layer for issuing and presenting credentials and can handle these different formats.[47][42][83] This is an important connection to the DID/VC ecosystem, but it does not mean that DIDs are mandatory across eIDAS: a 2024 mapping paper states that the eIDAS stack it examined did not support DIDs.[48]

The EUDI architecture also includes ISO/IEC 23220-2 among its credential formats and permits W3C VC Data Model 2.0 for non-qualified attestations; support for W3C VCs is optional in that architecture.[116]

The EUDI architecture uses OpenID4VCI for issuance, OpenID4VP with the W3C Digital Credentials API for remote presentation, and ISO/IEC 18013-5 for proximity presentation.[125][116]

The earlier framework’s practical results were uneven. A European Commission evaluation published in 2019 found that only 14% of key public services were available cross-border through notified eIDAS solutions.[22][40] The revised framework responds with implementing regulations, technical pilots and coordinated testing.

DC4EU, launched in 2023, tests how different wallet implementations work in practice across 27 Member States; the pilots provide feedback for standards and technical solutions rather than demonstrating completed interoperability.[42]

The 2025 EUDI Launchpad tested wallets, issuers, remote readers and proximity readers.[125][116]

The POTENTIAL wallet pilot recorded 249 cross-border transactions across six use cases, including bank opening and e-prescriptions.[126]

**eIDAS is the strongest of the five approaches for legally backed cross-border interoperability within a defined region.** Its direction is toward common wallet interfaces and mutually recognised national implementations, but uniform operation still depends on Member States implementing the standards consistently.[3][42]

Under eIDAS, Member States must accept another Member State’s notified electronic-identification scheme for access to public services when the required assurance level is substantial or high; EU-wide recognition began in 2018.[40]

Only eID schemes notified by Member States to the European Commission are accepted through the eIDAS gateway, and a country is not required to establish or notify a scheme.[29]

A relying party intending to use data received from a wallet user must register in the Member State where it is established and specify the types of data it will request and its reasons for requesting them.[51]

### What is the approach to interoperability of India’s Aadhaar?

Aadhaar is a domestic foundational identity system: a state-issued unique number designed to support delivery of financial assistance and other services to Indian residents. Its interoperability model is hub-and-spoke. Authorised government and private-sector services reuse a shared identity infrastructure rather than exchange holder-controlled credentials among independent wallets.[22][25]

Within India, Aadhaar is designed to be portable. It may be used as identity evidence by authorised government or private entities, rather than being limited to the issuing authority or one purpose. The Aadhaar Enabled Payment System (AEPS) supports digital payments across banks and is described as making the payment network interoperable.[25] This is domestic institutional integration: banks, welfare systems and other authorised services connect through Aadhaar-related infrastructure.

Aadhaar also added an offline route in 2018. UIDAI introduced verification through a digitally signed QR-code copy of demographic information on the Aadhaar card, enabling local verification without a live connection to the central database.[23] That improves interoperability in low-connectivity settings and reduces the need for every verification to query a central service, although it does not transform Aadhaar into an internationally reusable credential ecosystem.

One comparative paper labels Aadhaar interoperability as “No,” while the evidence above documents concrete cross-bank, government/private-sector and offline domestic integrations. The apparent difference is scope: Aadhaar has bounded domestic ecosystem interoperability, but not general cross-border or wallet-to-wallet credential portability.[22][25][23] EU–India work in 2026 concerned exploration of a pilot linking the EUDI Wallet and India’s DigiLocker, and technical exchanges on e-signature recognition; it was not an operating Aadhaar-to-eIDAS interoperability link.[84]

**Aadhaar is highly reusable within authorised Indian service networks, but its interoperability is centralised and principally domestic.** It makes one resident identifier usable across connected services rather than making independently issued credentials portable across jurisdictions.

### What is the approach to interoperability of Estonia’s e-Residency?

Estonia’s e-Residency is not Estonia’s national e-ID for residents; it is a state-issued digital identity for non-residents that lets them use specified Estonian digital services, notably business-related services and digital signatures. Its interoperability rests on Estonia’s established public-key infrastructure, e-ID ecosystem and X-Road data-exchange layer, rather than on a general-purpose international credential format. Estonia’s e-ID card and DigiDoc software support online authentication, signatures, encryption, data exchange and access to public and private online services.[22]

X-Road is the key infrastructure layer. It enables different information systems to communicate and exchange data; encrypted data move directly between participating systems through secure servers and do not pass through the X-Road centre, which retains statistical information about transfers.[85] Estonia applies a Once-Only Principle: data supplied to public administration should not need to be supplied again when another authority can obtain and reuse the relevant data package.[81][86] X-Road supports services including e-Residency and is described as interoperable with different systems.[65]

Estonia has also pursued cross-border signature compatibility. It developed business-register access with digital signing for people from partner countries from 2008, cooperated on signature formats and software with Finland, Latvia and Lithuania from 2013, and supported the more internationally accepted `.asice` signature format alongside its domestic `.bdoc` format.[62] Estonia makes software libraries publicly available for external parties to use e-Residency for login and verification, allowing third parties to build services around the platform.[64]

These mechanisms do not compel external relying parties to accept an e-Residency credential. They make integration technically feasible and extend signature compatibility, but foreign-service acceptance remains a legal, commercial and service-policy decision. **e-Residency is therefore interoperable most strongly inside Estonia’s digital-state and business ecosystem, with targeted cross-border signature and integration pathways rather than universal foreign credential acceptance.**[62][64]

### Synthesis

The five approaches operate at different interoperability layers. DIDs and VCs offer shared identifier and credential models but require agreement on methods, wallet formats, protocols and trust frameworks. FIDO/WebAuthn standardises a reusable authentication mechanism across browsers, devices and authenticators while deliberately keeping credentials scoped to each relying party.[17][2][14][78]

eIDAS adds the legal recognition, common interfaces and regional governance that technical standards alone lack, making it the broadest cross-border framework in this comparison, although implementation remains uneven. Aadhaar instead achieves extensive domestic reuse through a central service hub. Estonia’s e-Residency relies on interoperable national infrastructure and signature formats, but acceptance outside its ecosystem is not automatic.[3][22][25][62][64]

## Resistance to surveillance

Surveillance resistance asks a narrower question than general security: who can learn that a person used an identity credential, link uses across services, or assemble those records into a profile. A system can protect cryptographic keys and still produce an excellent activity log. The critical design variables are whether the issuer is contacted at presentation, whether the same identifier or metadata recurs, and which wallet, relying party, or government body can retain transaction data.

### How resistant to surveillance are W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and

W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) can resist issuer-side surveillance when a verifier checks a signed credential using an issuer public key it already holds. In that design, the issuer need not learn that the holder presented the credential. A NIST illustrative mobile-driver’s-licence flow makes this explicit: a bank verifies the licence with public keys already obtained from a trust service without notifying the motor-vehicle authority.[7] The W3C identity model likewise says that verification need not inform the issuer.[2]

DIDs support separation of contexts. DID Core permits an entity to create as many DIDs as needed to separate identities, personas, and interactions; its principal anti-correlation technique is the *pairwise DID*, a different pseudonymous DID for each relationship.[17] VCs can add *selective disclosure*, meaning that a holder reveals only the required claim, and *zero-knowledge proofs* (ZKPs), which prove a proposition such as being over 18 without disclosing the underlying birth date.[9][10][35] The VC Data Model also requires that credential-status specifications not notify an issuer whenever a verifier is interested in a particular holder.[10]

These protections are optional capabilities rather than a property that follows from calling a system decentralized. A reused DID is a correlation handle; pairwise DIDs also fail if their documents reuse verification keys or distinctive service endpoints. Public DID-document histories, especially on immutable registries, can support traffic analysis and endpoint correlation.[17] A status check that contacts an issuer can reveal credential use, while wallet telemetry can disclose credential types and where and how holders use them.[18] The VC Data Model also warns that IP addresses, browser fingerprints, mobile-network location, and GPS tracking remain available outside the credential exchange.[10]

A web-backed DID method can expose resolution metadata even when the DID document discloses little: `did:webvh`, for example, has a publicly retrievable version history and uses DNS/HTTPS resolution, which can expose metadata unless privacy-preserving DNS is used.[128]

**DIDs/VCs are relatively resistant to centralized issuer observation only when deployed with pairwise identifiers, minimal and unlinkable presentations, privacy-preserving status checks, off-chain personal data, and wallet software that does not collect use telemetry; decentralization alone does not prevent surveillance.**[17][10]

### How resistant to surveillance is FIDO/WebAuthn?

FIDO/WebAuthn is designed for authentication, not for presenting identity attributes. Its main surveillance protection is *relying-party scoping*: each public-key credential belongs to one relying party—the website or app accepting the login—and cannot be used by another origin. The WebAuthn specification says relying parties cannot detect the existence or properties of credentials scoped to other relying parties, and that malicious relying parties cannot correlate users without extra information such as a reused username or email address.[14]

A relying party receives a signed response to its challenge, not the private key. If a biometric is used to unlock the key, biometric matching occurs locally in the authenticator and the relying party receives only a successful user-verification indication.[14] Thus WebAuthn prevents a service from acquiring a reusable biometric template through the login flow, and its per-service keys remove a shared credential identifier that separate services could ordinarily compare.[78]

WebAuthn does not make a person anonymous from the service they log into: that service initiates the challenge, verifies the response, and can log the account session like any other service. Privacy also depends on deployment choices. The default `none` attestation mode avoids transmitting authenticator attestation information, but `enterprise` attestation can include uniquely identifying information for controlled organisational deployments.[14] The specification recommends per-credential signature counters because a global authenticator counter can become a cross-relying-party correlation handle.[14] Relying parties can also create account-enumeration leaks through `allowCredentials` lists or distinct responses for registered and unregistered accounts.[14]

Implementation flaws can defeat the intended unlinkability. A 2022 timing-attack study found two vulnerable devices among eight FIDO Level 1 hardware authenticators tested: HyperFIDO Titanium Pro showed an average 10 ms difference and Feitian K26 2 ms, enabling account linkage under the attack conditions. Chrome introduced a mitigation on 5 October 2021 that deduplicated the `allowCredential` list and capped it at 64 key handles; the researchers confirmed mitigation in Chrome Canary.[72]

**WebAuthn is strongly resistant to credential-layer correlation between independent sites, but it is not resistant to observation by the site being accessed and can regain linkability through account data, attestation, counters, or flawed relying-party and authenticator implementations.**[14]

### How resistant to surveillance is EU eIDAS?

The amended eIDAS framework, Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, establishes the European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI Wallet) as a regulated identity-and-attribute presentation system. Its anti-surveillance model combines legal duties with technical requirements. Wallet providers may not collect unnecessary wallet-use information, combine wallet data with unrelated-service data, or fail to keep wallet-related personal data logically separate.[3] The framework says wallet providers should achieve *unobservability*—not collecting data or having insight into user transactions—apart from specified services for which the user gives explicit prior consent.[3]

The framework also directs its protections at issuers and intermediaries. Its technical framework must not let attribute-attestation providers or other parties obtain post-issuance data enabling transaction tracking, linking, correlation, or knowledge of user behaviour unless explicitly authorised by the user. It must support privacy-preserving unlinkability where an attribute attestation does not require identification.[3] Wallets must not tell attestation trust-service providers how attestations are used; intermediaries acting for relying parties count as relying parties and may not store transaction content.[3] Users receive an active-by-default transaction dashboard showing relying parties, requested and shared data, time and date, and even incomplete transactions; they can request erasure from a relying party or report suspicious requests.[3]

The framework permits user-chosen pseudonyms when EU or national law does not require legal identification.[3] This is meaningful but not equivalent to anonymity. A relying party necessarily sees its own transaction.

Moreover, technical analysis finds that selectively disclosed attestations can retain persistent fingerprints in salts, issuer signatures, and holder-binding public keys; colluding issuers and relying parties can use this metadata to link presentations.[13] ZKPs can remove some such auxiliary data, but they are not mandatory in the Architecture Reference Framework, and anonymous-credential techniques face maturity and hardware-binding trade-offs.[13]

**eIDAS 2.0 provides stronger formal limits on provider and issuer surveillance than conventional federated login, but its real-world unlinkability depends on wallet implementation, credential format, status and revocation design, and whether relying parties collude on remaining metadata.**[3][13]

### How resistant to surveillance is India’s Aadhaar?

Aadhaar has limited resistance to surveillance because it is a nationwide, centralized biometric identity system built around a common identifier. Using one global identifier across online transactions enables the Central Identities Data Repository (CIDR) to correlate activity.[22] Aadhaar authentication returns only a Yes/No response to the requesting institution and does not record the location or purpose of the authentication request, but central online authentication creates visibility of requests and potential correlation.[87][22] Aadhaar privacy analysis further identifies authentication and identification records as potential trails containing location, time, transaction context, and services accessed.[24]

UIDAI has introduced mitigations that reduce disclosure to individual requesting entities. The 16-digit Virtual ID (VID) is temporary and revocable, can be used instead of the Aadhaar number for authentication or e-KYC, and is not reversible into the Aadhaar number. UID Tokens are different for different requesting entities even for the same Aadhaar number.[71] A Bank for International Settlements paper states that authentication responses are yes-or-no only and reports that Aadhaar authentication does not record the location or purpose of the request.[87] These measures reduce routine exposure of the underlying number and some transaction fields, but they do not remove the system’s central identity architecture.

A UID Token remains stable within its requesting entity but differs across requesting entities, limiting direct cross-agency matching by token.[120][121]

Aadhaar regulations prohibit requesting entities from storing a Virtual ID.[120][121]

The surveillance risk is compounded when Aadhaar becomes a bridge across sectors. Research describes a persistent biometric identity distributed among connected systems as a potential instrument of social or political control and personal surveillance.[88][32] The central privacy concern identified in a 2021 analysis is nationwide retention and profiling of Aadhaar authentication-log metadata.[57] The same analysis warns that combining metadata silos can reveal and predict lives and lifestyles.[57]

**Aadhaar’s privacy measures can reduce identifier exposure at the edge, but the global identifier and centralized authentication ecosystem make it materially less resistant to cross-service surveillance than systems designed to avoid a common transaction record.**[22][71][57][24]

An Aadhaar authentication record includes the time, requesting entity, and response, and UIDAI maintains that record; this provides accountability for authenticating entities while making activity traceable through the persistent Aadhaar identifier.[40]

The rules described in the Aadhaar litigation required UIDAI to retain authentication transaction data, including metadata, for six months and then archive it for five years.[57]

UIDAI recommends that Authentication User Agencies use domain-specific identifiers in their dealings with people while retaining a back-end mapping between those identifiers and Aadhaar numbers; UIDAI does not maintain that mapping.[24]

### How resistant to surveillance is Estonia’s e-Residency?

Estonia’s e-Residency should not be conflated with Estonia’s domestic e-ID. It is a state-issued digital identity for non-residents to access Estonia’s digital business environment, and it relies on a government-linked credential and participating-service records rather than on anonymity or pairwise pseudonymous use.Judgment — Its surveillance posture is therefore best understood as accountable and access-controlled identity infrastructure, not as an anti-observation identity system.

The national e-ID infrastructure uses public-key infrastructure (PKI): a private key is stored on the card chip, while a public key can be retrieved from public directories. Card software supports PIN1 authentication, PIN2 electronic signatures, encryption, and access to public and private services; Mobile-ID provides similar authentication and signature functions through a SIM card.[22] These features establish cryptographic accountability, but an identifiable credential used repeatedly across services can create linkable records.

An accepting public or private online service can authenticate an e-ID-card holder using PIN1; any identifiable authentication record held by that service is distinct from X-Road data-exchange evidence.[22][89] For an X-Road data exchange, data are transferred directly between the customer and provider, while evidence of that exchange is stored locally by those parties rather than being available to third parties.[89] X-Road controls access at the organisation level; each service client remains responsible for authorising its own staff, and weak procedures at a partner organisation can expose shared data.[89] X-Road’s trackable, time-stamped exchanges improve accountability, but the very same auditability creates records that authorised participants can inspect.[86]

**e-Residency is only partly resistant to surveillance: e-ID authentication can create identifiable service records, while X-Road exchanges transfer data directly and retain exchange evidence locally with the exchanging parties. Encryption, access controls, and audit controls restrict unauthorised access, but the model supports identifiable, auditable activity rather than making use unlinkable across participating services.**[22][89][86]

### Synthesis

Judgment — The five approaches divide into two broad patterns. DIDs/VCs and WebAuthn can reduce cross-service linkage technically: the former through pairwise identifiers, selective disclosure, and issuer-independent verification; the latter through per-relying-party credentials. Neither conceals activity from the verifier itself, and both fail when identifiers, metadata, status checks, or software telemetry are reused.[17][14][10]

eIDAS 2.0 adds unusually explicit legal controls against wallet-provider and attribute-provider observation, but its strongest privacy outcome still depends on unlinkable credential mechanisms and disciplined relying-party practice.[3][13] Aadhaar’s centralized, global identifier architecture creates the clearest structural capacity for population-scale correlation despite VID and token mitigations.[22][71][57] Estonia’s e-Residency model prioritises traceable, accountable state-linked transactions, so it limits unauthorised access more than it limits authorised observation.[89][86]

## Recovery mechanisms for lost credentials

Losing a device is not the same as losing an identity. Recovery has two jobs: stop a lost credential being used, then restore the person’s ability to authenticate or present identity evidence.

### How can a holder recover after losing W3C Decentralized Identifiers

DID recovery restores control of a decentralized identifier and its DID Document after device or key loss; it is separate from recovering the signed Verifiable Credentials (VCs) held in a wallet. DID methods may enable a controller to rotate to new key material through pre-arranged social recovery, multisignature control, Shamir secret sharing, or pre-rotated keys. The controller should revoke the lost key and add the replacement key; the method, not DID Core, defines the authorization and revocation process.[17][90]

A lost VC may require wallet restoration or reissuance by its issuer, which originally signs the credential. **DID/VC recovery is therefore not a password reset: restoring DID control does not itself restore credentials lost with a wallet.** Secure backup, trustees, and escrow can improve recovery, but introduce a party or recovery material that must be trusted and governed.[68][69][37][91]

The VC data model includes `credentialStatus` to describe suspension or revocation and `refreshService` to support credential refresh, but does not prescribe a universal protocol for either; wallet backup, key recovery and reissuance remain deployment choices.[129][130]

The `did:key` method does not support key rotation; when its key must change, credentials issued using that DID must be revoked and reissued with the new key.[92]

Because `did:key` derives the DID from its public key, it can resolve offline without a registry lookup; a compromised key requires a new DID and application-level reassociation of existing relationships. It has no hierarchy for restoring all pairwise-DID relationships.[115]

### How can a user recover after losing FIDO/WebAuthn credentials?

WebAuthn does not back up or transfer a device-bound private key: losing its authenticator generally loses the credentials on it. The primary preventive route is registering multiple separate authenticators—for example, platform authenticators on regular devices plus a roaming security key—and relying parties should encourage this.[14][70]

Synced passkeys can be restored through the user’s cloud-account provider; a lost device-bound passkey requires enrolment of a replacement. If neither a backup authenticator nor sync route remains, the relying party—the service holding the account—must re-run identity proofing or onboarding and bind a new credential, while removing the lost authenticator from the account.[47][79][93][70] **Multiple separately stored authenticators retain phishing-resistant sign-in without copying the private key.** Weak email or SMS recovery can instead become the account’s weakest link.[94][70]

Email, SMS or knowledge-based recovery for a device-bound FIDO credential can undermine the account security provided by phishing-resistant authentication.[131][98]

Knowledge-based account-recovery checks, like email or SMS fallback, can weaken a FIDO-protected account if the fallback channel is compromised.[131][98]

### How can a holder recover after losing EU eIDAS credentials?

For a lost or stolen European Digital Identity Wallet, Member States should provide a simple, secure route for immediate revocation; issuance, use, and revocation are free to natural persons.[3] If a wallet, its validation mechanism, or underlying scheme is compromised, the providing Member State must suspend it, withdraw and revoke it if unresolved after three months, and re-establish it once remedied.[3]

Replacement is handled by the applicable national eID scheme or issuer, whose notified arrangements cover suspension and revocation; a locked-out user is directed to that eID’s helpdesk. Free transfer of wallet data supports migration, not recovery of access.[3][95][96] **eIDAS provides a common security-lifecycle framework, not one EU-wide replacement procedure.**

Under the EUDI wallet architecture, lost device-bound credentials and person-identification data must be set up or issued again rather than restored from a cloud backup.[132][133][134]

In the current German state-wallet implementation, a remotely revoked wallet instance cannot be restored: replacement requires fresh wallet setup and person-identification-data initialization using a valid identity document.[134][132][133]

Device-bound person-identification data in that German wallet cannot be restored from a cloud backup, so credentials deleted with the device must be requested again; changed person-identification-data values also require new issuance.[134][132][133]

### How can a holder recover after losing India’s Aadhaar credentials?

Aadhaar’s permanent 12-digit number and centrally held enrolment record mean that losing a physical card does not itself remove the identity. Authentication can use demographic data, an OTP sent to the registered mobile number or email, fingerprint, iris, or combinations; iris and OTP are fallback options when ordinary authentication fails.[22][24][55]

The Aadhaar App supports digital updates to mobile numbers and addresses. However, an Aadhaar number is intended to remain valid for life, while fingerprints and iris data are difficult to revoke or replace after compromise.[54][24] **Aadhaar is resilient to card loss but weakly recoverable after biometric or identifier compromise.** Offline verification and human exception handling can preserve essential-service access, but uneven implementation can exclude legitimate holders.[71][55]

In practice, access to Aadhaar’s state-administered update and authentication channels can depend on supporting documents, mobile access and the holder’s ability to authenticate.[122][120]

### How can a holder recover after losing Estonia’s e-Residency credentials?

A lost or stolen e-Residency card can have its digital certificates and PIN codes remotely invalidated. The Police and Border Guard Board may immediately suspend e-Residency where misuse is suspected or the card is no longer with its holder, invalidating certificates and access to digital services.[64][60]

Cards expire after three years and require a new application; the ordinary issuance flow is online application, approval, and collection of a digital-ID kit, with a stated duration of 3–8 weeks.[64][59] **Certificate invalidation contains loss quickly, but can interrupt all services dependent on the card.** Documents encrypted to a lost card cannot be decrypted under the described certificate-based arrangement.[64]

### Synthesis

Recovery tracks credential custody. DID/VC systems can preserve holder control but require recovery governance chosen before loss; FIDO protects non-exportable keys but relies on backup authenticators, sync providers, or service-led re-proofing.[90][91][70] eIDAS and e-Residency prioritize rapid state-managed revocation, while Aadhaar makes ordinary document loss less consequential because authentication rests on its central record and factors.[3][60][22][24] The strongest practical design combines immediate loss reporting and revocation, a separately protected recovery route, and replacement proofing at least as strong as original enrolment.[17][70][3]

## Suitability for banking

Banking needs two distinct capabilities: **customer due diligence (CDD)** at onboarding—identifying the customer and assessing money-laundering, terrorist-financing and related risks—and strong authentication for later account access or transactions. No approach supplies the entire banking relationship by itself: source-of-funds checks, sanctions/PEP screening, credit decisions and transaction monitoring remain bank responsibilities.[97][1]

FATF’s technology-neutral guidance calls for identity verification using reliable, independent documents, data or information; assurance also depends on governance, processes and procedures, not technology alone.[112]

### How suitable are W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials

**DIDs and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) are conditionally suitable for bank onboarding and reusable electronic KYC (eKYC).** A VC can carry signed claims such as name, date of birth and address; banks can use such attributes in remote account opening, including alongside document, liveness, AML and politically exposed person (PEP) screening.[47]

Their advantage is holder-mediated presentation: the customer can disclose selected claims rather than send a full document scan. In NIST’s illustrative mobile-driver’s-license flow, a bank verifies the credential using issuer public keys already obtained from a trust service, without notifying the issuing DMV. That privacy result is implementation-dependent: a verifier can instead contact an issuer, and credential portability does not compel a bank to accept an issuer’s KYC result.[7][10][2][11]

Judgment — **The deciding banking criterion is issuer and bank acceptance under a governance framework, not use of a DID.** Cryptographic verification establishes that a credential has not been tampered with; the bank must also trust the issuer and the rules that govern issuance, status and use.[2] The W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model requires credential-status specifications not to track people: they must not notify an issuer when a verifier is interested in a particular holder or subject, including through per-presentation issuer contact.[10] The verdict is strong where an accepted issuer, adequate identity assurance, privacy-preserving status checking, reissuance or recovery through the wallet or surrounding identity-management system, and the bank’s own CDD controls operate together.[2][10][7][1] VCs provide signed identity evidence, not authorization or ongoing compliance.[1] For a full journey, use VCs at enrolment and for exceptional high-risk attribute requests, then a passkey for routine authentication.[47]

Using a Verifiable Credential or another entity’s identification work does not transfer customer-due-diligence responsibility away from the financial institution.[47][135][112]

### How suitable is FIDO/WebAuthn for banking

**FIDO/WebAuthn is highly suitable for passwordless, phishing-resistant access to an existing bank account, not for proving civil identity at onboarding.** A passkey is a cryptographic credential tied to a specific website or application account; the user unlocks it locally with a biometric, PIN or device secret.[98] WebAuthn can also request a user authorization gesture for a single payment or financial transaction.[14]

A bank should first verify identity, then bind a passkey to that verified account. FIDO Alliance guidance recommends multiple authenticators to reduce recovery events; if all are lost, the bank reruns identity proofing or onboarding, with recovery choices balanced against account risk.[47][70] FIDO authenticators do not reveal personally identifiable information by design, but the bank’s resulting authentication and transaction records can still support behavioural analysis and investigations.[70][1]

Judgment — **FIDO is the best of the five for recurring online account authentication, provided the bank has a secure enrolment and recovery process.** For regulated high-assurance payments, the verdict remains conditional: synchronized passkeys are less suited to some payment requirements, and some regimes have not formally listed passkeys as MFA.[2][98]

### How suitable is EU eIDAS for banking

**Within the EU, eIDAS is highly suitable, but conditional, for a bank’s identity-evidence and strong-authentication layer.** Under the European Digital Identity Wallet framework, covered private banking and financial-services relying parties, except micro and small enterprises, must accept compliant wallets at the user’s request where strong online authentication is legally or contractually required; the stated deadline is 24 December 2027.[3][96]

The wallet can support customer identification, exchange of due-diligence attributes and strong customer authentication. It does not replace source-of-funds, PEP, creditworthiness or continuing risk checks.[3][29] Cross-border public-sector recognition is established for notified national schemes, but banking onboarding still varies among Member States because local regulations and document requirements differ.[1][31]

A bank may issue a strong-authentication attestation for use with the European Digital Identity Wallet after onboarding.[136]

Judgment — **eIDAS is the strongest option for regulated EU cross-border identity interoperability, but not complete KYC or payment authorization.** Its banking verdict rises if private-sector acceptance, needed verified attributes, SCA/SUA treatment and payment liability become operationally uniform; it falls where available assurance is inadequate for the product or customer risk.[1][99]

Whether a bank’s obligation to accept the European Digital Identity Wallet also covers payment approval remains unsettled pending binding European Commission or European Banking Authority interpretation.[136]

The wallet can selectively share identity information, including proof of age, residency, bank-account details and education credentials.[100] Private-sector providers may request personal information through the Wallet, including for credit scoring, so mandatory wallet acceptance does not itself prevent banking-related profiling.[101]

### How suitable is India’s Aadhaar for banking

**For domestic Indian banking, Aadhaar is highly suitable as a high-volume identity-proofing and eKYC rail for enrolled holders.** Aadhaar is an officially valid identity for eKYC; the BIS reported 8 billion eKYC requests since 2012 and an average 3 million Aadhaar-based requests per day in the preceding 12 months.[87] It has reduced paper-based onboarding and helped expand account access alongside Jan Dhan and other government measures.[87]

It is not complete banking compliance: fully fledged accounts in the described regime required initial in-person verification, and banks must still apply risk-based CDD. Aadhaar is not legally mandatory for opening a bank account under the reported Supreme Court position, although survey respondents reported providers accepting only Aadhaar in practice.[87][1][102] Its single identifier and centralized CIDR enable cross-service correlation, while biometric failures can exclude genuine users unless OTP, QR-code, iris or possession-based fallbacks work in practice.[22][71]

The Reserve Bank of India also permits Officially Valid Documents and other customer-identification routes; video identification may use Aadhaar methods but records a live image and geolocation to establish the customer’s physical presence in India.[137]

Judgment — **Aadhaar’s domestic onboarding efficiency is substantial, but its surveillance and exclusion trade-offs make safeguards decisive.** The verdict improves with effective non-biometric alternatives, consent-based lawful use, anti-correlation protections and defined replacement procedures for lost credentials; existing cited fallbacks address authentication failure rather than credential loss.[1][71]

### How suitable is Estonia’s e-Residency for banking

**Estonia’s e-Residency is moderately suitable as a banking enabler for overseas entrepreneurs, not as a standalone banking credential.** It enables remote authentication, signing and business administration, while Estonian digital-ID solutions are used for onboarding identification and payment-related strong customer authentication.[28][1] However, e-Residency neither includes a bank account nor grants a right to one: banks separately assess the person, business and source of funds.[60]

Banks may require address evidence, beneficial-owner information, business plans, contracts, expected payment flows, source-of-wealth evidence and local business substance.[103]Judgment — **It is most useful after a bank accepts the customer, for remote company operation and account access; it does not solve entry-level KYC/AML.** Suitability rises for applicants meeting a bank’s risk-based requirements and falls where travel or Estonian business substance is required.[1][104]

### Synthesis

**The best banking design is layered, not a winner-takes-all choice.** DIDs/VCs are strongest for reusable, privacy-preserving evidence when issuer trust and recovery are designed in; FIDO/WebAuthn is strongest for subsequent account authentication; eIDAS offers the clearest regulated EU acceptance route; Aadhaar offers scaled domestic Indian eKYC with significant correlation and exclusion risks; and e-Residency supports Estonian cross-border business administration but leaves bank onboarding to each institution.[47][3][22][60]

1. Use a government or trusted-issuer credential for onboarding evidence, not a passkey alone.
2. Use FIDO/WebAuthn or an equivalent phishing-resistant authenticator for routine account access.
3. Retain bank-run, risk-based CDD, monitoring, recovery and accessible fallback channels in every model.

Conditional ranking by banking function and jurisdiction. For onboarding evidence, an accepted eIDAS route ranks first for an eligible EU applicant at a participating bank because qualifying banking and financial-services relying parties must accept a compliant wallet in the defined strong-user-authentication circumstances; Aadhaar ranks first for an eligible Indian applicant at an authorised bank as the domestic eKYC route; and accepted VCs rank next as reusable evidence where the bank accepts the issuer and governance framework.[3][87][2] For recurring account login, FIDO/WebAuthn ranks first because its cryptographic credentials are scoped to the bank’s relying-party account and are designed for account authentication.[14][47] Estonia’s e-Residency ranks as an enabler for a non-resident entrepreneur only after the bank’s separate admission decision, not as a route that guarantees an account.[60] Positions change when an issuer or bank does not accept the credential, when recovery and accessible fallback arrangements are unavailable, or when the bank’s risk-based CDD requires additional evidence and controls.[1][70][3][71]

## Suitability for healthcare

Healthcare identity must protect highly sensitive data while allowing rapid, reliable care. The key distinction is between proving a clinical fact or entitlement and securely signing in to a system.

Healthcare identity encompasses patient matching, clinician authentication, delegation and consent, and authorization to access a particular record; authenticating a clinician does not itself authorize access to that record.[47][119]

### How suitable are W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials

Judgment — DIDs and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) are conditionally well suited as a healthcare credential-presentation and consent layer, not as a replacement for electronic health records. They can express insurance coverage, prescriptions, professional licences and consent; a DID can authenticate a request, while a VC can support role-based access decisions when paired with a separate authorization framework.[10] Selective disclosure can let a patient prove coverage without releasing an entire history, but names, addresses and repeated identifiers can still enable correlation; one healthcare design logged each DID and timestamp.[37][10] **The verdict improves when clinical authorities and professional bodies issue the relevant credentials, providers accept those issuers and privacy-preserving credential-status checks, and health systems integrate the presentation flow with legacy records and authorization systems.** A cryptographically valid credential is insufficient for a medical diagnosis or professional claim unless the issuer has the required authority in that clinical context.[10][37] **Continuity of care requires an explicit lost-wallet and lost-key process: revoke compromised credentials, reissue authoritative clinical or entitlement credentials, and restore access without delaying emergency treatment.**[10][8][37][105]

**Conditional ranking — continuity of care with minimum necessary disclosure is the deciding criterion.** First is an accepted authoritative patient or clinician attribute route—VCs from trusted clinical issuers or EUDI attestations—because it can establish a specific entitlement, consent, qualification or prescribing claim while limiting disclosure to the needed data.[37] Accessible emergency access and recovery routes rank ahead of reliance on any single authenticator, because delayed access can delay treatment; multiple FIDO authenticators and re-proofing, wallet revocation and replacement processes, and Aadhaar offline or manual alternatives are therefore required.[10][37][3][105] Aadhaar ranks only as an optional population-scale input where these alternatives are enforced, because biometric and operational failure can exclude patients and the reusable identifier enables cross-context correlation.[70][3][71] e-Residency ranks last for general healthcare because its credential establishes neither health coverage nor access to a particular health service.[71][22][106] Provider acceptance and integration with clinical systems raise the authoritative-attribute routes; loss of wallet or device access, absent recovery, inaccessible hardware, or unavailable emergency routes lowers every digital route.[107][81][37][3][105][108]

A healthcare provider can use a VC to verify insurance coverage without receiving the patient’s entire medical history, and can use hidden diagnostic-record claims for selective disclosure to medical personnel.[37] A credential is suitable for a clinical claim only when the healthcare provider accepts the issuer’s authority for that context.[10]

USCDI provides a baseline for health-data exchange that includes demographics used for patient matching; its proposed Draft USCDI v7 Healthcare Agent represents a legally authorized decision-maker for a patient unable to communicate, not a clinician authenticator.[138]

### How suitable is FIDO/WebAuthn for healthcare

FIDO/WebAuthn is highly suitable for phishing-resistant authentication to patient portals, telehealth and clinician systems. A passkey signs a user into an existing account, while local biometric or PIN verification does not give the relying party concrete civil identity.[98] Healthcare organisations must separately verify patient identity and practitioners’ qualifications and prescribing authority.[14][105] It can reduce disruptive password and MFA prompts, but hospitals must integrate it with legacy applications, shared workstations, devices and identity directories, and must retain emergency-access and lost-authenticator procedures.[105] **Its strongest deployment is verified enrolment followed by passkey re-authentication, with backup authenticators rather than a single device.**[105][47][109]

FIDO can safeguard patient data, streamline medical-professional access, and authenticate patients and providers for telehealth, but it secures access to an account rather than proving insurance eligibility, patient identity or professional licensure.[79] Hardware-bound protection is applicable to healthcare accounts.[98]

Within the EU, eIDAS 2.0 and the European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI Wallet) are conditionally well suited to patient identification, strong online authentication and bounded workflows such as e-prescriptions. The framework anticipates offline prescription verification, and certain health providers required to use strong authentication must accept a compliant wallet on the user’s request.[3] Relying parties must declare requested data and purpose, supporting minimised health-data disclosure.[3] **Its advantage is regulated cross-border acceptance; its constraint is conditional coverage and possible exclusion where secure wallet use depends on inaccessible devices.**[110][108]

The European Digital Identity Wallet has been tested for e-prescriptions and other health uses.[126]

Presenting an identity credential is distinct from European Health Data Space rights to restrict professional access to all or part of exchanged health data, and from controls over cross-border exchange and secondary use.[139]

Aadhaar is conditionally suitable as a population-scale identifier and authentication rail for healthcare, but not as a clinical-record, consent or care-entitlement system. It operates across health, banking and government, yet successful authentication depends on data seeding, biometric matching, connectivity, power, equipment and servers.[32] Reported authentication failures reached 49% in Jharkhand and 37% in Rajasthan, making sole reliance unsafe for essential treatment.[71] Its reusable identifier and central repository create substantial correlation risks for sensitive health contexts.[22] **Use Aadhaar only with enforced offline, manual and non-biometric alternatives, never as the sole condition for care.**[71][32]

Estonia’s e-Residency has low suitability as a general healthcare identity. It was designed for non-residents conducting business, whereas Estonian health insurance is for citizens and legal residents; healthcare access for e-residents was described as a prospective extension.[106] Estonia’s domestic e-health system is much stronger: eID authentication, X-Road exchange, electronic prescriptions and patient controls support healthcare services, although personal-ID-based logs create an auditable activity trail.[107][81][111] **The deciding issue is legal eligibility, not credential strength.**[107]

### Synthesis

Judgment — For healthcare, combine rather than substitute: use authoritative VCs or EUDI attestations for identity, entitlement, consent and professional status; use FIDO/WebAuthn for routine authentication; keep clinical records and emergency access in healthcare systems. Aadhaar can support broad service delivery only where fallbacks prevent exclusion. Estonia’s domestic infrastructure illustrates the value of integrating identity with records and entitlement, but e-Residency itself does not confer those rights.[37][47][105][71][107]

1. Deploy credential systems for minimum necessary claims, not whole medical histories.
2. Require non-digital and emergency alternatives wherever identity failure could delay care.
3. Treat recovery, issuer trust and clinical-system integration as preconditions for production use.

## Suitability for humanitarian aid for stateless populations

Humanitarian systems must identify people without making state recognition a condition of aid. **DIDs and Verifiable Credentials — strongest fit.** Their task is identification and credential presentation: an aid actor can issue an attestation after identity proofing based on in-person applications and interviews, knowledge of country of origin and local culture, and regular contact and validation over time, rather than making pre-existing official documents the sole basis for proofing. A credential without a signature or proof is not verifiable because its authorship cannot be trusted; therefore an aid-issued attestation needs a trusted issuer. Pairwise DIDs give the holder a different pseudonymous identifier for each aid relationship, reducing cross-party observation and correlation. The recovery trade-off is humane reissuance by the trusted aid issuer, supported by the continuing contact and validation used to build identity assurance.[1] **FIDO/WebAuthn — returning-account security, not an aid identity.** Its task is authentication: it protects access to an existing account but does not itself identify a person or present an aid entitlement.[10][17] Its enrolment barrier is an existing account with registered authenticator(s); its recovery design reduces loss by registering multiple authenticators and, when recovery is necessary, re-running identity proofing or user onboarding.

A foundational ID supports identification across many services, whereas a functional ID serves one service or class of services; humanitarian organizations may issue either to people without recognized identity documents.[140]

In Egypt in 2018, telecommunications providers accepted UNHCR refugee credentials for SIM purchases; extending humanitarian credentials across sectors also requires common schemas and exchange formats, including alignment with FHIR for health.[140]

Research on refugee identification reports that refugees and stateless people often do not know which organizations have access to their data, increasing risks to privacy, dignity and agency.[141]

Rohingya biometric registration was linked to cards required for services that contained fingerprints and extensive attributes; limited practical consent and fear that Myanmar might gain access raised targeting concerns.[142]

Kenya’s refugee-aid biometrics have been used since 2013; reported problems include recognition failures, monthly verification, deactivation after three missed distributions, geographic restrictions and reduced autonomy.[143]

An Ethiopian result found fewer than 500 duplicate registrations among more than 500,000 refugees, while the studies underlying a claimed 20% fraud reduction were unavailable.[144]

The ICRC model verifies beneficiary-held tokens without retaining a biometric database; biometric submission is not mandatory for services, and people who object receive services without biometric processing.[145]

For travel documents, the ICRC model uses ink fingerprints without converting them into machine-readable form or retaining them centrally.[145]

It therefore works as the authentication layer for an aid account, not as the credential that establishes eligibility for aid.[70] eIDAS, Aadhaar and e-Residency depend on recognised governmental enrolment, excluding many stateless people. Recovery must include trusted aid-agency reissuance.

WebAuthn can secure humanitarian staff accounts as well as established beneficiary portals, but is not a primary identity mechanism for people without devices or accounts.[131]

Synced passkeys can reduce credential loss for connected aid recipients, while device-bound credentials can worsen exclusion after theft or displacement.[131]
