# ERP Navigation and Role-Based Views for Manufacturing Supervisors

## Executive summary

Use a role-based supervisor workspace with progressive disclosure for routine inventory and work-order work, plus persistent global wayfinding and a clearly labelled route to authorized advanced functions. This best protects primary-task attention while preserving recognition-based access to search, alerts, help, location, and exceptions.

The available evidence supports the learning and error-prevention mechanism, including a reported enterprise redesign from five to three onboarding days and a 35% common-task-completion improvement, but it does not establish comparable rates for 45–60-year-old production supervisors moving from AS/400 to cloud ERP. Persistent navigation supports orientation and occasional retrieval, but it is not evidence that a permanently exposed full enterprise menu improves completion or reduces training time.

For SAP S/4HANA, Oracle NetSuite, and Dynamics 365, configure inventory and work-order workflows around the supervisor’s frequent status, availability, movement, release, and exception decisions, then measure accurate unaided completion, error rate, task time, advanced-function findability, and time to a declared competency threshold in the actual plant.

## Navigation and supervisor task outcomes

For mid-sized manufacturers moving from locally customized legacy AS/400 systems to cloud ERP, cloud ERP changes more than screen layout. Evidence coverage: 19 November 2026. Progressive disclosure initially presents a few important options and reveals specialized options on request; persistent navigation keeps a stable navigation and utility layer available during work; role-based views present the apps and information needed by a particular business role; and comprehensive dashboards present a collection of real-time data and metrics.[1][2][3][4] For production-floor supervisors aged 45–60 with limited software experience beyond the AS/400 system, the migration can replace practiced command keys, menu paths, terms, shortcuts, and workarounds with web-based, integrated processes.

Keyboard mappings, saved macros, and Entry, List, Menu, Help, and selection-value panel conventions are documented IBM i possibilities to check against each plant’s practiced inventory and production workflow, rather than assumptions about every plant.[5][6][7] The central interface question is therefore not whether to make the new ERP merely simpler, but how to keep routine inventory and production actions obvious while preserving safe, findable access to exceptions and advanced functions.

**Claim.** For supervisors aged 45–60 with limited software experience beyond a legacy AS/400 environment, progressive disclosure is the stronger default for frequent inventory and work-order tasks when it is combined with persistent global wayfinding. It is a design hypothesis to validate in the plant, not a demonstrated percentage improvement in this specific population.

### How does progressive disclosure affect task completion rates and training

Progressive disclosure is an interaction pattern that initially presents only the most important options and reveals specialized options when the user requests them. Its central design decision is the **frequency split**: actions required frequently stay in the first layer; unusual, specialized, or consequential controls remain available through a labelled second layer.[1]

For a supervisor’s routine work, the first layer should make the immediate operational goal visible: investigate a material shortage, confirm a stock movement, review a production-order status, release an order once material is available, or record an exception. Complex configuration, analytics, bulk correction, unusual allocation rules, and infrequent master-data actions should not compete visually with those routines. This applies the pattern’s stated purpose: advanced or rarely used functions move to a secondary display while frequent functions remain available up front.[1]

The rationale matters particularly for a person newly learning a cloud interface. Progressive disclosure focuses novice attention on likely-useful functions, can prevent mistakes with advanced settings, and can eliminate time spent considering controls irrelevant to the current task. NN/g describes the pattern as improving learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate.[1] In a complex form, showing only fields relevant to the current task or prior answer reduces the need to scan and filter irrelevant questions; grouping related fields and giving sections clear headings further reduces context switching and establishes what comes next.[8]

A supervisor does not necessarily need a linear wizard. Progressive disclosure is hierarchical: the user begins at the primary display and moves to a secondary display only if required. Staged disclosure, by contrast, forces a linear sequence. The latter is useful only when workflow steps are distinct and have little interaction; it becomes problematic when users must move back and forth among interdependent steps.[1] Inventory investigations and work-order creation commonly require such interdependence—for example, checking stock, reviewing an exception, changing an order field, and returning to confirm the effect—so a layered object page is generally preferable to a rigid multi-page wizard.

The historical “Training Wheels” result supports an initial restriction of complexity. In a 1984 commercial word-processor study, beginners using an interface that blocked advanced functions learned a basic letter-typing task faster and scored better on a later comprehension test. The control group spent almost one quarter of its time recovering from error states that the restricted interface prevented.[9] That result demonstrates a learning mechanism—preventing early detours and recovery work—not an ERP performance estimate.

In an enterprise-dashboard redesign, the author reported that users struggled with basic tasks and missed functions buried in clutter, that new users spent 70% of their time on five core functions, and that the redesign used three layers: core functions always visible, secondary functions one interaction away, and advanced functions in dedicated modes.[10] The author reported average onboarding duration falling from five days to three days, described as a 40% reduction, and reported a 35% change in common-task completion. The report supplies neither a supervisor task-success numerator nor denominator.[10]

The applicable measures should be defined before interpreting such outcomes. ISO 9241-11 defines effectiveness as the accuracy and completeness with which users achieve specified goals, and efficiency as resources used in relation to results, including time and human effort. The standard explicitly treats learning by new users and effective, efficient reuse by infrequent users as usability conditions.[12] Task-completion rate is correct, independent completions divided by assigned scenarios.[12][13] ISO-derived measurement practice includes percentage of users completing a task, accuracy of completed tasks, completion time, and tasks completed per unit time.[13]

An ERP usability study of seven users classified as advanced beginners scored task success as 0, 0.5, or 1, counted errors, logged time, and collected satisfaction on a 1–5 scale. Reported payroll and leave task times ranged from 2 minutes 11 seconds to 4 minutes 9 seconds; these were not inventory or work-order tasks.[121]

Judgment — **Training time should mean elapsed instructor-led, coached, and independent practice time until the supervisor meets a declared proficiency threshold, such as successful completion of representative routine scenarios within a defined time and error limit on two consecutive attempts.** Time-on-task measures transaction efficiency after learning; it must not be relabelled as time-to-competency.

The progressive-disclosure case is not one-sided. Deferring features creates a discoverability risk: users may not learn that an advanced feature exists, call support for functionality already present, or construct spreadsheet workarounds.[14] The second layer must therefore have clear labels, stable location, and strong information scent—language that tells users what they will find before they open it. Frequently used items should remain visible; repeated opening of a secondary layer indicates that the frequency split is wrong.[15][14]

A menu-structure study separated navigation time into visual search, decision, and operation time and found that deeper hierarchies increased search time.[122]

The evidence also does not support treating cognitive load as a single automatic outcome. General guidance says that fewer visible choices make a new user’s first session lighter and lower cognitive load.[14] Yet a summarized 2026 study of AI training-dataset explanations found no meaningful reduction in mental effort from progressive disclosure in that setting, and newer work suggests that different forms of mental effort can move in different directions, including productive effort involved in learning.[15] A simpler initial screen can reduce irrelevant scanning while still increasing effort if users must hunt for a concealed comparison, field, or consequence.

**For AS/400-to-cloud supervisors, progressive disclosure supports primary-task performance when it visibly covers the frequent workflow and exposes advanced functions through a stable, labelled route; it does not justify hiding required comparison information, safety consequences, or controls used routinely.**[1][16][14]

A defensible test design is role- and frequency-segmented. First, conduct task analysis and field observation of current inventory and production work; existing-system usage data and instrumented control-use data can identify which features are actually frequent.[1][14] Second, give the same supervisor participants equivalent inventory and work-order scenarios in both designs, counterbalance the order, and record success, accuracy, time, errors, assistance, control use, and searching for advanced options.[15] Third, test whether novice supervisors can complete level-one tasks unaided and whether experienced or occasional advanced users can reach level two quickly.[9]

An ERP usability test scored task success on a 0–0.5–1 scale, counted errors, logged time with a keylogger, and collected satisfaction ratings on a 1–5 scale; its seven participants were advanced beginners, not manufacturing supervisors migrating from AS/400.[121]

In that ERP test, users averaged 4 minutes 9 seconds to reach a request step, 2 minutes 11 seconds to enter payroll, and 3 minutes 19 seconds to locate a relevant leave period. The researchers identified unclear labels, hard-to-reach controls, hidden layout areas, and ambiguous confirmation buttons as usability problems.[121]

This makes it possible to decide which controls truly belong in the primary workspace rather than assuming that every control a process owner requested deserves persistent display. In one manufacturing ERP case, 30% of overall functionality was removed 18 months after go-live because it was unused; the study says that it had burdened windows and the graphical interface and increased superfluous maintenance.[17] The same study records logistics and finance users describing an intricate interaction, with a logistics manager saying four additional employees were needed to perform the same activities after nearly a year.[17]

In a cognitive walkthrough, four usability and ERP experts attributed about 30% of identified usability problems to too many options; they also identified confusion between primary tabs and subtabs, unclear categories and labels, and unnecessary multistep paths.[121]

Proposed migration assessment. At baseline and at each follow-up—first unaided practice, hypercare, and three months after go-live—calculate independently correct inventory and order scenario completion as 100 × (number of eligible scenario attempts completed accurately and completely without assistance, critical error, or unauthorized workaround) ÷ (all eligible scenario attempts begun). Calculate proficiency gain as the follow-up completion percentage minus the baseline completion percentage, in percentage points. This applies ISO 9241-11 effectiveness—accuracy and completeness in achieving specified goals—and repeated measurement of learnability over time.[12][13]

Set the competency rule before testing: no critical errors, no assistance, and independently correct completion of each representative inventory and work-order scenario within its declared role-specific time limit on two consecutive attempts during a preset 14-calendar-day observation window. Start calendar-clock time-to-competency at the first role-specific training session; separately report instructor-led hours and independent-practice hours. A supervisor who has not met the rule at a follow-up remains not yet competent at the last observation rather than receiving an assumed completion time. Reassess proficiency before go-live, during hypercare, and several months later using scenario assessments, transaction-quality reviews, system-use data, manager validation, support trends, and process-compliance reviews.[18]

For transaction errors, calculate the transaction-error percentage as 100 × erroneous eligible transactions ÷ all eligible transactions, and separately report error events per 100 eligible transactions because a single transaction can contain multiple errors. Compare like-for-like roles, scenarios, items, sites or locations, transaction volumes, and observation periods. Code inventory item, site or location, quantity, and posting errors separately; code work-order assembly, quantity, date, routing, and release errors separately. Reconcile inventory-on-hand quantities and values, critical fields, record counts, relationships, and equivalent legacy and cloud reports alongside the user-transaction measure.[19][18][20]

The report states that general UX studies associate progressive disclosure, compared with persistent navigation, with approximately 15–25% lower error rates and 20–30% better task-completion times for users with limited experience.[21]

A manufacturing Fiori/UI5 deployment reported a 38% increase in task-completion rate, 43% better accuracy on tablets, a 62% reduction in deployment-preparation time, and 41% shorter deployment-execution time.[11]

The Fiori implementation table reports a 25–40% faster learning curve and 30–50% faster task processing than SAP GUI.[11]

### How does persistent navigation affect task completion rates and training

Persistent navigation keeps a stable route to application areas and cross-application services visible while work continues. In SAP Fiori, for example, the launchpad shell bar is always visible and provides navigation options, enterprise search, notifications, personalization, and other cross-application functions. After an app opens, a Back button appears in that shell bar.[2][3] A persistent navigation model therefore supports orientation and recovery: a user who is unsure where they are or needs a different function can use a consistent visible route rather than reconstructing a hidden path.

Its cognitive rationale is **recognition rather than recall**. A command-line interface demands remembered syntax, while visible menus and buttons let users recognize and select available commands. Recognition is easier because visible cues activate relevant information in memory and make a correct choice more likely.[22] Practice, recency, and context affect retrieval; this is especially relevant to supervisors who may use an advanced reporting, traceability, or correction function only occasionally.[22]

Legacy IBM i environments can reinforce recall-heavy habits. IBM i Access Client Solutions provides 5250 display emulation, a Session Manager, tabbed concurrent sessions, command-line functions, configurable keyboard mappings, saved macros, and function keys numbered 1 through 24.[6][23] Traditional iSeries panel conventions include Entry, List, Menu, and Help panels; list selections can use action values such as Select, Change, Copy/Hold, Delete, and Display details, while F11 can conditionally introduce extra detail fields.[7] These conventions are not evidence that every manufacturer’s supervisors used a particular pattern, but they show why a migration may disrupt deeply practiced command and screen-location knowledge.

The migration should not respond by recreating 5250 screens in a browser. The stronger continuity is semantic and structural: retain familiar shop-floor terminology, recognizable action names, stable locations for routine actions, and clear status signals. Legacy design guidance itself emphasizes consistent standards, verb/object titles such as Add Order or Deplete Stock, explanatory field text, and visible command-key explanations because consistency makes panels easier to understand and education simpler.[7]

Persistent navigation contributes to recognition only if it remains small enough to preserve the work area. Fixed navigation keeps all links and menu items visible as users scroll. It can also consume screen space, obstruct content, and impede keyboard access if the header is bulky.[24] Sticky navigation can instead retain only part of the navigation, adapting with scroll position; this can leave the most relevant navigation accessible while reducing information burden.[24]

A menu-depth and breadth study found that deeper menu trees increased search time and that top-level menu names substantially affected usability. It separated traversal time into visual-search, decision, and operation time.[122]

A cognitive walkthrough of an ERP interface attributed about 30% of identified usability problems to presenting too many options; it also found confusion when subtabs resembled primary tabs and when category labels were unclear.[121]

For equally likely choices, the candidate’s Hick’s Law illustration gives about 5 bits of decision information for 30 options versus 2 bits for three options—a 60% reduction in decision information, not in end-to-end task time.[9]

Judgment — **Persistent navigation should be treated as a wayfinding layer, not as a mandate to display every ERP function at all times.** A stable shell should retain Home or role workspace, search, alerts or notifications, current location, help, and an authorized route to advanced functions. It should not turn a production transaction into a comprehensive dashboard surrounded by a full enterprise menu.[2][3][24]

Persistent visibility supports occasional access, but no study measures its task-completion or training-time effect for the target supervisors. In December 2015, NN/g tested hidden, visible, and combo navigation across six sites with 179 UK participants, including 99 desktop participants; half of every participant’s tasks could be completed without using navigation. Twenty-one participants were aged 50–54 and five were 55 or older.[25][26][27]

The closest cited ERP navigation study used seven advanced beginners performing non-manufacturing payroll and leave tasks, rather than production-floor supervisors.[121]

For both interface conditions, use the same role permissions, tasks, data, practice exposure, device, and competency threshold, and record common task-level outcomes.[12][13][15]

- **Effectiveness:** percentage of supervisors completing inventory movement, inventory exception, work-order initiation, release-check, and correction scenarios accurately and completely. [12][13]
- **Efficiency:** median elapsed task time, navigation steps, assistance requests, and recovery time after an induced but safe error. Time and human effort are ISO efficiency resources. [12][13]
- **Learnability:** change in those measures across first use, coached practice, and delayed reuse. Learnability can be measured as a change in usability over time or as a comparison between experienced and inexperienced users. [13]
- **Advanced-function access:** percentage finding an authorized advanced action, time to locate it, number of false paths, and whether the user understands its consequences before using it. [15]

**Persistent navigation is a defensible access mechanism for occasional and cross-application functions, not a demonstrated performance advantage over progressive disclosure for production-floor supervisors.** Its value is strongest where users need stable cues for location, search, help, alerts, and escalation; its cost rises when the constant menu reduces the space and attention available to the production task.[2][22][24][12]

Participants aged 50 or older represented 14.5% of the persistent-navigation study sample (26 of 179 participants).[26]

Age is not a demonstrated causal explanation for navigation outcomes; evaluate age separately from legacy-system familiarity and cloud-software experience.[28][29]

A systematic review of 132 mobile-app studies involving adults aged 60 and over identified simple paths, clear menu hierarchies, and logical workflows as recurring features associated with reduced cognitive load and better task completion; the reviewed users were not the specified AS/400-to-cloud manufacturing supervisors.[123]

In a randomized trial with 105 participants, including 35 adults aged 55–73, a self-chosen-tab application was more time-efficient than an autonomous exhaustive-list application. The participants were not manufacturing ERP users transitioning from AS/400.[123]

A systematic review of 132 mobile-app studies involving adults aged 60–99 found simple navigation paths, clear hierarchies, and logical workflows recurrently associated with lower cognitive load and better task completion. In one randomized dietary-app trial covered by the review, a self-chosen-tab interface was more time-efficient than an exhaustive-list interface, while both exceeded 98% accuracy; the older subgroup was aged 55–73.[123]

### What features of mid-sized manufacturers’ established AS/400 inventory and production

Production supervisors coordinate manufacturing workers and oversee production from start to finish for quality, timeliness, cost, and safety. Their work includes maintaining records of materials used, products made, setup time, operating cycle time, and changeovers; they also manage shifts, changeovers, overtime, quality requirements, and process improvement.[30] Their ERP workspace must therefore support monitoring and decision-making as well as transaction entry.

Manufacturing supervisors commonly manage large crews across shifts, and shop-floor work often takes place around equipment, warehouse locations, production lines, and shift handovers rather than at an uninterrupted desktop.[124]

Manufacturing guidance notes that frontline work is often deskless and that supervisors have responsibilities across shifts.[124]

Manufacturing workflows impose changing information demands. In Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management’s production lifecycle, an order begins with creation of a production order, batch order, or kanban and ends with a finished manufactured item. Each lifecycle step requires different information, and completed steps change production status, although not every step maps to an explicit status.[31] Before release, schedule completion and material availability matter; the material-availability check specifically helps a shop-floor supervisor assess whether material is available for production or batch orders.[31] This pattern means the supervisor often needs a concise primary view of current status and exceptions, with contextual drill-down into material, scheduling, quality, and reporting details.

Legacy systems are often customized over years to local processes, terminology, and workarounds. Employees build considerable expertise in navigating those systems, but when an integrated ERP replaces them, the former local expert can become a novice again.[5] The transition is cognitively demanding because workers must replace established process habits with standardized procedures; the cited change-management account says forgetting a routine can be harder than learning from scratch because the old pathway competes with the new one.[5]

This explains why design must preserve operational language even where the process itself is standardized. A supervisor should see plant-recognizable concepts—material shortage, hold, release, scrap, rework, count variance, setup, and production status—rather than vendor terminology that requires translation under time pressure. Plain language and testing wording with actual users are recommended because technical jargon and internal terminology can slow users and cause hesitation.[8] The relevant point is not to preserve every legacy label, but to make each changed term explicit during the transition.

Cloud ERP adds broader operational change. A 2020 systematic review identifies six cloud-ERP implementation concerns: new work arrangements, legacy-data migration, security compliance, alignment between ERP functionality and business processes, integration with the application portfolio, and reliability.[32] SaaS ERP is remotely hosted and normally accessed on demand through a thin client such as a web browser.[32] In a manufacturing environment, loss of connectivity can prevent login, transaction posting, production-order confirmation, and goods movements.[5]

The interface therefore needs an **outage path** as part of the workflow: a visible current-connectivity state where appropriate, a documented manual capture process, and a controlled later-reconciliation route. This is not merely IT contingency planning. If a supervisor cannot confirm a movement or order at the point of work, the primary task and the integrity of subsequent inventory and production information are both affected. The migration design should treat the offline or degraded-service procedure as a trained exception workflow.

Role differentiation is necessary, but it should distinguish permission from visibility. SAP Fiori explicitly recommends role-based design and gives role-based app access through the launchpad, home page, and spaces.[2] In a multi-location ERP change framework, differentiated groups include local end users, superusers with functional and multi-location authority, and production-expert coordinators authorized across multiple functions and locations.[5] Those distinctions should shape authorization and escalation routes, not remove all awareness of what exists beyond the supervisor’s default role.

A role-based primary workspace can combine focused operations with broad organizational visibility. In an automotive-tooling SME case, ERP dashboards showed die setup status, cycle completion, rejection count, and material shortages; supervisors used them to pre-plan tool changes, allocate manpower, and identify downtime sources.[33] The same implementation used dashboards and alerts to make deviations from setup-time or quality thresholds visible, and role-based access helped execute operational changes consistently across shifts and departments.[33]

Those findings support dashboard use for monitoring, not a comprehensive dashboard as the default route for every transaction. A dashboard is useful when it helps the supervisor recognize an exception and choose the next action. It becomes counterproductive when it forces the supervisor to scan unrelated enterprise metrics before completing a routine receipt, issue, count, or work-order action. The primary workspace should therefore show only the operational measures that drive the supervisor’s current decisions, with summary cards or alerts expanding into context-specific actions.

**A role-based primary workspace with persistent global navigation offers the best supported access model: it protects routine-task attention while retaining visible search, alerts, navigation, and authorized escalation paths.**[2][5][33]

Training should mirror this interface structure. Manufacturing ERP guidance recommends covering changed workflows and responsibilities, not system mechanics alone, using classroom sessions, hands-on practice, job aids, and ongoing support for employees with varying technical comfort.[34] Role-based training and short, task-focused sessions embedded in real work—such as receiving, issuing, scheduling, and closing jobs—are recommended over generic system overviews.[35] Training should also span end-to-end processes rather than isolated modules because users work through cross-module processes, not through the software’s module names.[36]

A phased implementation can let staff focus on learning specific areas before proceeding, with early phases providing learning that improves later phases.[34] A comparative study of 13 industrial firms likewise found that user training covering both technical and business processes, combined with phased implementation, helped firms overcome assimilation knowledge barriers.[37] The firms nevertheless continued to experience assimilation concerns, indicating that learning does not end at go-live.[37]

Safe practice and timed reinforcement address the old-routine problem. A manufacturing change-management framework recommends simulation environments where users can practice without pressure or fear of disrupting the live system.[5] It also recommends differentiated training because a warehouse operator, production planner, and financial controller need different levels and kinds of knowledge; one generic programme will be too detailed for some operational users and too superficial for complex roles.[5] Training timing should be split: an earlier low-stress learning phase, then a refresher immediately before go-live, because early training may be forgotten and late training leaves users unprepared.[5] Post-go-live change management should continue for at least three months to monitor acceptance, provide additional training, and respond to resistance.[5]

The plant environment makes this especially important. Qualitative studies of 50 assembly operators in three Swedish manufacturing companies found that unclear, incorrect, or information-heavy instructions were cognitively demanding, and that noise, blinking lights, and parallel tasks could impair focus.[38] Novice assemblers found a visible countdown to the next product stressful and distracting, while time pressure combined with mistakes or unfamiliar requirements could lead to hurried errors.[38] These are not ERP-transition results, but they show why the production-floor interface must present the immediate task and exception clearly rather than asking a supervisor to interpret dense, unrelated information under operational pressure.

The same research finds that structured introductions, clear work steps, sufficient practice time, standardized routines, and practical learning approaches support onboarding.[38] Simplification has a boundary: shortened task sequences reduced information that assemblers had to learn, but could also limit their understanding of the whole product and why the work was done.[38] The corresponding ERP implication is to simplify the operational path without concealing the wider process consequences of a stock adjustment, material substitution, or production-order change.

For each high-frequency job, define one canonical cloud route and use that same route in the interface, work instructions, and training. Avoid multiple visually similar ways to post or change a transaction unless their different consequences are explicit.

Inventory and production exceptions require authorized access to availability details, serial- and lot-related information, alternative material or location data, reservation or allocation context, corrections and reversals, work-order components and operations, status history, documents, approvals, and escalation. These functions should be reachable from the role workspace without requiring supervisors to memorize the whole application.

Before migration, establish a legacy baseline for completion time, error and correction rate, help requests, and exception frequency for each representative supervisor task. Assess prior AS/400 process expertise, digital confidence, device literacy, language needs, accessibility needs, and task frequency so that training and evaluation reflect the actual users rather than age alone.

### Synthesis —

Progressive disclosure and persistent navigation solve different parts of the migration problem. Progressive disclosure reduces irrelevant scanning and early error paths by keeping frequent supervisor actions in the primary layer; persistent navigation supplies stable recognition cues for orientation, search, alerts, and occasional escalation. The evidence supports neither a universal completion-rate improvement nor a training-time percentage for supervisors aged 45–60 moving from AS/400 to cloud ERP. It does support a combined pattern: role-based routine workspaces, fixed global wayfinding, contextual advanced access, task-focused rehearsal, and post-go-live reinforcement. A comprehensive dashboard should serve exception monitoring and planning, while the routine transaction path remains focused.[1][2][14][5]

1. Define the supervisor’s five to ten high-frequency inventory and production actions from field observation and usage data, then place them in the default workspace. [1][36]
2. Keep Home or role workspace, current location, search, alerts, help, and authorized escalation routes persistently visible. [2][3]
3. Place rare advanced controls behind labelled, stable, context-specific paths; never conceal safety-critical consequences or information required for comparison. [15][16]
4. Test progressive disclosure and persistent-navigation variants with the same realistic tasks, users, training exposure, and competency threshold. [12][15]
5. Train changed production processes through safe simulations, hands-on task practice, a near-go-live refresher, and at least three months of monitored post-go-live support. [5]

## Documented ERP product workflows

Cloud ERP products do not implement inventory and production work as a single screen or a single “work order” concept. They combine an inventory record or transaction mechanism with production-order planning, release, execution, and reporting. For an ERP-interface designer serving supervisors moving from AS/400, the important distinction is where each product keeps the routine decision—material availability, count variance, release status, or required quantity—and where it places the deeper exception controls.

The practical SAP distinction is creation versus control: CO01 provides direct manual order creation, whereas Manage Production Orders provides monitoring, selected changes, release, material checks, and onward navigation to deeper functions.[47][53] That division makes it possible to design a supervisor home experience around exceptions and releases without placing every order-maintenance field on the initial screen.

### How does Microsoft Dynamics 365 implement inventory management and work

Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management distinguishes Inventory management from its separate Warehouse management module; a Warehouse management mobile workflow does not automatically apply to an Inventory management workflow.[139]

Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management implements inventory inquiry through the On-hand list. Users navigate to Inventory management > Inquiries and reports > On-hand list.[42] The list summarizes inventory and warehouse transactions, and displayed values vary according to chosen filters and display dimensions.[42] Users filter by product and location, add or remove filters, and apply them to generate the result set. More filters yield more specific results and therefore fewer rows and/or lower on-hand values.[42]

Users also choose inventory dimensions. Adding a dimension makes the list more granular; for example, adding Location adds a Location column and expands results from one row per warehouse to rows by warehouse location.[42] Users can save a frequently used dimension set so it opens automatically in later sessions.[42] The list calculates Available physical as Physical inventory minus Physical reserved, and Total available as Available physical plus Ordered in total minus On order.[42] This is a useful design distinction for supervisors: a visually simple stock total can conceal reservation and planned-supply conditions that matter to release decisions.

The On-hand list supports detail-on-demand. From the Action Pane, users can open Inventory transactions or Warehouse transactions, and the destination page retains the list’s filters and dimensions.[42] It also exposes Quantity adjustment and Intercompany on-hand actions.[42] For WMS-enabled items, the list marks use of warehouse-management processes and provides inspectable warnings; physical-reserved values follow the item’s reservation hierarchy.[42] **Dynamics 365 combines a persistent module-menu entry point with filterable aggregate inventory visibility and contextual drilling into transactions.**

For Warehouse management mobile devices, administrators create menu items and menus, create work-user accounts, and assign each worker a top-level menu. Dynamics 365 Production control is linked to Product information management, Inventory management, General ledger, Warehouse management, Project accounting, and Organization administration.[31] A production order produces a specified product or variant in a specified quantity on a specified date and is based on BOMs and routes.[31] It can be created manually, generated from demand signals, created by master planning through firming planned orders, created from sales orders, or created from pegged supply associated with higher-level production orders or kanbans.[31][51]

### Synthesis

Across all three products, inventory and production are linked but deliberately not collapsed into one generic dashboard transaction. SAP centers production supervision on a scoped Fiori worklist, contextual navigation, dedicated count entry, and production-order receipt apps; NetSuite centers it on transaction forms plus tablet and WMS execution routes; Dynamics 365 centers it on filterable inquiry, journal-specific posting, lifecycle-stage actions, and specialized dispensing tickets.[53][43][73][44][62][50][42][64][31][71]

For supervisors transitioning from AS/400, the common design pattern is a task-specific routine surface: SAP’s plant/work-center worklist, NetSuite’s work-order or build queue, and Dynamics 365’s on-hand or release decision. Advanced functions remain necessary, but each product places them behind feature conditions, related-app navigation, journals, secondary forms, or later lifecycle stages.[53][50][42][64] The strongest cross-product design direction is therefore role-scoped routine entry with contextual access to advanced controls, rather than an initial screen containing all inventory, planning, accounting, and execution options.

The documented navigation implementations are mixed rather than mutually exclusive. SAP uses the role-based Fiori Launchpad with tiles, global search, and a collapsible side menu; its inventory apps present filtered, role-specific information with expandable detail. NetSuite uses persistent tabbed menus and global search alongside customizable role-based dashboards. Dynamics 365 uses persistent left-panel navigation with Favorites, Recent, and Workspaces, plus contextual progressive disclosure in task-focused workspaces and role-based workflows.[21]

For an AS/400-to-cloud supervisor design, preserve a persistent route to the routine inventory inquiry, adjustment/count action, and work-order queue, then reveal fields and controls according to the selected transaction, product tracking requirement, production status, and responsibility. SAP’s role-scoped worklist, NetSuite’s feature-conditional forms and execution paths, and Dynamics 365’s inquiry-to-journal and lifecycle-stage paths all implement this combination of stable entry points and conditional depth.[53][44][50][42][64][31]

Occasional advanced access belongs in contextual drill-down and controlled secondary routes: SAP related apps and production-order details; NetSuite Inventory Detail, WIP, routing, count approval, and WMS-role actions; and Dynamics 365 transaction drill-down, journals, Inventory Visibility statuses, warehouse work, and lifecycle actions. This preserves the initial supervisor surface for availability, exception, release, count, and build decisions while retaining the advanced functions required for exceptional work.[53][44][50][63][42][74][75]

## Cognitive load during system transitions

Judgment — Cognitive load during an ERP transition is the mental work required to understand an unfamiliar interface, reconcile it with the production situation, choose an action, and learn a new routine while work continues. For production-floor supervisors moving from an established AS/400 environment to cloud ERP, that burden matters because the transition replaces not only screens and commands but also familiar terminology, locally evolved workarounds, and confidence in knowing what to do under production pressure.

### What evidence exists from change management studies about cognitive load

ERP change-management research establishes that learning demand arises from both the system and the altered work process. Robey, Ross and Boudreau’s 2002 *Journal of Management Information Systems* comparative case study of 13 industrial ERP implementations found that every firm faced two knowledge barriers: configuring the package and assimilating new work processes. The authors found that user training covering both technical and business processes, combined with phased implementation, helped firms overcome assimilation barriers; concerns about assimilation nevertheless continued in all firms.[37]

This distinction is important for an AS/400-to-cloud migration. A supervisor may know how to maintain inventory accuracy or release work, yet still lack the new system’s concepts, data dependencies, approval logic, and exception handling. The task is therefore not simply learning a cloud interface. A lower-demand interface alone cannot resolve uncertainty over inventory data, production permissions, or changed approval responsibilities: ERP transition also changes work processes, roles, responsibilities, controls, local autonomy, and performance visibility that determine what a supervisor is authorized and expected to do.[37][5][76]

A manufacturing change-management account of multi-location ERP projects describes why this mapping is difficult. It says local ERP systems are typically customized over time around local processes, terminology, and workarounds, and that experienced employees acquire substantial navigation expertise. Replacing those systems with an integrated ERP can make a former local expert “suddenly a novice again.” The same account characterizes replacing established habits with standardized procedures as a cognitive load because old routines remain active and compete with the new routine during the transition.[5]

**For legacy-to-cloud ERP change, the operative mechanism is routine replacement, not screen unfamiliarity alone: supervisors must inhibit practiced local actions while building a new relationship among process purpose, data, and system commands.** This conclusion follows from the displacement of locally learned expertise and the reported need to assimilate changed processes.[5][37] Interface and training design should therefore connect the new transaction explicitly to the supervisor’s existing shop-floor intent: what production condition it represents, what downstream consequence follows, and what legacy practice it replaces.

ERP complexity compounds the transition burden. One case-based study characterizes ERP systems as highly integrated: their relationships are numerous, nested, and interdependent. Greater integration and complexity tend to widen initiative scope and bring more stakeholders and expectations into the implementation.[17] In practical interface terms, an inventory adjustment or work-order action can affect planning, procurement, finance, traceability, and shop-floor execution.

Showing that whole dependency structure during every routine transaction would add irrelevant mental work; hiding all consequences would prevent users from forming an accurate mental model.

Judgment — Cognitive load theory usefully separates three demands in this setting. Intrinsic load comes from the inherent complexity of the production decision; extraneous load comes from avoidable interface, terminology, navigation, interruption, and training friction; germane load is effort spent forming usable schemas, meaning organized knowledge that allows several related elements to be treated as one. The manufacturing ergonomics literature uses these same terms and describes germane load as memory work involved in learning and schema development.[38] The design objective is not to eliminate all effort. It is to remove extraneous effort while preserving the productive effort needed to understand why an inventory or work-order action is correct.

General cognitive-load research supports that distinction. Sweller (1988, Cognitive Science, 'Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning')’s 1988 *Cognitive Science* article identifies domain-specific schemas as the principal difference between expert and novice problem solving.[77] It argues that conventional means–ends problem solving consumes substantial processing capacity that is consequently unavailable for schema acquisition.[77] This explains why asking low-experience cloud users to discover navigation paths, infer field meaning, and solve a production exception simultaneously is a poor substitute for guided practice.

Calisir and Calisir’s 2004 ERP-interface study relates usability characteristics to perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and end-user satisfaction; it does not directly measure cognitive load among production-floor supervisors aged 45–60 migrating from AS/400 to cloud ERP.

Qualitative reports of cognitive demands show the production context in which an ERP transition is used. The 2021 study of 50 assembly operators at three Swedish manufacturing companies found that cognitive and mental demands arose from task design, timing, physical loading, motivation, teamwork, and product-interface design.[38] Participants described unclear, incorrect, and information-heavy instructions as demanding; repeated notes could become unnoticed and add disorder.[38] They also reported that unfamiliar product variants affected available task time and that being behind schedule could lead to rushed mistakes.[38] In this environment, interface burden interacts with noise, time pressure, concurrent signals, and physical demands rather than occurring in isolation.[38]

This manufacturing evidence is directly relevant to a supervisor working on the floor, even though it is not an ERP-transition study and did not sample supervisors or managers.[38] It shows that a technically correct ERP workflow can still impose unsustainable demand if it requires reading dense instructions, retaining details across screens, or responding to exceptions while monitoring physical operations.

Clear, easy-to-read instructions, visual memory supports, practical guided learning, sufficient practice time, and support from experienced colleagues were all identified as resources that assist correct work.[38]

Change-management studies also show that cognitive burden is intertwined with, but is not identical to, resistance and technostress. Lapointe and Rivard (2005, MIS Quarterly, 'A Multilevel Model of Resistance to Information Technology Implementation1')’s 2005 *MIS Quarterly* study of three clinical-information-system implementations found that users first evaluate a system through the interaction of its features and their individual and organizational initial conditions, then project likely consequences; perceived threats lead to resistance behavior.[78] Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2005, MIS Quarterly, 'Understanding User Responses to Information Technology: A Coping Model of User Adaptation1')’s 2005 coping model similarly defines user adaptation as cognitive and behavioral effort in response to significant IT events, with strategies shaped by users’ assessments of expected consequences and their control over the situation.[79] These models mean that anxiety about visibility, loss of autonomy, or being judged incompetent can amplify the experience of a demanding new process.

The manufacturing ERP account makes that social dimension concrete: a shared ERP makes data entered at one location visible to central functions, other locations, and management, which workers accustomed to local autonomy may experience as oversight. It links the loss of local expertise and autonomy, plus increased performance visibility, to anxiety and resistance.[5] Thus, a supervisor who hesitates at a new cloud transaction may be managing both cognitive uncertainty and a perceived threat to status or discretion. Treating that behavior solely as a training deficit would miss a material part of the transition.

Technostress research identifies related work stressors. Ayyagari, Grover and Purvis’s 2011 *MIS Quarterly* study of 661 working professionals proposed links from usability characteristics, intrusiveness, and pace of change to work overload, role ambiguity, privacy invasion, work–home conflict, and job insecurity. Its results generally supported the model; work overload and role ambiguity were the two dominant stressors, while intrusive technology characteristics were the dominant predictors.[80] This is not a direct measure of cognitive load in manufacturing ERP migration, but it establishes that complexity, changing expectations, and ambiguous roles can create consequential technology-related strain.

Recent ERP-transition research similarly places workload in the early implementation period. Mengüç, Auh, Uslu and Yeniaras’s 2025 *Journal of Service Research* study of ERP introduction in 45 Istanbul retail stores surveyed 45 store managers and 368 frontline employees, an effective response rate of 62%.[81] It reports that users may need to adapt quickly, work more, unlearn old habits, and learn new processes; these demands can produce emotional exhaustion until employees become comfortable with the technology.[81] The study found an indirect crossover effect from managers’ technology-induced workload to employee workload through manager close monitoring.[82][81] Its setting is retail, not manufacturing, but the result warns against managing supervisor uncertainty by increasing monitoring pressure on floor employees.

The burden is especially salient after go-live. Haddara and Hetlevik (2016, Procedia Computer Science, 'Investigating the Effectiveness of Traditional Support Structures & Self-organizing Entities within the ERP Shakedown Phase')’s 2016 review calls this production period the ERP “shakedown phase” and reports that job satisfaction, technostress, and job stress can be highly affected after the system is up and running.[83] It recommends knowledge-management and advice networks because they can provide high-quality, timely, contextually appropriate information rather than relying only on traditional support structures.[83] A separate manufacturing-oriented account recommends a safe simulation environment in which users can practice without pressure or fear of disrupting live data.[5]

Judgment — For supervisors, contextual support means help attached to the production situation and transaction state, not a generic training library. It should answer the immediate question: what is happening, what must be checked, what action is safe, and when to escalate. That reduces the need to recall procedures from classroom training while a line, stock exception, or work order competes for attention.

Training design can reduce concentrated learning demand. In the 13-firm ERP study, technical-plus-business-process training and phased rollout helped firms address assimilation barriers.[37] A manufacturing implementation guide describes the contrasting risk of a big-bang rollout: all modules go live together and employees must learn all new processes at once.

It describes phased implementation as allowing staff to focus on one area before proceeding to another.[34] A multi-location manufacturing ERP account adds that warehouse operators, production planners, and financial controllers need different training; an undifferentiated programme can be too superficial for complex roles and too detailed for operational roles.[5]

The same account recommends two training episodes: first, early enough to be low stress, then a refresher immediately before go-live, balancing early forgetting against late unpreparedness. It also recommends continuing change management for at least three months after go-live for acceptance monitoring, additional training, and responses to resistance.[5] This supports a role-specific, repeated learning model for supervisors rather than a single pre-go-live course covering the entire cloud platform.

Practical manufacturing evidence supports that approach. In an Indian automotive-tooling SME ERP-Lean rollout, operators initially relied on established practices and resisted digital standard operating procedures and ERP validation checklists. Classroom sessions, live ERP simulations, Lean-champion mentorship, and Kaizen workshops that linked benefits to daily tasks gradually reduced resistance; early demonstrations of shorter tool-search time and fewer rejections built confidence.[33] Operator feedback in the same case reported that visual SOPs and digital checklists helped reduce cognitive load and improved first-time-right setups, although the account provides no load instrument or quantitative estimate.[33] This supports concrete, task-linked learning and visible feedback rather than abstract explanations alone.

Task-level workload measurement should be conducted during representative supervisor work and paired with performance rather than inferred from attendance, login frequency, or stated satisfaction. NASA-TLX is a subjective multidimensional workload instrument, whereas objective performance measures examine users’ speed and accuracy.[84][85] NASA-TLX is a subjective multidimensional workload instrument with six task-level dimensions: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, perceived performance, effort, and frustration.

Each dimension is rated from 0 to 100 in 5-point increments; weighted NASA-TLX uses pairwise comparisons and produces an overall 0–100 index, while Raw TLX omits weighting.[84] The instrument is useful here because a supervisor may report low mental demand but high time pressure or frustration, which require different remedies.

Subjective workload should be paired with performance. A cognitive-load measurement experiment assessed performance through accuracy and time on task: one point for each correct solution and seconds to submit the response.[85] Its authors found that perceived task difficulty and mental effort are distinct measures, and that ratings differ depending on whether collected immediately after each task or at the end of a learning phase.[85] For a migration evaluation, use the same wording and collection point at baseline, pilot, go-live, and shakedown; otherwise changes may reflect the measurement procedure rather than the transition.

Judgment — For each representative inventory-management and work-order scenario, record first-pass completion, error-free completion, time to completion, assistance or escalation, rework, and task-level Raw TLX or weighted TLX with the scoring method named. Ask supervisors to complete scenarios that include routine work, an exception, and an occasional advanced function. Segment results by prior AS/400 experience, shift, role, and frequency of use; aggregate results can conceal a high-load subgroup.

Age and technical familiarity make local testing essential. A 2021 cognitive-load study used 46 psychology students with a mean age of 22.4 years and explicitly cautioned that their learning capacity and working-memory functioning may be more optimal than those of older adults; the authors recommend replication with other age groups and tasks.[85] A 2024 ERP-workload survey of higher-education stakeholders used NASA-TLX and collected average daily ERP usage, psychological effort, physical effort, time pressure, task-completion expectations, hardness of ERP work, and stress; it found differences in perceptions of cognitive workload by gender and age.[86] Its review also reports higher workload among older faculty, attributed to steeper learning curves and lower technology familiarity.[86]

The conclusion for supervisors aged 45–60 with limited experience beyond a legacy system is therefore specific but bounded: their transition burden should be expected to include learning demand, uncertainty, and disruption of established routines, yet it should not be attributed to age alone. Role familiarity, interface clarity, production timing, prior cloud experience, local support, and perceived control all affect the result.[5][38]

1. Measure workload and performance on realistic supervisor tasks before rollout, during pilot use, immediately after go-live, and through the shakedown period.
2. Phase deployment and train by role, pairing system mechanics with the changed inventory and production process.
3. Provide a safe simulation tenant, contextual job aids, and rapid advice channels at the point of work.
4. Diagnose mental demand, time pressure, frustration, and error separately; a single adoption score cannot identify the design or change intervention required.

These findings support a transition design that protects routine production work with role-specific practice and contextual assistance while measuring cognitive burden as a task-and-workplace condition, not as a personal failure to adapt.[37][83][5][85][38]

The automotive-tooling SME ERP-Lean case reports operational outcomes separately from its cognitive-load feedback: inventory turnover rose by 2.3 turns per year, from 4.2 to 6.5, a 54.8% increase; traceability-resolution time fell by 2.7 days, from 3.5 to 0.8 days, a 77.1% reduction.[33]

The evidence separates into three different kinds of finding. **Task-level workload measures** assess perceived workload during a task and pair it with performance: NASA-TLX rates mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, perceived performance, effort, and frustration on task-level 0–100 scales, while the cognitive-load experiment measured performance as accuracy and seconds to submit a response.[84][85] **Qualitative reports of cognitive demands** describe what makes manufacturing work mentally demanding: the 2021 Swedish study interviewed 50 assembly operators at three companies, and its findings locate demands and resources in task design, timing, physical loading, motivation, teamwork, and product-interface design.[38] **ERP assimilation, resistance, and technostress outcomes** establish transition pressures without directly scoring task workload: Robey, Ross and Boudreau’s 2002 comparative study covered 13 industrial firms; Ayyagari, Grover and Purvis’s 2011 study analyzed field data from 661 working professionals; and Haddara and Hetlevik’s 2016 review addresses the post-production ERP shakedown phase.[37][80][83]

For manufacturing, it identifies intrinsic and extraneous load as the most relevant types: automation leaves complex, variable work to people, while unhelpful work structures, inadequate technological support, written instructions, manual data collection, and traditional tools add extraneous demand.[87]

This strengthens the interface implication for an AS/400-to-cloud move: preserve the supervisor’s attention for variable production decisions, and remove presentation, retrieval, and data-entry effort that does not change the decision.

Direct longitudinal evidence from a legacy-to-commercial-system transition establishes that workload can spike immediately after go-live and recover differently across users. A study of 74 pediatric nurses moving from a hybrid electronic-and-paper system to a commercial EHR measured data-entry and retrieval workload with NASA-TLX at baseline, after 1, 5, and 10 shifts, and four months after implementation.[84][88] Most participants had significant workload increases after one and five shifts; the rate at which workload abated was predicted by computer-attitude scores (p = 0.01).[88][84] The authors conclude that the early transition phase has substantially increased cognitive workload and call for support that accommodates variation between workers and can continue longer for some users.[88]

For production-floor supervisors, this adds a concrete measurement cadence to the ERP shakedown model: establish a pre-go-live workload baseline, measure after the first, fifth, and tenth production shifts, and measure again at four months, alongside task performance. Computer attitude and prior technology confidence belong in the baseline profile because adaptation rates differ with computer attitudes.[88]

Colligan, Potts, Finn and Sinkin’s July 2015 longitudinal study, 《Cognitive workload changes for nurses transitioning from a legacy system with paper documentation to a commercial electronic health record》, directly scored cognitive workload during a legacy-to-commercial-system transition.[89] It collected computer-attitude and computer-skills baseline data from 74 pediatric nurses, used NASA-TLX for data-entry and retrieval workload, and administered it before implementation, after 1, 5, and 10 shifts, and four months after implementation.[84][88]

The operators described unclear, incorrect, and information-heavy work instructions as demanding; noise, signals, and parallel-process monitoring as threats to focus; and methodical introductions, practical guided learning, precise readable instructions, visual memory support, sufficient practice time, and experienced-colleague support as work resources.[38]

A 2020 cognitive-manufacturing framework defines cognitive load as “the mental load that performing a specific task imposes on the human’s cognitive system.” It separates intrinsic load, the objective difficulty of a task; extraneous load, arising from how data or tasks are presented; and germane load, created through schema development for learning.[87] For an AS/400-to-cloud move, digital work instructions can progress with the worker through a complex procedure, present required data when needed, decrease stress, and remove common error sources; focused interactive digital-training modules can be designed for the exact assignment performed.[87]

In the 2025 SME ERP qualitative study, resistance to change appeared in 34.4% of Question 1 responses (112 of 326), while training and onboarding appeared in 31.6% of Question 3 responses (101 of 320). The counts show that both transition concerns were prominent in the respective question sets.[90]

Add navigation backtracking, time away from the task, and delayed-retention performance to the task measures. Measure these alongside completion time, error-free completion, help requests, and workload for both routine and exception scenarios before and after training.

## Measuring migration outcomes

Migration outcomes should show whether supervisors can run inventory and work-order processes accurately and independently in the new cloud ERP, not merely whether they attended training or logged in. For mid-sized manufacturers leaving AS/400, measurement must separate three outcomes: individual proficiency, transaction and data errors, and elapsed time until each supervisor can work independently in the live production context. ISO 9241-11 frames this around specified users, goals, tasks, resources, and environment; that makes the production floor, migrated data, shift conditions, and the supervisors’ limited cloud-software experience part of the measurement context rather than background detail.[12]

### How do successful migrations measure user proficiency gains?

**Proficiency gain** should mean an improvement in demonstrated independent performance of the supervisor’s actual role tasks. A useful definition is the demonstrated ability to perform a role effectively in the new operating environment, combining knowledge, practical skills, process and business understanding, decision-making, confidence, accuracy, efficiency, consistency, and appropriate system behaviour.[18] Course completion, attendance, and a knowledge-check pass are useful training records, but they are not proof that someone can execute a production transaction under normal operating conditions.

Dynamics 365 training, for example, awards a pass when a learner answers all module-assessment questions correctly, while its inventory module also includes an exercise to create and post an inventory journal.[91]

For AS/400-to-cloud supervisors, define two **role-realistic primary scenarios** before training begins: first, reconcile and post an inventory transaction; second, create and release a manufacturing order using the organisation’s actual routing, material, approval, and exception rules. Measure an **AS/400 pre-transition baseline** using equivalent business scenarios and the legacy data available in that system. Measure a separate **pre-instruction cloud-ERP assessment** before role-specific cloud training. Then assess the cloud workflow in a non-production pilot using migrated data, at go-live and during hypercare, and at 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live in normal production conditions.[12][19][18][92] The simulation environment enables consequential posting practice without disrupting the live information system, while user-acceptance testing uses real business scenarios and migrated inventory.[5][19][92] Migration user-acceptance testing already uses process owners completing real business scenarios with migrated data, including using migrated inventory and receiving against open purchase orders.[19] The repeated design turns a one-time test into a learnability measure: learnability is change in usability over time or comparison between experienced and inexperienced users.[13]

A role scorecard should combine four measures:

- **Independently correct completion:** Define a qualifying completion before testing as an accurate and complete task outcome without moderator, trainer, or floorwalker assistance and without a critical finding. Calculate independently correct completion rate as **(number of eligible task attempts completed independently, accurately and completely ÷ total eligible task attempts) × 100**. ISO defines effectiveness as the accuracy and completeness with which users achieve specified goals; moderator assistance means that the participant could not complete the task independently. [12][93][13]

- **Completion time:** Measure elapsed time from opening the task to posting the transaction or releasing the work order, and compare it with the supervisor’s own prior observations and with the agreed operating standard. ISO defines efficiency as resources used relative to results achieved; time and human effort are examples of those resources. [12]

For diagnostic task timing, separate navigation, decision, data-entry, validation, and recovery time where possible. A menu-structure study separated traversal into visual-search, decision, and operation time and found longer search time in deeper hierarchies.[122]

- **Transaction quality:** Check data completeness, transaction accuracy, correct process selection, appropriate master-data use, policy and control compliance, and the quality of downstream outputs. These checks reveal whether a supervisor reached the correct screen but created an unusable transaction. [18]

- **Operating independence:** Track support requests, manager observations, workarounds, offline spreadsheets, process bypasses, unused functions, incorrect transaction pathways, and inconsistent practices across shifts or sites. These behaviours show whether users have adopted the intended process rather than merely passing a controlled exercise. [18]

Confidence should be measured alongside demonstrated competence because users’ perceived capability affects adoption and willingness to use the system; confidence is not a substitute for competency.[18] The post-implementation ERP case study also distinguishes awareness—understanding how system output is produced, interpreted, and affects business processes—from knowledge of the commands and functions used to achieve a task purpose.[17] That distinction matters to designers: a supervisor who can repeat AS/400-like clicks may still be unable to resolve an inventory discrepancy or decide which work-order exception path is appropriate.

The decision rule is independently correct completion of both the inventory and manufacturing-order scenarios at the predeclared quality and time standard on two consecutive live-work attempts within the stated assessment window. Confirm the rule during hypercare and test retention again at 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live.[12][18][92] This rules out treating training attendance, a quiz pass, or self-reported confidence as the proficiency outcome.[18]

Add go-live readiness gates to the scorecard. In an AS/400-to-Dynamics 365 migration, DynaTech Consultancy used role-based training, process walkthroughs, and user documentation.[94] Bizowie recommends that all staff demonstrate minimum proficiency before go-live, while one SME respondent reported going live only after critical employees were trained and tested and major concerns were addressed.[95][90] Acumatica recommends role-based training with realistic business scenarios and hands-on exercises that cover both ERP use and changed responsibilities or workflows.[96]

Use pilots to test both routine and exception capability. Pemeco describes a Conference Room Pilot as testing the most probable 80% scenarios in every functional department and training core-team members; its Departmental Pilot tests all business processes, including 80% and 20% scenarios, validates updated and untested scenarios, resolves show-stopping issues, stress-tests ERP and infrastructure, and trains end users.[97] For supervisors, the two primary scenarios should therefore be supplemented with the actual legacy transactions that make up a day-in-the-life integrated pilot/UAT, including hand-offs between functional areas.[97] Acumatica likewise specifies that business users complete UAT, defects are documented and corrected, affected processes are retested, and formal user acceptance approval is obtained.[96]

Assess workload with performance rather than treating confidence or satisfaction as proficiency. NASA identifies the Bedford Scale and NASA Task Load Index (TLX) for determining whether a user can cognitively process information and execute actions within the required time; it calls for task analysis and iterative human-in-the-loop testing that measures workload, usability, and error.[98] NASA further links workload to usability and error rates, and specifies successful task performance within the appropriate timeframe and degree of accuracy as an interface-operability criterion.[98] This adds a fifth diagnostic field to the role scorecard—workload rating—so a slow, assisted inventory posting can be separated from a task that is accurate but imposes unsustainable cognitive demand.[98]

Davidson’s SME ERP study defines implementation success as meeting goals including performance improvement, strategic alignment, and user satisfaction, and identifies efficiency, system acceptance, and integration into organisational processes as outcome indicators.[90] Davidson measured user satisfaction, top-management support, and system quality as technical and organisational indicators; top-management support was positively associated with user satisfaction with an effect size of 0.749 and with implementation success with an effect size of 0.052, while user satisfaction was positively associated with implementation success with an effect size of 0.039.[90] Post-launch assessment should consequently retain satisfaction and usability-problem identification alongside the objective role-task scorecard.[90]

Report proficiency gain for each scenario as the follow-up independently correct completion rate minus the relevant baseline rate, in **percentage points**. Display the task mix, eligible attempts, assistance status, accuracy result, and completion time beside each baseline and follow-up rate, so a higher rate cannot conceal an easier scenario mix or slower performance.[12][13]

For occasional users, measure memorability as well as learnability: NASA uses memorability for systems used only occasionally.[99]

The Dynamics 365 manufacturing implementation guidance specifies a stronger transfer test: after a guided attempt, have the learner repeat the work with a different quantity or interruption, explain what the entry tells the next person, complete a representative activity without prompting, and describe the action for an exception. Exercises should also test decisions, role boundaries, required notifications, escalation, and authority.[75]

The assessed work includes finding assigned work, confirming applicable instructions, entering feedback, and recognising when an unexpected condition requires help.[75]

Assess transfer as well as rehearsal. Define inventory and work-order anchor tasks and collect legacy-system completion-time, error-rate, and proficiency baselines before migration; after a guided task, repeat it with a changed quantity or an interruption and ask the supervisor to explain what the entry communicates to the next person. Baseline tasks followed by these changed-condition repetitions show whether learning transfers beyond a rehearsed transaction.[21][75]

Build a skills matrix that maps every supervisor to the competency families required by the role and assigns a consistent proficiency level to each skill. Its four behavioural levels are Novice: cannot perform without direct oversight; Operator: performs under supervision or with reference aids; Autonomous: works independently and reliably meets cycle-time and quality targets; and Expert: trains others, troubleshoots, and owns SOP updates. Area supervisor and operator should co-validate the rating through gemba observation.[100]

Assess supervisors before and after training through practical demonstrations, written tests, and observation in real situations, then inspect each individual’s work and conduct regular performance evaluations. A standardized live-demonstration checklist, separately evaluated by the trainer and supervisor before sign-off, creates auditable evidence of mastery; mobile assessments can record the checklist and attach a video of the demonstrated task.[101][102]

For the AS/400-to-cloud supervisor cohort, measure proficiency and time-to-competency by observed autonomous performance rather than self-report. The operational target corresponds to Level 3, Autonomous: independent, reliable work meeting cycle-time and quality targets; a Level 4 result additionally covers training others, troubleshooting, and SOP ownership.[100][103]

Use a standardized checklist for each live skills demonstration, with the trainer and the supervisor separately evaluating the employee before signing off that the skill has been mastered.[102]

### How do successful migrations measure error reduction rates?

Measure errors by workflow and by origin. A migration can produce conversion-data defects, configuration defects, and supervisor execution errors; combining them in one number prevents a designer or change lead from identifying the right remedy. Data migration from an old system can be time-consuming and prone to inconsistencies and inaccuracies, while error analysis can also reveal shortcomings in knowledge, interface design, process clarity, role definition, embedded guidance, data quality, or governance.[104][18]

For **inventory transactions**, count qualifying supervisor execution errors such as incorrect quantity or valuation entries, wrong locations, incomplete processing, and transactions posted through the wrong pathway. Use **eligible inventory transactions posted** as the stable user-error-rate denominator. Report each period’s erroneous-inventory-transaction numerator, eligible-inventory-transaction denominator, and date range. Where one inventory transaction contains multiple qualifying errors, also report **error events per 100 eligible inventory transactions** separately from the percentage of erroneous transactions. Inventory variances by reason code are a diagnostic classification. **Counted-location accuracy** is reported separately as accurate counted locations divided by eligible counted locations, and **SKU accuracy** is reported separately as accurate SKU records divided by eligible SKU records.[18][20] **Migration reconciliation** is a distinct data-validity measure: compare source and target record counts, inventory on hand, valuation, critical fields, relationships, and equivalent legacy and cloud-ERP reports.[19]

An inventory-error classification can additionally distinguish wrong unit of measure or stock status, duplicate transactions, incorrect adjustment reason, and failed or reversed postings; ERP usability measures also define error rate for a specified task or scenario.[145]

For **work-order creation**, count incomplete or inaccurate order data, incorrect process or approval paths, wrong master-data selection, failures to release or route correctly, rework, and production-order transaction errors. An ERP post-implementation case found that advanced interaction enabled users to detect a mistake in an index used for production-order management; that is valuable error discovery, but it is distinct from an end-user work-order transaction-error rate.[17][18] The distinction protects against crediting improved defect detection as though it were evidence that fewer work orders were entered incorrectly.

Judgment — Calculate each workflow’s error rate as qualifying errors divided by its relevant exposure during the defined period. Use inventory transactions, counted locations, or SKU records as the denominator for inventory; use work orders created, released, or changed as the denominator for work orders. Calculate percentage error reduction as: baseline error rate minus post-go-live error rate, divided by baseline error rate, multiplied by 100. Report the baseline rate, post-go-live rate, numerator, denominator, and dates. A raw fall from 20 errors to 10 errors is not interpretable if transaction volume doubled.

Establish the baseline before implementation, run the pilot assessment before go-live, observe performance at go-live and during hypercare, and repeat the same measures at 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live.[20][92] “Before Metrics” are performance indicators tracked before ERP implementation to form the comparison baseline; project and post-implementation data can come from surveys, interviews, and system logs.[20] A practical weekly indicator is transaction errors per user per week; a continuing plateau after week three identifies a user group or workflow needing targeted follow-up.[36] The implementation team should track the metric at 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live as well as during hypercare, since short-term adaptation and sustained operating performance are different states.[92]

Set acceptance tolerances before migration testing. Reconciliation criteria should state what must match, what variance is acceptable, why an exception is accepted, and whether correction is required.[19] Continue validation after go-live by monitoring migration-related issues, reconciling critical reports, obtaining business-owner review of high-risk data, and tracking corrections through a defined resolution process.[19] This makes it possible to distinguish an incorrect inventory balance caused by conversion from an incorrect adjustment caused by a supervisor’s new-interface use.

Operational accuracy indicators are essential, but they answer a different question from interface error. One logistics case used a stock-value divergence tolerance of 0.015%, equal to a 99.85% compliance and accuracy target; it reported tolerance breaches in March and May 2019 and September 2020, followed by compliance in 2021.[104] The same case reported average SKU accuracy of 99.97% from January 2021 through July 2022.[104] These figures show inventory-control performance, not the number of supervisor navigation, comprehension, or data-entry errors.

**A credible error-reduction result is a workflow-specific, exposure-normalized before/after comparison with a declared period and error classification.** It rules out claims that automation, role controls, or training reduced user errors when the reported result has no rate, denominator, or separation of migrated-data defects from execution errors.[105][20]

### How do successful migrations measure time-to-competency?

**Time-to-competency** is elapsed calendar time from an individual supervisor’s first role-specific training session to the date that person first meets the predeclared independent-work threshold in live operations. It should not start at go-live, because users begin learning beforehand, and it should not end at training completion, because operational capability develops through application in hypercare and later independent work. The ERP proficiency progression is: controlled pre-go-live knowledge, real-condition application during hypercare, then greater independence, accuracy, and efficiency several months later.[18]

The predeclared threshold uses the same role scorecard: independently correct completion of practical inventory and manufacturing-order scenarios on two consecutive live-work attempts, manager validation, transaction-quality checks, production evidence, system-use evidence, error trends, and declining support demand. Use the 30-, 90-, and 180-day assessments to test retention after initial qualification.[12][18][92] Mature assessment links competency data with system usage, transaction quality, error rates, and support demand; practical simulations and manager validation sit between basic completion metrics and this operational evidence.[18] Record task success, accuracy, errors, time on task, and assistance at every observation.

Experimental usability measurement has used accuracy as correctly solved problems and time on task in seconds; ISO-derived measures similarly include the percentage of users completing a task, average accuracy, task-completion time, and tasks completed per unit time.[85][13]

Published durations should not become a common supervisor benchmark. One ERP-training article gives a general estimate of 3–4 months from first training session to proficiency for most roles.[36] By contrast, an ERP case reports that advanced interaction required almost two years after the end of shakedown.[17] These timelines measure different capability levels: the former is a general, unspecified proficiency claim, while the latter concerns advanced interaction after stabilisation.

Training, cutover, and competency have separate clocks. One integrated ERP-Lean implementation used a three-month shadowing phase in which digital processes ran in parallel with manual work.[33] Another case began implementation in June 2018, continued training and deployment through December 2018, and moved from the old system in January 2019.[104] These dates describe transition management and continuity, not the point at which a particular supervisor became independently competent. Similarly, migration rehearsals measure extraction, transformation, import, error processing, and total migration time within the cutover window; they do not measure human capability.[19]

Time-to-competency should therefore be reported per supervisor and per role scenario, with the threshold and observation dates stated explicitly: baseline before role training, pilot assessment, go-live and hypercare, and 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live. Use the non-production simulation environment for consequential posting tests. That approach distinguishes rapid completion of classroom training from durable capability to manage live inventory and work-order exceptions.[5][19][92]

### Synthesis

Successful migration measurement treats proficiency, errors, and time-to-competency as connected but non-interchangeable outcomes. Proficiency is repeated independent task performance; error reduction is an exposure-normalized workflow rate classified by origin; and time-to-competency is the elapsed time until a supervisor sustains the required live-work standard. The evidence supports baseline measurement, realistic migrated-data scenarios, repeated hypercare and later observations, transaction-quality review, and operational performance tracking.[12][19][18][20] General timelines can guide planning, but they do not replace a role-specific threshold; advanced ERP interaction may continue well after initial shakedown.[36][17]

1. Define inventory-posting and work-order-creation scenarios before training, including critical errors and quality criteria.
2. Capture AS/400 baseline task success, time, assistance, transaction quality, and error rates using the same denominators planned for cloud ERP.
3. Test supervisors at baseline before role training; in a pilot using migrated data in a non-production simulation environment; at go-live and during hypercare; and again at 30, 90, and 180 days after go-live. [5][19][92]
4. Report migration defects, configuration defects, and supervisor execution errors separately.
5. Record the staff time and other human effort required for observation, scorecard scoring, and transaction-quality review alongside task results at every assessment window; time and human effort are efficiency resources. [12][18]
6. Declare competence only when a supervisor completes both primary scenarios independently, accurately, and within the agreed operating standard on two consecutive live-work attempts within the stated window; record instruction and practice hours separately from elapsed calendar time and record the last observation for anyone not yet qualified. [12][18]

## Role-based views versus comprehensive dashboards

Judgment — For production-floor supervisors aged 45–60 moving from AS/400-style routines to cloud ERP, the interface should separate *doing daily work* from *monitoring the operation*. The daily workspace should make inventory exceptions and work-order actions immediately actionable; a dashboard should summarize conditions requiring attention, not become the default surface for every transaction, report, setting, and analytical tool.

### How well do simplified role-based views support primary-task efficiency

Progressive disclosure means showing a small set of important options first and making specialized options available on request. It is hierarchical: the user starts with a primary display, opens secondary options only if necessary, and commonly returns to the main view. This differs from staged disclosure, which moves users through a linear sequence of steps.[1] For inventory adjustments and production-order work, use progressive disclosure for occasional controls; use stages only where the transaction genuinely consists of separable, low-interaction steps. Staging is problematic when supervisors must repeatedly alternate between information, quantities, materials, and exceptions.[1]

NN/g’s rationale directly fits supervisors with limited experience outside a legacy system. For novice users, hiding advanced settings focuses attention on likely-useful functions, avoids mistakes, and avoids time spent considering irrelevant choices. Smaller initial displays can also save experienced users from scanning rarely used features. NN/g identifies learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate as the usability dimensions improved by progressive disclosure.[1] In complex entry forms, showing fields relevant to the current task and conditionally revealing further fields avoids attentional effort spent scanning and filtering irrelevant questions.[8]

SAP Fiori provides a documented role-based implementation of this principle. The launchpad displays apps according to the user’s role, and SAP recommends placing only the information and apps needed to begin daily business in a role space.[3] A space is an entry point for a business role; it can contain one or more pages that divide work into self-contained contexts.

SAP recommends selecting apps by examining the user’s day in the life: task importance, frequency, sequence, dependencies, recurring information needs, semantic groupings, and work contexts.[106] SAP also recommends lean pages with two to five sections and three to seven apps per section, and recommends “Quick Access” for important applications and “Insights” for important analytical apps.[106]

SAP’s Overview Inventory Management app (F2769) has seven named cards, including Recent Material Documents, blocked-stock overdue materials, stock values, warehouse throughput, purchase-order monitoring, and stock in transit. Users can rearrange, hide, or show cards, reset the layout, and create filter variants.[127]

The production-supervisor implementation is especially relevant. SAP’s Manage Production Orders app lists orders for the user’s area of responsibility, filtered by plant and work center selected at first use and adjustable later in settings.[59] SAP’s best-practice procedure has a Production Supervisor – Discrete Manufacturing user open Manage Production Orders and, in App Settings under Production Supervisors, leave Plant 1 IN 1810 and MTS DI- Valuated (YB1) assigned in the AOR Status column.[107] This is a practical model for an AS/400 migration: begin with the supervisor’s plant, work center, released orders, shortages, confirmations, and exception actions—not a company-wide production catalogue.

Role-based simplification must not mean removing authorized capability. SAP’s app finder is available from all Fiori screens, and apps omitted from a role page remain reachable through search and the app finder if they are in the user’s business catalog.[3][115] SAP navigation intents can also resolve differently according to the triggering user’s role, allowing the same business object and action to lead to an appropriate destination for that user.[117] In NetSuite, roles can set permissions, default and restricted forms, a dashboard, role-based preferences, search defaults, and department or location restrictions.[112]

Visibility remains necessary for recognition rather than recall. Recognition is easier because visible cues activate related information in memory; menus expose available commands for users to identify rather than remember.[22] Therefore, the secondary route must be visible, mechanically simple, and explicitly labeled for its contents.

A generic “More” label is weaker than a label such as Advanced filters: date, owner, status.[1][14] Do not place advanced inventory reconciliation, order-splitting, mass changes, or configuration behind multiple nested drawers: designs beyond two disclosure levels commonly cause users to get lost.[1][14]

**A simplified role-based workbench supports primary-task efficiency when it keeps observed daily and safety-critical actions at level one, while preserving a one-interaction, visible, named route to occasional advanced functions.**[1][9]

Examples of explicit secondary-action labels are “Resolve count variance,” “Order exceptions,” and “Advanced scheduling”; these indicate the destination more clearly than an ambiguous icon or a generic “More.”[1][146]

Persistent global navigation and progressive disclosure address different layers of the supervisor experience. A navigation-design article reports that hiding a website’s main navigation cuts discoverability almost in half, lengthens task time, and increases perceived task difficulty; it recommends visible horizontal tabs, a vertical sidebar, or both, so that users can recognize their location and available destinations. The same article assigns progressive disclosure to goal- and task-based interaction rather than to global navigation. For an AS/400-transition supervisor, keep the role workspace, current plant/work-center context, and route to other authorized areas persistently visible; progressively reveal specialized transaction controls within the workspace.[114]

Persistent global navigation and progressive disclosure address different layers and can be combined rather than treated as competing choices: keep global navigation visible so users can recognize their location and available navigation, while applying progressive disclosure to goal- or task-based actions. Keep task controls in fixed, learnable locations and vary only how much is expanded.[114][118]

### How well do comprehensive dashboards support primary-task efficiency

Comprehensive dashboards support supervisory awareness, prioritization, and decision-making. In an SME automotive-mould manufacturing implementation, ERP dashboards showed die setup status, cycle completion, rejection count, and material shortages. Shop supervisors used them to pre-plan tool changes, allocate manpower, track downtime sources, and troubleshoot deviations.[33] The same case describes dashboards and automated alerts as supporting sustained performance monitoring through real-time visibility of deviations from setup-time and quality thresholds over the 12 months after implementation.[33]

NetSuite documents a role-dependent dashboard at login. Its home page is a collection of real-time data whose content depends on the user role; portlets can provide direct access to raw data or show synthesized information such as KPIs, scorecards, trend graphs, and report snapshots.[4] The Warehouse Manager demonstration shows the useful distinction between operational reminders and executive monitoring: cycle-count approvals and items to count were presented as daily action reminders, while inventory trend and order trend report snapshots were described as helpful at executive level but not day-to-day tools.[108]

Dashboards can also preserve advanced access without keeping every detail in view. NetSuite users can personalize most dashboard configurations according to their role; administrators can share a configured dashboard with users whose roles share the same center.[113] The Shortcuts portlet gives access to commonly used records, transactions, lists, and reports, and the same shortcuts also appear in a navigation-menu shortcut list.[111] SAP overview pages similarly use actionable cards for daily tasks, smart filters to populate card contents, and variants to retain alternate layouts for complex filtered lists.[110]

A dashboard is not automatically a good primary transaction interface. SAP’s overview-page guidance says to display only cards supporting daily work and to avoid overwhelming users with too much information.[110] In one ERP case, 30% of overall functionality was removed 18 months after go-live because unused functions burdened windows and the graphical user interface, created superfluous maintenance cost, and complicated other functionality.[17] In the same case, logistics and finance users described interaction as intricate; the logistics manager reported that four additional employees were needed to perform the same activities after almost one year in the onward/upward phase.[17]

Dashboard controls can themselves be hidden. In NetSuite, portlet controls appear only when the pointer moves over the portlet; titles may be underlined only on hover to show that a record can be opened.[4] This creates a design tension: a dashboard can display large amounts of status information, yet its actionable controls may still depend on users noticing transient cues.

For supervisors new to cloud UI conventions, make routine action links permanently visible in a designated action zone rather than relying on hover-only discovery.[4]

**Comprehensive dashboards are strongest for at-a-glance shop-floor monitoring, exceptions, planning, and cross-context decisions; they are not documented as superior for routine inventory posting or work-order creation.**[33][12]

In the automotive-tooling SME’s combined ERP-Lean implementation, scheduling accuracy increased by 25 percentage points, from 68% to 93%, equivalent to 36.8% relative growth.[33]

### Do simplified role-based views or comprehensive dashboards better support users

Judgment — Rank simplified role-based views with progressive disclosure first, and comprehensive dashboards second, for this target group. The deciding criterion is independent, accurate, and efficient routine inventory and work-order work with discoverable authorized exceptions.[12][106] Put high-frequency, urgent, safety-critical, and routine shift actions at level one; provide low-frequency, low-criticality advanced actions through one visible, named secondary route, contextual object actions, and persistent search or app-finder access.

Determine this split from task analysis, shop-floor observation, instrumented feature-use data, support logs, and workflow dependencies.[1][9][3][14] Use a role-based primary workspace, a concise role-tailored operational overview, and a shallow advanced route; assign a named role/workspace owner to update role permissions, secondary links, and escalation routes as duties change.[106][112][119] Move the comprehensive dashboard into first place when matched supervisor tests show at least equal routine-task success, speed, and training-to-competency with materially better advanced-action discovery, or when continuous exception monitoring is the supervisor’s primary job.[106][113][112][119] Measure routine and occasional tasks with completion success, elapsed time, human effort, mistakes, first-use learning, training-to-competency, and infrequent reuse.[33][9][12]

The strongest documented pattern is a hybrid rather than a choice between two isolated screen types. SAP supports role spaces containing role-assigned work contexts, an optional Overview page for the most important apps across contexts, top-level navigation across assigned spaces, and direct launch of rarely used apps through the app finder.[106] This supports a three-part architecture: first, a role-based primary workspace; second, a concise operational overview; third, a shallow advanced route for infrequent but authorized work.

The target supervisor should see daily actions rather than all possible controls. Place inventory availability, material-shortage exceptions, cycle-count approvals, released-order actions, work-center priorities, and safety- or production-critical warnings on the primary workspace. Retain advanced filters, detailed analytics, mass maintenance, cross-plant analysis, and uncommon correction functions through a labeled advanced panel, a contextual action on the affected object, search, or an app finder. This follows the documented rule that frequent functions belong in the initial display and specialized options should be requested only rarely.[1][9]

The major exception is criticality. A warning, stockout, release block, quality hold, or daily-used advanced control must not be hidden merely because it is technically advanced. Progressive-disclosure guidance warns that a hidden convenience costs a click while a hidden alarm can cause an incident.[9] It also advises against hiding information needed to complete essential tasks or forcing users through excessive layers that interrupt work.[120] Conversely, a dashboard should not display every possible analytical measure just because it can: NetSuite’s broad KPI scorecards include inventory value, COGS, turnover, and days on hand, but its Warehouse Manager presenter distinguished such snapshots from day-to-day operational questions.[108]

In the author’s enterprise-dashboard redesign case, common-task completion improved by 35%, advanced-feature use increased by 20%, and average training time fell from five days to three days, a 40% reduction in onboarding time.[10] Treat those figures as a design-case outcome, not as a target for mid-sized manufacturers moving from AS/400 systems.

Judgment — Test the *Frequency–Criticality Split* before configuration: high-frequency or high-criticality functions stay visible; low-frequency and low-criticality functions move to one discoverable secondary layer. Determine the split through task analysis, shop-floor observation, instrumented feature-use data, support logs, and workflow dependencies—not by job-title assumptions alone.[1][9][14]

Training should reinforce the same structure. Teach the five to ten transactions each transactional role performs daily to mastery, then rehearse exceptions and advanced functions in their real production context rather than in a separate, generic tutorial.[36] Contextual tips are more useful than broad tutorial material because users can recognize actions when the cue appears on the page where the action is needed.[22] This matters for occasional reuse: ISO 9241-11 treats both learning by new users and infrequent use as usability conditions, and defines usability through effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context.[12]

The reported redesign-case satisfaction score rose by 1.2 points on a five-point scale, from 3.2 to 4.4; this is a 37.5% increase relative to the starting score.[10]

### Synthesis —

Simplified role-based views with progressive disclosure are the stronger default for AS/400-transition supervisors because they focus daily inventory and work-order activity on the supervisor’s plant, work center, exceptions, and actions, while reducing irrelevant scanning and choice.[1][8][59][106] Comprehensive dashboards add real value for monitoring, exceptions, planning, and cross-context visibility, but their breadth becomes counterproductive when unused features or metrics obscure routine work.[33][17][110] The resolution is a hybrid: a selective role-based workbench, a limited role-tailored overview, and a visible one-step route to advanced functions. Persistent recognition should apply to routine actions, urgent consequences, and the route to secondary tools—not to every advanced control.[1][22][106][120]

1. Configure a supervisor workbench around daily inventory and production-order actions, scoped to the responsible plant and work center.
2. Put urgent exceptions and consequences visibly on the primary page; do not hide warnings or daily-used controls.
3. Provide one named Advanced route, contextual object actions, and persistent search/app-finder access for uncommon authorized work.
4. Use a dashboard for shift monitoring, triage, KPIs, and planning, but limit it to information that supports daily supervisory decisions.
5. Validate both routine and occasional tasks with representative supervisors, measuring completion success, elapsed time, mistakes, effort, first-use learning, and infrequent reuse. [12][15]

### Open questions

The unresolved comparison is the task-completion rate and time-to-competency difference between progressive disclosure and persistent full navigation for the specified AS/400-experienced production supervisors. It would be settled by a counterbalanced plant usability trial using equivalent inventory and work-order scenarios, the same permissions and data, and predefined accuracy, assistance, error, task-time, delayed-reuse, and competency criteria. The appropriate frequency split for advanced functions also remains local: it should be determined from observed supervisor work, transaction logs, and repeated-use patterns after go-live.
