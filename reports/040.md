# Mechanistic Controversies in Cellular Senescence and Aging

## Executive summary

The best-supported verdict is that cellular senescence is a heterogeneous, time-dependent programme in which DNA-damage signalling commonly initiates arrest, mitochondrial dysfunction often amplifies and maintains it, and chromatin remodelling consolidates it.

These mechanisms jointly explain the paradox: early or transient senescence suppresses cancer by stopping damaged or oncogene-stressed cells from dividing, whereas persistent senescent cells can impair regeneration and alter tissue environments through arrest, metabolic dysfunction and selected secretory outputs.

SASP factors can be causative in particular contexts, but neither p16 expression nor a SASP signature establishes that a cell is pathogenic, and spatial transcriptomic co-localization does not by itself prove a causal SASP mechanism.

Mouse clearance and genetic models support a contribution of persistent senescent cells to some age-associated phenotypes, while human dasatinib-plus-quercetin trials remain evidence of short-term feasibility and measured disease-relevant outcomes rather than lifespan extension or proof that senolysis corrected a specific initiating mechanism.

Baker et al.'s 2011 experiments make timing decisive: benefits of clearance depend on when senescent cells emerge, how long they persist, which tissue they occupy, and whether removal sacrifices a still-useful tumour-suppressive or wound-repair response.

## Mechanistic frameworks of cellular senescence in aging

Cellular senescence is a stress response in which a cell enters growth arrest, resists apoptosis, can contribute to stem-cell exhaustion, and produces pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors that alter its tissue environment.[1] The central controversy is not whether DNA damage, mitochondrial change, or chromatin reorganization occur in senescent cells: all three do.

It is whether one is the initiating lesion, which processes lock the arrest in place, and which ones create the inflammatory and regenerative consequences that matter in aging.

**The three named frameworks are best read as linked layers of a senescence program rather than mutually exclusive explanations.** The DNA damage response (DDR) framework most directly explains how damaged genomes initiate arrest; the Passos mitochondrial framework explains how a delayed metabolic feedback loop can stabilize and amplify it; and the Narita chromatin framework explains how altered genome packaging can consolidate durable repression of proliferation genes while reshaping gene expression. The balance among them varies with cell type, initiating stress, and time since induction.[2][3][4]

### How does the DNA damage response model associated with d'Adda

The DNA damage response (DDR) is the cell’s surveillance-and-repair system for DNA lesions. A DNA double-strand break, or a chromosome end that has lost its protective structure, activates sensor and signalling proteins that pause cell division while repair is attempted. If the signal is transient and damage is resolved, the cell can resume cycling; if it persists, the cell can enter senescence, a stable growth arrest.[5][6][7]

In d’Adda di Fagagna and colleagues’ 2003 Nature study, *A DNA damage checkpoint response in telomere-initiated senescence*, the initiating lesion was telomere dysfunction. Telomeres are the protein-capped repetitive DNA structures at chromosome ends. Their protective T-loop structure normally prevents chromosome ends from being mistaken for broken DNA. In human fibroblasts, when telomeres could no longer fulfil that protective role, senescent cells displayed phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX) foci together with 53BP1, MDC1 and NBS1, and contained activated CHK1 and CHK2 checkpoint kinases. Chromatin-immunoprecipitation and genome-scanning experiments further found that chromosome ends themselves contributed directly to the DDR and that uncapped telomeres associated with many, though not all, DDR proteins.[8]

The fibroblasts examined by d’Adda di Fagagna et al. (2003) included senescent MRC5 and BJ human fibroblasts.[8]

The molecular sequence is therefore: first, a dysfunctional telomere or another DNA lesion is detected; second, DDR sensors and kinases such as ATM, ATR, DNA-PK, CHK1 and CHK2 transmit the signal; third, p53 induces the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21; and fourth, p21 and, often later, p16INK4a prevent cyclin-dependent kinases from inactivating retinoblastoma protein (RB). Active RB suppresses E2F transcription factors, which normally turn on genes needed for DNA synthesis and cell-cycle progression. The result is a durable barrier to S-phase entry.[9][10][11][2]

d’Adda di Fagagna et al. supplied functional as well as descriptive evidence. Inactivating DDR checkpoint kinases in senescent cells restored progression into S phase, indicating that checkpoint signalling participates in maintaining the arrest rather than merely marking cells that have already stopped dividing. In a related human-fibroblast study, Chk2 inactivation reduced p21 and extended proliferative lifespan; transient Chk2 knockdown in established senescent cultures increased BrdU incorporation and reduced senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity, although the cells returned to senescence after the siRNA effect waned.[8][10]

Later telomere studies found that cells can continue proliferating with a small number of telomere-associated DNA-damage signals; increasing numbers of dysfunctional telomeres accompany the approach to senescence.[8][154]

That result defines the framework’s core claim: persistent damage signalling can be both an **initiator** and a **maintenance mechanism**. It does not mean that every DNA-damage focus must remain physically unrepaired forever. Passos et al. found that during stress-induced premature senescence, about half of observed damage foci were short-lived; if damage was unresolved for one to two days, however, a self-renewing ROS–DDR programme could keep signalling above the threshold needed to establish stable arrest. Their estimate of roughly five damage foci per nucleus was specific to their experimental conditions and cell types.[12]

The telomere version of the DDR model has two routes. The classical replicative route follows repeated cell division: because conventional replication cannot completely copy chromosome ends, telomeres progressively shorten until one or a few uncapped ends trigger a DDR. The second route does not require short telomeres. Genotoxic stress, including irradiation and oxidative injury, can generate telomere-associated damage foci (TAF) at telomeres of normal length, including in non-dividing cells. Thus, telomeres function not only as a replication clock but also as sites unusually prone to persistent damage signalling.[13][2]

Hewitt et al. measured this second route in male C57BL/6 mouse liver and small intestine at 3, 12, 22, 36 and 42 months, with three mice in each age group. Mean TAF per cell rose exponentially with age in hepatocytes and enterocytes, while the fraction of damage foci that were telomere-associated increased linearly. The authors observed this in tissues from mice with long telomeres and active telomerase, arguing against a simple explanation in which only progressive telomere shortening causes the age-associated signal.[7]

Persistent telomeric signalling has a plausible molecular basis. Telomere-associated proteins prevent inappropriate end joining, which protects chromosome ends from fusion but can also impede repair of lesions at telomeric DNA. Telomeric sequences are especially sensitive to oxidative modification, and oxidative injury can reduce binding of the protective factors TRF1 and TRF2. The exact position of a break within a telomere-associated focus remains unresolved because γH2AX spreads hundreds of kilobases beyond telomeric repeats; a TAF therefore localizes the DDR to a telomeric region, not necessarily to the physical chromosome terminus itself.[7]

Telomere-dysfunction-induced damage foci can retain telomere DNA and the protective proteins TRF2 and RAP1 without end-to-end chromosome fusions, indicating that a partially dysfunctional chromosome end can signal damage without being completely deprotected.[154]

The framework explains tumor suppression directly. A cell with a damaged genome, a dysfunctional telomere, or oncogene-driven replication stress is prevented from continuing to divide and passing genetic errors to daughter cells. Oncogene-induced senescence follows an initial hyperproliferative phase in which excessive origin firing and stalled replication forks generate genomic damage and engage ATM, ATR, CHK1 and CHK2. Experimental loss of DDR components can allow escape from oncogene-induced senescence and promote transformation.[2][3][11]

The same checkpoint logic creates an aging cost. In tissues that depend on stem and progenitor-cell proliferation, p53–p21 and p16–RB arrest can limit repair, replenishment and regeneration. Telomere loss and uncapping in telomerase-deficient mice are associated with tissue atrophy, stem-cell depletion, organ failure and impaired responses to injury. In a proposed stem-cell model, the p53 response to critically short telomeres reduces mobilisation of stem cells: this lowers the opportunity for abnormal clones to expand but, if prolonged, causes tissue degeneration.[14][15]

The DDR model also reaches beyond arrest through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), the changing collection of cytokines, growth factors, proteases and other molecules released by some senescent cells. Rodier et al. showed in human fibroblasts that 0.5 Gy X-irradiation induced a transient DDR: damage foci resolved within 10 hours, cells resumed growth, and IL-6 remained at control levels. At 10 Gy, persistent damage foci and arrest appeared first, followed by five- to six-fold higher IL-6 and IL-8 secretion within two to four days.[16][17]

ATM, NBS1 and CHK2 were necessary for this cytokine output. ATM depletion reduced IL-6 secretion 50-fold and IL-8 secretion 10-fold among the selected factors, while nine of 16 measured secreted factors were essentially unchanged. The DDR therefore regulates an important inflammatory subset of the SASP, not one uniform secretory programme.[17]

Crucially, arrest and inflammatory propagation are separable outputs. Fibroblasts driven into arrest by p16INK4a expression had few persistent damage foci and little IL-6 secretion, whereas cells senescing under DNA-damaging conditions had both persistent foci and high IL-6. Damaged cycling cells, and cells that had bypassed senescence, could also produce the cytokine response. The DDR model consequently cannot equate p16 positivity, permanent arrest, persistent DDR and a pro-inflammatory SASP as interchangeable properties of every individual cell.[16][17]

**The DDR framework therefore explains senescence in aging as a protective checkpoint that becomes pathogenic when persistent damage signals accumulate in cells whose continued arrest, cell loss, or inflammatory communication compromises tissue renewal.** It establishes a strong route into senescence but does not make short telomeres the universal initiating lesion: intestinal crypt enterocytes in old mice showed largely non-telomeric γH2AX foci, and DDR-independent senescence has been reported in several experimental settings.[18][3]

### How does the mitochondrial dysfunction hypothesis associated with Passos et

Mitochondrial dysfunction refers to impaired mitochondrial performance, often including reduced respiratory capacity per mitochondrion, reduced membrane potential, altered metabolic flux, and dysregulated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. ROS are chemically reactive oxygen-containing molecules that can act in normal signalling but can damage proteins, lipids and DNA when chronically excessive or misplaced. In senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction is not one invariant state: senescent cells can have increased mitochondrial mass while producing less ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and showing lower membrane potential and more ROS.[19][20][21]

Passos et al.’s 2010 Molecular Systems Biology study, *Feedback between p21 and reactive oxygen production is necessary for cell senescence*, did not propose a simple mitochondria-first model. It identified a delayed positive-feedback loop: first, telomere-dependent or telomere-independent DDR activates p53 and its target CDKN1A, which encodes p21; second, sustained p21 signalling passes through GADD45A, p38MAPK, GRB2, TGFBR2 and TGFβ; third, this programme induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS; fourth, the ROS replenish short-lived DNA-damage foci, sustaining DDR signalling and locking cells into deep senescence after a delay of several days.[12][22]

The temporal order is important. In irradiated MRC5 human fibroblasts exposed to 20 Gy, mitochondrial superoxide and cellular peroxide signals did not rise immediately. They increased after 24 hours and remained elevated from day 2 onward; increased mitochondrial mass and lower mitochondrial membrane potential appeared in the same delayed phase. In this model, mitochondrial dysfunction is a consequence of the initial DDR and a feedback maintainer of senescence, rather than the experimentally demonstrated initiating event.[12]

After 20 Gy irradiation of MRC5 fibroblasts, the delayed mitochondrial changes included UCP-2 induction and roughly twofold higher oligomycin-resistant oxygen consumption.[29][155]

After 20 Gy irradiation of MRC5 fibroblasts, Passos et al. also observed increased *UCP2* transcription and roughly twofold higher oligomycin-resistant oxygen consumption, consistent with mitochondrial uncoupling. Dominant-negative TRF2-induced telomere dysfunction produced comparable delayed mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, loss of growth potential and γH2AX foci.[29]

Passos et al. further concluded that the p21–ROS loop was necessary and sufficient for stability of growth arrest during senescence establishment. This challenges a strictly lesion-centred interpretation in which one persistent irreparable focus must itself continually maintain arrest. The feedback loop can replace resolved lesions with newly generated stochastic damage, maintaining a signalling threshold long enough for further mechanisms, including p16 activation, autocrine signals and chromatin remodelling, to secure a more stable arrest.[12]

In Passos et al.’s fibroblast experiments, blocking components of the p21, p38 or TGFβ pathways reduced both reactive oxygen species and DNA-damage foci; scavenging reactive oxygen species reduced damage foci and p21 induction.[29][155]

Antioxidants preserved colony formation after otherwise irreversible p21 exposure, whereas a non-antioxidant analogue did not.[29][155]

After irradiation, inhibiting p21, p38/MAPK14 or TGFβ signalling reduced ROS and DNA-damage-response foci; combined blockade was not additive, consistent with these components acting in one pathway. ROS scavenging also reduced damage foci and p21 induction.[29][155]

In an inducible p21 system, antioxidants preserved colony formation after five days of p21 expression, including after p21 was switched off; a chemically similar non-antioxidant control did not.[29][155]

Mitochondria can nevertheless be upstream initiators in other settings. Mitochondrial ROS may damage nuclear DNA and telomeres, and reducing mitochondrial ROS production or release has been reported to postpone telomere-dependent senescence. Mitochondrial impairment can also expose mitochondrial DNA or transcription factor A, mitochondrial (TFAM), which act as damage-associated molecular patterns and engage innate-immune pathways, including cGAS–STING. Such pathways can stimulate inflammatory signalling without requiring the same lesion sequence as classical telomere erosion.[23][12][20]

A distinct case is mitochondrial dysfunction-associated senescence (MiDAS). In Wiley and colleagues’ MiDAS experiments, mitochondrial dysfunction lowered the cytosolic NAD+/NADH ratio, activated AMPK, and then activated p53 to impose growth arrest. Pyruvate prevented MiDAS whereas an antioxidant did not, and the cells showed no evidence of nuclear DNA damage. That result supports a metabolic redox-to-AMPK-to-p53 route in that model, rather than ROS-driven nuclear damage as the immediate cause.[24]

MiDAS also demonstrates that senescence does not produce a standard SASP. Its secretory phenotype lacked the canonical IL-1-dependent arm, including IL-6 and IL-8 in the reported human-cell experiments, but included IL-10, TNF-α and CCL27, with other factors such as amphiregulin remaining present. Conditioned medium from mitochondrially compromised cells inhibited adipocyte differentiation in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and promoted keratinocyte differentiation, showing that MiDAS-derived secreted material can alter neighbouring cells even though those experiments did not identify the responsible individual factors.[24]

Mitochondrial removal separates features of senescence that are often bundled together. Correia-Melo and colleagues used Parkin-driven mitophagy to deplete mitochondria from human fibroblasts. Across oxidative-stress, oncogene and replicative-exhaustion conditions, mitochondrial depletion reduced pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory senescence features while cells retained ATP through compensatory glycolysis. RNA sequencing found that 38% of all senescence-associated transcriptional changes, and 60% of significantly changed SASP genes, were reversed after mitochondrial depletion.[25][26]

Removing mitochondria after senescence was established reduced SASP activity, ROS, heterochromatin modifications, p21 and p16, but did not restart proliferation. Reduction of mitochondrial content through mTORC1 inhibition or PGC-1β deletion also prevented senescence in aging mouse liver. These results place mitochondrial activity prominently in maintenance of the pro-inflammatory and metabolic phenotype, while showing that an established cell-cycle block can persist when those features are suppressed.[27][28]

In the mitochondrial-depletion experiments, cells still divided very slowly before stopping proliferation by day 20.[156][26]

The mitochondrial framework offers a route to age-related pathology through escalating feedback. Dysfunctional mitochondria can add ROS-mediated damage; ROS and mitochondrial material can activate inflammatory pathways; and SASP and ROS can stabilize senescence or induce senescence-like responses in neighbouring cells. The p21–ROS feedback described by Passos et al. (2010) is cell-intrinsic; it does not itself establish that a senescent cell induces senescence in its neighbours. Hydrogen peroxide diffusion and SASP- or ROS-mediated neighbouring-cell senescence describe propagation routes.[29][25][19]

The same framework accommodates the tumor-suppression/pathology paradox. Mitochondrial stress can engage p53–p21 or p16–RB arrest, preventing metabolically abnormal or genomically damaged cells from proliferating. Yet mitochondrial-driven senescence may generate inflammatory, differentiation-altering or paracrine effects that degrade tissue function when cells persist. Mitochondrial depletion’s retention of arrest while reducing the SASP suggests a possible mechanistic separation between the anti-proliferative cancer barrier and some pro-aging outputs.[20][27][21]

**Passos et al. supports an integrated DDR–mitochondria model: mitochondria are potent initiators in some metabolic or oxidative contexts, but in the 2010 experiments they principally formed a delayed feedback system that stabilised DDR-induced arrest.** This rules out treating mitochondrial dysfunction as either merely an inert consequence or a universal first cause; its causal position depends on the stressor and senescence subtype.[12][24][20]

Antioxidants reduced but did not abolish DNA-damage foci in telomere-dependent senescence in the Passos et al. experiments.[29]

Mitochondrial depletion reversed 60% of significantly changed SASP genes but 38% of all senescence-associated transcriptional changes: a 1.58-fold enrichment of reversal among SASP genes. In the Parkin-expressing MRC5-fibroblast system, this quantitative enrichment supports the conclusion that mitochondrial activity is disproportionately important for the secretory programme relative to the aggregate senescence transcriptome.[26]

Mitochondrial dysfunction also helps explain stochastic heterogeneity in telomere-dependent senescence: fibroblasts need not enter arrest at one identical telomere length or division count. Cell-to-cell differences in mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial function can alter telomere-associated damage and the timing at which individual cells commit to senescence.

The timing of stabilization is experimentally resolvable in the Passos et al. (2010) system. The DDR–ROS loop was necessary and sufficient to maintain arrest for at least 6–10 days after irradiation; up to 30% of cells regained Ki67 positivity when MAPK14 inhibition or radical scavenging began within 9 days. Rescue became progressively less effective after day 9, while SAHF and HP-1γ foci were low at day 6 and increased during subsequent weeks in parallel with irreversible arrest. This directly supports a temporal sequence from checkpoint and metabolic-feedback dependence to chromatin-stabilized deep senescence.[30]

### How does the chromatin remodeling framework associated with Narita et

Chromatin is the DNA–protein material that packages the genome and controls access to genes. Chromatin remodelling means reorganizing that packaging so that some genes become easier and others harder to express. The chromatin framework associated with Narita et al.’s August 2006 Cell paper, *A novel role for high-mobility group a proteins in cellular senescence and heterochromatin formation*, treats senescence as an actively built transcriptional state, not simply a cell that passively stopped cycling.[31][4]

Judgment — Narita et al.’s central concept is the senescence-associated heterochromatin focus (SAHF): a conspicuous domain of condensed, transcriptionally repressive chromatin that develops in some senescent cells. High-mobility group A (HMGA) proteins act as architectural chromatin proteins in this programme. Their accumulation and redistribution help reorganize chromatin so that proliferation-associated genes, particularly E2F-regulated genes required for DNA replication, remain switched off.

In DAPI-stained nuclei from the human-cell systems studied, senescence-associated heterochromatin foci appear as approximately 30–50 bright puncta and are more resistant to nuclease digestion than chromatin in proliferating cells.[157][45]

In senescent human cells studied for chromatin organization, senescence-associated heterochromatin foci appeared as approximately 30–50 bright DAPI-stained nuclear foci. They were more nuclease-resistant than chromatin in growing cells and incorporated the proliferation-associated *CCNA2*/*cyclin A* gene.[45]

The arrest machinery links this chromatin programme to tumor suppression. RB is uniquely important in senescence for repression of a subset of DNA-replication genes; unlike quiescence, where RB, p107 and p130 act redundantly, senescent cells rely on RB for this specific repression. Because the p16INK4a–RB and p53–p21–RB pathways prevent E2F-dependent S-phase entry, chromatin compaction and RB-dependent transcriptional silencing make a proliferative pause more durable.[3][32][33]

The proliferation-promoting loci incorporated into and repressed by these heterochromatin foci include the E2F target gene *cyclin A*.[157][45]

The framework therefore assigns chromatin remodelling its strongest role in **maintenance and consolidation**. DNA damage, telomere dysfunction, oncogene activity, oxidative stress or metabolic disturbances may initiate a checkpoint response. HMGA- and RB-associated heterochromatin then supplies an additional memory system: it reprogrammes gene accessibility and reinforces exclusion from the cell cycle even when upstream signalling fluctuates. Consistent with this interpretation, replicative senescence in BJ fibroblasts could be reversed by p53 depletion, whereas attempts to reverse WI38 fibroblast senescence were less successful when p16–RB activity was intact; WI38 cells were also more able to form SAHF.[3]

In activated-RAS human-cell models, ectopic expression of either histone chaperone HIRA or ASF1a accelerated senescence-associated heterochromatin-focus formation, whereas ASF1a knockdown blocked it. HMGA depletion abolished the foci throughout their formation, while p16^INK4a was principally required at the initial stage.[45][158]

Chromatin changes also connect senescence to secretion. HIRA is a histone chaperone, meaning a protein that deposits histones into chromatin. In senescent human cells, HIRA and the histone variant H3.3 redistributed with transcriptional changes; 1,919 genes newly acquired both HIRA and H3.3, and these genes were enriched for TNF-signalling functions, a component of inflammatory SASP biology. This finding links chromatin remodelling to altered gene expression but does not establish that the redistribution itself causes tissue dysfunction.[34]

In Ras-induced senescence, expression of the histone chaperones HIRA or ASF1a accelerates heterochromatin-focus formation, whereas ASF1a knockdown blocks formation induced by activated Ras.[157][45][159][158]

The chromatin route into inflammatory signalling also includes cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs), pieces of nuclear chromatin that appear in the cytoplasm. cGAS detects such DNA-containing fragments and activates STING and NF-κB, which can drive SASP transcription. Mitochondrial dysfunction can promote CCF formation through ROS and JNK signalling, providing a direct intersection between the mitochondrial and chromatin frameworks.[19][35]

DNA damage can likewise alter chromatin-mediated inflammatory output. GATA4 is a transcription factor whose autophagic degradation is inhibited by DDR activity; its accumulation activates a TRAF3IP2–NF-κB cascade and induces SASP-related genes independently of p53 status. Thus p53 may enforce arrest while GATA4–NF-κB helps govern secretory activity, reinforcing the broader conclusion that growth arrest and SASP production are mechanistically separable.[36][37] (Kang et al., 2015)[37]

Chromatin restructuring provides a coherent tumor-suppression mechanism. Cells experiencing oncogenic stress or genome damage can silence programmes needed for DNA replication and cell-cycle progression, reducing the chance that damaged cells expand into neoplasms. It also provides a plausible aging mechanism: the same stable repression can deplete proliferative capacity, while chromatin-controlled inflammatory gene programmes can alter immune recruitment, extracellular matrix turnover and neighbouring-cell behaviour.[38][39]

But SAHF is neither universal nor sufficient as a definition of senescence. Senescence is heterogeneous across cell types and triggers, and DNA damage foci and heterochromatin foci can coexist without substantially colocalizing in oncogene-induced senescence. Moreover, developmental senescence can share p21, p15, SA-β-galactosidase and heterochromatin marks without a detectable DDR. A chromatin feature therefore identifies one route or stabilizing state, not a universal molecular origin.[40][3]

WI38 and IMR90 fibroblasts and primary melanocytes form pronounced senescence-associated heterochromatin foci, whereas BJ fibroblasts form less marked structures. NOTCH-induced senescence in IMR90 cells suppresses HMGA1/2 and produces broader chromatin changes without those foci.[45][158]

Chromatin and mitochondrial biology intersect biochemically as well as through inflammation. Mitochondria supply Krebs-cycle metabolites required for histone modification and chromatin remodelling, and mitochondrial stress can invoke retrograde signalling that alters nuclear gene expression. One recent example links DNA-damage-activated ATM to mitochondrial BNIP3, fatty-acid oxidation, acetyl-CoA production, increased histone acetylation, p16 expression and arrest in lung fibroblasts.[21][35]

The temporal implications are substantial. Chromatin remodelling may be late enough to convert an initially reversible, p53-dominated arrest into a more stable p16–RB state. This agrees with the broader temporal ordering in which p21 tends to rise early during senescence entry and p16 later, probably contributing to maintenance. It also explains why a cell can retain arrest when mitochondrial outputs such as SASP and ROS are diminished: the chromatin/RB layer may continue to suppress proliferation.[2][27]

**The chromatin-remodelling framework explains how senescence can become durably self-enforcing and transcriptionally reprogrammed, but it cannot be reduced to SAHF formation because chromatin architectures, arrest stability and SASP outputs differ among senescence subtypes.** It shifts the question from whether a cell is simply arrested to which genes and tissue-facing programmes its reorganized chromatin permits.[4][3][34]

Narita et al. (2006) identified HMGA proteins as essential structural components of SAHF in senescent fibroblasts. HMGA proteins cooperate with p16INK4a to promote SAHF formation, proliferative arrest and repression of proliferation-associated genes; HDM2 plus CDK4 coexpression cancels these antiproliferative HMGA activities.[41] This provides a direct chromatin-based account of tumour suppression: a stress-exposed cell can remain unable to reactivate proliferation-associated genes despite mitogenic signals.[41][42]

Later chromatin studies specify that SAHF formation is a three-dimensional reorganization rather than genome-wide spreading of repressive marks. In Ras-induced human-fibroblast senescence, H3K9me3 forms a core and H3K27me3 a surrounding ring, while the global landscapes of both marks remain largely unchanged; SAHFs therefore arise through spatial repositioning and coassociation of pre-existing repressively marked chromatin.[43] Lamin B1 (LMNB1) loss facilitates relocation of perinuclear H3K9me3-positive chromatin and SAHF formation, and LMNB1 depletion together with HMGA1 or HMGA2 expression induces SAHFs more efficiently than LMNB1 depletion alone.[44] SAHF formation is progressive: in inducible Ras senescence it was established at approximately six days, and chromosome condensation precedes enrichment with H3K9 methylation, HP1 and macroH2A.[43][45]

Narita et al. (2006) found that HMGA proteins accumulated on chromatin in senescent fibroblasts and were essential structural components of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHFs).[46]

Forced HMGA expression can induce senescence-associated heterochromatin foci, while HMGA1 inhibition can dissolve established foci; reducing both HMGA1 and p16INK4A permits escape from senescence in the cited cell system.[157][45][159][158]

HMGA1 depletion disrupted SAHFs and made cells more able to bypass senescence.[47]

In fibroblast experiments, HMGA2 depletion blocked senescence-associated heterochromatin focus formation and was associated with cellular transformation.[160][45]

Narita et al. (2006) also found that coexpression of HDM2 and CDK4 cancelled the antiproliferative activities of HMGA proteins.[46]

HMGA1 is an architectural protein and an essential structural component of SAHF that is required for SAHF establishment and maintenance; RB was also required for SAHF establishment and maintenance in the reported experiments.[43]

Narita et al. (2006) identified HMGA1 as necessary for the structural integrity of SAHF.[43]

SAHF are a cell- and insult-dependent chromatin configuration rather than a universal senescence structure. They occur in only some forms of senescence, are rarely observed in vivo, and are dispensable for cellular senescence; senescent mouse cells have not displayed the pronounced punctate SAHF reported in some human cells.[48][45] The chromatin framework consequently extends beyond SAHF: senescence-associated genes are often lineage-specific and epigenetically controlled, SASP genes can be induced by extensive three-dimensional enhancer–promoter rewiring, and HMGA1 directly buffers inflammatory SASP-gene expression.[49][50] Chromatin remodelling thus supplies both stable anti-proliferative repression and a route to cell-type-specific tissue communication.[41][49]

Notch-induced senescence in IMR90 human fibroblasts can proceed with broad chromatin changes while HMGA1 and HMGA2 are suppressed and senescence-associated heterochromatin foci are absent.[45][158]

In IMR90 fibroblasts, RAS-induced senescence involved HMGA proteins and SAHF, whereas NOTCH-induced senescence suppressed HMGA1/2 and produced broader chromatin changes without SAHF.[45][158]

Pronounced SAHF have also been observed in primary melanocytes, while BJ fibroblasts show less marked SAHF than WI38 and IMR90 fibroblasts.[45][158]

Later work specifies separable chromatin controls of inflammatory output. HMGB2 binds key SASP-gene loci and prevents their incorporation into transcriptionally repressive SAHF; HMGB2 inhibition limits the SASP without releasing proliferative arrest. BRD4 recruitment to H3K27-acetylated super-enhancers, MLL1-dependent NF-κB signalling, and DDR-driven degradation of G9a and GLP, which lowers repressive H3K9 dimethylation at SASP promoters, each promote SASP expression. Inhibition of BRD4 or MLL1 suppresses SASP expression without affecting arrest.[51]

Chromatin remodelling begins before visible SAHF in some cells. Senescence-associated distention of satellites (SADS), the decondensation of pericentric satellite DNA, occurs independently of SAHF and precedes nuclear enlargement and SAHF formation during senescence induction. This identifies an early nuclear reorganization distinct from the later HMGA-associated SAHF programme.[51]

Recent work gives the Narita framework a three-dimensional-genome and single-cell dimension. In 2024, the HMGA1 study of HRAS-induced senescence in IMR90 human fibroblasts found stronger separation of active A and repressive B chromatin compartments, with increased long-range B–B contacts and fewer A–B contacts. HMGA1 depletion largely reversed the enhanced compartmentalization and suppressed SAHF formation, yet had little effect on cell-cycle-arrest markers. Thus, HMGA1-dependent architecture organizes a senescence-associated chromatin state, whereas SAHF formation and arrest can be experimentally separable.[52]

The same study retained approximately 11,000 cells after quality filtering for single-cell RNA sequencing and found substantial cell-to-cell heterogeneity in cell-cycle markers and SASP factors. Its four clusters separated low-HMGA1 cells with strong inflammatory SASP and reduced fibrogenic features from cells with the highest HMGA1 and p16 but only a modest inflammatory SASP; a further cluster retained fibroblastic features with little inflammatory SASP, and another expressed cell-cycle genes. HMGA1 therefore links three-dimensional chromatin organization to heterogeneity in proliferation, inflammatory secretion and fibroblast identity, and demonstrates that p16 and SASP are uncoupled markers.[52]

Chromatin remodeling can restrain as well as permit inflammatory output. HMGA1 depletion increased IL1B, IL8, IL6, CXCL1–3, MMP1 and MMP3 expression, with increased IL-8 and IL-6 protein, establishing HMGA1 as a buffer of inflammatory SASP expression in that oncogene-induced-senescence model.[52]

### Cellular senescence is a response to stresses that can halt

Cellular senescence should not be treated as synonymous with organismal aging. It is a cell fate triggered by many stresses—telomere dysfunction, DNA damage, oncogene activation, oxidative and metabolic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, chromatin perturbation and inflammatory or tissue-damage signals—and it can occur at any age. Its shared feature is stable proliferative arrest; its associated features, including persistent DDR, p16 or p21 expression, altered chromatin, apoptosis resistance and SASP composition, are variable and non-exclusive.[53][4][54]

A useful distinction is the **three-stage model**. First, an initiating insult produces a temporary checkpoint or stress response. Second, if the stress persists or feedback loops activate, the cell establishes durable arrest through p53–p21, p16–RB, metabolic and chromatin mechanisms. Third, some cells develop tissue-facing outputs—SASP, ROS, extracellular-matrix remodelling, immune signalling and paracrine senescence—that can influence neighbours. The three named frameworks map chiefly onto different points in this sequence, although each can also influence the others.[53][12][2][25]

The difference between transient stress and senescence is experimentally clear in the DDR literature. Low-dose irradiation can produce a short p53–p21 checkpoint, repair and resumed proliferation, whereas persistent foci and continued signalling cause arrest and delayed cytokine secretion. This distinction prevents a common error: a cell that expresses p21 or γH2AX shortly after injury is not automatically an established, persistently senescent cell.[16][17][6]

The p53–p21 and p16–RB pathways explain why senescence is a tumor suppressor. Both impose a proliferation block in cells with damaged genomes or abnormal mitogenic signalling. Telomerase-deficient mice with short telomeres are cancer resistant except when p53 is absent; p53-dependent arrest and/or apoptosis in response to telomere damage is therefore implicated in their cancer resistance, although the cited observation does not separate the contribution of senescence from apoptosis.[55][56]

The same programmes create age-related pathology when chronic arrest removes functional cells from proliferative pools or leaves dysfunctional cells alive in tissues. Senescent cells resist apoptosis and can persist for long periods, allowing a small burden to accumulate gradually. In late-generation telomerase-deficient mice, reactivation of telomerase extended telomeres, reduced DDR and checkpoint responses, and eliminated degenerative phenotypes in testes, spleen and intestine; it also restored neural progenitor, newborn-neuron and oligodendrocyte populations and improved olfactory responses.[33][14]

That rescue supports telomere dysfunction as a driver of degeneration in this severe mouse model, but it does not assign all benefits specifically to reversal or clearance of established senescent cells. Telomerase reactivation can reduce the initiating damage signal, restore proliferative capacity in quiescent cells and prevent additional cell loss. The telomerase model therefore tests damage-driven tissue failure more directly than it tests a universal senescent-cell-burden mechanism.[14][57]

The biological paradox is consequently temporal rather than contradictory. Acute senescence can block malignant expansion, participate in wound repair or developmental remodelling, and signal immune systems to remove compromised cells. Persistent senescence can instead limit stem-cell function, sustain inflammation and remodel tissue niches in ways that impair regeneration or support tumour progression in cells that escaped the arrest. The functional direction depends on tissue, inducer and time since induction.[58][7]

**Ranking for telomere-driven initiation —** d’Adda di Fagagna et al.’s 2003 DDR framework ranks first because dysfunctional telomeres directly activate the checkpoint and checkpoint-kinase inactivation restores S-phase entry; Passos et al.’s 2010 mitochondrial feedback and Narita et al.’s 2006 HMGA–SAHF framework rank as maintenance mechanisms in that sequence. There is no universal ranking across contexts for maintenance or tissue-wide propagation.[8][29][60] The DDR model has the clearest direct evidence for a damage-to-checkpoint-to-arrest sequence; the Passos model explains how a delayed metabolic loop makes this state self-sustaining; and the chromatin model explains why an initially checkpoint-dependent pause can harden into persistent transcriptional reprogramming.

Context-specific necessity tests could change this verdict: MiDAS initiated without detectable DNA damage would lower the DDR framework’s standing in that context; retained arrest after mitochondrial depletion would lower the mitochondrial-feedback framework’s standing for maintenance; and arrest without SAHF would lower the proposed chromatin-structure framework’s standing.[24][27][61][8][12][3]

This integrated interpretation also explains why marker-based identification is difficult. γH2AX can mark an active DDR but is not exclusive to permanent arrest. p21 can rise early after injury. p16INK4a often appears later and is useful for identifying subsets of senescent cells, but p16 expression is neither a complete census of senescence nor proof of a pro-inflammatory SASP. The later sections, *Single-cell, mouse, and human intervention evidence* and *Tumor suppression, age-related pathology, and timing*, assess what single-cell profiling, genetic models and clearance interventions can establish about those distinctions.[62][16][3]

Single-cell RNA sequencing reinforces that senescence is not a uniform state. Putative senescent populations differ by cell type and inducer in CDKN2A/p16INK4a and CDKN1A/p21 expression, DDR and interferon programmes, antigen-presentation and extracellular-matrix genes, and secreted-factor transcripts; p16INK4a-positive and p21-positive cells can overlap but are not identical populations.

Baker et al. (2011) used the INK-ATTAC transgene to drug-inducibly eliminate p16Ink4a-positive cells in BubR1-hypomorphic progeroid mice. Clearance delayed cataracts, sarcopenia and adipose-tissue loss, and benefit was observed when clearance began after pathology had started. This timing supports the conclusion that persistent p16Ink4a-positive cells can drive selected established pathologies, whereas their earlier appearance may still have served a protective arrest function.

### Synthesis —

The mechanistic controversy is best resolved provisionally as an architecture of causation, not a contest for one universal trigger. Persistent DDR provides the strongest direct route from telomere dysfunction or genomic injury to arrest; mitochondrial dysfunction can initiate distinct redox, calcium, respiratory-chain and quality-control states or reinforce DDR-driven senescence; and chromatin remodelling can stabilize arrest, diversify cell states and reshape inflammatory transcription. Their shared tumour-suppressive function is to prevent propagation of damaged or oncogene-stressed cells. Their shared aging risk appears when arrest persists, regenerative capacity falls, and inflammatory or paracrine outputs alter tissue homeostasis. The decisive temporal question is which states are transiently useful and cleared, and which persist long enough to acquire stable metabolic, chromatin and secretory programmes that become harmful.[2][12][27][3][63][52][64]

A useful temporal model separates **initiation**, **maintenance**, and **propagation**. First, a transient insult can activate p53, DNA repair and a reversible proliferative pause. Second, unresolved or persistent stress sustains p53 and activates p16INK4A, producing long-lasting arrest through checkpoint, metabolic and chromatin mechanisms. Third, some arrested cells acquire tissue-facing outputs—including SASP, ROS, extracellular-matrix remodelling, immune signalling and paracrine senescence—that affect neighbouring cells.[53][12][17][34]

## Single-cell, mouse, and human intervention evidence

Single-cell methods, mouse genetics, and human senolytic trials answer different causal questions. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) measures gene-expression states one cell at a time; mouse genetics can alter a defined pathway or remove a marker-defined population; clinical trials test whether a treatment changes human biomarkers or outcomes. They converge on one central point: cellular senescence is not a single, uniform cell fate.Judgment — This makes any claim that one marker, one mechanism, or one senolytic explains all age-related pathology untenable.

### What do single-cell RNA sequencing studies identify about senescent cell

scRNA-seq identifies heterogeneity at four levels: the cell of origin, the stressor that induced arrest, the route into arrest, and time spent in the senescent state. Senescence-associated markers themselves are non-exclusive: persistent DNA-damage-response signalling, p16INK4A and p21CIP1 expression, loss of LMNB1, senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)—secreted inflammatory, growth-regulatory, and matrix-remodelling factors—do not occur uniformly in every senescent cell.[65][54]

SenCID, SenMayo, SenePy, scResolve, SenFlag and SenNet-related atlas pipelines use differing gene lists, reference annotations, scoring methods and training material to classify candidate senescent cells.[161][77]

Hernandez-Segura et al. (2017) identified heterogeneous senescent-cell transcriptional states that varied by inducing stress, cell type, and time after induction.[65]

Cultured-cell studies first made this visible. Chen et al. (2020, *Frontiers in Genetics*) resolved 175 replicatively aging mouse embryonic fibroblasts into six clusters and three inferred lineages. One main trajectory progressed from arrest to SASP/inflammatory expression and then cell-death-associated features; two smaller branches suggested either regained proliferation or increased translation coupled to ATP synthesis. SASP genes including *Il6* and *Cxcl1* rose only in a subset of cells, explaining why a bulk average can miss a biologically active minority.[66][59]

The Tabula Muris Senis atlas (2020) provides age- and tissue-resolved mouse single-cell transcriptomic data from 23 tissues and organs and assessed senescence hallmarks by cell type.[67]

This is not confined to mouse fibroblasts. Fard et al. (2023, *GeroScience*) identified pre-senescent states before three distinct senescent states in serially passaged human embryonic-stem-cell-derived mesenchymal stem cells. Evans et al. identified two subpopulations in each of replicative, oncogene-induced, and DNA-damage-induced senescence in clonal human fibroblasts: one was more TP53/DNA-damage-, inflammatory-, SASP-, and HMGA1-rich, whereas the other was more TGF-beta-, extracellular-matrix-, and collagen-associated. HMGA1 is a chromatin-associated architectural protein, linking transcriptional diversity to the chromatin changes discussed in the preceding mechanistic section.[68][69][70]

Fernandez et al. (2025, *Nature Communications*) added a functional temporal dimension by pairing live cell-cycle tracking with scRNA-seq after etoposide chemotherapy. They found two routes to stable arrest: first, a gradual mitosis-to-G0 route, where G0 is a non-dividing state; second, direct arrest after mitotic slip, meaning failed mitosis followed by cell-cycle exit. The study identified four senotypes—distinct transcriptional forms of senescence—within irreversibly arrested cells, but the distributions were graded rather than sharply separated. Mitotic-slip cells had stronger SASP, inflammatory, and protein-biosynthesis programs; the mitosis-to-G0 route was more enriched for wound-healing, pro-growth, and epithelial–mesenchymal-transition-associated programs.[71][72]

The same study also exposes a classification problem. Quiescence is reversible withdrawal from the cell cycle, whereas senescence is conventionally defined as irreversible withdrawal; after chemotherapy, however, the two formed a transcriptomic continuum, and canonical markers did not specifically identify senescent cells except for a graded increase in *CDKN1A*/*p21*.[71][72] **This means that arrested cells cannot be assigned a uniform pathological function from p16, p21, or a SASP transcript alone.**

In vivo studies support real tissue-context dependence. Moiseeva et al. (2022, *Nature*) enriched rare senescent cells from damaged young and old mouse muscle, identifying multiple senescent cell types with conserved identity traits and shared inflammatory and fibrotic hallmarks. In radiation- or immunotherapy-injured mouse lung, scRNA-seq found senescence-like fibroblast, alveolar epithelial, B-cell, and myeloid-cell states.[73][74] Sanborn et al.

(2025, *Nature Communications*) built SenePy from 72 mouse and 64 human cell-type-weighted signatures and found no universal marker set: only 58 of 1,540 cell-type pairs had significant marker overlap, and *CDKN2A* was age-dynamic in only 26% of tested human and 32% of tested mouse cell types.[75]

SenePy nevertheless found age-associated increases in cells classified as senescent across mouse and human datasets, with different affected cell types and kinetics; mouse fibroblasts showed their largest increase between 18 and 24 months. Its mouse tongue-keratinocyte example separated a cytokine/TNF-rich, pro-inflammatory type-A program from a type-B innate-immune program, while heart, lung, and tracheal fibroblasts also each contained two distinct signature hubs.[75] Single-cell data therefore make it possible to identify candidate senescent populations and pathways, but not to treat every marker-positive cell or every inflammatory program as equivalent.

Single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse β cells divided the cells into six clusters and identified p16Ink4a-positive, p21Cip1-positive, and two p16Ink4a-negative/p21Cip1-negative subpopulations.[76]

In this β-cell analysis, the p16Ink4a-positive subpopulation was non-proliferating, whereas p21Cip1-positive cells and p16Ink4a-negative/p21Cip1-negative cells expressed proliferation-associated genes.[76]

The p21Cip1-positive β-cell subpopulation was the principal producer of the β-cell SASP factors examined and persisted after INK-ATTAC clearance of p16Ink4a-positive cells.[76]

Walter et al. (2024) add an age-, time-, and location-resolved in vivo example. Across 273,923 single-cell transcriptomes from mouse tibialis-anterior regeneration at six time points after injury, in young, old, and geriatric mice, they identified 29 cell types, eight of which had age-dependent abundance dynamics. Senescent-like muscle stem/progenitor subsets occurred at transitional self-renewal states at every age, increased in aged muscle, and were elevated specifically within injury zones of aged muscle by spatial transcriptomics.[77]

Walter and colleagues’ muscle atlas drew on 65 mouse skeletal-muscle samples from young (4–7 months), old (20 months) and geriatric (26 months) animals, sampled before notexin injury and at 1, 2, 3.5, 5 and 7 days afterward.[161][77]

In Walter et al. (2024), the fraction of candidate *Cdkn2a*/*Cdkn1a* double-positive muscle stem and progenitor cells rose from baseline to 3.5 days after injury and then returned close to baseline. At day 3.5, these cells were more frequent in old and geriatric than young muscle, particularly in geriatric self-renewing MuSC 1 cells and in old and geriatric muscle progenitors; no age-specific difference occurred in MuSC states 2–6. This identifies a transient, state-specific rather than uniformly persistent senescence-like program during repair.[77]

Walter and colleagues benchmarked 14 senescence gene lists using two scoring approaches in muscle stem cells and progenitors. Their experimentally derived one-way foreign-body-response score classified cells coexpressing *Cdkn2a* and *Cdkn1a* with an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.86, compared with 0.88 for a Gene Ontology SASP score and 0.63 for SenMayo.[162][77]

The foreign-body-response score retained AUC values of 0.85–0.86 across young, old and geriatric mice in Walter and colleagues’ analysis.[162][77]

In regenerating mouse muscle, Walter et al. compared 14 gene lists and two scoring strategies for identifying candidate *Cdkn2a*/*Cdkn1a* double-positive muscle stem cells and progenitors. Their foreign-body-response score achieved a receiver-operating-characteristic area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86, versus 0.88 for GO:SASP and 0.63 for SenMayo under the same single-sample gene-set-enrichment method.[162][77]

Walter et al.’s foreign-body-response score retained AUCs of 0.85–0.86 across young, old and geriatric mice. Its reported threshold was 2,412.562, at which 50% of score-high cells coexpressed *Cdkn2a* and *Cdkn1a*.[161][77]

The muscle atlas also directly connects heterogeneity to cell identity. Moiseeva et al. (2022) profiled 21,449 cells enriched by live SPiDER-β-gal sorting and found senescent-enriched myeloid cells, fibroadipogenic progenitors (FAPs), satellite cells and their progeny, plus smaller antigen-presenting, neutrophil, endothelial, B-, T-, and natural-killer-cell populations. The three principal populations shared a 16-gene upregulated core that included the inflammatory and matrix-remodelling SASP factors *Ccl2*, *Ccl7*, *Igfbp4*, and *Timp2*, but had 2,251, 1,805, and 4,958 differentially expressed genes in senescent satellite cells, FAPs, and myeloid cells, respectively, with little overlap.[78]

The three principal senescent-cell-enriched muscle populations also shared 33 downregulated genes.[78]

In the injured-muscle atlas, live-cell SPiDER-β-gal sorting yielded a population classified as 93.6% senescent by a four-marker assessment of enlarged cell size, reduced lamin B1, arrest and DNA damage.[78][163]

Across the three principal senescent-enriched populations in the injured-muscle atlas, only 33 genes were commonly downregulated.[78]

Moiseeva et al. enriched muscle cells by sorting for high SPiDER-β-gal activity, a lysosomal β-galactosidase readout; 93.6% of the sorted population was positive by their four-feature senescence score. The enriched cells also showed large size, reduced lamin B1, cell-cycle arrest and increased DNA damage.[78][163]

Across the three principal senescent-cell lineages in Moiseeva et al.’s muscle atlas, only 33 genes were commonly downregulated.[78][163]

Common 3′ scRNA-seq cannot reliably distinguish CDKN2A transcripts encoding p16INK4a from those encoding p14ARF.[54]

Walter et al. reported that *Cdkn2a* and *Cdkn1a* transcripts were not correlated at the individual-cell level in their myogenic-cell analysis; dropout of low-copy transcripts is a possible explanation.[161][77]

Whole-cell RNA sequencing captures cytoplasmic RNA but requires tissue dissociation, whereas single-nucleus RNA sequencing preferentially captures nuclear RNA and may under-detect cytoplasmic senescence transcripts. Neither assay alone measures irreversible arrest, secreted protein or a cell’s causal contribution to pathology.[164]

### What do comparative lifespan analyses in p16-INK4a knockout mouse models

p16INK4a is a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor encoded at the *Cdkn2a* locus. It restrains cell-cycle progression and contributes to tumor suppression, but its age-related effects are strongly model dependent. Constitutive knockout experiments test loss of that gene throughout life; they are fundamentally different from inducible killing of already p16-positive cells in adulthood.

The following comparison distinguishes the key mouse manipulations and their lifespan meaning.

| Model and manipulation | Survival result | What the result establishes |
|---|---:|---|
| BubR1-hypomorphic progeroid mice with *p16Ink4a* inactivation | Median lifespan was 25 weeks, a 25% extension versus BubR1-hypomorphic controls; maximum lifespan was not extended. [79] | p16INK4a contributes to median-survival loss and selected progeroid phenotypes in this chromosome-segregation-defect model, but is not the sole determinant of ultimate lifespan. [79] |
| *Pot1b*Δ/Δ mice with *p16INK4a* deletion | Median lifespan was approximately 43 weeks versus approximately 65 weeks in *Pot1b*Δ/Δ controls. [56] | Under telomere-protection failure, p16INK4a protects high-turnover tissues and survival rather than simply imposing harmful arrest. [56] |
| *Pot1b*Δ/Δ;*mTerc*+/− mice with *p16INK4a* deletion | Median survival was 12.5 weeks versus 24 weeks in *Pot1b*Δ/Δ;*mTerc*+/− controls. [56] | Loss of p16INK4a worsened combined telomere dysfunction and telomerase insufficiency. [56] |
| Naturally aged INK-ATTAC mice treated from 12 months to clear p16-positive cells | Median lifespan increased by 27% in mixed-background mice and 24% in C57BL/6 mice, with both sexes combined. [80] | Adult clearance of p16-positive cells can improve median lifespan without deleting the tumor-suppressor gene in every cell from development onward. [80] |

The BubR1 model displays the tumor–aging trade-off directly. p16INK4a inactivation delayed certain aging phenotypes and increased median survival, but accelerated lung tumorigenesis. The authors considered loss of a p16-mediated tumor-suppressive response a plausible explanation, while also noting that chromosome instability could contribute.[79] In a distinct Werner-syndrome-like *mTerc*−/−*Wrn*−/− background, p16 deficiency increased mean lifespan in generation 3 and generation 5 cohorts, whereas p21 deficiency shortened survival sharply; these contrasting checkpoint effects show that p16 and p21 cannot be treated as interchangeable senescence markers or targets.[81]

Telomere-dysfunction models reverse the simple inference that p16 removal is beneficial. In *Pot1b*Δ/Δ;*p16*−/− mice, bone marrow, intestinal, and testicular impairment worsened, 3 of 5 examined animals developed complete bone-marrow failure by 40–45 weeks, and dysfunctional telomeres coincided with elevated ATR–CHK1–p53–p21 checkpoint signalling and apoptosis.[56] **The decisive conclusion is that p16INK4a knockout is not a general anti-aging intervention: it can relieve a damaging proliferative barrier in one progeroid setting and preserve viable tissue under dysfunctional telomeres in another.**

In *Pot1b*-deficient mouse bone marrow, signal-free telomere ends measured 58.88% ± 8.79% after *p16* deletion, versus 17.35% ± 1.65% with *Pot1b* deficiency alone.[56]

In the telomere-dysfunction comparison, 90% of *Pot1b*Δ/Δ;*p21*−/− mice remained alive at 80 weeks, whereas all *Pot1b*Δ/Δ;*p16*−/− mice had died by 68 weeks.[56]

The genetic interpretation also depends on the altered allele. Late-generation *mTerc*−/− mice lacking the whole *Ink4a/Arf* locus did not show attenuated degenerative phenotypes, but that deletion removes both p16INK4a and p19ARF; it cannot be read as a p16-specific result.[81] Moreover, p16INK4a- or p19ARF-deficient mice can die from cancer before ordinary late-life disorders emerge, limiting their use as direct models of normal aging.[79]

There is no established extension of overall lifespan from p16-INK4a knockout in ordinarily aging mice. Reported survival benefits are model-specific, whereas p16-INK4a loss can also increase cancer risk or worsen survival under telomere dysfunction.

p16INK4a inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 and thereby helps maintain retinoblastoma-protein-mediated cell-cycle arrest. Its loss can therefore alter the establishment of arrest, survival of damaged cells, tumour burden, and tissue cell composition rather than rejuvenating tissue by clearing pre-existing senescent cells.

In the Werner-syndrome-like *mTerc*−/−*Wrn*−/− model, p16 deficiency increased mean lifespan from 281 ± 24 to 334 ± 16 days in generation-3 mice and to 236 ± 19 days in generation-5 mice with severely short telomeres. In generation-5 mice, p16 deficiency also rescued bone loss, testis degeneration, and intestinal-crypt loss, with reduced senescence and apoptosis and increased proliferation and intestinal stem-cell reservoir.[82]

The cancer consequence of bypassing this barrier is also visible at the cellular level: late-passage p16Ink4a−/−*Wrn*−/− fibroblasts with telomere dysfunction accumulated chromosomal fusions in 12 of 30 metaphases, compared with 2 of 30 in late-passage p16Ink4a−/− fibroblasts.[83]

### What do comparative lifespan analyses in telomerase-deficient mouse models establish?

Telomerase-deficient mice establish that inherited telomere reserve, not merely the binary absence of telomerase, determines when organismal failure emerges. Telomerase maintains chromosome ends; in *mTR*/*Terc* null mice, telomeres shorten further over generations, become critically short, and are associated with chromosome fusions, genetic instability, sterility, impaired hematopoiesis and immune-cell proliferation, small-intestinal atrophy, reduced stress tolerance, and shortened lifespan.[84][85][86]

This is a causal telomere-maintenance result, but it is not a pure senescence experiment. Continued growth without telomerase produces fusions and translocations, and the affected organism can fail through a mixture of stable arrest, p53/p21 checkpoint activation, apoptosis, stem-cell depletion, and genome instability.[84][29] In late-generation *TERC*−/− mice, p21/*Cdkn1a* deletion partially rescued shortened lifespan, indicating that a damage-checkpoint arm contributes to mortality; in *Pot1b* deficiency, p21 deletion improved hematopoietic proliferation and increased lifespan despite persistently dysfunctional telomeres and elevated DNA-damage responses.[29][56]

In late-generation telomerase-deficient mice with critically short telomeres, deleting *p21* improved intestinal-crypt and haematopoietic-stem-cell regenerative capacity.[160][156][26][49]

The generation effect matters because mouse telomeres are initially long and telomerase genotype does not fully specify inherited telomere length. Heterozygous *tert*+/− animals retain reduced telomerase activity and lose telomere length across generations, while even telomerase-proficient offspring from such crosses may inherit short telomeres and milder telomere-dysfunction phenotypes.[87] **Thus, telomerase-null lifespan experiments establish a requirement for functional telomere reserve, while leaving the relative contributions of senescence, apoptosis, failed regeneration, and chromosome instability to each death unresolved.**

Intervention results reinforce this distinction. Reducing p66SHC, a regulator linked to oxidative stress and mitochondrial apoptosis, partially rescued sterility, weight loss, and organ atrophy in late-generation *TERC*-deficient mice but neither telomere erosion nor shortened lifespan.[88] Rapamycin extended median longevity by 39% in wild-type mice but reduced median lifespan by 16% in generation-2 *Terc*−/− mice, with a 19% reduction in treated males and no median-survival change in females.[55] This genotype reversal shows that a pathway intervention beneficial in telomere-replete animals can be harmful when survival depends on compensatory metabolic signalling under short-telomere stress.

In aging *mTR*−/− mice, age-dependent telomere shortening and accompanying genetic instability were associated with shortened life span and reduced capacity to respond to wound-healing and hematopoietic-ablation stress. The same aging *mTR*−/− cohorts had an increased incidence of spontaneous malignancies.[85]

Genetic restoration of telomerase activity in short-telomere mouse settings can improve degenerative phenotypes. This manipulation restores telomere function and proliferative capacity in addition to altering DNA-damage signalling, so it is mechanistically broader than senescent-cell clearance.

In a survival study followed for 32 months, 68 wild-type, 17 generation-1, 31 generation-2, and 22 generation-3 *Terc*-null mice showed progressively reduced median and maximum lifespan across successive telomerase-deficient generations.[15]

A conditional restoration experiment establishes reversibility of important short-telomere consequences. Late-generation G4 *TERT*-ER mice had median survival of 43.5 weeks, versus 86.8 weeks in telomere-intact mice; a four-week course of 4-OHT to reactivate endogenous telomerase increased median survival, reduced DNA-damage signalling and checkpoint responses, and reversed atrophy in highly proliferative organs.[89] This complements constitutive *Terc*-null survival curves, in which progressively shortened inherited telomeres across G1–G3 were accompanied by progressive reductions in both median and maximum longevity.[90]

### What have pharmacological interventions using senolytics (dasatinib+quercetin) in human clinical

Dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) is a senolytic combination intended to trigger apoptosis in senescent cells by disrupting senescent-cell anti-apoptotic pathways. Dasatinib and quercetin have different cell-type preferences, which is one reason they are combined; that heterogeneity also means D+Q cannot be assumed to eliminate every senescent population.[91][92]

In very old INK-ATTAC mice, eight weeks of oral dasatinib at 5 mg/kg plus quercetin at 10 mg/kg, given for three consecutive days every two weeks, reduced Stone T-maze errors relative to aged vehicle controls (P = 0.019); maze-completion time did not decline significantly.[164]

Human D+Q studies have measured feasibility, adherence, adverse events, physical and pulmonary function, patient-reported health, tissue and circulating senescence-associated markers, cerebrospinal-fluid drug penetration, cognition, imaging, DNA-methylation clocks, skeletal responses, and—in fibrotic metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)—paired-biopsy histology and liver single-nucleus RNA sequencing. None measured human lifespan.[93][94][95][96][97][98][99]

- **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF):** Justice et al. (2019, *EBioMedicine*) gave 14 stable-IPF participants dasatinib 100 mg/day plus quercetin 1,250 mg/day, three days per week for three weeks. The open-label pilot’s primary endpoints were retention and completion; retention was 100%, 13 of 14 completed planned assessments, and one serious adverse event occurred. The mean 21.5-m increase in six-minute walking distance was a physical-function measure. Pulmonary-function measures, clinical chemistries, frailty index, and reported health did not significantly change. [93][100]

- **Diabetic kidney disease:** Hickson et al. (2019, *EBioMedicine*) administered three days of dasatinib 100 mg plus quercetin 1,000 mg to nine participants and sampled adipose tissue, skin, and blood before dosing and 11 days after treatment. These were biological measures: senescent-cell, macrophage/Langerhans-cell, and circulating SASP markers. Reported biological changes included lower senescent-cell numbers and inflammation in adipose tissue after treatment. [94][101][102]

- **Mild Alzheimer’s disease:** Gonzales et al. (2023, *Nature Medicine*) conducted an open-label 12-week feasibility study in five completers, mean age 76 ± 5 years. Both drugs increased in blood; dasatinib was detected in cerebrospinal fluid in four participants at 0.281–0.536 ng/ml, whereas quercetin was not detected. Central-nervous-system IL-6 and GFAP increased while several cytokines and chemokines decreased, showing that biomarker responses were mixed rather than uniformly anti-inflammatory. [95]

- **Fibrotic MASH:** Koning et al. (2026, *Nature Metabolism*) randomized 31 participants, 17 to D+Q and 14 to placebo, in a phase-2 double-blind trial. The primary paired-biopsy endpoint was a histological change: at least one fibrosis-stage improvement without MASH worsening. It occurred in 8 of 17 D+Q participants (47%) versus 1 of 14 placebo participants (7%; *P* = 0.021); histological MASH resolution occurred in 53% versus 7%. Adverse events were more frequent with D+Q, 82% versus 43%, though reported as self-limiting. [99][98]

The IPF evidence illustrates why functional signals and mechanism must be separated. A post hoc pool of two small IPF pilots included 20 D+Q-treated participants and 6 placebo recipients. In contrast to the open-label six-minute-walk physical-function result, it found no significant D+Q-versus-placebo difference in frailty, physical function, or pulmonary function, despite biological measures showing decreased GPNMB and increased urinary alpha-klotho after D+Q. Likewise, the diabetic-kidney and Alzheimer’s studies demonstrate biological measurement and short-term exposure, not lifespan extension or proof that observed changes arose specifically from senescent-cell elimination.[93][103]

At least one adverse event was 39.5 percentage points more frequent with D+Q than placebo in the same fibrotic-MASH trial (82.4% versus 42.9%).[98]

Dasatinib is a kinase inhibitor used clinically in leukemia, whereas quercetin is a flavonoid with broad biological actions. Their effects in D+Q trials therefore cannot be attributed solely to senescent-cell killing from drug exposure alone.

- **Randomized IPF pilot:** Nambiar et al. (2023) randomized 12 IPF participants 1:1 to D+Q or placebo for three weeks. All 12 completed all nine intended doses and assessments. The trial was designed for feasibility and tolerability; exploratory frailty, pulmonary, and physical-function outcomes did not differ meaningfully between groups. D+Q recipients reported 65 non-serious adverse events versus 22 with placebo, including sleep disturbance in 4 of 6 versus 0 of 6 and anxiety in 3 of 6 versus 0 of 6; there were no serious adverse events related to D+Q. [1]

D+Q did not significantly change the bone-resorption marker CTx: the reported changes were −4.1% with D+Q and −7.7% in controls (*P* = 0.611). A bone-formation marker increased relative to control at weeks 2 and 4 but not week 20; an exploratory subgroup with the greatest senescent-cell burden had a 2.7% increase in radius bone density. [104]

- **Older adults at risk for Alzheimer’s disease:** Millar et al. (2025, *eBioMedicine*) treated 12 participants for 12 weeks with D+Q on two days every two weeks. Mean Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score increased by 1.0 point without statistical significance; the lowest-baseline-MoCA subgroup increased by 2.0 points. [105][106]

[107] The primary endpoint, percentage change at 20 weeks in the bone-resorption marker C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTx), did not differ between D+Q and control; the reported changes were −4.1% and −7.7%, respectively (*P* = 0.611). ### Synthesis —

Single-cell studies establish that senescence contains multiple cell- and time-specific states, including inflammatory/SASP-rich, TGF-beta/matrix-remodelling, and metabolically distinct programs. Mouse models show that p16INK4a can either worsen age-associated dysfunction or protect damaged high-turnover tissues, whereas telomerase-deficient models demonstrate the importance of telomere reserve but not a single senescence-only route to death. Human D+Q trials now show tissue-marker changes, drug penetration, feasibility, and one randomized histological efficacy signal in MASH; IPF and Alzheimer’s results remain short-term and mixed. The practical conclusion is that senescence-targeting interventions should be matched to the senescent-cell subtype, tissue, stress history, and disease endpoint rather than deployed as a universal anti-aging treatment.[75][69][56][80][85][99][103]

## Tumor suppression, age-related pathology, and timing

Cellular senescence poses a real biological trade-off. It prevents a damaged or oncogene-activated cell from continuing to divide, but the surviving arrested cell can remain metabolically active and alter its tissue environment. The central question is therefore not whether senescence is beneficial or harmful in the abstract, but which mechanism initiated it, what maintains it, whether it is cleared, and how long its non-proliferative and secretory activities persist.

### How does the DNA damage response model explain the tumor-suppressive

The DNA damage response (DDR) model associated with d’Adda di Fagagna et al. (2003) treats senescence as a checkpoint response to damage that cannot be adequately repaired. DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), uncapped telomeres, and dysfunctional telomeres activate the sensor kinases ATM, ATR and DNA-PK; damage foci containing phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX) form; and checkpoint pathways activate p53 and the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21 (CDKN1A). In parallel or later, p16INK4A (CDKN2A) inhibits CDK4/6, maintains retinoblastoma protein (RB) in its active, hypophosphorylated state, and blocks entry into S phase, the DNA-replication phase of the cell cycle.[7][33]

This provides a direct tumour-suppression mechanism: an at-risk cell stops copying damaged DNA before it can form an expanding clone. d’Adda di Fagagna et al. showed that inactivating DNA-damage checkpoint kinases could restore S-phase entry in telomere-initiated senescent cells, establishing that checkpoint signalling functionally enforces the arrest rather than merely marking it. In human fibroblasts, Chk2 inactivation reduced p21 expression and extended proliferative lifespan, placing Chk2 between telomere erosion and p53-dependent arrest.[8][10]

The model also explains oncogene-induced senescence (OIS). Strong oncogenic Ras or Raf signalling initially drives excessive proliferation; abnormal DNA replication creates stalled replication forks and DNA damage, which activate ATM, ATR, CHK1 and CHK2 and induce arrest. Experimental weakening of key DDR components can inhibit OIS in particular oncogene and cell-type settings, while experimental DDR inactivation in normal human cells abrogated OIS and promoted transformation.[3][40][2]

Mouse cancer genetics makes the causal tumour-barrier argument stronger than a correlation between senescence markers and premalignancy. In Pten-loss prostate and oncogenic-Nras haematopoietic models, the initiating lesion produced senescent premalignant lesions; combining the lesion with deletion of p53 or the H3K9 methyltransferase SUV39H1 was associated with loss of senescence markers and full malignancy. Other models found that deletion of Cdkn2a or Trp53 abolished senescence and permitted malignant progression.[113] Senescence markers also occur in several premalignant mouse and human lesions and are uncommon in the corresponding malignant stages, a pattern consistent with selection for cells that escape the arrest barrier.[113][114]

The DDR framework includes a second, cell-extrinsic layer of tumour suppression. The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)—a variable collection of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and proteases—can reinforce arrest in the senescent cell and its neighbours and recruit immune cells. In an OIS liver model, SASP from premalignant senescent hepatocytes recruited immune cells; SCID mice lacking adaptive immunity did not clear those cells and developed hepatocellular carcinoma.[115][116][6]

This protection is conditional. Cancer cells can evade senescence by disabling p53, altering checkpoint signalling, re-expressing telomerase, abnormally elongating telomeres, or increasing homology-dependent telomere repair.[13] Telomerase-deficient mice with short telomeres have been reported as resistant to skin tumorigenesis in a p53-associated setting, whereas an aging mTR-null cohort showed increased spontaneous malignancies; dysfunctional telomeres can limit proliferation but also create chromosomal instability that enables cancer if checkpoint control fails.[55][85][117]

Persistent SASP can also reverse the net tumour outcome. It may induce secondary senescence and immune clearance early, but a chronic inflammatory microenvironment can support tumour-cell proliferation, angiogenesis, metastatic seeding and impaired antitumour immunity.[33][3] Thus a p16-positive stromal signal is not automatically protective: fibroblasts engineered to express p16INK4A produced L-lactate and, when co-injected with MDA-MB-231 breast-cancer cells, increased xenograft tumour size twofold.[118]

**Compared with the mitochondrial and chromatin frameworks, the DDR model supplies the most direct explanation for tumour restraint: checkpoint-enforced arrest prevents replication of damaged or oncogene-stressed cells, and SASP-dependent immune clearance can eliminate them.** Checkpoint escape and persistent inflammatory signalling can instead support cancer progression.[7][113][116][33]

Genetic evidence from BubR1 hypomorphic mice further illustrates the trade-off built into the p16INK4a checkpoint. BubR1H/H mice have progressive severe aneuploidy but rarely develop tumours, whereas BubR1H/H;p16Ink4a−/− mice had significantly more tumours at death or morbidity; eight of nine tumours were lung adenocarcinomas, and the tumour-incidence curves differed with log-rank P = 0.0027.[121] Thus, p16INK4a-dependent arrest can restrain expansion of genomically abnormal cells even while the same pathway contributes to selected progeroid phenotypes.[121]

### How does the DNA damage response model explain the role

The same DDR that protects against cancer can become pathological when damage persists. A transient DDR permits repair and return to proliferation; persistent DDR maintains prolonged p53–p21 arrest and can be stabilized later by p16INK4A–RB signalling. In irradiated human fibroblasts, 0.5 Gy produced transient damage foci, p53/p21 activation and subsequent resumed growth, whereas 10 Gy produced persistent DNA-damage foci, senescence, and a five- to sixfold increase in IL-6 and IL-8 secretion within 2–4 days.[16][53]

Telomeres are a particularly important persistence mechanism. Critically short or uncapped telomeres resemble DSBs, activate a DDR, and can initiate senescence even when only one or a few telomeres signal damage.[2] In stress-induced senescence, up to half of damage foci were telomere-associated irrespective of telomerase activity, and live-cell imaging found that every persistent focus was telomere-associated.

In irradiated mouse embryonic fibroblasts, non-telomeric foci lasted no more than about 3 hours, whereas approximately half of telomere-associated foci persisted more than 6 hours.[7]

This mechanism can impair tissue maintenance in two ways. First, persistently arrested cells cannot replenish proliferative or progenitor compartments. Second, persistent DDR signalling produces inflammatory and oxidative outputs that alter neighbouring cells and stem-cell niches. Telomere loss and uncapping in telomerase-deficient mice are associated with tissue atrophy, stem-cell depletion, organ-system failure and impaired injury responses; in TERC-knockout lungs, alveolar stem cells showed p53-dependent premature replicative senescence and apoptosis, p53-independent altered differentiation, and senescence-associated low-grade inflammation.[14][122]

The DDR-to-SASP link is mechanistically separable from arrest. In damaged human fibroblasts, persistent damage foci induced inflammatory cytokines through ATM, NBS1 and CHK2, but not through the p53 and pRB arrest enforcers. ATM depletion reduced IL-6 secretion 50-fold and IL-8 secretion 10-fold, but reduced only 7 of 16 selected SASP factors; nine changed by no more than 1.4-fold.[16] This demonstrates that DDR signalling regulates an important inflammatory subset of SASP rather than a single, obligatory, all-or-none secretory program.

The Passos et al. 2010 p21–reactive oxygen species (ROS) feedback loop also belongs within this DDR account. Persistent p21 signalling induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS through GADD45, p38 MAPK, GRB2, TGFBR2 and TGFβ; those ROS replenish short-lived DNA-damage foci and keep DDR signalling active. In late-generation TERC-deficient mice, loss of CDKN1A reduced oxidative-damage measures and DNA-damage foci in intestinal crypts and neurons, supporting a p21-dependent route from telomere dysfunction to oxidative damage in vivo.[120][29]

Age-associated observations fit the model but do not make every DDR-positive cell a proven cause of dysfunction. In male C57BL/6 mouse liver and small intestine, mean telomere-associated DNA-damage foci (TAF) per cell increased exponentially with age and the fraction of foci that were TAF increased linearly; each age group contained three mice.[7] In another aging-mouse survey, γH2AX foci increased in lung, spleen, dermis, liver and gut epithelium from 12 to 42 months, but intestinal crypt foci showed only random telomere co-localization even in very old animals, demonstrating that age-associated DDR-positive states need not be telomere-driven in every tissue.[18]

Inflammatory secretion also cannot be equated perfectly with stable senescence. Damaged cycling cells near senescence, and cells that have bypassed it, can produce the DDR-dependent cytokine response; p53 inactivation did not prevent IL-6 secretion in the tested settings.[17][16] This means the tissue-level pathology model includes persistent damage signalling in a broader population than fully established senescent cells alone.

**The central DDR explanation of age-related pathology is a persistence model: unrepaired damage initially creates a protective checkpoint, but chronic telomeric or non-telomeric DDR locks cells out of renewal and drives inflammatory and oxidative changes that can degrade tissue function.**[7][29][123]

A 2022 senescence-atlas study of injured mouse muscle adds tissue- and time-resolved support for this persistence account. Telomeric DDR staining was higher in regenerating muscle from old than young mice, and senescent-cell-enriched populations had both telomeric DDR and the ROS-induced DNA-lesion marker 8-oxoguanine. The authors found that a subset of niche cells accumulated ROS and DNA damage after injury and entered senescence, linking lifetime damage, including telomere damage, to heightened susceptibility to senescence in old age.[78]

In the same model, senescence-associated signals arose by 3 days after injury, remained elevated at 7 days, and then declined; old muscle contained more senescent cells that persisted longer and recovered less efficiently. Thus, the DDR model gains a concrete temporal prediction: damage-associated senescence becomes pathological when damage-bearing cells remain in the regenerative niche after the acute response should have resolved.[78]

In cardiotoxin-injured muscle, senescence-associated signals were more abundant and persisted longer in mice aged at least 28 months than in mice aged 3–6 months.[163][78]

Conversely, senescence-like states can arise with weak or no canonical DDR, so persistent DDR is a major route into senescence rather than a universal explanation for every senescent state.

### How does the mitochondrial dysfunction hypothesis explain the tumor-suppressive role

The mitochondrial dysfunction hypothesis associated with Passos et al. (2010) does not replace the DDR model. It explains how mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS help establish or stabilize senescence after checkpoint activation. Long-term p21 activation produces mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS, and ROS replenish DNA-damage foci; the resulting positive-feedback circuit maintains DDR signalling and stable arrest.[120] Mitochondria are therefore a reinforcing component of the tumour-suppressive barrier: they help sustain cytostasis, meaning the durable halt to cell proliferation.

Mitochondrial dysfunction can also feed into arrest upstream of or alongside the DDR. Inefficient oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) raises ROS, which can damage DNA and telomeres, activate p53 and pRB pathways, and induce senescence.[19] In mitochondrial dysfunction-associated senescence (MiDAS), a low NAD+/NADH ratio activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), followed by p53; p53 then limits the IL-1-mediated arm of the SASP. These routes converge on canonical tumour suppressors rather than creating an independent mitochondrial arrest machinery.[24]

The anti-tumour implication is straightforward: ROS-induced damage, low-energy stress or oncogenic metabolic stress can activate p53/p21 and RB-associated growth arrest, preventing expansion of a potentially transformed cell. Yet mitochondrial stress can yield apoptosis rather than senescence depending on redox buffering and mitochondrial sensitivity. In p21-induced cancer-cell experiments, mitochondria-targeted catalase reduced oxidant-induced cell death, whereas cytoplasmic catalase did not, showing that mitochondrial oxidative sensitivity influences the decision between survival/arrest and death.[124]

A key experimental distinction is that mitochondrial activity appears more necessary for the pro-oxidant and inflammatory phenotype than for the arrest itself. In senescent MRC5 fibroblasts engineered for Parkin-mediated mitochondrial depletion, 38% of all senescence-associated RNA changes reversed, including 60% of significantly changed SASP genes, whereas cell-cycle genes showed generally no rescue.[26] This separates the stable growth arrest from many mitochondria-dependent features of the senescent phenotype.

That separation supports the idea that mitochondrial targeting could preserve an anti-proliferative arrest while reducing harmful signalling. The investigators proposed mitochondria as therapeutic targets that might affect pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory features while maintaining tumour-suppressor capability, but this was a mechanistic implication rather than an in-vivo tumour-prevention result.[26] It also has an important limitation: p21-mediated arrest can protect damaged cells from DNA-damage-induced apoptosis and thereby, in some cancer contexts, favour transformation.[124]

**Relative to DDR’s checkpoint function and chromatin’s gene-silencing lock, the mitochondrial framework adds a p21–ROS feedback circuit that reinforces tumour-suppressive arrest and governs many inflammatory and pro-oxidant senescence features.**[120][19][26]

### How does the mitochondrial dysfunction hypothesis explain the role of

For age-related pathology, the mitochondrial hypothesis is strongest as an amplification model. Dysfunctional mitochondria generate ROS, altered NAD+/NADH balance, reduced membrane potential, defective quality control and inflammatory mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These signals reinforce DDR, activate p53/p21 or p16/RB arrest pathways, and can sustain non-cell-autonomous inflammation.[19][20][35]

Mitochondria can propagate pathology without acting only through nuclear DNA damage. Dysfunctional mitochondria release mtDNA and TFAM, which can activate Toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors and inflammatory signalling. Cytosolic mtDNA can activate cGAS–STING, a pathway that regulates senescence and the SASP. Reduced OXPHOS gene expression and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential can increase ROS, activate JNK, promote cytoplasmic chromatin fragments, and activate cGAS–STING, thereby amplifying inflammatory SASP factors and reinforcing senescence in neighbouring cells.[20][35]

This makes a plausible route from persistent mitochondrial dysfunction to chronic tissue damage: ROS and inflammatory signals generate bystander senescence, reshape stem-cell niches, and reduce regenerative capacity. Hydrogen peroxide is relatively long-lived and diffusible, so it may contribute to the bystander effect in which cells with activated DDR affect previously unstressed neighbours.[29][12] Senescent cells are normally cleared after acute physiological roles, but inefficient clearance in aging allows their accumulation and is associated with disrupted tissue function and damaged stem-cell niches.[20][21]

The hypothesis also predicts heterogeneity in the SASP. MiDAS does not simply reproduce the canonical inflammatory programme: its AMPK–p53 response limits the IL-1-mediated SASP arm.[24] More broadly, mitochondrial dysfunction can be associated with inflammatory cGAS–STING or NLRP3 signalling, but its output depends on the injury, metabolic state, cell type and the balance between compensation and failure.[20][35]

The evidence does not support treating severe mitochondrial dysfunction as the dominant source of senescence in ordinary aging. The physiological contribution of senescence induced by severe mitochondrial dysfunction remains unresolved, and whether mtDNA damage reaches levels sufficient to cause senescence during normal aging has not been determined.[19][35] Conversely, mitochondrial ROS clearly interact with telomere damage: telomere uncapping induces mitochondrial dysfunction through ATM and p21, while telomere shortening activates p53 and inhibits PGC-1α and PGC-1β, impairing mitochondrial function.[19]

**The mitochondrial hypothesis best explains the harmful effector arm of persistent senescence—ROS, metabolic failure, inflammatory signalling and bystander propagation—while the arrest itself remains integrated with DDR and p53/RB checkpoint control.**[120][20][26][19]

The 2022 senescence-atlas study supplies functional support for ROS as an entry signal in an injured tissue. Satellite cells and fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) with high ROS one day after injury had more 8-oxoguanine than ROS-low cells; ROS-high cells became senescent in culture, N-acetylcysteine blocked their senescence entry, and ROS inhibition reduced senescence during regeneration. Senescent cells were enriched for ROS, oxidative-phosphorylation and lipid-metabolism pathways while DNA-damage-repair and mitochondrial-response pathways were downregulated.[78]

These findings place mitochondrial and oxidative stress upstream of senescence entry in this muscle-injury setting and connect them to the DDR framework: ROS-associated DNA damage can trigger the durable arrest, while defective mitochondrial-response programmes accompany the established senescent state.[78]

Mitochondrial dysfunction is widespread in aged tissues and is not synonymous with senescence: it can occur in post-mitotic cells that do not undergo classical cell-cycle arrest. Reduced mitochondrial function in an aged tissue may also be downstream of inflammation, altered cell composition, reduced activity or disease rather than the initiating cause of senescence.

### How does the chromatin remodeling framework explain the tumor-suppressive role

The chromatin-remodeling framework associated with Narita et al. (2006) explains durable senescence as a gene-regulatory lock. Senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF) are compacted chromatin domains that silence proliferation-promoting genes, particularly E2F target genes required for DNA synthesis. p16INK4A–RB signalling supports this repression: p16 blocks CDK4/6, RB remains active, and RB represses E2F-dependent transcription.[32][5]

High-mobility group A (HMGA) proteins provide an architectural mechanism for this lock. HMGA proteins cooperate with p16INK4A to form and stabilize SAHF, compact chromatin, repress proliferation-associated genes and reinforce RB-mediated inhibition of E2F targets.[4] This gives the framework a tumour-suppressive logic distinct from damage sensing: DDR or oncogenic stress may initiate arrest, but chromatin reorganization helps make escape from that arrest difficult.

In RAS-induced senescence, loss of ATM, ATR, CHK1 or CHK2 increased DNA replication; combined inhibition of DNA-damage-response and p16^INK4a pathways allowed over 60% of cells to replicate DNA, compared with approximately 5% of control cells.[165]

Chromatin regulation also directly controls activation of p16INK4A. The INK4/ARF locus is normally silenced by Polycomb repressive complexes PRC1 and PRC2; disrupting CBX7, BMI1 or EZH2, with loss of H3K27 trimethylation or PRC1 occupancy, can activate p16INK4A and induce senescence.[6] Oncogenic RAS can override Polycomb repression by activating the H3K27 demethylase JMJD3 and reducing EZH2; in human fibroblasts, JMJD3 activated p16INK4A and caused p16INK4A-dependent arrest.[125]

Chromatin control is functionally relevant to cancer protection in selected settings. HIRA, a histone chaperone that deposits histone H3.3, dynamically redistributes during senescence and participates in the gene-expression changes that accompany it. HIRA and newly deposited H3.3 were acquired at 1,919 genes in senescent cells, enriched for TNF-signalling genes within the SASP. HIRA-dependent chromatin regulation therefore connects the stable cell-cycle arrest to an altered secretory and immune-interacting phenotype.[34]

SAHF are not universal senescence markers or universal arrest requirements. BJ fibroblasts can reverse replicative senescence after p53 depletion, whereas WI38 fibroblasts are more SAHF-competent and harder to reverse when p16–RB activity is intact.[3] This supports a stabilizing role for chromatin organization, but not an assertion that every senescent cell must contain visible SAHF. The broader arrest machinery remains p53/p21 and p16/RB, which can act in contexts with differing chromatin architectures.[53][4]

Chromatin-dependent SASP regulation creates a further tumour-suppression trade-off. SASP can reinforce senescence and recruit immune clearance, but interventions that suppress inflammatory output must preserve permanent arrest because bypassing arrest could drive tumorigenesis.[115][33] Conversely, prolonged chromatin-enabled secretory programmes can support tumour-promoting inflammation, immune evasion and invasion.[33][3]

**Relative to DDR initiation and mitochondrial feedback, the chromatin framework explains tumour suppression as execution and maintenance: it durably represses proliferation genes, while associated enhancer and secretory remodeling can become tumour-promoting when senescent cells persist.**[4][6][33]

Later chromatin studies refine the Narita framework from a simple gain of repressive marks to a three-dimensional reorganization model. During Ras-induced senescence in human diploid fibroblasts, lamin B1 (LMNB1) declined globally and was preferentially depleted from H3K9me3-enriched lamina-associated domains; LMNB1 knockdown promoted relocation of perinuclear H3K9me3-positive heterochromatin and SAHF formation, whereas ectopic LMNB1 inhibited SAHF formation. Co-depleting LMNB1 and expressing HMGA1 or HMGA2 induced SAHF more efficiently.[44]

The global H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 landscapes changed little during Ras-induced senescence, indicating that SAHF formation is spatial repositioning of pre-existing repressively marked chromatin rather than genome-wide spreading of those marks. SAHF assembly is a multi-step process in which visible chromosome condensation precedes H3K9 methylation, HP1 binding and macroH2A incorporation.[44][45]

This refinement strengthens the maintenance interpretation: p16INK4A–RB, HMGA proteins, LMNB1 loss and nuclear repositioning cooperate to make proliferation genes difficult to reactivate. It also identifies a route of oncogenic escape, because HDM2 and CDK4 coexpression cancels the antiproliferative activity of HMGA proteins; these oncogenes are often coamplified with HMGA2 in human cancers.[41]

### How does the chromatin remodeling framework explain the role of

Chromatin remodeling explains age-related pathology by making senescence an enduring change in cell identity rather than a temporary response to stress. SAHF and related chromatin reorganization silence E2F-driven proliferation genes and stabilize withdrawal from the cell cycle.[5][4] This is intrinsically costly in tissues that depend on replacement of stem, progenitor or differentiated cells.

The framework also distinguishes arrest from tissue-disruptive signalling. Senescence-associated chromatin changes can preserve repression of cell-cycle genes while activating inflammatory and secretory loci. HIRA/H3.3 redistribution parallels altered gene expression and includes acquisition at TNF-signalling genes, while DNA-damage-induced SASP can be mediated through GATA4 and NF-κB. Thus, chromatin remodeling can simultaneously impose a local proliferation block and generate signals that alter neighbouring cells.[34][115]

This distinction helps explain why persistent senescence can impair tissue function through more than cell loss. Senescent cells may reduce regeneration because they cannot divide, while their cytokines, growth factors and extracellular-matrix-degrading enzymes can remodel the local niche.[123] Chronic SASP signalling may explain how a relatively small number of senescent cells exert durable local and systemic effects through inflammaging, the low-grade sterile inflammation associated with aging.[53]

The heterogeneity of chromatin states limits simplistic interpretation. Pronounced SAHF form in some human cell systems, but are not established as comparable punctate structures in mouse senescent cells; the direction of age-related heterochromatin change is also not uniform across primate skin, progeria fibroblasts and fibroblasts from people older than 80 years.[32] The relevant pathology is therefore not “more” or “less” heterochromatin overall, but stress-, cell-type- and locus-specific reprogramming of proliferation, repair, inflammatory and lineage genes.

**Chromatin remodeling provides a maintenance-and-propagation explanation for pathology: it can lock a damaged cell out of regeneration while independently enabling secretory programmes that remodel its tissue, so growth arrest and inflammatory dysfunction must be analysed as separable outputs.**[5][34][123]

Recent single-cell and spatial work makes the chromatin framework more explicitly cell-state- and location-specific. Spatial transcriptomics, a method that measures transcript profiles while retaining each profile’s position in a tissue section, mapped 273,923 single-cell transcriptomes and high-resolution spatial profiles across muscle repair in young, old and geriatric mice. It identified senescent-like stem/progenitor subsets at transitional self-renewal states across all ages, with enrichment in aged muscle and within aged injury zones.[77]

The enrichment was restricted to particular regenerative states: at 3.5 days after injury, geriatric MuSC 1 cells and old and geriatric muscle progenitor cells had higher *Cdkn2a*/*Cdkn1a* double-positive fractions than their young counterparts, whereas MuSCs 2–6 showed no age-specific difference. The study-defined Sen Score-high category used a threshold of 2,412, at which 50% of cells coexpressed *Cdkn2a* and *Cdkn1a*.[77]

These maps show that age-related senescence-linked transcriptional states occupy defined regenerative niches rather than forming one uniform chromatin state. They spatially associate senescent-like cells with impaired repair; they do not themselves identify a specific chromatin alteration or SASP component as the tissue-dysfunction cause.[77]

### What temporal dynamics question is raised by Baker et al.'s

Baker et al.’s 2011 INK-ATTAC study asked whether timing changes the consequences of removing p16Ink4a-expressing cells. INK-ATTAC is a transgenic system in which AP20187 activates an engineered caspase to induce apoptosis in targeted p16Ink4a-positive cells. In BubR1 hypomorphic progeroid mice, AP20187 was given every third day either from weaning at 3 weeks of age or from 5 months of age, when age-related phenotypes were already apparent.[126]

The INK-ATTAC construct uses the *p16Ink4a* promoter to drive a drug-inducible caspase-8 suicide mechanism in cells with high promoter activity.[164][166]

Life-long clearance delayed lordokyphosis and cataracts and preserved skeletal-muscle function and adipose tissue. Treated mice performed better on treadmill measures of exercise duration, distance and total work.[126] This establishes that the targeted population contributes early to the onset of particular p16Ink4a-dependent phenotypes in this accelerated-aging model.

Late-life clearance answered a different question: can established decline still be modified? Mice treated from 5 months and assessed at 10 months had larger muscle fibres, better treadmill performance and improved fat measures. Baker et al. concluded that these changes represented attenuation of further age-related decline rather than reversal of aging to the 5-month state.[126][127]

The benefits were selective. Cardiac arrhythmia and arterial-wall stiffening, which arose in a p16Ink4a-independent manner in this model, were not attenuated, and overall survival was not substantially extended.[126] Thus the experiment does not establish p16-positive senescent cells as a universal cause of progeroid decline. It identifies a causal role for the targeted population in selected tissues and phenotypes.

The temporal question becomes sharper when acute repair is considered. In later INK-ATTAC experiments, p16-positive-cell clearance during wound closure delayed healing in both aged and young mice, whereas pausing AP treatment during healing allowed previously treated aged mice to repair wounds.[80] Senescence can therefore be useful during a short repair interval yet harmful when cells or their signalling persist beyond that interval.

A recent irradiation model illustrates that the pathogenic population itself can arise late. In INK-ATTAC mice, clearing p16Ink4a-positive cells 1 month after irradiation did not improve lifespan or functional outcomes, whereas clearance beginning 4 months after irradiation reduced frailty, improved neuromuscular and cognitive measures, improved blood–brain-barrier integrity and hepatic metabolic dysfunction, and increased median survival, particularly in females.[62] In brain and liver, p21Cip1 positivity rose at 1 month, while robust p16Ink4a accumulation appeared later at 4 months; TAF persisted across both time points.[62]

In the later irradiation study, clearance begun four months after irradiation increased median survival from 725 days to 786.5 days in the combined cohort, a difference that was not statistically significant. In females, median survival increased significantly from 696.5 days to 743 days.[62]

This later work supports a two-phase interpretation: early p21-associated responses may represent an acute stress programme, while a later p16Ink4a-positive population becomes amenable to targeted clearance. Yet this is not a universal clearance schedule, because pharmacological senolytics remove broader populations than INK-ATTAC, and different tissues contain functionally distinct p21-positive, p16-positive and dual-positive states.[62][128]

**Baker et al.’s 2011 experiment distinguishes prevention from modification of established disease: repeated clearance of p16Ink4a-positive cells from 3 weeks delayed selected phenotypes, whereas clearance begun at 5 months attenuated subsequent muscle and fat decline without restoring the 5-month state.** The intervention removed a p16Ink4a-positive cell population; cardiac arrhythmia and arterial-wall stiffening were unaffected and overall survival was not substantially extended.[126][127]

Baker et al. tested two distinct clearance histories in BubR1 hypomorphic progeroid INK-ATTAC mice: AP20187 was administered every third day from weaning at 3 weeks of age for life-long clearance, or was begun at 5 months for late-life clearance.[126] Repeated clearance from 3 weeks delayed selected phenotypes, including lordokyphosis and cataracts, and preserved skeletal-muscle function and adipose tissue.[126] Clearance begun at 5 months improved muscle-fibre diameter, treadmill performance and fat measures at 10 months by attenuating subsequent decline rather than restoring the mice to their 5-month state.[126][127]

Muscle studies sharpen, and complicate, the timing question. A 2022 mouse muscle atlas found senescent cells to be scarce in uninjured muscle even in old age but to emerge after injury; in old injured muscle they persisted longer. This means that senescent-cell burden can be generated by an acute tissue challenge in a previously low-burden tissue, rather than simply accumulating continuously with chronological age.[78]

The functional consequence in this tissue depended on senescent-cell presence in the regenerative niche. Daily dasatinib plus quercetin reduced SA-β-gal-positive cells, accelerated regeneration, increased force, and reduced matrix deposition and inflammation in young and old injured mice. Transplanted senescent, but not non-senescent, cells increased host-tissue senescence, inflammatory-cell recruitment and fibrosis and delayed regeneration.[78]

These experiments establish that senescent cells can impair regeneration even when introduced transiently after mild acute injury, challenging a simple rule that transient senescence is beneficial whereas only chronic senescence is harmful. The temporal question is therefore which cell type, injury context, tissue location and duration make a given senescent state protective or pathological.[78]

The 2011 design did not track individual cells from senescence induction through persistence and clearance, so it cannot determine the birth date or lifetime of every targeted cell. Nor can it establish that p16Ink4a labels all senescent cells, identify the secreted factor responsible for benefit, or specify an optimal clearance window for normal mice or humans.

The later Baker et al. INK-ATTAC study extended the timing result from progeroid mice to naturally aging mice. In two cohorts, AP20187 treatment started at 12 months and continued until death; median lifespan increased by 27% in the mixed-background mixed-sex cohort and by 24% in the congenic C57BL/6J mixed-sex cohort.[129] Maximum lifespan increased in the mixed-background cohort but not in the congenic C57BL/6J cohort.[129] Clearance delayed tumour onset without changing overall tumour incidence or the variety of tumour types, reduced adipose loss, glomerulosclerosis and kidney-function decline, and preserved cardiac stress tolerance, but did not alter age-related motor coordination, memory or muscle strength.[129] This establishes that persistent p16Ink4a-positive cells can drive selected late-life pathologies and delay tumour emergence in ordinary mouse aging as well as in accelerated aging.[129]

Late-life INK-ATTAC clearance was partial and tissue-selective; colon and liver were relatively resistant to clearance.[80]

In naturally aging INK-ATTAC mice, AP20187 reduced p16Ink4a and other senescence-marker transcripts in most examined tissues, but not in the colon or liver.[80]

Baker et al. (2016) separately tested clearance of naturally occurring p16Ink4a-positive cells in naturally aged, non-progeroid mice and reported increased median lifespan in both sexes and across different genetic backgrounds.[100][2]

### Synthesis —

The three frameworks describe connected layers rather than mutually exclusive explanations. The DDR model best explains initiation by genomic or telomeric damage and the checkpoint logic of tumour suppression. The mitochondrial model explains ROS-, metabolic- and inflammatory-feedback processes that stabilize damage signalling and amplify non-cell-autonomous pathology. The chromatin model explains durable execution of arrest and locus-specific reprogramming of secretory genes. Together, Baker et al.’s timing experiments favour a two-phase model: acute, spatially restricted senescence can support repair and cancer control, while failed clearance permits persistent arrested cells and their diverse outputs to impair tissue function. The important distinction is therefore between initiating senescence, maintaining arrest, and propagating tissue effects; removing one does not automatically remove the others.[120][4][126][80][26]

Recent spatial and functional muscle studies add a hierarchy of evidence to this synthesis. Spatial transcriptomic maps localize senescent-like cells to aged injury zones and establish that their abundance, cell identity and timing vary across regenerative states. Senescent-cell transplantation, depletion and D+Q treatment then show that senescent cells can drive inflammatory recruitment, fibrosis and delayed regeneration in this model.[77][78]

The SASP is a plausible propagation mechanism rather than a uniform entity: the 2022 atlas identified 78 to 363 SASP components depending on cell type and conditions, with inflammation and matrix remodelling/fibrosis as major functions, and confirmed secretion of CCL2, IGFBP6, CD40, IL13, CXCL1, IL1A, CXCL11 and MMP2 from senescent cells in injured aged muscle. Ligand–receptor analysis predicted that these SASP signals activate senescence, apoptosis and inflammatory responses while inhibiting MAPK- and AKT-linked proliferative pathways in receiving satellite cells.[78]

The injured-muscle atlas associated those cell-type- and condition-specific SASP repertoires with cytokine and TNF/NF-κB signalling and with matrix organization, collagen metabolism and TGFβ signalling.[78]

In sorted muscle stem cells, fibroadipogenic progenitors and myeloid cells, the inferred secretory programmes included complement, cytokine and TNF/NF-κB inflammatory pathways alongside collagen, matrix and TGF-β-associated fibrotic pathways.[78]

Accordingly, current evidence supports a causal harmful effect of senescent cells in defined regenerative settings and supports SASP-mediated paracrine signalling as a mechanism for that effect. Spatial co-localization and transcriptomic SASP signatures establish where the relevant states and signals occur; cell transplantation and senescent-cell depletion establish the tissue-level causal contribution of the senescent-cell population.[77][78]

The case that SASP factors cause tissue dysfunction rests on interventions that change secretory signalling and phenotype without removing the arrest programme. In old mice, blocking mtDNA release reduced bone SASP factors and improved frailty, coordination, grip strength, and trabecular bone microarchitecture despite unchanged bone p16, p21 and p53.[63] In mice, BAX inhibition similarly reduced bone SASP-factor expression and improved healthspan measures without changing lifespan.[63] SASP components can also propagate dysfunction: TGFβ-family ligands, VEGF, CCL2 and CCL20 induce paracrine senescence, and CCL2 from senescent melanoma-cell conditioned medium increased 53BP1 damage foci in recipient cells.[130]

The case that SASP factors are correlates is supplied by their contextual and separable regulation. SASP composition, targets and outcomes vary with cell type and stress; ATM depletion abolished IL-6 and IL-8 secretion in irradiated senescent fibroblasts but did not impede MCP1, TIMP2 or IGFBP2 induction.[130] Mitochondrial depletion nearly abolished IL-6, IL-8, GRO and MCP-1 secretion in several fibroblast senescence models, yet cells ultimately remained arrested without SASP. Thus, stable arrest, mitochondrial dysfunction and individual SASP modules are distinct outputs that can coexist or be uncoupled.[63][130]

## SASP causality and spatial evidence

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is the changing set of molecules released by senescent cells: inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, growth and angiogenic factors, proteases, extracellular-matrix modifiers, lipids, extracellular vesicles, and other signals. In ordinary terms, senescent cells not only stop dividing; they also communicate intensely with their neighbours. This matters because the same secretion can recruit immune cells and support repair after acute injury, or, if it persists, remodel tissue, spread senescence, and sustain chronic inflammation.[53][58][131]

### What is the case that senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors

SASP is separable from the permanent cell-cycle arrest that defines senescence. Persistent DNA-damage response (DDR) signalling—the continuing alarm produced by unrepaired or irreparable DNA lesions—can initiate inflammatory secretion through ATM, NBS1 and CHK2, whereas p53 and pRb principally enforce arrest. In irradiated human fibroblasts, ATM depletion reduced 7 of 16 selected SASP factors, reducing IL-6 secretion 50-fold and IL-8 10-fold, while nine measured factors changed little. Conversely, fibroblasts arrested by p16^INK4a^ expression had few persistent DNA-damage foci and secreted little IL-6; high IL-6 tracked with persistent damage foci rather than arrest alone.[16][17]

This distinction supplies a direct mechanistic case: a senescent cell’s inflammatory output is regulated, rather than being an inevitable by-product of non-division. The DDR can therefore generate two partly independent outputs: p53-dependent arrest and, after several days of persistent signalling, a p53-independent cytokine programme. A transient DDR after low-dose radiation resolved without increased IL-6, whereas 10 Gy irradiation caused persistent foci and increased IL-6 and IL-8 secretion 5- to 6-fold within 2–4 days.[17]

Several experiments show that secreted products can produce defined effects in recipient cells. Conditioned medium from senescent cells promoted invasion by T47D breast-cancer cells; depletion of ATM in the senescent donor cells removed most of this activity, and recombinant IL-6 largely restored it. This establishes a causal IL-6-dependent component of the invasion phenotype in that assay.[16][17] Acosta et al.

(2013) showed that senescent-cell conditioned medium induced reactive oxygen species (ROS), DNA-damage signalling and senescence in normal bystander cells.[132] In the Acosta et al. (2013) experiment, blocking either IL-1β/NF-κB or TGF-β/SMAD signalling reduced ROS and DDR activation, and blocking both fully suppressed the measured bystander DDR.[132]

The case extends beyond cultured cells. In young and old mice, reducing senescent-cell burden or neutralizing CD36 to reduce the inflammatory secretome accelerated muscle regeneration; transplantation of senescent cells delayed regeneration. These paired interventions indicate that secreted inflammatory activity contributes to impaired repair, rather than dysfunction arising solely because senescent cells themselves cannot proliferate.[73] In traumatic osteoarthritis models, senescent chondrocytes impaired cartilage deposition in explant culture, while genetic clearance or local senolytic treatment reduced pain and promoted cartilage repair.[33]

Specific factors can also have tissue-relevant causal actions. Senescent fat progenitors secrete activin A, which directly inhibits adipogenesis; clearance of senescent cells in aged mice reduced circulating activin A, rescued fat loss and enhanced adipogenesis.[81] In aged osteoporotic mice, senescent osteocytes promote receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand production and osteoclast activity, while genetic clearance or senolytic treatment reduced SASP production in the bone-marrow environment and improved bone mass and microarchitecture.[133] These examples show that particular SASP-related signals can be effectors of dysfunction in defined tissues.

**The causal conclusion is therefore specific, not universal: persistent SASP can propagate senescence, alter matrix and immune behaviour, and impair repair, but the relevant factors depend on the cell, tissue, stressor and time after senescence begins.** SASP composition and magnitude vary with the inducing stimulus, duration, environment and cell type, and some senescent cells acquire little detectable SASP. This makes factor- and context-specific intervention more informative than treating any inflammatory marker as the universal toxic output of senescence.[53][58]

Mouse muscle regeneration supplies a stronger separation of secretory from cell-number effects. In the senescence atlas study, CD36 blockade left the number of senescent cells unchanged but reduced several SASP proteins, improved regeneration in young and old injured muscle, reduced inflammation and fibrosis, and increased muscle force. Silencing *Cd36* specifically in transplanted senescent cells likewise abolished their regeneration-delaying and satellite-cell-proliferation-suppressing effects. This identifies a CD36-regulated SASP programme as a causal paracrine effector in that model.[78]

The same study showed that sorted senescent cells secreted inflammatory and matrix-remodelling proteins, including CCL2, IGFBP6, CD40, IL13, CXCL1, IL1A, CXCL11 and MMP2 in injured aged muscle. Transplanting senescent, but not non-senescent, cells into preinjured muscle increased host senescence, inflammatory-cell recruitment and fibrosis and delayed regeneration; in Transwell culture, senescent cells reduced non-senescent satellite-cell proliferation.[78]

A distinct factor-level experiment in senescent skeletal-muscle fibroadipogenic progenitors (FAPs) found approximately 20-fold more CCL2 and approximately 50-fold more osteopontin (OPN) in conditioned medium than from control FAPs. This medium caused a 12-fold greater macrophage-migration response, and neutralizing either CCL2 or OPN reduced recruitment; it also increased M2-associated *Cd206* and *Lyve1* expression and the proportion of M2 macrophages.[134]

PDGF-AA is a SASP-related factor with a context-dependent beneficial action: it promotes wound closure in vivo.[33]

Demaria et al. (2014) found that transient senescence-associated platelet-derived growth factor AA (PDGF-AA) contributes to optimal wound healing: selective elimination of p16^INK4a^+ cells in p16-3MR mice delayed wound closure, and exogenous PDGF-AA rescued the delay.[135]

Acosta et al. (2013) identified an inflammasome-orchestrated complex secretory program that controls paracrine senescence.[26]

Transplanting small numbers of senescent cells into young healthy animals recapitulated age-related impairment of physical function and spread senescence to host tissues.[136] These transplantation experiments establish that the introduced senescent cells can drive recipient dysfunction, but they do not isolate a particular SASP factor as its cause.[136]

Across female-mouse hypothalamus, heart, liver and kidney, *Mmp12* was the only assayed SASP factor with a significant age-dependent increase in all four tissues.[137] Mice lacking *Mmp12* were protected from vascular injury, M2-macrophage accumulation and perivascular heart fibrosis.[137]

### What is the case that SASP factors are correlative in

The correlative interpretation begins with heterogeneity. SASP is not a single exposure: it is a composite of many proteins and non-protein signals, and its composition can vary with cell lineage, senescence inducer, tissue environment and time. A tissue sample with elevated IL-6, MMPs or TGF-β may therefore represent a changed mix of immune cells, stromal cells and senescent subtypes rather than a common pathogenic secretory programme.[53][33]

Single-cell RNA sequencing makes this problem concrete. In senescent mouse embryonic fibroblasts, *Il6* and *Cxcl1* were upregulated in only a subset of cells, explaining why a bulk assay could miss the signal.[59] In etoposide-treated MCF10A cells, inflammatory SASP pathways appeared in one senescent cluster, while another expressed wound-healing, pro-growth and matrix-remodelling pathways, and a third lacked SASP-related pathways.

Yet 21 SASP genes were shared across all senescent clusters and doses, showing that common elements coexist with substantial subtype variation.[72] Thus, an aggregate SASP measurement can reflect cell-state composition or severity of injury as readily as it identifies a single causal agent.

Other mechanisms can directly impair tissue independently of SASP. Senescent cells lose proliferative contribution to repair; persistent DDR-positive cells can cause ROS-mediated damage in their microenvironment; extracellular-matrix production and transforming growth factor-β signalling can change cell-autonomously; and aging alters immune-cell composition and clearance.[33][12][138] In the aging lung, for example, IL-6 increases in bronchoalveolar lavage, but its source remains unresolved: it may derive from senescent parenchyma or from age-related changes in immune-cell composition and function.[116]

The human intervention record reinforces this distinction. In the first-in-human open-label idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis D+Q pilot, functional changes correlated with changes in 23 of 48 SASP-related matrix-remodelling proteins, microRNAs and cytokines at r ≥ 0.50, but the treatment effect on circulating SASP factors was inconclusive.[93] In diabetic kidney disease, skin p16^INK4A^+ and p21^CIP1^+ cells and circulating IL-1α, IL-6, MMP-9 and MMP-12 all declined after D+Q. These are concurrent changes after removal or alteration of a cell population, not isolation of a factor that produced a functional outcome.[94][101]

Inflammatory signals also have phase-specific effects that defeat a simple toxin model. In bleomycin lung injury, IL-6 neutralization during the early inflammatory phase worsened fibrosis, whereas neutralization during the later fibrotic phase reduced fibrosis.[116] In liver damage, senescence of activated hepatic stellate cells reduces extracellular-matrix production and generates signals that enable natural-killer-cell clearance; disabling stellate-cell senescence increases fibrosis.[138] Therefore, raised SASP factors can be part of a necessary repair programme, a marker of ongoing injury, a cause of later pathology, or all three sequentially.

**The correlative case does not mean SASP is inert; it means that a tissue-level SASP signal cannot by itself identify the initiating cause, the responsible cell type, or the harmful factor.** The practical implication is that causation in a given disease requires selective manipulation of defined SASP signals while holding senescent-cell burden, injury burden and immune context as constant as possible.

A CellChat analysis inferred 810 SASP-mediated ligand–receptor pairs and higher aggregate SASP interaction strength in aged than young muscle (51.8 versus 47.2), including elevated CXCL-family interaction strength and frequency and 54 age-increased pairs involving muscle stem cells (MuSCs). These observations identify candidate communication routes, including MIF signalling from MuSCs to B/T/NK cells and MIF or FN1 signalling to FAPs or smooth-muscle cells, rather than demonstrating that a particular secreted ligand produced sarcopenia.[139]

Neither *CDKN2A*/*p16INK4a* nor *CDKN1A*/*p21* is an interchangeable universal marker of senescence, and low transcript capture in single-cell data can further obscure either programme. A high local SASP score may therefore report injury burden, immune infiltration or repair-state stromal cells rather than a senescent-cell-derived initiating signal.

Mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced stem-cell or progenitor-cell renewal, extracellular damage and altered immune-cell composition can each contribute to tissue dysfunction alongside SASP expression.[140][141]

### What do recent spatial transcriptomics data show about SASP and

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression while preserving approximate tissue location. It therefore addresses a question that dissociated single-cell sequencing cannot: whether senescence-associated cells and their putative target cells occupy the same local niche. This is important because enlarged, rare senescent cells can be lost during tissue dissociation, and cell suspensions largely erase spatial relationships.[142]

In a 2021 spatial-transcriptomic analysis of postmortem human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, 342 of 4,226 voxels (8%; 1,000-permutation *p* < 0.01) were enriched for the M82 senescence-gene module. This mapped senescence-associated niche resembled endothelial, microglial and astrocytic distributions; compared with non-enriched voxels, module-positive voxels had higher probabilistic annotations for endothelial cells (*p* = 9.6e-116), microglia (*p* = 6.9e-36) and astrocytes (*p* = 6.8e-53).[143] A subsequent human cortex preprint measured 13,702 genes across 276,701 spatial spots and identified age-associated increases in AGT, BMP4, BMP7, EFEMP1, IGFBP7, TGFB2 and VEGFA, especially in cortical layers 3–5. It also found distinct age-elevated astrocyte programmes and a white-matter microglial programme.[144]

That cortex study found that SASP signalling and apoptosis-resistance signatures were spatially coherent, whereas cell-cycle-arrest and DDR signatures were more dispersed. The authors identify paracrine SASP signalling as one possible explanation, but also identify cell-type heterogeneity and independently induced arrest programmes as alternatives.[144] In other words, spatial coherence supports local communication as a biological hypothesis, but does not distinguish a SASP-driven neighbourhood from multiple cells responding to the same inflammatory or degenerative environment.

A spatial multi-omics study of human colorectal adenoma-to-carcinoma progression identified a spatially confined epithelial niche co-expressing senescence, stemness and proliferation programmes that expanded from adenoma to carcinoma.[145] The Research Square record identifies a preprint version as not peer reviewed and lists a *Nature Aging* publication date of 14 July 2025.[146] In this cancer-progression niche, GDF15-high epithelium was associated with lower local CD8^+^ T-cell density in adenoma regions than GDF15-low epithelium (paired Wilcoxon *P* = 0.018), with the association significant in advanced adenomas (*P* < 0.001) but not nonadvanced adenomas (*P* = 0.31).

In cancer cores, higher mean epithelial *GDF15* expression was associated with greater median epithelial-to-nearest-CD8 distance (adjusted *P* = 0.037; adjusted *P* = 0.041 after accounting for per-core CD8 count).[145] In advanced adenomas, adjustment for GDF15 modestly reduced the senescence–stemness spatial association (paired Wilcoxon *P* = 0.009; sign-flip permutation *P* = 0.007), while adjustment had no significant effect in nonadvanced adenomas (*P* = 0.62).[145]

These findings identify a stage-dependent, spatially organized candidate route from senescence-associated signalling to immune exclusion and cancer progression. However, the colorectal authors explicitly characterize their data as correlational and call for functional tests before causality is assigned.[145] The distinction matters: GDF15 could contribute to CD8 exclusion, be induced by a shared dysplastic state, or mark epithelial regions with other unmeasured exclusionary signals.

Spatial studies resolve anatomical association and candidate sender–receiver neighbourhoods, not factor-level causation. A spatial spot or voxel generally contains more than one cell: the 2021 cortex analysis explicitly noted multi-cell voxels; the muscle atlas used 10-μm beads to which multiple cells contributed transcripts; and each 10x Visium BPH spot was approximately 55 μm in diameter and could contain multiple neighbouring cells. The newer cortex work integrates spatial data with single-nucleus RNA sequencing to improve cell-type attribution.[143][144][77][147]

Walter and colleagues deconvolved their 10-µm spatial-bead profiles against their single-cell RNA-sequencing reference, identifying 15 cell types and defining muscle injury zones by local immune-cell density.[77]

**Spatial data show that SASP-associated programmes occupy restricted, cell-type-specific niches in aging and disease; they make local propagation plausible but do not prove that SASP caused fibrosis, dysfunction or immune exclusion.** The decisive experiment is spatially targeted blockade or restoration of a nominated factor, followed by measurement of local tissue function while the underlying injury and senescent-cell abundance are independently measured.

Recent mouse skeletal-muscle work adds a regeneration-focused spatial example. A lifespan atlas combining 273,923 single-cell transcriptomes with spatial maps from approximately 5-, 20- and 26-month-old mice found senescent-like MuSC and progenitor subsets at transitional self-renewal states at every age, but enriched in aged muscle and, spatially, uniquely elevated in aged injury zones. At 5 days after injury, geriatric MuSC/progenitor senescent-like cells were more abundant in the whole spatial dataset and injury zone (*P* = 0.02 and *P* = 0.03); senescent-like non-MuSC/progenitor cells were also enriched, specifically in the noninjury zone (*P* = 7.53 × 10−38).[77]

Walter et al. classified candidate senescent-like muscle stem and progenitor cells using a 44-gene foreign-body-response score benchmarked against *Cdkn2a*/*Cdkn1a* co-expression. Its area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve was 0.86, versus 0.63 for the SenMayo score in that classification task.[161][77]

In Walter and colleagues’ spatial maps, young muscle injury zones contained relatively more fusing myocytes and fibroadipogenic progenitors, whereas geriatric injury zones contained relatively more monocytes/macrophages, T cells and myonuclei.[77]

A human benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) spatial/single-cell study places a hypoxia-linked senescence-associated programme predominantly in luminal epithelium. In four BPH specimens comprising 10,675 spatial spots, luminal-dominant spots had the highest senescence-associated transcriptional scores and senescence-score-high luminal spots jointly enriched hypoxia, hypoxia-adaptation and pro-inflammatory programmes while proliferation signatures were negatively enriched. Hypoxia-response, NF-κB and senescence scores were positively associated across spots, most strongly for NF-κB and senescence.[147]

Senescence-associated arrest, DNA-damage, inflammatory and matrix-remodelling programmes need not occupy the same cells or the same tissue locations.

The BPH study also connected this mapped state to a defined paracrine assay: conditioned medium from hypoxia-treated prostate epithelial cells promoted stromal proliferation, EdU incorporation and S-phase entry, while epithelial *HIF1A* knockdown attenuated these responses. Hypoxia increased epithelial secretion of IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-15 and CXCL12, and *HIF1A* knockdown generally reduced them. Thus, the spatially localized hypoxia–NF-κB–senescence programme has an experimentally active SASP-like secretome in vitro.[147]

Recent muscle studies refine, rather than remove, the temporal model. During acute mouse-muscle repair, SA-β-Gal-positive cells appeared at day 3 after injury, remained elevated at day 5 and became undetectable by day 21; spatial profiling localized elevated senescence- and SASP-gene activity to active repair and inflammatory zones. Eliminating these cells during regeneration reduced myofibre cross-sectional area and Pax7-positive MuSC abundance.[148]

Persistence remains associated with harmful repair states: senescent cells were more abundant and lasted longer in injured old mouse muscle, accompanying poorer recovery, and at 28 days after injury SA-β-Gal-positive cells remained elevated only in old mice, correlating with impaired regeneration. D+Q reduced SA-β-Gal-positive-cell burden and improved regeneration in old mice but impaired regeneration in young mice.[78][148]

Recent human cortex data reinforce cell-type and anatomical specificity. A 2026 study combined Visium spatial transcriptomics with single-nucleus RNA sequencing in nonpathological dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and identified age-enriched astrocyte- and vascular-cell-related programmes in white matter and layer 1 that were enriched for senescence-associated genes; homeostatic astrocytes increased and somatostatin inhibitory neurons decreased with age.[149]

A spatial-analysis study using DeepScence, a continuous gene-expression-derived senescence score, assigned higher scores to injured than healthy regions of mouse muscle at 2 and 5 days after notexin injury and to microglia from severe Alzheimer’s disease samples than to microglia from normally aging individuals. In normally aging mouse mammary tissue, almost all highest-scoring spots occurred in 18-month-old rather than 3-month-old mice and localized to the lymph-node region.[150] These results map senescence-associated transcriptional states to injury-, disease- and age-associated niches.[150]

Recent colorectal spatial and single-cell analysis likewise places senescence-associated programmes at tissue interfaces. Across eight primary colorectal-cancer spatial samples, high–high Senescence Index clusters localized predominantly to the tumor periphery, invasive front and adjacent stromal or immune-infiltrated regions (87.3% overall; range 79.1–94.2%), compared with 12.7% overall in the tumor core (range 5.8–20.9%).[151] CellChat nominated MIF and midkine as the dominant inferred signalling pathways from high-senescence-score subpopulations, followed by galectin, PAR, CCL and EGF pathways.[151]

This spatial pattern is consistent with SASP-associated remodelling and immune communication at the tumor–host boundary. High-senescence-score epithelial populations comprised 24.7% of carcinoma cells, compared with 0.42% for high-senescence-score fibroblasts, whereas fibroblasts had the highest per-cell senescence score; aggregate inferred sender activity therefore reflects both population abundance and cell-state score.[151]

In mouse myocardial infarction, spatial spots with high senescence-signature scores were strongly associated with fibrosis and colocalized with the fibrotic marker *Col1a1* from day 7 after infarction, whereas this association was absent in control hearts.[75] The myocardial-infarction spots contained multiple cells, so these spatial data could not determine the number of individual senescent cells responsible for the fibrosis-associated signal.[75]

### What is the current status of the debate regarding whether

Judgment — **The current position is that SASP factors are causal effectors in particular pathways and tissues, but not established as the primary general cause of age-related tissue dysfunction.** This conclusion follows because factor-level perturbations establish causality for defined outcomes—IL-6-dependent invasion, IL-1β/TGF-β-dependent bystander senescence, activin-A-mediated inhibition of adipogenesis—while senescent-cell clearance, human biomarkers and spatial maps generally alter or observe whole cell populations and tissue environments simultaneously.[16][132][81][126][101][145]

The strongest general conclusion from senolysis experiments is that senescent cells can be bioactive drivers of pathology. It does not follow that SASP alone mediates the benefit, because clearance also removes non-secretory effects: loss of repair capacity, altered metabolism, ROS generation, abnormal matrix production and physical occupation of tissue niches. The 2011 transgenic clearance results discussed in Tumor suppression, age-related pathology, and timing show that removing p16^INK4a^-positive cells can improve age-related phenotypes, but those experiments did not selectively suppress the SASP while preserving senescent cells.[126][33][2]

Conversely, SASP suppression can be separated experimentally from arrest. Knockdown of NF-κB p65, MLL1, BRD4, HMGB2 and related chromatin regulators can reduce SASP expression without reversing arrest.[116][33][152] Mitochondrial depletion likewise drastically reduced IL-6, IL-8, GRO and MCP-1 secretion in irradiated fibroblasts while eventual arrest remained possible.[26] These separations make it possible in principle to test whether secretory activity, rather than arrest, produces a particular tissue phenotype.

The temporal answer is central. Short-lived SASP can reinforce arrest, recruit immune clearance, aid wound closure and limit fibrosis; persistent SASP can generate chronic inflammation, matrix remodelling, secondary senescence, stem-cell-niche failure and tumour-promoting conditions.[61][138][153][36] Senescence is therefore not resolved into a protective early state and harmful late state by a fixed clock. The transition depends on persistence, cell type, injury phase, immune clearance and the specific secretome being produced.[53][116]

The updated evidence also shows that duration alone does not assign biological value. In transplantation experiments, equal numbers of senescent muscle stem cells, FAPs or myeloid cells impaired regeneration through SASP activity even when that activity was transient. Conversely, injury-induced senescent cells can support acute repair: their elimination by ABT-263 or in p16-3MR mice reduced cellular plasticity during muscle regeneration, an effect linked to IL-6.[78][148]

### Synthesis —

**Integrated, criterion-led verdict —** The criterion is direct perturbation evidence for each step: initiation of stable arrest, maintenance of the established phenotype, and propagation to neighbouring cells or tissue function. On that criterion, the DNA-damage-response framework ranks first overall: d’Adda di Fagagna et al. (2003) showed that inactivating DNA-damage checkpoint kinases restored S-phase progression in telomere-initiated senescent cells, and Passos et al. (2010, 'Feedback between p21 and Reactive Oxygen Production Is Necessary for Cell Senescence') identified a p21–ROS feedback loop that was necessary and sufficient for maintenance of replication arrest during senescence establishment.[119][8][12]

The ordering would change if a cell-type-resolved, spatially targeted mitochondrial or chromatin intervention improved a defined tissue function while senescent-cell abundance, stable cell-cycle arrest and the initiating DNA-damage burden remained unchanged; that result would establish framework-specific propagation rather than regulation of senescence markers or secretory output alone.[27][3][73]

SASP is therefore a regulated, heterogeneous and context-specific propagating programme: selected factors cause defined effects, including IL-6-dependent invasion and impaired muscle regeneration, whereas acute SASP can support immune clearance and repair and persistent, cell-type-specific SASP can amplify dysfunction. The colorectal spatial data identify a cancer-progression and immune-neighbourhood association, not a measured age-related loss of tissue function; their authors state that functional studies are required for causality.[73][16][61][116][145]

### Open questions

It remains open which senescence subtypes, in which tissues and life stages, are net harmful rather than protective; whether particular SASP molecules are necessary and sufficient drivers of human tissue dysfunction; and whether senolytics can safely improve long-term healthspan or lifespan in humans. These questions require longitudinal, cell-type-resolved perturbation experiments that couple spatial location and secretory activity to functional tissue outcomes, together with adequately powered clinical trials measuring durable clinical endpoints.
