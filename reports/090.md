# UCC determination of off-color widget rejection and acceptance

## Executive summary

Buyer did not rightfully reject the widgets and likely accepted them by installing and operating all 1,000 for eight weeks, repainting 600, and waiting until May 10 to complain.[1][2][3] The navy delivery may still have been nonconforming if the charcoal-gray reference required that precise shade, because perfect tender does not treat functional performance as automatic conformity.[4][5] But the May 10 notice came 56 days after March 15 and about 46 days after the contract’s 10-day notice period, making initial rejection ineffective absent an unusually strong reason the visible color difference could not reasonably have been discovered earlier.[1] Vendor’s two-week replacement offer is a practical proposed cure, but it does not undo Buyer’s prior acceptance; Buyer may pursue only post-acceptance remedies on proof of timely breach notice, breach, and actual loss, and it is unlikely to revoke acceptance or compel a 40% refund while retaining the widgets in use.[6][7][8][9]

## Perfect tender and color conformity

Under the general Article 2 framework, Buyer likely accepted the widgets and did not rightfully reject them, although the specified charcoal-gray color and delivery in navy favor a finding of nonconformity if the color reference was part of the bargain.[1][10][11][9] Tender conformity and later acceptance point in different directions: the color specification governs whether the March 15 delivery conformed, while Buyer’s eight weeks of installation and operation, painting of 600 widgets, March performance-praise emails without a color objection, and May 10 notice govern whether Buyer retained a rejection right.[4][1][9][6] Under Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), the governing sales-law statute for movable goods, functional success does not automatically excuse departure from another contractual specification. The issue is therefore not whether the widgets worked, but whether charcoal gray was part of what Vendor promised to deliver.[12][1]

### What is the UCC perfect-tender rule

The **perfect-tender rule** is the UCC rule for a non-installment sale—one delivery rather than a sequence of separately accepted installments. UCC § 2-601 provides that, if goods or the tender of delivery fail “in any respect” to conform to the contract, the buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept any commercial unit and reject the rest. A **commercial unit** is a unit that commercial practice treats as a single whole for purposes of sale or use. The rule is subject to installment-contract rules and agreed contractual remedy limits, neither of which appears on the stated facts to displace the basic rule for this single delivery.[11][5][13]

UCC § 2-106(2) defines conforming goods or conduct as goods or conduct that are in accordance with the obligations under the contract.[10][14] That definition directs the analysis to the parties’ actual bargain, including specifications and descriptions, rather than to a generalized assessment of whether the goods are useful.

A description that becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty—that is, a seller’s enforceable promise that the goods will match that description. The load-capacity, durability, and color requirements can operate cumulatively: functional performance does not displace a separate color obligation. The specification-priority rule applies only when an exact or technical specification conflicts with a sample, model, or general description. UCC § 2-317(a).[14][1] Thus, a color reference number can be a contractual obligation alongside the purchase order’s load-capacity and durability requirements.[14] UCC §§ 2-106(2), 2-313(1)(b).[4][8][1]

Vendor delivered 1,000 custom widgets on March 15, 2024, that met every stated functional specification but were navy rather than charcoal gray. If the purchase order’s charcoal-gray reference was part of the bargain, that difference is a nonconformity at tender: the widgets would fail one contractual requirement even though they satisfy the performance requirements. The absence of a specially drafted clause authorizing rejection for shade variance does not make color irrelevant; § 2-601 already supplies the default rejection right for a proven contractual departure.[1][5][15][16]

The navy widgets likely were nonconforming because the purchase order specified charcoal gray, and their functional compliance does not eliminate that separate color obligation.[4][1][8] The reference number’s commercial meaning and a proven, applicable trade usage permitting the navy-to-charcoal variation could alter that conformity conclusion.[1][17]

Vendor’s best response is not that the widgets substantially performed, but that the purchase order, properly interpreted, permitted ordinary color variation in functional components. The UCC permits parties’ agreement to be informed by **usage of trade**—a regularly observed industry practice sufficiently established that parties in that trade may reasonably expect it to apply. The asserted widget-industry practice treats minor color variation as acceptable for functional components. A usage of trade is a practice regularly observed in a place, vocation, or trade sufficiently to justify expecting observance in the transaction, and its existence and scope must be proved as facts. If Vendor proves that practice, its scope, and its applicability to this transaction, it may help define what the color reference required; Vendor also must have given Buyer notice of the trade usage sufficient for the court to find that its admission will not cause unfair surprise.[14][1][17]

That defense has a limit. The UCC directs courts first to read express contract language and trade usage consistently if reasonable. But where the two cannot reasonably be reconciled, express terms prevail over usage of trade. Accordingly, Vendor cannot simply invoke an asserted general tolerance for variation to erase an express, exact charcoal-gray requirement; it must establish that the reference itself reasonably allowed a navy-to-charcoal departure as a permissible variation.[17]

The decisive interpretation is whether the color reference required charcoal gray or permitted the delivered navy shade as a commercially accepted minor variation. UCC § 1-303(c)–(e).[1][17] A specific reference number favors Buyer because it looks like a defined product specification; the performance-focused nature of the order, lack of a shade-variance rejection clause, and industry tolerance favor Vendor’s reading that modest visual variation was contemplated. The issue is unsettled until the reference number’s commercial meaning and the claimed trade practice’s regularity are established.[1][17]

**This initial inquiry makes a pre-acceptance rejection legally possible if Buyer proves a color nonconformity, but it does not make every asserted shade difference nonconforming.**[5][17]

The custom widgets are goods for Article 2 purposes because Article 2 includes specially manufactured items that are movable when identified to the contract for sale.[14]

Under UCC § 2-313(1)(b), a description of goods that is part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods will conform to that description. UCC § 2-313(2) does not require formal words such as warranty or guarantee or a specific intent to create a warranty, but a seller’s statement that is merely opinion or commendation does not create an express warranty.[18][19] Thus, the purchase order’s color reference can be an enforceable description even though it was not labeled as a warranty.[18][19]

A reported carpet decision illustrates that specified quality and color can both be part of the bargain in a sale of goods.[78]

UCC § 2-317 construes express and implied warranties as consistent and cumulative where reasonable, but the parties’ intention determines which warranty is dominant if that construction is unreasonable; an exact or technical specification displaces an inconsistent sample, model, or general description. Thus, if the charcoal-gray reference number is an exact or technical specification, the performance-focused general language in the purchase order does not displace that color requirement.[18][19]

State law controls most private sales disputes because each state has enacted its own version of UCC Article 2; the governing-law clause and the governing state’s current statute identify the controlling enactment.[20]

Article 2 applies to transactions in goods, but the governing state’s enactment controls because state legislatures enact their own UCC versions and wording and related remedies may vary. Because the delivery occurred in March 2024, the applicable text is the governing state’s UCC version effective in March 2024. The Illinois statute database warns that statutory changes may appear in its database before their effective dates and identifies Public Act 103-1036 as effective January 1, 2025.[21][14]

Perfect tender is also limited by Buyer’s obligation of good faith, Vendor’s cure rights, and historical court-imposed limitations where Buyer was not harmed by the nonconformity. A rejection that is not in good faith is wrongful.[9]

In *Ramirez*, the court treated the perfect-tender rule as qualified by the seller’s opportunity to cure and considered the parties’ changed positions, buyer inconvenience, the time needed for correction, and the seller’s resale prospects.[79]

### What role do substantial performance and the significance of the

**Substantial performance** is a common-law doctrine under which a party that has performed nearly all promised obligations may sometimes recover the contract price less damages. It is not the test for this single-delivery sale of goods; the initial question is conformity under the perfect-tender rule. UCC § 2-601.[22][5][1] Article 2 instead uses the perfect-tender rule: the question is whether the tender conformed to the contract, not whether Vendor came close enough overall.[3][22][5]

In *Jacob & Youngs*, a substantial-performance case involving comparable rather than specified pipe, the analysis considered the requirement’s purpose, the expected benefit, the reason for the deviation, and the hardship of literal correction.[80][81][82]

Buyer’s strongest conformity case is that the color reference was a bargained-for showroom requirement, reinforced by the architect’s confirmation that navy created contrast issues. Vendor’s strongest response is that the order emphasized performance, included no shade-variance rejection clause, and incorporated an industry tolerance for minor color variation in functional components. Those positions concern the meaning of the color term; substantial performance does not replace the § 2-601 conformity test. UCC §§ 2-601, 1-303(c)–(e).[1][5][17]

The UCC’s **substantial impairment** standard is different. It asks whether a nonconformity substantially impairs—meaning seriously diminishes—the value of the goods to this buyer. That is not the threshold for initial rejection under § 2-601. It applies to rejection of an installment under § 2-612 and, more importantly on these facts, to a later **revocation of acceptance**, which is the buyer’s attempt to undo an acceptance already made.[9][15][7][8]

Under UCC § 2-608, revocation after acceptance requires both substantial impairment and one of two further conditions: Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that the nonconformity would be cured but it was not seasonably cured, or Buyer accepted without discovering the nonconformity because discovery before acceptance was difficult or Vendor’s assurances reasonably induced acceptance. The buyer-specific inquiry considers the effect on this Buyer’s intended use rather than market value alone, and asks whether the nonconformity in fact substantially impaired Buyer’s value even if Vendor lacked advance knowledge of Buyer’s particular showroom circumstances.[23][24][3]

The architect’s later confirmation that navy created showroom-design contrast issues supports Buyer’s account that color mattered to its particular use. That evidence is relevant to substantial impairment because the inquiry includes the buyer’s own needs and circumstances, not merely the needs of an average purchaser. But the buyer must also show that the departure actually and objectively substantially impaired value. An aesthetic conflict in a showroom is relevant evidence; it is not itself a finding that all 1,000 otherwise functional widgets lost substantial value.[1][3][25][21]

Vendor has the stronger substantial-impairment position on the stated facts. The widgets met all functional requirements, the trade allegedly accepts minor variation for functional components, and Buyer itself painted 600 widgets black rather than treating them as unusable. Buyer can respond that the widgets were ordered for a showroom, where design coherence is part of the value received, and that the architect confirms a concrete contrast problem rather than a post hoc preference. That response strengthens the significance of color but does not convert the post-acceptance inquiry into the more forgiving perfect-tender standard.[1][26][25]

Substantial impairment is not a substitute for perfect tender. It is the higher hurdle if Buyer, after acceptance, seeks revocation of acceptance. UCC §§ 2-601, 2-608.[5][7][8]

Vendor’s trade-tolerance evidence also bears on the separate implied warranty of merchantability: merchantable goods must pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, be fit for ordinary purposes, and run within variations permitted by the agreement. Those merchantability criteria support Vendor’s position that the widgets remained commercially usable, but they do not displace an express warranty created by a color description that was part of the bargain.[14]

Revocation after acceptance also requires a statutory basis for the acceptance: Buyer must have accepted on a reasonable assumption that Vendor would seasonably cure the nonconformity and cure did not occur, or accepted without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances. Revocation must occur within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered the ground, before a substantial change in condition not caused by the goods’ defects, and only upon notice to Vendor.[18][19]

To cancel an installment contract for substantial impairment of the whole contract, the buyer must present objective evidence concerning its own needs.[9]

In Colonial Dodge, Inc. v. Miller (1982), the court treated the absence of a spare tire as substantially impairing value to a buyer who purchased a vehicle for long-distance towing because the absence created a significant safety risk. The case illustrates that substantial impairment can depend on the particular buyer’s intended use; Buyer’s showroom-design concern is therefore relevant to its buyer-specific argument.[24][1]

### The dispute arose from Vendor’s March 15

Vendor delivered 1,000 navy widgets on March 15, 2024. Buyer then installed and operated all widgets for eight weeks, painted 600 black, praised performance in March emails without mentioning color, and first gave color notice on May 10 despite the contract’s 10-day inspection-and-acceptance notice requirement.[1]

Those later acts do not retroactively make a navy-for-charcoal delivery conforming. Conformity is measured against contractual obligations at tender. They do, however, sharply affect whether Buyer retained the power to invoke the perfect-tender rule through a timely initial rejection. The next section, **Rejection, acceptance, and notice**, addresses that separate issue: whether installation, operating use, painting, praise emails, and a May 10 notice amounted to acceptance or ineffective rejection.[4][5][1]

The architect’s later assessment also belongs on both sides of the analysis, but at different stages. It supports Buyer’s interpretation that the color reference had a genuine showroom-design purpose and was not decorative surplusage. Yet it does not change the original contractual language, establish that trade usage tolerated this particular navy/charcoal difference, or negate the commercial significance of Buyer’s conduct before it objected.[1][17]

Vendor’s proposed replacement with a two-week lead time is a proposed **cure**, meaning a seller’s correction of an improper tender. For an effective rejection, UCC § 2-508 permits cure within the contract time when the seller seasonably notifies the buyer, and may permit further reasonable time when the seller had reasonable grounds to expect acceptance. The offer does not reverse an acceptance already made.[1][27][9]

Buyer may accept nonconforming widgets with or without an agreed allowance for the nonconformity, but it cannot unilaterally impose a 40% refund while retaining and operating the widgets. Absent an agreed allowance, an accepting buyer that gives the required breach notice must prove damages: ordinarily, breach-of-warranty damages are the difference, at the time and place of acceptance, between the value of the widgets as accepted and the value they would have had as warranted, subject to properly recoverable incidental, consequential, or other proximate damages. Buyer may deduct proven breach damages only from a contract price still due and only after notifying Vendor of its intention to do so; that remedy does not authorize an automatic refund of sums already paid. A valid revocation would require substantial impairment and the other elements of UCC § 2-608.[23][28][1][9][7][8]

**The March 15 tender may have been nonconforming if the color reference required exact charcoal gray, but the later timeline governs whether that initial defect remained available as a rejection claim.**[1][5]

Buyer’s eight weeks of conduct following this one delivery is not itself a statutory course of performance for interpreting the color term. A course of performance requires repeated occasions for performance by a party and knowing acceptance or acquiescence by the other party without objection; the stated transaction involved one delivery of 1,000 widgets. The installation, operation, painting, and emails nevertheless remain material to acceptance, rejection, revocation, and remedy issues addressed in the later sections.[14][1]

The May 10 color notice was 56 days after the March 15 delivery and 46 days after the agreed 10-day inspection-and-acceptance period expired. Under UCC § 2-601, a buyer may reject a seller’s imperfect tender, but rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery or tender; UCC § 2-602 also makes rejection ineffective unless the buyer seasonably notifies the seller.[23][19][1]

Maryland Commercial Law § 2-606 and UCC § 2-606 provide that, after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, acceptance occurs when the buyer signifies that the goods conform or will be retained despite nonconformity, fails to make an effective rejection, or acts inconsistently with the seller’s ownership; an act inconsistent with ownership that is wrongful against the seller is acceptance only if the seller ratifies it. Installation and operation of all 1,000 widgets and painting 600 widgets strongly support acceptance as ownership-type conduct.[19][29][1]

Buyer had a qualified UCC right to inspect the widgets to determine conformity before deciding whether to reject; the stated facts identify no waiver, C.O.D. term, or payment-against-documents arrangement that would qualify that right. A navy-versus-charcoal difference is an apparent, rather than latent, discrepancy if observable on delivery or ordinary inspection, so the eight-week delay does not fit the rule that can defer the reasonable-time period for a hidden defect until discovery or when it should have been discovered.[23][30]

The contract’s 10-day notice requirement supplies the parties’ agreed inspection-and-acceptance timetable. Buyer’s May 10, 2024 color notice was 56 days after the March 15 delivery and 46 days after that 10-day period expired. The May 10 communication therefore followed both an opportunity to inspect an apparent color difference and Buyer’s extended ownership-type use.[1][30][23][29]

The reasonable-time requirement applies separately to the remedy Buyer pursues: initial rejection must occur within a reasonable time after receipt and be promptly communicated, while a buyer that has accepted must notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after discovering or when it should have discovered the breach or lose its remedies. The May 10 notice must therefore be assessed as attempted rejection or as notice of an accepted-goods breach, as applicable.[31][32]

Painting 600 widgets supports treating Buyer’s conduct as inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership and may independently prevent revocation as to those painted widgets if the painting substantially changed their condition without being caused by the widgets’ own defect.[23][33]

### Synthesis

The perfect-tender rule gives Buyer a strong starting point, not an automatic final remedy. A single-delivery seller must tender goods conforming to all contractual obligations; therefore, satisfaction of load capacity and durability does not eliminate a separately promised charcoal-gray color. Buyer’s case turns on the specific color reference and the architect-supported showroom purpose. Vendor’s meaningful counterargument is that established trade usage permitted this navy-to-charcoal variation for functional components, but trade usage must be proved and cannot override an irreconcilable express specification.[4][1][17]

The key legal distinction is temporal. Before acceptance, a contractual color mismatch may trigger perfect-tender rights without proof of substantial impairment. After acceptance, Buyer must meet the more demanding substantial-impairment and revocation requirements; the architect’s contrast evidence is relevant but does not alone satisfy them. The subsequent inspection, use, painting, notice, cure offer, and refund demand therefore determine the remedy question, not whether functional performance automatically made the navy widgets conform.[5][7][3][25][8]

The color-reference number should be treated as a potentially exact or technical specification rather than as mere general styling language. If it was part of the bargain, the navy delivery did not accord with the contract obligation to supply charcoal-gray widgets; if the established trade usage consistently qualifies that reference to permit the delivered navy shade, the widgets conform.[14]

Under UCC § 2-607, including Maryland Commercial Law § 2-607 and Nebraska UCC § 2-607, acceptance requires Buyer to pay the contract rate for accepted goods, precludes rejection, and precludes revocation for a known color nonconformity unless Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that Vendor would seasonably cure it. Acceptance does not itself eliminate other remedies for nonconformity.[19][32][34]

For accepted goods, the separate UCC § 2-607(3)(a) notice rule requires Buyer to notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered it; otherwise Buyer is barred from any remedy. This accepted-goods breach-notice rule is distinct from the contract’s 10-day inspection-and-acceptance requirement for rejection.[32][1]

## Rejection, acceptance, and notice

Judgment — This issue is not whether Buyer eventually found a real showroom problem. It is whether Buyer preserved the *rejection* remedy—the pre-acceptance right to refuse nonconforming goods—by inspecting and notifying Vendor on time. The answer matters because, once Buyer accepted the widgets, a May 10 communication could no longer be an initial rejection; it could only serve as notice of breach or as an attempted *revocation of acceptance*, the narrower remedy for undoing an acceptance.

### What UCC rules govern inspection

Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code governs sales of goods, including a sale of custom widgets. The applicable state’s enactment controls because states enact their own Article 2 versions, but the core provisions discussed here are substantially uniform. The Uniform Law Commission identifies the 1951 Article 2 text as the most recent official version after the 2003 revision was never adopted by any state and was withdrawn in 2011.[12][21]

UCC § 2-513 creates the buyer’s *right of inspection*: before payment or acceptance, Buyer may examine tendered or delivered goods at a reasonable place, time, and manner. When Vendor sends goods to Buyer, the inspection may occur after arrival. Inspection is the buyer’s opportunity to decide whether the shipment meets the bargain; it is not itself acceptance or rejection.[35]

The parties may vary many UCC effects by agreement. They may also set a specific period for an action that the UCC otherwise requires within a reasonable time, provided the agreed period is not “manifestly unreasonable”—that is, plainly outside commercially sensible bounds. The contract’s 10-day inspection-and-acceptance provision therefore supplies the relevant period for a color objection if it gave Buyer a reasonable chance to discover the variance.[36][37][1]

UCC § 2-602 governs *rightful rejection*, meaning the buyer’s timely refusal to take goods that do not conform to the contract. Rejection requires affirmative action within a reasonable time after delivery or tender, and it is ineffective unless Buyer seasonably notifies Vendor. “Seasonably” ordinarily means within the agreed time; where no time is agreed, it means within a reasonable time under the transaction’s circumstances.[38][39][26]

The notice must do more than register dissatisfaction. A rejection notice must make clear that Buyer does not intend to keep the goods, so Vendor can retrieve them or exercise any applicable cure rights. A notice that merely identifies a problem may instead signal that Buyer plans to retain the goods and seek money damages.[40][41]

A buyer that rightfully rejects and has possession must hold the goods with reasonable care at the seller’s disposition long enough to permit removal. After rejection, the buyer’s exercise of ownership over a commercial unit is wrongful as against the seller. Those duties fit a rejection remedy because rejection returns control of the goods to the seller rather than allowing the buyer both to keep and use them.[8][9][13]

UCC § 2-606 defines *acceptance*, a term of art meaning that Buyer has taken the particular contract goods as its own. Acceptance occurs in three principal ways:

- **Express retention after inspection:** After a reasonable opportunity to inspect, Buyer tells Vendor that the goods conform or that Buyer will keep them despite a nonconformity. [2][39]
- **Ineffective rejection:** Buyer fails to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect. [2][3][39]
- **Ownership-inconsistent conduct:** Buyer does an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership. If the act is wrongful as against Vendor, it counts as acceptance only if Vendor ratifies it. [2][39]

Temporary possession alone does not establish acceptance because Buyer must first have a reasonable opportunity to inspect. Nor does payment or possession alone conclusively prove acceptance. But the rule changes when Buyer goes beyond custody and uses, installs, alters, incorporates, or otherwise treats the goods as its own after having an opportunity to examine them.[42][3][22]

The scope of acceptance can also extend beyond the individual item handled. Acceptance of part of a *commercial unit*—a unit that commercial practice treats as a single whole—constitutes acceptance of the entire unit. Whether each widget, the painted group, or the 1,000-widget delivery is the relevant commercial unit depends on the contract and commercial context.[2][43][13]

Acceptance and rejection are mutually exclusive positions as to the accepted goods. Under § 2-607, acceptance precludes rejection. A buyer that has accepted may still have Article 2 remedies for nonconformity, but must give notice of breach within a reasonable time after discovering, or when it should have discovered, the breach; the buyer bears the burden of proving the breach as to accepted goods.[6]

**The governing distinction is therefore straightforward: inspection gives Buyer time to decide; rejection requires timely, affirmative notice that Buyer will not keep the widgets; and silence, ownership-like conduct, or an ineffective late rejection can become acceptance once Buyer had a reasonable opportunity to inspect.**[35][38][2][3][39][40]

The UCC defines the parties’ agreement as their bargain in fact, drawn from their language and relevant circumstances, including course of performance, course of dealing, and usage of trade. Trade usage may give particular meaning to, supplement, or qualify a term, but an express term prevails if the two cannot reasonably be construed consistently. Thus, industry acceptance of minor color variation can inform the meaning of the color reference, but cannot displace an express charcoal-gray requirement.[14]

The UCC also defines notice functionally: a party gives notice by taking steps reasonably required to inform the other party in the ordinary course. Every UCC contract and duty must be performed and enforced in good faith.[14]

A rejection notice must identify an ascertainable defect when the omission would deprive Vendor of a seasonable cure. If Buyer fails, in connection with rejection, to state a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection, Buyer may be precluded from using that unstated defect to justify rejection or establish breach where Vendor could have cured it if Buyer had stated it seasonably.[14]

The agreed 10-day term also fits the UCC definition of seasonable action: an action is seasonable when taken within the agreed time, and parties may fix a time for an action otherwise required within a reasonable time if the time is not manifestly unreasonable.[19]

UCC § 2-605 adds a related particularization rule. Buyer did identify color in its May 10 communication; the controlling problem is that the identification came after the contractual inspection period and extensive ownership-like conduct.[19][1]

For a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection, a rejection notice must identify the particular defect. Buyer cannot later use an unstated defect to support rejection or breach if Vendor could have cured it had it received notice. The navy-versus-charcoal issue was therefore a defect Buyer needed to identify in a timely rejection notice, rather than reserve while it used the widgets.[44][1]

Post-acceptance breach notice under § 2-607(3)(a) is a materially different mechanism from rejection. A scholarly review found that 67 of 72 cases considered under that provision—93 percent—involved breach-of-warranty claims against the seller, consistent with its principal role in preserving a claim over goods the buyer has accepted.[45][46]

The UCC defines notice separately from actual receipt: a party gives notification by taking steps reasonably required to inform the other party in the ordinary course, and an organization has notice for a transaction when the information reaches the person conducting it or would have reached that person through due diligence. Thus, the architect’s knowledge becomes Buyer’s transaction-level knowledge when it reached, or ordinarily should have reached, the person handling this purchase.[47][19]

The *perfect-tender rule* permits a buyer to reject goods that fail in any respect to conform to the contract. Subject to the Article 2 installment-contract rules and any agreed contractual limitation of remedy, § 2-601 gives the buyer three choices upon an improper delivery: reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept any commercial unit or units and reject the rest.[33][11]

UCC § 1-204 provides that an agreed time for an action is effective unless it is manifestly unreasonable; what is reasonable otherwise depends on the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances; and an action is seasonable when taken within the agreed time or, if none is agreed, within a reasonable time.[26]

Maryland UCC § 1-205 likewise provides that reasonableness depends on the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances, and that action is seasonable if taken within the agreed time or, absent an agreement, within a reasonable time. Maryland UCC § 1-302 permits the parties to vary the UCC’s effect by agreement, while preserving duties of good faith, diligence, reasonableness, and care, and permits an agreed non-manifestly-unreasonable time for an action otherwise required within a reasonable time.[19]

The § 2-606 acceptance routes are distinct: after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, Buyer accepts by signifying conformity or retention despite nonconformity, by failing to make an effective rejection, or by an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership; an ownership-inconsistent act that is wrongful against Vendor is acceptance only if Vendor ratifies it.[13]

Trade customs and the parties’ past practices are particularly relevant to whether the time, place, and manner of § 2-513 inspection are reasonable. The stated industry practice treats minor color variations as acceptable for functional components, but the record does not classify this navy-versus-charcoal difference as minor.[42][1]

UCC § 2-601 is the *perfect-tender rule*: subject to the Article 2 installment-contract rules and any agreed limitation of remedy, if goods or tender fail “in any respect” to conform to the contract, Buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept and reject by commercial units. Thus, a color deviation need not substantially impair value before acceptance to support an initial rejection; it must be a failure to conform to the parties’ agreement.[11][48][49]

A rejection may be effective as a refusal to accept yet wrongful because the goods conformed; an effective but wrongful rejection does not itself constitute acceptance.[39]

If a nonconforming tender gives Buyer a right of rejection, risk of loss remains on Vendor until cure or acceptance.[18]

UCC § 2-508 governs the seller’s cure of a rejected nonconforming tender. Before the performance time expires, Vendor may seasonably notify Buyer of an intent to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time. After that time, Vendor may obtain a further reasonable time to substitute conforming goods only if it had reasonable grounds to believe its tender would be acceptable with or without a money allowance and seasonably notifies Buyer.[19][22]

### Did Buyer accept the widgets through inspection

Buyer had far more than temporary possession of the widgets. Buyer installed and operated all 1,000 widgets for eight weeks before notifying Vendor of the color issue on May 10, and painted 600 widgets black during that period. Buyer’s March emails praised the widgets’ performance and made no color objection.[1]

Installation and operation corroborate acceptance through failure to reject after a reasonable inspection opportunity. They may also be acts inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership, as installation of seller-supplied heavy equipment has been treated as ownership-inconsistent conduct rather than temporary possession; but if either act was wrongful as against Vendor, it constitutes acceptance under § 2-606(1)(c) only if Vendor ratified it.[2][3][50][42][39]

Painting 600 widgets is the strongest acceptance indicator in the stated facts. Buyer changed 60% of the delivered 1,000-widget lot to suit its own showroom. Painting 600 widgets is conduct potentially inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership; if the painting was wrongful as against Vendor, however, it counts as acceptance through § 2-606(1)(c) only if Vendor ratified it.[1][2][39][51]

**Buyer accepted all 1,000 widgets by failing to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect the visible navy-versus-charcoal difference and after the agreed 10-day notice period. Installation and eight weeks of operation of all widgets, plus painting 600 widgets, strongly corroborate that acceptance.**[1][2][3]

Buyer’s March emails reinforce that conclusion but are not independently decisive. Praise for performance does not expressly say that navy complied with every contractual term, and the emails did not mention the color. Yet the emails accompanied extensive use and no timely color objection, so they support the inference that Buyer treated the goods as retained rather than promptly rejected.[1][2][3]

Buyer has a meaningful counterargument: acceptance does not arise simply because a buyer receives goods, and continued use is context-dependent. Courts have recognized that continued use need not automatically defeat revocation where a buyer gives prompt notice, seeks a replacement or refund, and lacks a practical alternative. In *Franklin v. Augusta Dodge, Inc.*, the buyer’s continued use of a truck did not warrant taking revocation from the jury where the buyer could not afford another vehicle and had no other transportation. By contrast, *Griffith v. Stovall Tire & Marine, Inc.* treated the buyer as having reaccepted a vehicle after purported revocation where the buyer refused seller access, pursued repairs through others, retained and used the vehicle, and drove it more than 120,000 miles.[42][3][50]

That counterargument has limited force here because Buyer did not merely continue unavoidable use after promptly reporting a hidden operational defect. Buyer used all widgets for eight weeks before raising color, altered 600 widgets for its own aesthetic plan, and sent favorable performance communications during March. Those facts fit ownership-like retention more closely than necessity-driven continued use while a prompt complaint is being resolved.[1][50]

The legal treatment of post-notice use is not completely uniform. One authority reports that most courts treat continued use after attempted revocation as destroying revocation, while another view treats the use as wrongful and compensable without necessarily invalidating an otherwise valid revocation. Courts also recognize practical-necessity exceptions.[3][50]

That disagreement does not alter the more basic point here. Buyer’s decisive use and alteration occurred before its May 10 notice, during the period in which Buyer needed to inspect and make a rejection decision. Continued use after a prompt revocation can raise difficult remedial questions; prolonged pre-notice installation, operation, and repainting strongly support that acceptance had already occurred.[1][22][13]

Acceptance of some widgets does not automatically prove acceptance of every widget unless the relevant goods form a commercial unit. But Buyer installed and operated all 1,000 widgets, not merely the 600 that it painted. The acceptance conclusion consequently rests on Buyer’s treatment of the whole delivery, while the painting independently makes the case especially strong as to the altered 600.[1][2][43]

Judgment — Buyer should not characterize May 10 as a rejection. Its better procedural characterization, if it pursues any claim, is notice of breach after acceptance or attempted revocation of acceptance. That framing does not decide whether Buyer can ultimately establish a color-related breach; it recognizes the legal effect of Buyer’s own handling of the widgets before notice.

This analysis rules out the clean pre-acceptance rejection remedy for the widgets Buyer installed, operated, and materially altered, while leaving the narrower post-acceptance paths for the next section, *Cure and remedies*.[6][13][52]

Buyer’s alteration left only 400 of the 1,000 widgets unpainted. Put differently, Buyer painted 1.5 widgets for every widget left in its delivered navy condition, which reinforces that the alteration was a predominant, rather than isolated, part of Buyer’s use of the shipment.[1]

Section 2-606 also makes the route to acceptance important. Buyer’s failure to make an effective rejection after a reasonable inspection opportunity independently constitutes acceptance; an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership is another route, although an act wrongful as against Vendor counts as acceptance only if Vendor ratifies it. Buyer’s missed timely rejection supplies the independent acceptance ground here, while installation, operation, and repainting evidence its decision to retain the widgets.[14][1]

The eight weeks of operation also exceeded an ordinary inspection use. Running equipment through a standard test cycle can be inspection, but putting equipment into daily production for two months is not; Buyer’s eight-week installation and operation is materially closer to the latter use than to a limited evaluation.[53][1]

The line between inspection use and acceptance is whether Buyer used the widgets only to evaluate conformity or instead extracted their commercial value. A reasonable amount of testing need not accept goods, but installation in regular operations for eight weeks and repainting goods for the showroom go beyond evaluation; incorporation, sales-floor use, and material alteration are recognized ownership-like examples.[29][1]

New York UCC § 2-606(1)(b) specifically provides that failure to make an effective rejection is acceptance only after the buyer has had a reasonable opportunity to inspect. That route, rather than mere possession, supports acceptance here.[43][3][42][1]

The reported cases have treated continued use following an attempted rejection as making the rejection ineffective. Continued use after a complaint can also signify an intention to retain the goods despite the asserted nonconformity.[3]

Continued use can communicate that a buyer intends to retain goods despite nonconformity, and conduct inconsistent with the seller’s ownership supports acceptance under § 2-606 after a reasonable opportunity to inspect.[3][51]

The inspection-and-acceptance inquiry remains fact-specific. In *Bicknell v. B & S Enterprises*, the buyer had no later right to reject a vehicle after ample pre-possession opportunity to inspect the asserted features and conduct taking the vehicle; by contrast, *La Villa Fair v. Lewis Carpet Mills* held that a nine-month delay in inspecting carpet did not constitute acceptance under that case’s facts and circumstances.[50][39]

### Was Buyer’s May 10 notice a timely rejection under the

The contract required notice within 10 days under its inspection-and-acceptance clause. Delivery occurred on March 15, 2024; if the 10 days are calendar days counted from delivery, notice was due approximately March 25. Buyer first gave color notice on May 10, 56 days after delivery and after eight weeks of installation and operation.[1]

Derived — May 10 followed March 15 by 56 days. Buyer’s notice was therefore approximately 46 days after the contractual 10-day period expired. This calculation uses the stated March 15 delivery date, the 10-day contractual period, and the stated May 10 notice date.[1]

The UCC generally enforces agreed timing provisions when they are not manifestly unreasonable. Contractual limits on inspection or rejection are effective if they give the buyer a fair opportunity to discover the defect. The contract’s 10-day clause thus sets the seasonable time for notice unless Buyer can show that the period was manifestly unreasonable as applied to the asserted issue.[36][37][3][54]

Ten days was a commercially reasonable opportunity to discover the visual difference between navy and the specified charcoal gray under the facts given. Buyer received the widgets, installed them, sent March communications praising performance, and did not identify color until May 10. The color issue is a visible appearance issue rather than one that required extended load testing, destructive testing, or prolonged operation to detect.[1][22][55]

The UCC’s inspection standard remains contextual. UCC § 2-513 permits inspection at a reasonable time, place, and manner, and trade customs and the parties’ past practices are particularly relevant to reasonableness. A longer period may be reasonable for goods requiring field testing under varying conditions, and seller assurances to cure can extend the time in which a buyer may reasonably reject.[42][3][54]

Those contextual principles do not support Buyer’s 56-day delay here. Buyer’s claimed problem was not that the widgets’ color changed after installation or that a latent functional failure appeared only after use. Buyer’s later complaint was that the delivered navy shade created aesthetic contrast issues in its showroom, a consequence of a color difference that was capable of visual identification on delivery and installation.[1][22]

Buyer may argue that its showroom-design consequences became clear only after the architect later assessed the design. That argument can matter to the seriousness of the aesthetic effect, but it does not convert the visible navy-versus-charcoal variance into a hidden characteristic. A buyer need not understand every commercial consequence of a known nonconformity before acceptance can occur; awareness of some defect or nonconformity can suffice.[1][3][25]

The agreed clause is also important because prompt notice protects Vendor’s practical interests. Prompt reporting enables inspection, mitigation, and cure while goods remain near their tendered condition. Here, Buyer’s delay coincided with installation and repainting, actions that made a replacement or recovery strategy more complicated than it would have been during the first 10 days.[40][1][56]

The May 10 communication also had to clearly reject, rather than merely complain about, the widgets. Buyer cited aesthetic concerns and demanded a 40% refund while keeping the widgets in use. That position points away from a traditional rejection because a valid rejection tells Vendor that Buyer does not intend to retain the goods.[40][41][1]

**Buyer’s May 10 notice was not a timely rejection: the visible color variance and the intervening eight weeks of use made rejection untimely under the stated 10-day clause and difficult under § 2-602’s reasonable-time rule.**[1][38][36][37]

This conclusion also follows under the UCC’s default reasonable-time standard. Rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery or tender and requires seasonable notice. Eight weeks may be reasonable for some hidden or use-dependent defects, but it is not reasonable here for the visible shade difference that Buyer had the opportunity to observe while installing and operating the widgets.[38][54][3][1]

Buyer’s strongest position is that the widgets’ showroom function includes visual integration and that inspection timing is circumstance-sensitive. Vendor’s timeliness position is stronger because Buyer had an express 10-day period, the color mismatch was observable, Buyer affirmatively used the widgets, and Buyer did not mention color in its March performance emails.[55][1][3]

The consequence is acceptance through failure to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect. Buyer cannot preserve a rejection theory by recasting its delayed May 10 demand for a partial refund as a refusal to accept goods that it had already installed, operated, and painted.[43][3][9][6][13]

If May 10 is treated as notice of breach after acceptance, it is evaluated differently. Section 2-607(3)(a) measures post-acceptance notice from the time Buyer discovered or should have discovered the claimed breach, not simply from delivery or the contractual rejection deadline. That rule may preserve a damages claim if Buyer satisfies it, but it does not revive the missed right to reject.[6][8][57]

This subsection therefore decides the initial-rejection issue: the 10-day clause and Buyer’s conduct make May 10 ineffective as a rejection, although the distinct question whether it was timely notice of breach after acceptance remains relevant to later remedies.[36][37][38][6]

The UCC permits the parties to fix by agreement a time for an action otherwise required within a reasonable time, unless the agreed time is manifestly unreasonable. An action is seasonable when taken within the agreed time, or, absent an agreed time, within a reasonable time measured by the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances. The 10-day notice requirement therefore governs Buyer’s rejection timing.[14][1]

Buyer identified the color issue on May 10, so this is not a case in which Buyer omitted the asserted defect from its rejection notice. But color was ascertainable by reasonable inspection, and Vendor’s two-week replacement proposal confirms that prompt identification would have allowed Vendor to address the stated variance.[14][1]

The distinction between rejection notice and post-acceptance breach notice is material. For accepted goods, UCC § 2-607(3)(a) requires notice within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered breach; notice need only tell Vendor that the transaction is “troublesome” and claimed to involve a breach, rather than list every future objection, demand damages, or threaten litigation. Buyer’s May 10 identification of the navy color and showroom concern therefore identifies the asserted breach, even though it cannot make the communication a timely rejection.[18][45][1]

Prompt notice protects Vendor by giving it an opportunity to correct a defect, prepare for negotiation or litigation, and investigate before a claim becomes stale. Buyer’s installation, operation, and repainting during the 56-day delay directly frustrated those purposes by changing the shipment before Vendor received the color complaint.[32][1]

The purpose of timely rejection confirms this result. Prompt, specific notice gives Vendor the opportunity to address the stated defect, minimize losses, and recover goods before their value or condition is diminished. Buyer’s failure to identify the readily observable color issue until after installation, operation, and repainting deprived Vendor of that prompt opportunity.[31][1]

For accepted goods, Florida Statutes § 672.607(3)(a) requires the buyer to notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time after discovering or having reason to discover it, or the buyer is barred from any remedy. Nebraska’s § 2-607 annotation identifies three functions of that notice: allowing the seller to correct a defect, prepare for negotiation and litigation, and protect against stale claims too late to investigate.[18][32]

The perfect-tender rule permits rejection for any failure to conform, but the rejection deadline must still afford Buyer a reasonable opportunity to discover the defect; reasonable industry practice is relevant to that timing assessment. One reported example upheld inspection and rejection within one month after receipt when it complied with reasonable industry practices.[11][42]

Timeliness is generally a factual question measured by all the circumstances. *J.N.K. Machine Corp. v. TBW Ltd.* illustrates the consequence of a much longer delay: buyers first attempted rejection about five months after possession, and the court held as a matter of law that the rejection was not within a reasonable time and that the buyers had accepted by failing to reject effectively.[32][52]

### Does the architect’s later confirmation of showroom contrast issues change

The architect later confirmed that navy created contrast issues in Buyer’s showroom design. That confirmation supports Buyer’s assertion that color mattered to Buyer’s commercial and aesthetic use of the widgets. It is evidence directed to the buyer-specific impact of the variance, not merely a generalized dislike of the shade.[1][3][25]

Under § 2-608, the relevant post-acceptance standard is *substantial impairment*: the nonconformity must substantially impair the goods’ value to this buyer. The test is stricter than the perfect-tender standard for initial rejection. Courts and commentary describe the inquiry as both subjective and objective: Buyer’s particular needs and circumstances matter, but Buyer must also show that the nonconformity actually impaired the goods’ value in an objectively supportable way.[7][26][25]

The architect’s confirmation therefore improves Buyer’s evidence on one part of a possible revocation argument. The confirmation may help show that the widgets’ navy appearance diminished their utility or value in a showroom designed around charcoal gray. A seller’s lack of advance knowledge of Buyer’s particular circumstances does not necessarily defeat substantial impairment if the variance in fact substantially impairs value to Buyer.[3][25][1]

**The architect’s opinion strengthens Buyer’s proof that the color mismatch had showroom consequences, but it does not undo the acceptance and late-rejection consequences of Buyer’s earlier installation, operation, repainting, and delay.**[1][6][13]

The timing point is decisive. For post-acceptance breach notice under § 2-607(3)(a), the relevant inquiry is when Buyer discovered or should have discovered the asserted color nonconformity, not when the architect later assessed its showroom consequences. Navy rather than charcoal was visible on delivery and installation, so the later assessment does not restart that inquiry.[8][6][1]

The distinction is between discovering a nonconformity and later appreciating its full consequences. Section 2-608 permits revocation where the buyer accepted without discovering the nonconformity and acceptance was reasonably induced by difficulty of discovery or seller assurances. A later professional assessment can matter where a defect is genuinely difficult to detect, but the statute does not allow revocation merely because the buyer later learns more about a readily observed discrepancy.[7][3][58]

Buyer’s acceptance was also accompanied by a substantial change in the widgets’ condition. Section 2-608 requires revocation before any substantial change in condition not caused by the goods’ own defects. Painting 600 widgets black was not caused by the navy color’s defect; it was Buyer’s chosen effort to make them fit the showroom. That alteration creates a substantial obstacle to revocation because it changed a majority of the widgets from their delivered condition before notice.[7][3][1][25]

Buyer could argue that repainting was mitigation: an effort to reduce the showroom impact while keeping operations going. Continued use can sometimes be excused where it is practically necessary, and questions of revocation, reasonable notice, and substantial impairment may sometimes go to the factfinder. The practical-necessity cases, however, involved circumstances such as a buyer without alternative transportation after prompt complaint, not elective alteration of 600 goods before any complaint.[50][1]

The architect’s evidence also cannot transform Buyer’s March praise into an express representation that color conformed. The emails praised functional performance, and functional success does not by itself establish that all specifications were met. Yet the emails remain relevant to acceptance because they occurred during Buyer’s prolonged use without a color objection and therefore support Vendor’s inference that Buyer intended to retain the shipment.[1][2][39]

Once acceptance occurred, Buyer needed to pursue one of the post-acceptance paths. First, Buyer could give timely notice of breach under § 2-607(3)(a), with the burden of proving the alleged color-related breach. Second, Buyer could attempt revocation, but only by proving substantial impairment plus one of the statutory circumstances: acceptance on a reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur, or acceptance without discovery because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances.[13][6]

The architect’s report bears chiefly on substantial impairment. It does not itself show that Buyer accepted expecting a cure or that Vendor gave assurances that delayed discovery. Nor does it change the statutory requirement that revocation occur within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered its ground and before a non-defect-caused substantial change in condition.[7][8]

Judgment — Buyer should use the architect’s analysis to quantify any actual showroom-related loss in a damages claim, not to describe the May 10 notice as a timely rejection. The stronger legal narrative is that a specified color mattered and caused measurable aesthetic harm, while recognizing that Buyer’s handling of the goods foreclosed the simpler remedy of refusal.

The architect’s evidence may preserve an argument for post-acceptance damages if Buyer gave breach notice within a reasonable time after actual or constructive discovery. It does not make Buyer’s requested 40% refund while retaining and using altered widgets equivalent to a valid rejection or valid revocation; those remedy questions are addressed in *Cure and remedies*.[13][59][25]

Acceptance with knowledge of a nonconformity cannot be revoked because of that nonconformity unless Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that it would be seasonably cured. Buyer’s ability to see navy during delivery and installation, combined with the absence of any stated pre-May-10 cure assurance, weighs against treating the architect’s later assessment as a basis to revoke acceptance of the visible color variance.[14][1]

The statutory consequence of acceptance is precise: acceptance precludes rejection of the accepted goods, but does not itself eliminate every Article 2 remedy for nonconformity. To preserve a post-acceptance remedy, Buyer had to notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after it discovered or should have discovered the asserted breach.[14]

If Buyer knew of the navy color when its conduct constituted acceptance, UCC § 2-607(2) independently bars revocation based on that known nonconformity unless Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that Vendor would seasonably cure it. The facts identify no pre-acceptance assurance or cure arrangement; Vendor made its two-week replacement offer only after Buyer’s May 10 complaint.[18][1]

The architect’s confirmation can establish the magnitude of Buyer-specific showroom harm, but it does not supply the separate statutory basis for revocation. Revocation requires substantial impairment and either an unseasonably unfulfilled reasonable assumption of cure or acceptance without discovery reasonably induced by difficulty of discovery or seller assurances; it also requires notice before a substantial non-defect-caused change in condition.[18][1]

That route applies when Buyer accepted with a reasonable assumption that Vendor would cure a known nonconformity and the cure was not seasonably made; the stated facts instead show that Buyer accepted and used the widgets without raising the color issue until May 10.[60][46][1]

Illinois provides a further state-specific illustration. In *Accettura v. Vacationland*, decided September 19, 2019, the Illinois Supreme Court held that a buyer proceeding under Illinois Commercial Code § 2-608(1)(b)—acceptance without discovering a substantially impairing nonconformity because discovery was difficult or seller assurances induced acceptance—need not first give the seller an opportunity to cure before revoking. That rule does not assist Buyer on these facts because the visible color difference was available for inspection before Buyer’s acceptance.[60][1]

If Buyer pursues an accepted-goods breach claim rather than revocation, acceptance does not create an automatic 40% refund. Buyer must generally pay the contract price for accepted goods; after required breach notice, its ordinary damages measure is the difference between the value of the widgets accepted and the value they would have had if delivered as warranted, plus available incidental and consequential damages. The architect’s evidence is relevant to proving that actual value loss, not to fixing damages at 40 percent merely because Buyer demanded that amount.[44][20][1]

Florida Statutes § 672.607(2) provides that acceptance precludes rejection of accepted goods. If acceptance was with knowledge of a nonconformity, revocation based on it is unavailable unless Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that it would be seasonably cured; acceptance itself does not impair other statutory remedies for nonconformity.[18]

The two § 2-608 revocation circumstances are different. Under subsection (1)(a), the buyer knows of the nonconformity but accepts expecting the seller to cure it; under subsection (1)(b), the buyer accepts a presumably conforming unit without knowledge of a substantially impairing nonconformity and discovers the defect after acceptance.[60]

May 10 also does not clearly communicate revocation of acceptance. Effective revocation requires notice, and the notice must state that Buyer is revoking acceptance and identify the nonconformity; a statement of dissatisfaction alone is insufficient. Buyer identified the color concern and demanded money while retaining the widgets, rather than stating that it revoked acceptance.[24][1]

### Synthesis

Buyer had a contractual 10-day period to inspect and notify Vendor but did not raise the color issue within that period. Buyer instead installed and operated all 1,000 widgets and painted 600 black, conduct that strongly corroborates acceptance after its failure to make an effective rejection. The architect’s later confirmation supports Buyer’s claim that navy harmed this showroom design, but it goes to the seriousness of a possible post-acceptance claim rather than to whether Buyer timely rejected. The stronger conclusion is that Buyer accepted the widgets and did not rightfully reject them; any remaining claim must proceed under the stricter post-acceptance notice, revocation, or damages rules rather than as a rejection.[1][2][3][6][13]

## Cure and remedies

### Does Vendor’s offer of immediate replacement with a two-week lead

Judgment — Cure is the UCC term for a seller’s opportunity, in defined circumstances, to correct a nonconforming tender rather than have the transaction end at the buyer’s first objection. It matters here because a replacement with widgets in the specified charcoal gray would correct the asserted defect completely, but cure operates differently before and after acceptance. The procedural question—timely rejection versus acceptance followed by a claimed revocation—therefore controls more than the practical attractiveness of Vendor’s two-week proposal.

UCC § 2-508 supplies the principal cure rule for a **rejection**, meaning a buyer’s refusal to take nonconforming goods before acceptance. The Illinois enactment expressly makes both cure routes available only where the buyer has rejected a nonconforming tender.[14] Section 2-508(1) permits a seller whose tender was rejected as nonconforming to seasonably notify the buyer of an intent to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time, if that performance period has not expired.[61][62] Section 2-508(2) supplies a second route: when the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the tender would be acceptable, with or without a price allowance, seasonable notice can entitle the seller to a further reasonable time to substitute conforming goods.[27]

The two routes have separate functions. The first is straightforward: if March 15 was not the final contractual deadline and Buyer had promptly rejected the navy widgets, Vendor could notify Buyer and supply charcoal-gray replacements before the deadline. The second applies where the original performance date has passed but Vendor reasonably expected acceptance. It does not make every late replacement permissible; it requires reasonable grounds, seasonable notice, and a further period that is reasonable for the transaction.[61][62]

If Buyer had timely rejected after the contract performance time expired, Vendor could invoke § 2-508(2) only by proving reasonable grounds to believe the navy tender would be acceptable, seasonably notifying Buyer, and substituting conforming goods within a further reasonable time.[27][61] The identified industry practice regards minor color variation as acceptable for functional components, while the purchase order stressed load capacity and durability and did not state that exact shade was a condition of acceptance.

Those circumstances support an inference that Vendor had reasonable grounds to think a navy tender would be accepted, perhaps with a price adjustment, even though the specified color reference remains contract language that Buyer may enforce. Trade usage, course of dealing, course of performance, and contracting circumstances are recognized bases for the reasonable-belief inquiry.[9][61][55]

Judgment — Vendor promptly proposed delivery of replacement widgets after a two-week lead time; the proposal is not an immediate replacement and does not itself establish Vendor’s entitlement to two weeks for cure.[1][27][61] The asserted mismatch is color, not operational failure; Vendor proposed replacement rather than repair; and the widgets remained functionally usable.

Two weeks is not automatically reasonable under § 2-508(2), because reasonableness depends on the product, the parties’ delivery schedule, and whether timing was essential, but it is a proportionate response to a readily curable aesthetic nonconformity rather than a demand that Buyer tolerate the wrong shade indefinitely.

Commentary supports requiring reasonable minor repairs or adjustments during cure, while a fully conforming replacement provides greater protection to the buyer.[83]

In assessing whether a seller’s proposed cure is reasonable, relevant considerations include Buyer’s inconvenience and changed position, the time needed to correct the defect, and Vendor’s ability to salvage the original goods.[79]

Vendor promptly offered to deliver replacement widgets after Buyer raised the color issue, with a two-week lead time.[1] Section 2-508 requires seasonable notice for either cure route.[14] That immediate response is important, but it is not the same as having completed cure.

Under § 2-508, notice announces the seller’s intent; cure is accomplished by a conforming tender within the applicable contract time or reasonable further period. The offer here therefore gives Vendor a strong basis to seek an opportunity to replace, but Buyer’s refusal means no replacement occurred.[1][27]

Buyer has a meaningful counterargument. Perfect tender allows a buyer to object to a contractual deviation before acceptance, and color can be an express specification rather than a dispensable preference. A seller cannot invoke § 2-508(2) merely because it did not know the goods departed from the contract; the reasonable-grounds requirement is meant to prevent cure from becoming an all-purpose excuse for careless performance. Buyer can say the color reference number, custom nature of the order, and showroom purpose placed Vendor on notice that exact appearance mattered.[11][61]

The architect’s later confirmation that navy created showroom contrast problems strengthens Buyer’s account that the color was commercially significant. It does not, however, transform the replacement proposal into an unreasonable cure. A conforming charcoal-gray replacement directly addresses the contrast problem, and a two-week lead time is not shown to frustrate a time-sensitive display event or other deadline. The contrast evidence thus supports the existence and practical importance of a color breach, while Vendor’s offer supports the conclusion that the breach was remediable without a permanent 40% price reduction.[1][26]

The contract’s 10-day inspection-and-acceptance clause reinforces that conclusion. The scenario characterizes Buyer’s eight-week delay before the May 10 notice as outside both the contractual 10-day requirement and a reasonable time for rejection.[1] Timely rejection is designed to tell the seller that the buyer will not keep the goods, allowing the seller to cure or retrieve them.

A notification that merely identifies a problem can preserve a damages claim, but it does not necessarily communicate rejection; a rejection notice must make clear that the buyer does not intend to keep the goods. Prompt notice also allows cure sooner and helps preserve the goods’ resale value and condition.[40]

Judgment — Had Buyer rejected within the contractual 10 days and identified the navy color, Vendor would have had the stronger claim to a short replacement opportunity. The industry practice supporting tolerance of minor functional-component shade variations would be evidence of reasonable grounds under § 2-508(2), and the immediate two-week replacement proposal would likely qualify as a reasonable further time. That conclusion does not negate Buyer’s original perfect-tender objection; it means a rightful objection would not necessarily entitle Buyer to insist on a cash refund instead of a prompt conforming replacement.

Acceptance changes the cure analysis fundamentally. As addressed in **Rejection, acceptance, and notice**, Buyer’s installation, extended operation, alteration of 600 widgets by painting, and performance-praising March communications strongly indicate acceptance after an opportunity to inspect. UCC § 2-607 provides that acceptance precludes rejection of accepted goods. If acceptance occurred with knowledge of a nonconformity, Buyer cannot revoke acceptance on that basis unless its acceptance rested on a reasonable assumption that the nonconformity would be seasonably cured.[14][57][8]

That rule means Vendor cannot use a cure offer to “undo” acceptance, and Buyer cannot label its later request a rejection merely because Vendor offered replacement. Rejection is necessarily pre-acceptance; an effort to take back an already-made acceptance is **revocation of acceptance**, a narrower remedy governed by § 2-608. The distinction protects the buyer’s initial perfect-tender right while imposing a higher threshold once conduct has treated the goods as the buyer’s own.[63][49]

For a revocation, Buyer must establish both substantial impairment and one of the statute’s qualifying acceptance circumstances. The color variance must substantially impair the widgets’ value *to this Buyer*, and Buyer must have accepted either on a reasonable but unfulfilled assumption of seasonable cure, or without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances. Revocation must also occur within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered the basis, before a substantial non-defect-caused change in condition, and upon notice to Vendor.[26][13]

Buyer has difficulty fitting either qualifying circumstance. Navy versus charcoal was visible on delivery and during installation. The March praise of performance did not reserve an expectation of correction, and Vendor’s replacement offer appears to have followed, rather than induced, Buyer’s acceptance. The later architect’s assessment may explain why the visual mismatch mattered to the showroom design, but it does not readily establish that the color was difficult to discover or that Buyer accepted because Vendor promised a seasonable correction.[26][9][63]

The paint work is significant to revocation, but § 2-608 asks whether it was a substantial change in condition and whether it was caused by the asserted defect.[7][3][26] Section 2-608 requires revocation before a substantial change in condition not caused by the goods’ defect.

Buyer painted 600 widgets black to match its showroom. Whether that alteration was a substantial change in condition and whether it was caused by the alleged navy-color nonconformity are questions required by § 2-608(2).[1][7][26] The painting also strongly supports acceptance through conduct inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership and makes return or replacement logistics materially more difficult.[1][7][26][2] The UCC does not mechanically define every alteration as substantial, but painting 60% of the 1,000-unit shipment is powerful evidence against a later attempt to undo acceptance.[26][63]

Buyer may cite removal, reinstallation, operational disruption, and the treatment of the 600 painted widgets as practical difficulties with Vendor’s proposed replacement.[61][79][27]

There is a genuine jurisdictional disagreement about cure after revocation. Some courts treat § 2-608(3)—which gives a buyer who validly revokes the same rights and duties as a rejecting buyer—as importing the seller’s § 2-508 cure right. Other courts, including the Illinois Supreme Court described in the cited authority, reject that reading: a seller may lose the right to cure after revocation but receives the protection of the more demanding substantial-impairment standard. The Illinois Supreme Court held that § 2-608(3)’s incorporated rights and duties are those in §§ 2-602, 2-603, and 2-604, not the seller’s cure provision in § 2-508.[49] Commentary reports that most courts deny a statutory seller cure right after revocation, though the issue remains jurisdiction-dependent.[49][9]

That disagreement does not materially improve Buyer’s position. Even under a rule that does not require a cure opportunity before a § 2-608(1)(b) revocation, Buyer must first prove substantial impairment, a qualifying undiscovered nonconformity or assurance, timely notice, and no disqualifying change in condition. Conversely, if the governing jurisdiction permits post-revocation cure, Vendor’s immediate offer to supply conforming widgets in two weeks further supports Vendor’s position.[49][13]

**Vendor’s offer is a concrete proposal to replace the widgets with conforming charcoal-gray widgets, but § 2-508 makes an available cure after timely rejection contingent on its performance-time or reasonable-belief conditions, seasonable notice, and, under subsection (2), a further reasonable time. The proposal is not a completed cure and cannot reverse Buyer’s apparent prior acceptance.[1][27][61][57]** After acceptance, the offer is relevant chiefly to whether Buyer can prove an assumed-but-unsatisfied cure or to the practical measure of any damages; it does not automatically oblige Buyer to surrender accepted widgets or give Buyer a renewed right to reject.[1][57][40]

Vendor’s proposed two-week replacement lead time was 25% of the eight-week pre-notice period during which Buyer installed and operated the widgets. That comparison supports Vendor’s position that its proposed response was comparatively prompt once Buyer raised the color issue.[1]

The new statutory text confirms that the decisive question for cure is not whether Vendor ultimately offered replacement, but whether Buyer had first rejected a nonconforming tender. If the contract time remained open, Vendor could cure only by seasonably notifying Buyer and making conforming delivery within that time; if it had expired, Vendor needs reasonable grounds to have expected acceptance, seasonable notice, and a further reasonable time to substitute conforming widgets.[14]

Section 2-508’s statutory cure right is tied to a buyer’s rejection of a nonconforming tender; it is not an unrestricted seller right to make successive repair or replacement attempts after the buyer has accepted the goods.[19][45][46]

A timely rejection also must identify a particular defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection when that defect could have been cured if disclosed. The observable navy color was such a defect, and prompt identification would have allowed Vendor to address it through replacement.[44]

The statutory cure routes do not prescribe a fixed number of days. They require seasonable notification and, under § 2-508(2), a further time that is reasonable; the two-week period is therefore evaluated in relation to this color-only deviation and Vendor’s immediate response, not by a numerical UCC deadline.[19][44]

For a post-acceptance revocation theory, the cure issue depends on the applicable jurisdiction. Under the Illinois Supreme Court’s account in Accettura, § 2-608(3) incorporates §§ 2-602, 2-603, and 2-604, rather than § 2-508.[60][49] Accettura holds that § 2-608(3) imports the buyer’s rejection-related rights and duties from §§ 2-602 through 2-604, not the seller’s § 2-508 cure right; it treats the substantial-impairment threshold as the protection afforded the seller after acceptance.[60]

T.W. Oil, Inc. v. Consolidated Edison Co. illustrates § 2-508(2)’s protection against surprise rejection: after relying on its supplier’s certificate that oil met the sulfur specification, the seller promptly offered replacement oil and received a further reasonable time to supply conforming oil.[62]

The purchase-order terms select Washington law to govern interpretation and enforcement of the purchase order.[64]

The UCC good-faith standard is “honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing,” and every UCC contract or duty carries that obligation in performance and enforcement.[47][14] Schmitt and Frisch identify the good faith of both buyer and seller as central, and possibly pivotal, to analysis under § 2-508(2); together, the UCC duty and that analysis make both parties’ good faith relevant to Vendor’s exercise of the § 2-508(2) cure right.[61][14]

Because § 2-508 cure depends on rejection and, under subsection (1), on an unexpired contract performance period, delayed notice can deprive the seller of a cure opportunity and render the attempted rejection ineffective, resulting in acceptance.[59][8]

For revocation, UCC § 2-608 Comment 5 requires Buyer to act consistently with “considerations of good faith, prevention of surprise, and reasonable adjustment.”[49]

A legitimate cure effort requires Buyer’s cooperation where § 2-508 applies. Replacement is a recognized cure method, and a charcoal-gray replacement would directly address the specified color variance rather than merely compensate Buyer for keeping navy widgets.[20][53][29]

The distinction between the two revocation grounds matters: § 2-608(1)(a) expressly concerns acceptance on the reasonable assumption that a nonconformity would be cured and that cure was not seasonably made, whereas § 2-608(1)(b) is silent on a seller cure opportunity.[49]

### What remedies remain after acceptance

Acceptance does not erase every remedy for a proven nonconformity. Maryland Commercial Law § 2-607(3)(a) likewise requires an accepting buyer to notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time after discovery or when it should have discovered the breach, on pain of being barred from a remedy, and § 2-607(4) places the burden of establishing breach on the buyer.[19] It ends ordinary rejection and requires Buyer to pay the contract rate for accepted goods, but § 2-607 expressly preserves other Article 2 remedies. The post-acceptance route is ordinarily a claim for damages, not a demand to return the goods while keeping their use.[8][13]

Buyer must first satisfy the **notice-of-breach rule**. Buyer also bears the burden of proving the breach concerning accepted goods. The contract’s 10-day inspection-and-acceptance requirement governs the inspection-and-acceptance notice that it specifies. Separately, § 2-607(3)(a) requires Buyer, after acceptance, to notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered the color breach.[8][6][65][56]

Judgment — Buyer’s May 10 notice did not comply with the stated 10-day inspection-and-acceptance requirement. It may constitute § 2-607 breach notice only if given within a reasonable time after Buyer actually discovered or should have discovered the apparent navy-for-charcoal breach; later recognition of showroom contrast consequences does not substitute for notice of that visible color variance.[1][8][6]

The ordinary damages measure for accepted goods does not use a preset percentage. Under § 2-714(1), an accepting buyer that has given the § 2-607(3) notification may recover reasonably measured loss resulting in the ordinary course from a nonconforming tender; § 2-714(2) supplies the ordinary time-and-place-of-acceptance value-difference measure for warranty breach, and § 2-714(3) permits incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[8] Illinois UCC § 2-714 permits recovery, after the required notification, of loss from nonconforming tender determined in a reasonable manner; its ordinary warranty measure is the value difference at the time and place of acceptance, with incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[14] Section 2-714(1) allows loss resulting in the ordinary course from nonconforming tender, determined in a reasonable manner, after the required notice.

For breach of warranty, § 2-714(2) ordinarily measures damages as the difference, at the time and place of acceptance, between the value of widgets received in navy and the value they would have had in specified charcoal gray; special circumstances can justify a different proximate amount.[8][66]

That measure requires proof. Buyer must prove the objective market-value difference at the time and place of acceptance between functional navy widgets and otherwise identical charcoal-gray widgets, or prove another proximate showroom-specific loss. Expert appraisals, comparable-sales data, and industry pricing guides are recognized means of proving the valuation figures.[67][68][41] The architect’s contrast assessment may support the latter theory, but it does not itself establish a 40% loss in value.

Relevant evidence of actual loss includes reasonable repainting, removal, replacement, and reinstallation costs, as well as a demonstrable showroom loss.[84][8]

Buyer must also connect the claimed loss to Vendor’s color deviation rather than to its own painting decision or a preventable design choice.[6][68][41]

Buyer may seek qualifying **incidental damages**—reasonable expenses for inspection, receipt, transportation, and care and custody of goods rightfully rejected; commercially reasonable charges, expenses, or commissions in connection with cover; and other reasonable expenses incident to delay or breach. **Consequential damages** include loss from general or particular requirements and needs that Vendor had reason to know at contracting and that Buyer could not reasonably prevent by cover or otherwise, and injury to person or property proximately resulting from breach of warranty.[8] The showroom use is potentially relevant to consequential damages only if Vendor had reason to know of that specific requirement when the parties contracted and Buyer can show the loss could not reasonably be avoided.[8]

Any claimed consequential showroom loss must be foreseeable, causally established, and reasonably certain.[84][8]

Vendor will argue that the offered two-week replacement materially limits any claimed loss. Section 2-712 permits a buyer, after a § 2-711 breach, to cover through a good-faith and timely reasonable substitute purchase and to recover the cover-cost/contract-price difference plus qualifying incidental or consequential damages less expenses saved; failure to cover does not bar another remedy.[8] The UCC does not require a buyer to cover by obtaining substitutes, and failure to cover does not automatically bar a remedy.

But consequential loss that could reasonably have been avoided by cover or another practical cure is not recoverable. UCC § 1-305(1) requires remedies to place the aggrieved party in as good a position as full performance would have done and permits consequential, special, or penal damages only when the UCC or another rule of law specifically provides them.[69][8]

Buyer’s requested 40% refund while retaining and using the widgets does not match the stated UCC measures. Section 2-714 requires proof of actual loss; it does not declare that an aesthetic variance produces a 40% price reduction. Section 2-717 permits a buyer, after notifying the seller of its intention, to deduct breach damages from an amount of the price still due under the same contract.[14] It does not create an automatic refund of 40% of a price already paid.[8][68][70]

A negotiated contract could change that result. Article 2 permits parties to provide additional or substitute remedies, including return and repayment or repair and replacement, and a remedy becomes exclusive only if the agreement expressly makes it exclusive. No stated term here gives Buyer an automatic price-reduction remedy, makes exact-color nonconformity noncurable, or makes Vendor’s replacement remedy exclusive.[8]

Buyer’s alternative is revocation of acceptance, but that remedy is materially more demanding than damages. Under § 2-608, Buyer must show that the nonconformity substantially impaired value to Buyer and that it accepted either on a reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur or without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances.[3] Revocation requires substantial impairment of value to Buyer and a qualifying basis for the acceptance, then timely notice before a substantial non-defect-caused change in the goods’ condition.

If valid, revocation gives Buyer the same rights and duties regarding the widgets as if Buyer had rejected them, including the possibility of recovering price paid and other available damages.[3][26][8]

The architect’s evidence gives Buyer its strongest revocation argument because substantial impairment has a buyer-specific element: a nonconformity can matter because of the buyer’s actual needs rather than an average purchaser’s needs. Yet the inquiry also requires evidence that those needs were in fact substantially unmet. Here, fully functional widgets, industry tolerance of minor color differences for functional components, eight weeks of use, and Buyer’s painting of 600 units all weigh strongly against treating the color variance as a substantial impairment that justifies unwinding the deal.[3][26]

Continued use is particularly problematic if Buyer attempts revocation. Most courts described in the cited authority treat continued use after attempted revocation as destroying the revocation, although some excuse use where it is economically necessary. Other commentary argues that wrongful post-revocation use should yield a seller counterclaim or use-value adjustment rather than automatically invalidate revocation; the treatment depends on governing law and facts.[3][71]

Even if Buyer established a valid revocation, retaining the widgets for continued ordinary operation while recovering their price would remain difficult to justify. A rightful rejecting or revoking buyer may have a security interest in goods in its control for price payments and reasonable specified expenses, but that is a protective possessory interest, not a general license to use the goods for business purposes. Authorities discussing return-based recovery also recognize that continued use can require an offset for the fair value of that use.[8][3][21]

Vendor’s corresponding position is that it may recover the contract price for accepted goods, subject to any proven breach-of-warranty damages. Where goods are wrongfully rejected, § 2-602 directs the seller’s rights to Article 2’s general seller-remedies provision, § 2-703.[38] Illinois UCC § 2-709 authorizes recovery of the price of accepted goods when the buyer fails to pay when due.[14] If Buyer’s attempted rejection or revocation is wrongful, Article 2 directs Vendor to seller remedies; commentary identifies an action for the price as a potential remedy for goods accepted or wrongfully revoked. That makes Buyer’s choice to keep and use the widgets while withholding 40% commercially and legally risky.[38][3][41]

**Buyer may pursue a timely noticed and proven accepted-goods damages claim, but it has no automatic 40% credit merely by naming that percentage while retaining the widgets in service.** Buyer may, after notifying Vendor of its intent to do so, deduct proved breach damages from any unpaid price due under this contract under § 2-717; Buyer and Vendor may also agree on a credit. Its monetary claim otherwise is the demonstrable value shortfall or other properly proven loss, subject to causation and avoidability.[59][8][14][69]

The newly identified statutory text confirms the post-acceptance allocation of obligations: Buyer must pay the contract rate for accepted widgets, bears the burden to establish the alleged color breach, and must notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after it discovered or should have discovered it.[14]

If Buyer wrongfully rejects or wrongfully revokes acceptance, Vendor may invoke seller remedies, including withholding or stopping delivery where applicable, resale and damages, recovery of nonacceptance damages or, in a proper case, the price, and cancellation.[14]

The notice-of-breach requirement has distinct commercial functions after acceptance: courts attribute to § 2-607(3)(a) notice the purposes of providing an opportunity to cure, aiding preparation for negotiation and litigation, and protecting against stale claims.[45] Buyer’s May 10 notice did prompt Vendor’s immediate replacement proposal, but the eight-week delay and the contractual 10-day requirement remain central to whether Buyer preserved a remedy.[32][45][19]

Whether statutory breach notice was given within a reasonable time is generally a fact question measured under all the circumstances. Buyer’s strongest damages argument is that the architect’s later showroom assessment identified the commercial consequence of the color mismatch; Vendor’s stronger response is that the navy color itself was visible upon delivery and installation.[32][19]

If the 10-day inspection-and-acceptance clause is an express contractual condition for breach remedies rather than merely a rejection procedure, courts generally require strict or full compliance unless Vendor waived the condition. The clause therefore provides Vendor a separate basis to oppose Buyer’s delayed monetary claim, in addition to the UCC’s reasonable-time notice rule.[45][19]

Buyer bears the burden of establishing both the asserted color breach as to accepted goods and the resulting loss. A buyer that has accepted goods and given notification may recover damages for a nonconformity measured reasonably, but the statutory warranty measure remains the value difference at the time and place of acceptance unless special circumstances establish a different proximate amount.[19]

Buyer may expressly reserve rights while performing or assenting to performance. UCC § 1-308(a) provides that a party that explicitly reserves rights does not prejudice those rights by performing, promising performance, or assenting to performance demanded or offered by the other party; sufficient language includes “without prejudice” and “under protest.”[47]

Maryland UCC § 2-714(1) likewise permits an accepting buyer that gave § 2-607 notice to recover reasonably measured loss resulting in the ordinary course from the nonconformity, and § 2-714(2) measures warranty damages by the difference, at the time and place of acceptance, between the value received and the value as warranted, unless special circumstances establish different proximate damages.[19]

Peterson v. Denny-Renton Clay & Coal Co. held that late notice did not preclude a buyer’s warranty-damages offset, reasoning that failure to give notice waived buyer rights only where the buyer sought rescission.[45] Martin likewise held that delayed but specific notice did not foreclose warranty damages where the seller recognized and acted on the notice, the notice purposes were fulfilled, and the seller suffered no detriment; when rescission is not sought, Martin treated late notice as bearing on whether the buyer accepted defective performance as satisfactory.[45]

Zabriskie Chevrolet, Inc. v. Smith (1969) treated a new car’s failed transmission as sufficiently serious that the buyers’ shaken faith in the vehicle’s integrity made the seller’s repair offer inadequate.[24] The visible navy/charcoal variance and fully functional widgets do not present that failed-transmission circumstance.[24][26]

Buyer painted 600 of the 1,000 widgets; 400 widgets remained unpainted when Buyer raised the color issue.[1]

If Buyer validly revoked, it would have the same rights and duties regarding the widgets as a rejecting buyer: it would be entitled to a refund, would have to take reasonable care of the widgets pending Vendor’s recovery, and could not continue ordinary business use. Continued use after revocation can be treated as re-acceptance.[33][24]

Revocation notice need not be as detailed or as prompt as rejection notice, but it must be given within a reasonable time.[3] A revocation notice must be timely and advise Vendor that Vendor owns the goods; thus, timely notice of a color problem alone may not communicate that Buyer is reclaiming the widgets.[40][72]

### Synthesis

Judgment — The remedies path follows the procedural status of the widgets. A timely pre-acceptance rejection would have given Buyer a perfect-tender objection but also given Vendor a strong opportunity to replace the navy widgets with conforming charcoal-gray units in a likely reasonable two-week period. Buyer’s apparent acceptance instead forecloses ordinary rejection; Vendor’s offer neither erases that acceptance nor becomes an automatic post-acceptance cure right. Buyer’s remaining practical remedy is a properly noticed damages claim for actual color-related loss, unless it can meet the much higher revocation standard despite visible color, extensive use, and alteration of 600 widgets.[27][57][3][26][8]

1. **Buyer should negotiate Vendor’s proposed replacement delivery after a two-week lead time or an agreed, evidence-based credit. Buyer has no automatic 40% credit, but may deduct proved breach damages from an unpaid price under § 2-717 after notice of intent to deduct.** [1][8][14]
2. **If Buyer retains the widgets, it should give clear breach notice and substantiate actual value loss and any qualifying showroom-related damages.**
3. **Buyer should not characterize its May 10 demand as rejection; any return-based remedy would have to be pleaded and proved as revocation of acceptance.**

## Determination

The dispute turns on a sequence that matters under Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC): a buyer may initially reject a nonconforming tender under the **perfect-tender rule**, but it must do so promptly and before accepting the goods. Once acceptance occurs, the buyer ordinarily cannot relabel a late complaint as rejection; it must satisfy the narrower rules for **revocation of acceptance** or pursue damages for a proved breach. The functional success of these widgets and the specified charcoal-gray color therefore do different legal work.

### What is Buyer’s strongest case that it rightfully rejected the

Buyer’s strongest merits argument begins with **conformity**—whether the delivered goods matched the contract—not with whether the widgets worked. UCC § 2-601 supplies the perfect-tender rule for a single delivery: if the goods “fail in any respect to conform to the contract,” the buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept commercial units and reject the rest. The rule does not require the buyer to show a material or substantial defect before acceptance.[8][9][57]

The purchase order’s charcoal-gray reference number is important because a description that is part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods will conform to that description. Buyer can therefore argue that charcoal gray was not merely an informal preference but a specified attribute of the custom widgets. If that reading is adopted, widgets delivered in navy were nonconforming on March 15, 2024, notwithstanding that they met every stated load-capacity and durability requirement.[8][63]

Buyer should resist Vendor’s likely reliance on **substantial performance**, the common-law idea that a party may satisfy a contract by performing nearly all material obligations. That doctrine is not the conformity test for an ordinary UCC sale of goods. For initial rejection of a single delivery, Article 2 asks whether the tender conformed to the parties’ agreement; it does not ask whether the seller substantially performed.[63][22] Full functional performance thus does not automatically make navy widgets conforming if the agreement required charcoal gray.

Buyer also has a credible commercial-use argument. The widgets were intended for showroom display, and Buyer’s architect later confirmed that navy created contrast problems in the showroom design. That confirmation helps Buyer show that color had a practical presentation and design function rather than being a wholly subjective preference. Under the later, stricter revocation standard, value is assessed with attention to the particular buyer’s needs and circumstances as well as objective proof of actual impairment; the architect’s assessment is relevant evidence on the buyer-specific side of that inquiry.[1][25][3]

Buyer can further argue that the stated industry practice—minor color variations are acceptable for functional components—does not erase an express color requirement. A **usage of trade** is a regularly observed practice in a trade that justifies an expectation that it will be followed in the transaction; its existence and scope must be proved as facts. A proven usage can inform or qualify contract language, but the UCC directs courts to read it consistently with express terms where reasonable, and gives the express terms priority if the two cannot reasonably be reconciled.[17] A reference to a particular charcoal-gray number can therefore control over a general tolerance for minor shade variation if the reference objectively calls for that designated color.

Buyer’s argument is strongest if the color reference was a clear, bargained-for visual specification and if the navy-to-charcoal difference was commercially significant in the planned showroom. The absence of a special clause expressly permitting shade variance also assists that reading. Conversely, Buyer must confront the factual possibility that the reference described an approximate finish in a market where minor variation is customary. The asserted trade practice favors Vendor only if Vendor proves that it applies to this type of component and that the parties reasonably understood the color reference in light of it.[1][17]

**Buyer therefore has a substantial argument that Vendor’s March 15 tender was nonconforming, because functional performance did not supersede an express charcoal-gray description under the perfect-tender rule.** That merits point does not itself establish that Buyer later exercised its rejection right effectively; timely rejection and acceptance are separate questions.[8]

Buyer’s best response to Vendor’s acceptance argument is that physical possession alone is not acceptance. Acceptance does not arise merely because a buyer receives goods; a buyer must have a reasonable opportunity to inspect them. The fact that Buyer installed the widgets could, in some circumstances, reflect necessary testing or an effort to determine whether the goods met specifications.[42][3][8] But that position is difficult to sustain here because color is ordinarily visible at delivery and installation, and Buyer did not raise the issue while it installed and operated all 1,000 widgets.

Buyer can also argue that the architect’s later confirmation was the first reliable identification of the showroom-level consequence of the shade difference. A later-discovered problem can matter where a nonconformity was difficult to discover or seller assurances reasonably induced acceptance.[7][9][63] Yet the relevant nonconformity was the widgets’ navy color, not the later professional articulation of its design consequences. The later opinion gives Buyer evidence of impact; it does not make the readily observable shade difference latent.

The practical consequence is that Buyer’s best position separates two propositions: first, navy may have been nonconforming at tender; second, the navy color later caused a genuine showroom-design problem. Neither proposition alone overcomes the much stronger evidence that Buyer did not reject within the agreed and reasonable inspection period.

The color reference also has priority consequences. A description that is part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty of conformity to that description, and an exact or technical specification displaces inconsistent general descriptive language, a sample, or a model. Thus, if the charcoal-gray reference number is an exact or technical specification, it controls over a general understanding that functional components may have minor shade variation.[14]

Buyer also has a policy argument against forfeiture of every post-acceptance remedy based solely on delayed notice. UCC Comment 4 describes the notice rule as designed to defeat commercial bad faith rather than deprive a good-faith consumer of a remedy, and commentary argues that delayed, specific notice that the seller recognizes and acts upon can preserve warranty damages where the notice purposes are fulfilled and the seller suffers no detriment. Vendor’s immediate replacement offer gives Buyer an argument that the May 10 communication identified a concrete, curable problem and enabled a commercial response.[45]

The purchase order is also the principal record for identifying the promised attributes: an accepted purchase order is a binding agreement, and an item description may include color or a reference code. Buyer can therefore characterize the charcoal-gray reference number as part of the agreed description, rather than as an unenforceable showroom preference.[73]

A party offering evidence of a relevant usage of trade must give the opposing party notice sufficient to prevent unfair surprise before that evidence is admissible.[17]

Merchantability is a separate standard: goods must pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, be of fair average quality within that description, be fit for ordinary purposes, be of even kind, quality, and quantity within and among units or lots, be adequately packaged and labeled, and conform to container or label promises or affirmations of fact.[44]

This determination applies the model UCC. The UCC is a model commercial law adopted in some form by all 50 states, Article 2 governs sales of goods except in Louisiana, and the governing state’s enacted version and its interpretation of reasonable time and ownership-inconsistent conduct control the March 2024 transaction. The Illinois legislative database also identifies a statutory change effective January 1, 2025, so the version effective in March 2024 must be identified before a state-specific result is reached.[72][51][14]

The navy color may fail an express charcoal-gray contract requirement even if the industry tolerance for minor variations supports merchantability. Merchantability asks, among other things, whether goods would pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, are of fair average quality within that description, and are fit for ordinary purposes; it does not displace the perfect-tender question whether the delivered goods conformed to the parties’ express description.[19][44]

### What is Vendor’s strongest case that Buyer did not rightfully

Vendor’s strongest argument is procedural and conduct-based. Vendor need not prove that the widgets were perfectly conforming to prevail on rejection. It can concede, for purposes of argument, that the charcoal-gray reference made the navy delivery nonconforming and still argue that Buyer lost its pre-acceptance rejection remedy by accepting the widgets before May 10.

A rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery or tender, and it is ineffective unless the buyer seasonably notifies the seller. The parties also agreed to a standard inspection-and-acceptance clause requiring notice within 10 days. UCC § 2-602’s Official Comment states that contractual provisions limiting rejection time are effective if the stated period gives the buyer a reasonable time to discover defects.[42][38][8] Here delivery occurred on March 15, 2024; Buyer did not notify Vendor of the color issue until May 10, after eight weeks of installation and operation. That notice was far outside the express 10-day period.[1]

The 10-day clause is particularly powerful because the alleged defect was **patent**—readily observable—not latent or difficult to discover. A navy finish instead of specified charcoal gray could be seen upon delivery or at least during the earliest installation stages. Buyer’s March emails praised performance and did not mention color. Those communications are not necessarily an express declaration that the widgets conformed in every respect, but they reinforce Vendor’s argument that Buyer had an opportunity to inspect, found the functional performance satisfactory, and elected to proceed without a color objection.[1][39][51]

Under UCC § 2-606, acceptance occurs in three relevant ways:

- **Express or implied retention:** after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, the buyer signifies that the goods conform or that it will keep them despite nonconformity. The buyer’s words or conduct can make that showing. [2][39]

- **Ineffective rejection:** a buyer accepts by failing to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect. Silence is not rejection; rejection requires affirmative action. [2][3][39]

- **Ownership-inconsistent conduct:** a buyer accepts by doing an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership, subject to the statutory qualification concerning a wrongful act and seller ratification. [2][39][8]

Vendor can rely on all three. Buyer installed and operated every widget for eight weeks. Installation and putting equipment into production are recognized examples of conduct that can move a buyer out of the straightforward rejection position and into the narrower post-acceptance remedies.[52] Buyer also painted 600 of the 1,000 widgets black.

Painting is not passive custody; it is an alteration aimed at integrating the goods into Buyer’s own showroom design. Under UCC § 2-606(1)(c), an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership constitutes acceptance; courts have treated a buyer’s use of goods as raw material in manufacturing other products as such an act. Painting 600 widgets similarly supports Vendor’s position that Buyer exercised ownership-like control that impaired return or resale in their tendered condition.[3][51]

The 600 painted widgets make Buyer’s claimed all-or-nothing rejection especially problematic. Even if the widgets are separate commercial units rather than one integrated commercial unit, painting 600 units is substantial evidence that Buyer treated most of the shipment as its own inventory. If the relevant units are grouped as a commercial unit, acceptance of part constitutes acceptance of the whole unit.[39][13][43] The precise unit definition may require contract and industry evidence, but Buyer’s alteration of 60% of the shipment strongly supports acceptance at least as to the altered widgets.

Vendor can also characterize Buyer’s conduct as commercially inconsistent with a true rejection. After rejection, a buyer may not exercise ownership over the rejected goods; it must hold goods in its possession with reasonable care at the seller’s disposition for enough time to permit removal.[8][9][13] Buyer instead kept the widgets in service and demanded a 40% refund while continuing to use them.

That demand reads more naturally as a request for a negotiated price allowance after acceptance than as an intention to return or hold the goods for Vendor.

The notice itself also matters. A communication that merely identifies a problem may preserve a claim for damages but does not necessarily constitute rejection. To reject, the buyer must communicate that it does not intend to keep the goods, allowing the seller to cure or recover them.[40][41] Buyer’s demand for a partial refund while retaining and using the widgets cuts against an interpretation that it unequivocally rejected them.

Vendor may use functional performance and industry practice, but these points should be kept in their proper role. They do not automatically negate a clear color specification under perfect tender. Instead, they support Vendor’s factual claim that the variance was minor, commercially tolerable for functional components, and reasonably believed acceptable—possibly with a money allowance. This can support a cure opportunity under § 2-508(2), which permits a further reasonable time to substitute a conforming tender when the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the initial tender would be acceptable with or without a money allowance and seasonably notified the buyer.[1][8][9][27][31][44]

Vendor’s replacement proposal was immediate and contemplated replacement within two weeks. The UCC’s first cure route applies when rejection occurs before the contract’s performance time expires and the seller seasonably notifies the buyer of its intent to cure with a conforming delivery within contract time. The second route permits a further reasonable time after rejection when the seller had qualifying reasonable grounds to expect acceptance.[8][27] The stated industry tolerance for minor color variation is evidence favoring Vendor on the reasonable-grounds question, although it does not conclusively establish it.[1][59][61]

**Vendor’s prevailing case is that Buyer’s eight-week delay, missed 10-day deadline, installation, continued operation, March performance praise, and painting of 600 widgets established acceptance and barred ordinary rejection.**[42][1][2][3]

The architect’s later confirmation does not erase these acceptance indicators. It strengthens Buyer’s claim that the wrong shade had a genuine showroom consequence, but it came after Buyer had installed, used, and altered the goods. The confirmation may inform damages or the substantial-impairment analysis; it does not retrospectively turn a visible condition into a concealed defect or make Buyer’s earlier notice seasonable.[1][7]

The May 10 notice came 56 days after the March 15 delivery and 46 days after the contractual 10-day notice period ended on March 25. For a readily visible color difference, that sequence establishes that Buyer failed to reject within a reasonable time and failed to give seasonable notice, making rejection ineffective under § 2-602(1).[1][14]

Vendor can add that the color difference was a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection. Under UCC § 2-605, a buyer that fails, in connection with rejection, to state an ascertainable defect may be precluded from relying on it to justify rejection or establish breach where seasonable notice would have enabled cure. A prompt statement that navy rather than the specified charcoal-gray color was objectionable would have allowed Vendor to replace the widgets before Buyer installed, operated, and painted them.[19][44][31]

The 10-day inspection-and-acceptance term also has independent contractual force. Parties commonly displace default notice rules with express notice requirements, and courts typically enforce them; an Illinois-law decision described such a definite contractual notice period as a material condition precedent to enforcement. Applied here, Buyer’s May 10 notice followed the March 15 delivery by 56 days, exceeding the agreed 10 days by 46 days.[45][1]

The statutory post-rejection duties independently reinforce Vendor’s position. After rejection, Buyer may not exercise ownership over a commercial unit and must hold possessed goods with reasonable care at Vendor’s disposition long enough for removal. Buyer’s continued operation and its painting of 600 widgets are inconsistent with that required preservation of the goods.[30][23]

Vendor can characterize its offer as a cure effort only if the § 2-508 conditions are met. Before the performance deadline, the seller must seasonably notify Buyer and make a conforming delivery within contract time; after that deadline, Vendor must have had reasonable grounds to believe navy would be acceptable and must seasonably notify Buyer before receiving a further reasonable time to substitute conforming goods. The asserted trade practice accepting minor color variation supplies Vendor’s strongest factual basis for reasonable grounds, but the two-week period is not automatically reasonable.[62]

Prompt inspection and reporting encourage earlier cure and mitigate harm to both parties; if cure does not occur, prompt recovery permits the seller to maximize resale value by recovering goods as nearly as practicable in their tendered condition.[40]

Some courts applying UCC § 2-607(3)(a) take a strict approach: the buyer must communicate not only the facts of its claim, but also that those facts constitute a breach and that the buyer intends to hold the seller accountable.[45]

Inspection and rejection timing remain circumstance-specific. Here, however, the stated trade practice concerns tolerance for minor shade variation, while the alleged navy finish was visible and the parties specified a 10-day inspection-and-acceptance period.[63][9][39][1]

Eight weeks of retention and use without a color complaint supports an inference that Buyer accepted the widgets despite the asserted defect. The delay also postponed the prompt cure or recovery that could have mitigated harm and enabled Vendor to recover the widgets in substantially their tendered condition, preserving resale value.[45][40]

Whether Buyer’s rejection was timely and effective is distinct from whether rejection breached the contract. A rejection may be effective yet wrongful, and an effective but wrongful rejection does not itself constitute acceptance.[74][39]

### Did Buyer rightfully reject the widgets or must Buyer accept

Buyer did not rightfully reject the widgets. The better legal characterization is acceptance of potentially nonconforming goods, followed by a late complaint about a visible aesthetic specification. Buyer accepted potentially nonconforming widgets and therefore cannot reject them; Buyer must pay the contract rate for the accepted goods, subject to any damages it establishes for Vendor’s breach.[8][6]

This conclusion does not rest on the mistaken proposition that performance conformity equals total conformity. The widgets met the stated functional requirements, but the charcoal-gray reference could still have been an express contractual requirement, making navy widgets nonconforming at tender. Perfect tender governs that initial question. **Substantial impairment**, by contrast, is the more demanding standard governing a buyer’s attempt to undo an acceptance through revocation.[8][7][26][49]

The distinction is decisive. Before acceptance, Buyer could potentially reject even a limited contractual variance. After acceptance, Buyer must establish that the nonconformity substantially impaired the value of the lot or commercial unit to Buyer and that one of the statutory acceptance circumstances applies: either Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption of a seasonable cure that did not occur, or Buyer accepted without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Seller’s assurances induced acceptance.[7][8]

Buyer is unlikely to meet that standard on the stated facts. The architect’s confirmation supports a buyer-specific showing that navy impaired the showroom’s visual design. Substantial impairment has subjective and objective components: the inquiry considers Buyer’s particular needs but also requires proof that value was in fact objectively impaired to this Buyer.[25][3] The showroom evidence is therefore relevant, but full functional performance, the stated tolerance for minor color variation in functional components, and Buyer’s continued use all weigh against a finding that the widgets’ value was substantially impaired.[1]

More fundamentally, the color variance was apparent. Buyer knew, or at minimum should have known, that the widgets were navy rather than charcoal during installation. Buyer cannot plausibly rely on the theory that it accepted without discovering the nonconformity because the color was difficult to discover.[1][7][9] Nor do the facts state that Buyer accepted because Vendor promised before acceptance to correct the shade.

Vendor’s replacement offer came only after the May 10 notice, so it cannot supply the reasonable assumption of cure that would explain an earlier acceptance.[1][7][8]

Painting 600 widgets creates an additional obstacle to revocation. Revocation must occur before a substantial change in the goods’ condition that was not caused by the nonconformity. Painting is a change caused by Buyer’s showroom-design choice, not by the navy color itself.[7][25][9] The statute does not mechanically define whether this particular alteration is a substantial change, but painting 600 of 1,000 widgets—60% of the delivery—is a strong factual basis for Vendor to argue that it is.

Buyer’s continued use also weakens any attempted revocation. Authorities differ on whether continued post-revocation use always invalidates revocation, but most courts discussed in the cited commentary treat continued use as destructive of revocation unless special circumstances justify it; at minimum, the seller is entitled to compensation for the fair value of post-revocation use.[3][25][71] No necessity or inability to stop using the widgets appears in the stated facts. The circumstances therefore favor Vendor’s position that Buyer cannot retain the commercial benefit of the widgets while asserting a return-type remedy.

Vendor’s two-week replacement proposal does not reverse Buyer’s prior acceptance. Cure under § 2-508 is principally a rule governing rejected nonconforming tenders, not a device that converts accepted goods back into unaccepted goods.[8][22] Had Buyer promptly rejected within the 10-day period, Vendor’s immediate offer to replace with charcoal widgets within two weeks would have been a strong proposed cure.

Under § 2-508(2), Vendor could have had a further reasonable time to substitute conforming widgets if it seasonably notified Buyer and had reasonable grounds to believe navy would be accepted, with or without a money allowance; the stated trade practice supports those grounds.[1][8][59]

After acceptance, cure is more complicated. Some authority describes a split over whether a seller has a right to cure after revocation, while the Kansas statutory comment states that a seller has no right to cure after revocation; the Illinois Supreme Court has similarly distinguished rejection, where § 2-508 cure may apply, from revocation, which carries the higher substantial-impairment standard.[9][25][49] That disagreement does not change this outcome because Buyer’s principal failure is earlier: it accepted the widgets and has not shown facts supporting a valid revocation.

Buyer also cannot demand an automatic 40% refund while keeping the widgets in service. Acceptance precludes rejection but does not, by itself, eliminate every Article 2 remedy. A buyer that accepted goods may pursue damages for a proved nonconformity if it gives the seller notice within a reasonable time after discovering or when it should have discovered the breach; the buyer bears the burden of proving breach as to accepted goods.[8][9][65][57] The requested 40% figure has no stated contractual or statutory basis. A price reduction may be negotiated, but it is not a unilateral remedy established merely by asserting an aesthetic defect.

If Buyer proves that navy rather than charcoal breached an express description or warranty, its remaining remedy is post-acceptance damages rather than rejection while retaining unrestricted use. Buyer’s May 10 notice identified the color issue and prompted Vendor’s replacement response; that conduct supports Buyer’s argument that Vendor received notice that the transaction was troublesome and claimed to involve a breach, although the agreed 10-day clause strongly favors Vendor on timeliness.[45] Any recovery associated with a valid return remedy would also need to account for the fair value of Buyer’s use; continued use and deterioration unrelated to the alleged nonconformity may support an offset.[3][25][21] Buyer’s use of all widgets for eight weeks, plus the painting of 600, would be central to that calculation.

The legal outcome is not altered by the absence of a specific clause authorizing rejection for shade variance. The absence of such a clause does not waive the color specification, but neither does it displace the express 10-day inspection-and-acceptance provision or the UCC’s requirement of seasonable rejection notice.[42][1][59][21] Buyer had a potential perfect-tender objection on March 15; it did not preserve that objection as a rejection remedy through May 10.

For any remaining damages remedy on accepted goods, Buyer must notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered it; otherwise Buyer is barred from a remedy. Buyer also bears the burden to establish the alleged breach as to accepted widgets.[19]

Acceptance has a specific remedial consequence: it precludes rejection of the accepted goods. When acceptance was with knowledge of the nonconformity, revocation is unavailable unless the acceptance rested on a reasonable assumption of seasonable cure; acceptance nevertheless does not itself impair other Article 2 remedies for nonconformity.[34][32]

On these facts, neither statutory route to revocation fits. The known-defect route requires acceptance on a reasonable assumption that the seller would cure seasonably, but Vendor made its replacement offer only after Buyer’s May 10 complaint. The undiscovered-defect route requires that the buyer’s acceptance without discovery be reasonably induced by difficulty of discovery or seller assurances; the visibly navy finish does not meet that description.[75][60][46]

Under Maryland Commercial Law § 2-709(1)(a), when a buyer fails to pay the price as it becomes due, the seller may recover the price of accepted goods, together with seller incidental damages. Maryland Commercial Law § 2-710 defines those incidental damages as commercially reasonable charges, expenses, or commissions incurred in stopping delivery; transporting, caring for, or holding goods after breach; returning or reselling goods; or otherwise resulting from the breach.[19] Under Maryland Commercial Law § 2-703, where a buyer wrongfully rejects or revokes acceptance, fails to make a payment due on or before delivery, or repudiates, the aggrieved seller may withhold delivery, stop delivery by a bailee, resell and recover damages, recover damages for nonacceptance or, in a proper case, the price, or cancel. Section 2-602(3) likewise directs that a seller’s rights concerning wrongfully rejected goods are governed by § 2-703.[19][76]

Revocation is ineffective until Buyer notifies Vendor, and effective revocation notice must state that Buyer is revoking acceptance and identify the nonconformity.[24]

The facts do not fit the circumstances in which extended use has supported revocation. In Jorgensen v. Pressnall (Oregon Supreme Court, 1976), buyers lived in a defective mobile home for several months and continued living there for a year while repeatedly seeking repairs; the court ruled for them because continued residence was reasonable for a home, they had given notice and an opportunity to repair, and their delay reflected giving the seller a chance to cure.[33][77] In North American Lighting, Inc.

Rejection applies at delivery, while UCC § 2-608 provides the mechanism to undo acceptance through revocation. Section 2-508 gives a seller a right to cure a nonconforming defect and thereby limits the buyer’s § 2-601 right to reject; once Buyer has accepted nonconforming goods by business use or failure to inspect timely, the § 2-508 cure process generally no longer applies. Thus Vendor’s offer is not a mechanism that makes Buyer’s completed acceptance conditional or converts the May 10 complaint into a timely rejection.[24][22][62]

If Buyer had effectively revoked acceptance, UCC § 2-608(3) would give Buyer the same rights and duties concerning the widgets as if Buyer had rejected them.[33]

Every UCC contract and duty imposes an obligation of good faith in performance and enforcement. That duty, and the importance of both parties’ good faith to § 2-508(2) cure analysis, make Buyer’s prolonged use and painting and Vendor’s replacement response relevant in assessing their conduct alongside the statutory timing rules.[14][61]

For an accepted-goods damages claim, the ordinary UCC measure is the difference, at the time and place of acceptance, between the value of the navy widgets as received and the value they would have had if they conformed to the charcoal-gray specification, plus any provable incidental or consequential loss. The architect’s assessment may help prove a design-related loss, but it does not itself establish Buyer’s demanded 40% reduction.

If Buyer had timely rejected and Vendor made a conforming replacement tender within the allowed cure period, Buyer would have been required to accept that conforming replacement rather than treat the initial shade variance as automatically ending the transaction.[29]

### Synthesis

The strongest combined reading is that the widgets may have been nonconforming at tender because navy did not match a specified charcoal-gray reference, even though they fully met functional performance criteria. That issue initially favored Buyer under perfect tender. But Buyer’s conduct and timing favor Vendor decisively: Buyer waited until May 10, 2024—well beyond the agreed 10-day period—after eight weeks of installation and operation, March praise of performance, and the painting of 600 widgets. Those facts establish acceptance rather than timely rejection. The architect’s later confirmation supports a real showroom-design concern, but it does not overcome the visible nature of the color difference, the alteration of the goods, or the lack of a prompt objection. Vendor’s two-week replacement offer could have supported cure after a timely rejection only if it would supply conforming widgets and met § 2-508’s notice and timing conditions; it does not retroactively undo Buyer’s acceptance.[20][62][8][42][1]

1. **Buyer should withdraw the claim of rightful rejection** and pursue the May 10 communication as post-acceptance notice of a claimed breach. Buyer should evaluate a specified conforming replacement or a negotiated credit against the cost and feasibility of replacing the 600 painted widgets, while documenting any timely noticed, provable loss. [1][8][27]

2. **Buyer should not withhold 40% unilaterally while retaining and operating the widgets;** it must pay the contract rate for accepted goods unless it negotiates a credit or proves a legally available damages claim. [8][9]

3. **Buyer should preserve evidence of the charcoal-gray reference, the architect’s design analysis, and the commercial effect of the contrast issue** if it seeks damages for breach of an express description or warranty. [8][1][65]

4. **Vendor should maintain the offer of conforming replacement as a commercial resolution,** while reserving its position that Buyer accepted the widgets and that the proposed replacement does not validate Buyer’s late rejection. [1][8]

The UCC is a model statute rather than federal law; a state must adopt it, may revise it, and its courts may interpret it differently. The governing state’s enacted version and case law therefore control questions including reasonable time and conduct inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[44][51]

The Illinois statutory database warns that statutory changes can appear before their effective dates; its source note for § 1-201 identifies Public Act 103-1036 as effective January 1, 2025. Because delivery occurred in March 2024, the version effective at that time governs any Illinois-law analysis.[14]

### Open questions

The remaining merits questions are whether the color reference number required an exact charcoal-gray shade or allowed ordinary trade variation, whether navy materially impaired the widgets’ showroom value to Buyer, and what provable loss the color difference caused. The purchase order’s color-language context, established trade usage, the architect’s design assessment, and reliable valuation of any diminished value would settle those questions.
