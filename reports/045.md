Choose Seldon Core 2 with Triton as the default production-serving stack if the decisive requirement is a low-latency, mixed CPU/GPU XGBoost or LightGBM plus neural-network ensemble under one explicit inference DAG. At 50M payments per day, average demand is about 579 predictions/s and the specified sustained 10× burst is about 5,787 predictions/s, so warm capacity rather than reactive autoscaling must protect the payment p99.

Triton offers the clearest model-level queue, transfer, compute, p95, and p99 instrumentation, while Seldon adds weighted and shadow routing for safe variant evaluation. Ray Serve is the strongest alternative when the ensemble needs Python-native distributed application logic and independently scaling heterogeneous stages, while KServe suits a Kubernetes-standard platform team and BentoML suits a smaller team prioritizing rapid packaged-service delivery.

No platform has a transferable published p95, p99, 10×-burst throughput, or cost-per-million result for this exact heterogeneous fraud path, so the deployment decision should be confirmed with a common gateway-to-decision benchmark that includes online features, warm-headroom cost, and incident rollback drills.

## MLOps platform comparison

Real-time fraud scoring is an end-to-end system, not just a model-server choice. The comparison covers Seldon Core 2 with NVIDIA Triton Inference Server, Ray Serve, KServe on Kubernetes, and BentoML; their distinct composition and traffic-control locations are an inference-server DAG plus MLOps pipeline, Python deployment graph, Kubernetes custom resources, and packaged Service graph. NVIDIA’s March 13, 2023 Technical Blog article says Triton was renamed NVIDIA Dynamo Triton as part of the NVIDIA Dynamo Platform on March 18, 2025; the Ray material used here documents Ray 2.59.0 and 2.60.0, and BentoCloud canary deployment through its CLI or Python SDK requires BentoML 1.4.17 or later.[1][2][3][4]

The requested company landscape is Razorpay in India, Gojek in Indonesia, and PayMongo in the Philippines. Razorpay described Mitra as the data platform powering Thirdwatch for real-time fraud prevention by serving machine-learning models at scale; its account places Apache Flink feature generation, data augmentation, validation, identity clustering, and velocity profiling ahead of outputs through dashboards, APIs, Kafka topics, or downstream operators. Razorpay separated training and serving clusters after training resource allocation and bulk feature traffic impaired serving requests, allowing each cluster to scale for its own load and requirements.[5] Gojek published Merlin support for XGBoost, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and arbitrary Python models; its Feast account describes online feature lookup before prediction and a unified feature-serving API, while GoSage represents customers and merchants as graph nodes and transactions or shared devices as edges.[6][7][8] PayMongo describes Protect as transaction-level fraud monitoring in which a machine-learning engine evaluates each transaction in real time, applies configured rules, and decides allow, review, or block before completion.[9] Razorpay, Gojek, and PayMongo publish no company-specific cold-start latency for new model deployments or quantified operational complexity for simultaneously running multiple model frameworks in these accounts.[5][6][9]

The gateway-to-decision boundary begins when the scoring gateway receives the payment request and ends when it returns the allow, review, or block decision. Online feature lookup, request routing, queueing, CPU–GPU transfers, model execution, and audit handling all sit within that boundary and can contribute to p95 and p99 latency.[9][10]

**Claim — All four configurations can serve a gradient-boosting-plus-neural-network ensemble, but they allocate routing, batching, scaling, and framework-integration responsibility differently. No common benchmark establishes p95, p99, 10× burst throughput, or cost per million predictions for the requested 50M-payment-per-day workload, so those figures must be measured on identical artifacts and infrastructure rather than inferred from vendor examples.** Triton’s own documentation separates queue time, GPU-transfer time, inference compute, endpoint overhead, and client-side transport time; platform-level performance also depends on the model, hardware, optimization stack, and workload.[10][11]

### Comparison basis

Treat the client’s volume as one shared India–Indonesia–Philippines payment workload, not three separate 50M-transaction workloads.Derived — 50,000,000 predictions per day divided by 86,400 seconds is 578.7037 predictions per second, or approximately **579 TPS** average. The defined 10× burst is therefore **5,787.037 predictions per second**, or approximately **5,787 TPS**.

That average is only a capacity-planning starting point. Fraud volume follows payment traffic, which commonly has diurnal peaks, merchant campaigns, payroll cycles, sale events, issuer incidents, and retry storms. The test must therefore use an open-loop request-rate workload: first ramp to the target RPS, second hold it long enough to expose queue growth and scale-out delay, then increase monotonically through stages until saturation. Seldon’s load-testing guidance recommends precisely this staged open-loop method; unlike closed-loop virtual users, it does not reduce offered load when latency rises and therefore avoids coordinated omission.[12]

Judgment — **Use two operational test profiles, not one average-rate test.** The first should sustain 579 TPS long enough to establish normal p95/p99 and utilization. The second should begin from that warm baseline and sustain 5,787 TPS long enough to show whether the system remains within its payment-decision latency objective while replicas, nodes, and feature-store clients are under pressure. A brief spike test is useful for overload behavior, but it is not a substitute for a sustained burst test because queues may grow slowly while autoscalers appear healthy.

The latency boundary should run from scoring-gateway receipt of the payment request to return of the allow, review, or block decision. Report four distributions separately:

1. online-feature retrieval;
2. model-server queue time;
3. model computation, including CPU–GPU transfer where it occurs; and
4. complete gateway-to-response latency.

Triton’s metrics distinguish client response waiting and send/receive time from server queue, GPU input transfer, inference, and GPU output transfer. Ray Serve’s HTTP and gRPC latency histograms instead measure from proxy receipt to response sent. Those scopes are both useful, but they are not interchangeable.[10][13][14][15]

The online feature path must be included in the main latency result. In Gojek’s published Feast serving flow, each prediction first retrieves the selected feature values from Feast and then passes them to the model. Feature stores address a real fraud-serving problem: the decision needs current, entity-level context such as recent card use, account averages, and merchant-category behavior, while training requires point-in-time-correct historical values.[8][16] A model-only p99 can look excellent while the payment decision path fails because Redis, Feast, a graph lookup, TLS, or a cross-region call is slow.

Judgment — **Hold the following constant across all four candidates:**

- the same XGBoost or LightGBM artifact, neural-network artifact, feature schema, ensemble combiner, thresholds, and calibration;
- the same request body, including feature-vector width and distribution of categorical and missing values;
- the same regional topology, Kubernetes version, node pools, ingress protocol, TLS policy, and audit-log policy;
- the same online feature-store deployment and locality relative to serving nodes;
- the same minimum warm capacity and the same allowance for failed or shed requests; and
- the same definition of a successful prediction: a response returned within the fraud-decision deadline, with the expected model version and complete audit fields.

Dedicated nodes matter during measurement. Seldon recommends running tested inference servers on Kubernetes nodes where unrelated components are not consuming a large share of shared compute, memory, or I/O, because noisy co-tenancy obscures the model and serving-stack result.[12]

The shared mixed-infrastructure design should initially place gateway, feature retrieval, tree inference, aggregation, and light preprocessing on CPU replicas, while placing the neural stage on GPU replicas. This is a starting hypothesis rather than a universal optimum. NVIDIA Triton is open-source inference-serving software that deploys and runs models at scale on CPU and GPU; its ensemble pipeline can run entirely on GPU, entirely on CPU, or across a mix of both. Triton permits each component model to use CPU or GPU instance groups, and its ensemble DAG can execute across a mix of both. It also shows why placement must be measured: its tutorial’s full-GPU pipeline avoided intermediate CPU–GPU transfers and had lower end-to-end latency than the split CPU-pre/postprocessing plus GPU-model configuration.[1][17]

A single numerical p95 or p99 target should not be assumed from a tutorial. One fraud-oriented infrastructure article allocates a sub-50 ms p99 budget to the inline scoring subsystem, while the serverless fraud study reports that all three tested models met a typical payment-authorization p99 below 200 ms when warmed up and that cold start added 210–420 ms latency. Those are different architectural and operational scopes.[18][19] For a payment gateway, the relevant internal target should reserve time for issuer or acquirer interactions and leave sufficient budget for retries or rule evaluation; the model platform should not consume the entire authorization deadline.

Capacity should be sized from a measured **single-replica saturation point**: the greatest sustained throughput before queueing makes the selected p95/p99 unacceptable. Seldon defines the saturation point as the maximum one model replica can sustain, warning that traffic above it increases latency through queueing at the model, CPU, memory, network, or another bottleneck. Its guidance is to operate each replica 10–20% below measured saturation, leaving capacity while new inference and model replicas become ready.[12]

That guidance decides the burst strategy. **Conclusion — Sizing solely through reactive autoscaling is unsafe for a synchronous payment-decision path.** At 5,787 TPS, pre-provision enough warm CPU, GPU, feature-store, and ingress capacity to stay below saturation with the chosen headroom; use autoscaling for sustained excess demand, not as the first defense against an immediate 10× spike.[12]

The cost metric should be **cost per one million successful predictions**, rather than cost per received HTTP request. The numerator should include:

- CPU and GPU node-hours;
- idle capacity deliberately retained for burst headroom;
- feature-store, stream-processing, and model-artifact storage;
- ingress, inter-zone or internet egress, and observability;
- managed-control-plane or commercial platform fees where applicable; and
- the cost of failed, retried, mirrored, or shadow-scored traffic.

Derived — calculate the metric as total monthly serving cost divided by **1,500,000,000 monthly successful predictions**, then multiply by 1,000,000. This should be reported in separate scenarios: on-demand, committed-use, and capacity-reserved. AWS bills On-Demand compute by the hour or second, with a 60-second minimum; EC2 Auto Scaling itself has no additional fee, but launched instances and applicable CloudWatch monitoring remain chargeable. Unused On-Demand Capacity Reservation capacity is also charged at the equivalent On-Demand rate, while Savings Plans can reduce bills by up to 72% in exchange for commitment.[20][21]

This prevents a misleading conclusion. A platform that appears cheap at average utilization can become expensive when it requires many warm GPU replicas for a burst SLO. Conversely, a platform with higher control-plane complexity can be cheaper if it packs several low-utilization model stages efficiently. The platform software and infrastructure economics must therefore be shown separately.

### Seldon Core with Triton Inference Server

Seldon Core 2 is the most direct architecture for an ensemble whose components must use different inference runtimes. Core 2 defines Models, Pipelines, routers, and experiments; a Pipeline is an orchestrated sequence of models and supporting components. It supports multiple inference servers, including MLServer and NVIDIA Triton.[22][53]

Seldon Core 2 Servers deploy replica sets of MLServer or Triton, and its Pipelines can connect synchronous as well as asynchronous stages.[151]

The proposed implementation has four logical stages:

- **Feature adapter and validation:** retrieve already-materialized online features, validate schema and freshness, append transaction-time fields, and produce tensor inputs. This can run as a Seldon processor or Triton Python/custom backend component. Seldon documents feature transformations and detectors as model artifacts, while Triton allows custom Python or C++ logic when a pipeline needs unsupported or specialized linking behavior. [53][24]

- **Gradient-boosting stage:** serve XGBoost or LightGBM through Triton FIL. FIL supports XGBoost, LightGBM, Scikit-learn, and cuML tree models; its quickstart lists XGBoost JSON/binary and LightGBM text artifacts among supported formats. [23]

The cited Triton FIL XGBoost setup requires a Pascal-or-newer NVIDIA GPU, a driver, Docker, and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit.[146]

- **Neural stage:** serve the PyTorch or TensorFlow network through Triton’s corresponding backend. Seldon’s inference-artifact documentation lists PyTorch and TensorFlow for Triton, with artifact-specific requirements such as a traced TorchScript `model.pt` and a Triton `config.pbtxt` for PyTorch. [54]

- **Score combiner:** run both model branches in parallel when the ensemble is not sequential, then combine scores, rules, reason codes, and any policy threshold into one fraud decision. Triton ensemble models are DAGs in which outputs from one model feed another; NVIDIA’s worked example runs two models simultaneously and combines their scores in a postprocessing stage. [1][17]

Triton’s architecture minimizes client-to-server hops because the client calls the ensemble once and Triton executes the graph internally. It can also reduce host–device transfers where compatible stages remain on GPU. NVIDIA specifically describes Triton pipelines as avoiding unnecessary transfer between client and server or host and device.[24][17] That is material for an ensemble with a neural component and high burst demand.

There are nonetheless concrete packaging obligations. Each Triton model needs its own configuration covering backend/platform, maximum batch size, inputs, and outputs. A Triton ensemble has no physical instance group of its own; each constituent model specifies its own instance group and can execute in parallel. The ensemble’s maximum batch size must not exceed that of any component.[55][1][17] Tree artifacts and neural artifacts also bring backend compatibility constraints.

NVIDIA’s example requires the model’s Treelite version to match the Triton container’s Treelite version, and Python preprocessing that needs a different Python version from the container requires a custom Python backend stub and custom execution environment.[1][17]

Judgment — **This is a good trade when the startup already operates Kubernetes and can maintain model-artifact contracts.** Its supported advantage is an explicit Triton model DAG combined with Seldon Pipelines and Experiments, not lower mixed-framework operational complexity. Each Triton component requires its own model configuration, and tree, neural, and Python stages require validation of tensor schemas, artifact formats, backend and container compatibility, Python/CUDA/driver compatibility, and rollback-safe model repositories. Treelite model serialization must match the Triton-container Treelite version; Python preprocessing or postprocessing with a different Python version requires a custom Python backend stub and custom execution environment.[1][17][22][55]

For p95 and p99, Triton gives the strongest instrumentation and tuning path among the four choices. Performance Analyzer measures throughput and latency while testing optimization strategies; Model Analyzer can sweep instance counts, batch sizes, CPU utilization, query sizes, and concurrency. Its detailed report includes p99 latency, latency components, throughput, GPU utilization, and GPU memory utilization, with a default profiling maximum of 1,024 concurrent requests.[56][25] The tool distinguishes queue, compute—including GPU copies—and endpoint overhead, which is essential for diagnosing a payment-tail-latency breach.[15][10]

An illustrative, unmeasured Seldon Core with Triton model estimates **18–35 ms p95** and **30–60 ms p99** for a pre-warmed CPU tree stage, GPU neural stage, and local aggregator **without feature-store retrieval in the critical path**; these are not gateway-to-decision fraud measurements.[146][147][25]

Triton `perf_analyzer` can replay representative transaction payloads from JSON input and stabilize measurements across repeated windows.[152]

Published Triton figures demonstrate mechanisms, not capacity for this client. NVIDIA’s FIL fraud example reports p99 below 2 ms and more than 400,000 inferences/s on a DGX-1 with eight V100 GPUs, described as approximately 20× CPU throughput.[24] That is far above the derived 5,787-TPS burst in raw tree-model terms.

But it does not include online feature retrieval, score aggregation, Seldon routing, the neural model, the intended hardware, or the client’s request distribution. It cannot size the end-to-end system.

Other Triton tutorials show the same issue. A text-recognition tutorial at request concurrency 16 reported 3,187.76 inferences/s, p95 10.567 ms, and p99 10.713 ms with dynamic batching; dynamic batching plus multiple instances reported 4,134.09 inferences/s, p95 8.244 ms, and p99 8.563 ms. These results use a specific model, one GPU, batch-size constraints, and tutorial inputs.[57] A different Inception example recorded 272 inferences/s and 35,988 microseconds p95 at concurrency 8 with dynamic batching.[26] The correct inference is not that Triton produces a specific p99; it is that queue, batching, instance count, model type, and hardware must be jointly tuned.

Dynamic batching is particularly relevant at 5,787 TPS. Triton combines individual requests into a larger batch, which often executes more efficiently. Operators can configure a limited scheduling delay to collect requests, but that delay is added to request latency.[26][57] For a payment model, the tree branch may have little to gain from batching while a neural branch can gain materially; using the same batch policy across both can create avoidable queue time.

Triton also supports multiple model instances and can place a heavier ensemble member on another GPU, which is useful when tree and neural branches have different throughput limits.[57]

Triton Model Analyzer can search batch-size, dynamic-batching and instance-group settings for single-model, multi-model, ensemble and business-logic workloads, reporting throughput, latency, GPU use, memory and power trade-offs.[147][25]

**Conclusion — Seldon Core plus Triton is the technically direct choice when the priority is a single, explicit inference DAG across XGBoost/LightGBM and neural frameworks with CPU/GPU placement under one serving runtime.** It reduces client-visible orchestration and gives strong model-level profiling, but it does not eliminate artifact compatibility work or feature-path latency.[1][17][23][24][10]

Seldon provides the platform-level experiment mechanism that Triton itself does not. Core 2 Experiments define traffic splits between Models or Pipelines; each candidate receives a share equal to its configured weight divided by total weights. A mirror can receive traffic while its response is withheld from the caller, enabling shadow scoring.[58] It also offers an `x-seldon-route` response header for cohort consistency on subsequent calls, although this preserves the model/pipeline sequence rather than affinity to one replica and is therefore unsuitable for stateful models needing the same replica.[58]

A Seldon Experiment activates only once its candidate models or pipelines are Ready.[58]

For fraud experiments, shadow mode is usually the safer first step because it compares scores and reason codes without changing payment outcomes. Weighted A/B routing becomes appropriate only after the startup has defined the business evaluation: fraud capture, false-positive rate, approval rate, review load, issuer or merchant strata, and delayed-label joining. Seldon provides traffic allocation, not that outcome-analysis workflow.

Feature-store integration remains an application architecture decision. Seldon documents components including data processors and routers, but the supplied material does not identify a built-in Feast, Redis, or other feature-store connector for the Seldon–Triton configuration.[22] The lowest-latency design retrieves online features at the gateway or a dedicated processor colocated with the store, then sends a compact feature vector into Triton. Putting remote retrieval inside a synchronous Python backend is feasible, but makes network, serialization, timeout, and retry behavior part of model p99.

The cost comparison must likewise be empirical. Triton can provide per-GPU utilization, power, and memory metrics during benchmark runs.[15][10] Combine those measurements with actual CPU/GPU node-hours, warm spare capacity, Seldon licensing or support where chosen, feature infrastructure, telemetry, storage, and egress. Do not use the DGX-1 FIL throughput to calculate cost per million because it is not the target ensemble or node type.

Derived — In Triton’s text-recognition tutorial, dynamic batching plus multiple model instances increased throughput from 975.048 to 4,134.09 inferences/s (**324.0%**) and reduced p99 from 34.241 to 8.563 ms (**75.0%**). This quantifies why batching and instance-count tuning must be tested jointly rather than treated as independent optimizations.[57]

For an incident rollback, retain the stable Pipeline and its numerically versioned Triton component artifacts, then use the Seldon Experiment traffic split to return production traffic to the stable model or pipeline; Triton model repositories organize each model version in a numerically named directory and apply a version policy to the versions made available. For data drift, deploy and test a Seldon Alibi-detect drift detector separately from the classifier, then join it into the Pipeline and alert on its output. This produces native Seldon detector components, while feature freshness and feature-store availability remain separate operational signals.[22][53][59]

Initial deployment effort is concentrated in Kubernetes, Seldon Core 2, Triton repositories, per-component `config.pbtxt`, model formats, and the model DAG. Ongoing maintenance is concentrated in artifact and backend compatibility, capacity tuning, detector baselines, experiment cohorts, and stable-artifact retention for rollback.[22][54][55][17]

Seldon defines inference lag as the difference between incoming and outgoing requests during a period. Its autoscaling threshold is shared by models hosted on the same Server, so components with different saturation behavior may need separate Servers or conservatively calibrated thresholds.[153]

Release and experiment on an immutable complete ensemble version, rather than independently assigning tree and neural component variants. This preserves a tested score-combiner, feature-schema, threshold, and calibration contract during rollback.

### Ray Serve

Ray Serve treats the ensemble primarily as a programmable distributed application. A deployment can wrap a model or arbitrary Python business logic; multiple deployments can be composed into a pipeline or ensemble, and preprocessing, inference, and postprocessing can be independently configured and scaled.[2][3][27] This maps naturally to a fraud architecture with a CPU feature/gateway stage, a CPU XGBoost or LightGBM stage, a GPU neural stage, and an aggregator.

Ray’s key technical advantage is heterogeneous resource allocation at the deployment level. Different deployments can reserve CPUs and GPUs, use different machines, and be independently scaled. Its documentation specifically describes model composition in which calls across heterogeneous compute are written like function calls, while Ray schedules resources across the cluster.[2][27] It also documents fractional GPU allocation, which can improve economics when the neural network is too small to justify one exclusive GPU per replica.[3][27]

A sound Ray graph fans out to independent branches concurrently. A `DeploymentHandle` returns a future-like `DeploymentResponse`, which can be awaited or passed to downstream deployments; Ray explicitly warns against calling `.result()` inside a deployment because it blocks that deployment, whereas `await` lets it process other requests while waiting.[2] This implementation detail matters: an otherwise fast neural model can be hidden behind a blocking aggregator that serializes the tree and neural branches.

Ray’s own composition guidance warns that every deployment has different latency and concurrency requirements and that autoscaling a composed application requires experimentation.[32] Its illustrative example showed exactly why: downstream mock services held 200 ms and 100 ms latency while a single Driver deployment rose from 230 ms to 400 ms at 100 Locust users, indicating the orchestration stage itself had become the bottleneck.[32] In another test, scaling Driver replicas as well as downstream deployments kept application latency consistent while HeavyLoad reached more than 90 replicas and LightLoad 47.[32]

In Ray Serve’s published 100-user composition test, latency rose from 230 ms to 400 ms when HeavyLoad reached only 65 replicas. A revised configuration exceeded 90 HeavyLoad replicas and reached 47 LightLoad replicas with a three-second ingress upscale delay, keeping latency consistent as traffic increased.[32]

**Conclusion — Ray Serve is strongest when independent scaling of the tree, neural, preprocessing, and aggregation stages is more valuable than a single inference-server DAG.** It gives fine-grained resource and code control, but the aggregator is a first-class production bottleneck that must be benchmarked and scaled like a model stage.[2][32]

Ray exposes measurement rather than a workload-applicable latency number. It publishes Prometheus-format HTTP and gRPC end-to-end latency histograms from proxy receipt to response sent, alongside request and error counters.[13][29] Default histogram buckets span 1 ms through 600,000 ms; Ray cautions that sparse bucket coverage can make percentile calculations inaccurate and recommends setting boundaries near the expected latency distribution. Changes require restarting Serve actors.[29][13]

An illustrative, unmeasured Ray Serve model estimates **22–45 ms p95** and **40–85 ms p99** for the composed serving path; it does not establish latency for the requested fraud workload.[3][30]

For this fraud service, configure fine-grained buckets around the internal SLO—for example, below 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, and 100 ms if that is the expected range—then calculate p95/p99 separately by endpoint and model version. Use router metrics to separate queue time from inference time: Ray exposes queued queries, requests in flight, and router fulfillment time.[29] Its batch metrics separately report batch wait, batch execution, queue length, utilization, actual batch size, and processed batches.[29]

The published Ray DLRM result is encouraging but non-transferable. Anyscale reported that, with HAProxy ingress and inter-deployment gRPC, a two-stage DLRM recommendation pipeline rose from 490 to 1,573 QPS while p99 fell 75%.[30] The benchmark used a CPU ingress deployment and GPU ranker with batched neural inference.[30] It does not include XGBoost or LightGBM, online fraud features, the client’s geographic topology, or a 5,787-TPS burst.

Ray’s own documentation explains why direct extrapolation would be unsafe. Its default Python HTTP/gRPC proxy can become a high-concurrency bottleneck, increasing per-request latency and capping throughput independently of healthy replicas; HAProxy can replace it but must be available on every node running a Serve proxy.[31][30] Inter-deployment gRPC is described as most beneficial for high-throughput payloads below about 1 MB because it bypasses Ray’s object store, but it is discouraged for payloads above about 1 MB and certain chained payloads above 100 KB. Fraud vectors are normally small enough to be candidates for this path, but embeddings, device graphs, or enriched payloads should be measured rather than assumed.[31]

Ray’s burst controls require explicit tuning. By default, `max_ongoing_requests` is 5 per deployment replica, changed from 100 in Ray 2.32.0. Set too low, it throttles throughput as requests queue at the proxy; set too high, it can produce imbalanced routing and very high tail latency during scale-up because existing replicas receive too much traffic before new replicas start.[32][31] The default autoscaling target is two ongoing requests per replica, with a 30-second default scale-up delay and a 10-second metrics interval.[32]

Ray Serve’s documented autoscaling defaults also include a **30-second look-back window**, alongside the **30-second scale-up delay** and **10-second metrics interval**.[32]

For burstier traffic, Ray recommends lowering `upscale_delay_s`, increasing `upscaling_factor`, ensuring `metrics_interval_s` is no greater than `upscale_delay_s`, and lowering `max_ongoing_requests` where it is too high relative to the target.[32] These levers improve reaction time; they do not create immediate warm capacity. Ray also supports predictive scaling based on historical patterns or forecasts, which is appropriate for known sale windows or salary-day peaks.[32]

Once queues grow, Ray recommends load shedding rather than allowing tail latency and system instability to escalate indefinitely. `max_queued_requests` caps requests at each DeploymentHandle including the proxy; excess HTTP requests default to 503, or can use 429 with a `Retry-After` header. gRPC returns `RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED`.[33] In payment authorization, this should be paired with an explicit fallback policy: route to a deterministic rule set, invoke a lower-cost backup model, or put the payment into a review/retry state. Blind client retries can turn overload into a retry storm.

Conclusion — At 5,787 TPS, Ray autoscaling must be treated as follow-on capacity, while pre-warmed replicas and bounded queues protect the p99 path. Its controls make overload visible and manageable, but they do not prove that the configured graph sustains the requested burst.[32][33]

Ray’s documented current pattern is programmable variant routing. Ludwig’s Ray Serve example presents 80%/20% routing to separate v1 and v2 deployments, and a Ray tutorial implements a 20% canary through a router’s random draw. The earlier Ray forum discussion said then-current traffic splitting was being deprecated in favour of rolling upgrades and customizable pipelines.[60][61][34] Ray’s production configuration can update `user_config` without restarting replicas, including model weights, versions, traffic-splitting percentages, feature flags, A/B tests, and hyperparameters, provided the application implements `reconfigure`.[62]

Judgment — **For fraud experimentation, use an external gateway or service mesh for deterministic cohort routing, or implement it in a lightweight Ray ingress deployment with a stable customer/card hash.** Do not use independent random assignment for repeated payments from the same entity when the experiment affects risk outcomes; it makes merchant and user experience harder to interpret. Ray’s programmable composition makes this possible, but the experiment-control logic remains the team’s responsibility.

Feature-store access is also application-owned. Ray’s XGBoost tutorial demonstrates loading artifacts from MLflow storage readable across clusters and packaging dependencies through a locked requirements file or pre-built image.[3] It does not supply an online feature-store integration pattern. The practical design is a feature client in the ingress/preprocessing deployment, with timeout, freshness, fallback, and schema-validation behavior explicit in code.

In Ray’s documented XGBoost deployment pattern, each replica loads its model and preprocessor once rather than loading them for every prediction.[3]

Ray Serve tree-model deployments need explicit CPU and OpenMP allocation: Ray can set `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` when CPU count is not specified.[149]

The Ray cold-start and cost story is operationally important. Ray exposes `ray_serve_replica_startup_latency_ms`, measured from replica creation to ready state, including node provisioning, runtime bootstrap such as package installation or image pull, actor scheduling, and constructor execution.[13] It flags starts exceeding 30 seconds by default, but that is a warning threshold, not a readiness guarantee.[29]

A Vannevar Labs Ray-on-EKS case showed the value of image specialization. The team replaced a 25 GB monolithic image with specialized 2–12 GB images and reported 50–90% lower loading time depending on image. It included model weights and Python environments in images, eliminating public-internet downloads during container initialization.[35] The same case used fractional GPUs—for example, one CPU and 0.2 GPU for an embedding deployment—and reported 45% lower inference costs after combining Ray Serve, Karpenter, specialized clusters, and fractional GPUs.[35]

Those results support a direction, not a per-million estimate. **Conclusion — Ray can lower mixed CPU/GPU waste when component utilization is uneven, but its cost advantage depends on artifact packaging, GPU occupancy, and cluster scheduling rather than on Ray Serve alone.**[35]

For a Ray Serve production incident, use RayService incremental-upgrade rollback while an upgrade is in progress; this rollback requires KubeRay v1.7.0 or later. Ray Serve’s composed deployments require deployment-level autoscaling experimentation because each deployment has different latency and concurrency requirements. Data-drift detection and feature-drift monitoring are external integrations in this configuration: collect scored feature distributions and prediction outcomes through the application and observability path, then evaluate them with a chosen detector.[63][32]

Initial Ray Serve effort is Python graph implementation, non-blocking fan-out, resource reservations, ingress, and autoscaling configuration. Ongoing maintenance is tuning each deployment’s queue, concurrency, batching, autoscaling, artifact image, and rollback path; the startup metric explicitly includes node provisioning, runtime bootstrap, image pull or package installation, actor scheduling, and constructor execution.[2][32][13]

Ray Serve can target accelerator nodes with labels and use spread placement or replicas-per-node controls to reduce contention. Hard label constraints can leave replicas pending indefinitely, making a separately planned GPU, CPU or rules-based fallback important.[148]

In Anyscale’s two-stage DLRM benchmark, the optimized configuration increased throughput by 1,083 QPS, from 490 to 1,573 QPS, or 3.210×.[30]

Vannevar Labs’ specialized 2–12 GB images were 52% to 92% smaller than its former 25 GB monolithic image, alongside its reported 50–90% loading-time reduction.[35]

Retrieve and validate online features once in the ingress or a dedicated feature deployment, then pass a compact immutable vector to both model branches. Repeating feature-store calls in downstream deployments can duplicate lookups and worsen p99.

Ray’s autoscaler is layered on the Ray Autoscaler: Serve requests replica actors from demand, Ray requests additional CPU or GPU nodes when it cannot place them, and the cloud provider then adds nodes. Ray documentation recommends setting `max_replicas` approximately 20% above estimated peak need and retaining nonzero `min_replicas` where additional scale-up tail latency is unacceptable. A manually configured autoscaler has a default `max_replicas` of one unless the team sets a higher value.[64]

When `num_replicas="auto"` is set, Ray Serve applies a default `max_replicas` of 100. With manually configured autoscaling without `num_replicas="auto"`, the default `max_replicas` is 1, so no scale-out occurs unless a higher maximum is set explicitly.[65] Ray 2.51 custom autoscaling policies can use custom emitted metrics or external metrics and triggers, at deployment or full-application scope.[66] Ray Serve can add replicas but does not provision GPU nodes: replicas remain pending when the cluster lacks GPUs, so burst scaling requires external node provisioning or pre-provisioned spare GPUs.[67]

### KServe on Kubernetes

KServe is Kubernetes-native serving infrastructure centered on the `InferenceService` custom resource. It can deploy one or multiple trained models on different serving runtimes and can also host custom model servers built with KServe primitives or tools such as BentoML. For a mixed-framework fraud ensemble, its natural pattern is multiple predictors joined by an `InferenceGraph`.[37]

InferenceGraph Ensemble nodes execute multiple steps in parallel and combine their responses.[36] That makes KServe suitable for a tree branch and neural branch that receive the same feature vector.

The important architectural limitation is business aggregation: the graph can coordinate calls, but the final fraud score, threshold decision, reason codes, and policy action need an explicit downstream predictor or transformer. This is useful rather than accidental: it keeps the ensemble combiner independently testable and versioned.

Judgment — **Use separate predictors for the tree and neural stages, then a lightweight custom aggregator for final score calibration and decision policy.** Avoid embedding business thresholds in a model-serving graph where risk, operations, and product teams cannot change or audit them independently.

KServe’s published numerical examples illustrate ingress and saturation behavior rather than the requested workload. An Alibaba Cloud KServe example using a Scikit-learn Iris service through NGINX ingress reported 727.3875 requests/s in a 120.0296-second test at concurrency 20, with p95 response time 0.0315 seconds and p99 0.0381 seconds.[39] The request path includes ingress and service behavior, not isolated model inference.

In KServe’s separate Iris benchmark, the raw Kubernetes Service path at **500 requests/s** recorded **1.713 ms p95** and **2.205 ms p99**; these are not measurements of the mixed-model fraud ensemble.[40]

KServe’s benchmark repository shows why that result cannot be multiplied to 5,787 TPS. With queue proxy and activator enabled, a one-replica Scikit-learn Iris service had p95/p99 of 4.929/5.642 ms at 500 requests/s, but 2.945/3.691 seconds at 1,000 requests/s, even though success was reported as 100%.[40] The raw Kubernetes Service path had lower p95/p99 through 500 requests/s, but at 1,000 requests/s it reported p95 16.669 seconds, p99 20.307 seconds, and 93.72% success.[40]

KServe’s benchmark repository warns that the Kubernetes version used in its historical KFServing/KServe tests had a CFS-throttling bug; the tests used simple Iris or Flowers models in environments with Knative and Istio.[40]

In KServe’s historical Knative-path Iris benchmark, p95/p99 were 6.992/7.615 ms at 5 requests/s and 6.483/6.801 ms at 50 requests/s.[40]

In the same benchmark’s Flowers workload with unlimited concurrency, p95/p99 were 116.116/117.298 ms at 1 request/s, 148.195/153.291 ms at 5 requests/s and 4.635/6.934 s at 10 requests/s; limiting concurrency to one changed the 10-requests/s result to 3.469/3.831 s.[40]

**Conclusion — KServe’s available examples show that tail latency can change abruptly at one-replica saturation, so the 5,787-TPS requirement cannot be inferred from the 727.3875-RPS Iris example.** A feature-enriched, mixed CPU/GPU ensemble must be capacity-tested at multiple replica counts and with the intended autoscaling mode.[39][40]

An illustrative, unmeasured KServe serving-path model estimates **25–50 ms p95** and **45–100 ms p99** in Standard mode, versus **35–70 ms p95** and **70–150 ms p99** with Knative’s queue proxy and activator on the path; neither range is a measured gateway-to-decision fraud result.[40][68]

KServe offers two main operating modes with different burst implications:

- **Standard or raw deployment mode:** KServe uses Kubernetes-native deployments and HPA. In this mode HPA can watch CPU or memory; KServe documents concurrency and RPS metrics only through Knative Pod Autoscaler. [41]

- **Serverless mode with Knative:** KServe uses queue proxy and activator behavior, enabling request/concurrency-aware scaling and scale-to-zero patterns. KServe’s benchmark authors state that queue proxy and activator add 2–3 ms overhead in their example, while providing KPA and smart load balancing. [40]

KServe’s documented KEDA example scales from one to five replicas at 50% CPU; KEDA also supports Prometheus and Graphite metrics, and a documented Prometheus example targets two concurrent requests.[154]

The benchmark authors found KPA reacted faster and performed better than HPA in their 1,000-RPS Iris and Flower comparisons, but the results are model-specific.[40] For fraud scoring, use Standard Deployment for the primary path: KServe identifies Standard Deployment as having no cold start, while Knative has cold-start latency. Keep a non-zero minimum replica count in each production region and use Knative’s traffic controls only for excess capacity or lower-priority shadow paths.[68]

KServe’s ModelMesh is intended for dense fleets of smaller models; its shared-runtime approach changes model loading and runtime sharing relative to separate inference services.[155][68]

KServe’s canary mechanism is more explicit than Ray’s documented current routing path. KServe automatically tracks the last revision that completed rollout with 100% traffic. `canaryTrafficPercent` directs the stated percentage to the new revision, with the remainder to the last rolled-out revision.[42][69] In the documented 10% canary, both old and new revision pods run and traffic is split 90%/10%; a bad or unhealthy revision does not receive traffic, and rollback pins 100% to the previous healthy revision.[42][43]

InferenceGraph Splitters provide another form of weighted allocation. Their weights must sum to 100, and each request goes to exactly one target according to the defined probability distribution.[36] This supports exposure control; it does not perform fraud-outcome analysis, label attribution, or statistical decisioning.

Conclusion — KServe provides strong declarative traffic allocation for canary release and controlled variant exposure, but fraud A/B testing still needs external experiment metrics and delayed-label evaluation.[36][42][43]

KServe has a documented feature-store pattern that the other candidates do not match in the supplied material. A KServe Feast Transformer sits in front of prediction and retrieves online features before calling the predictor. The worked example includes a Feast service, Redis online store, Transformer configuration, and separate images for Feast and Transformer components.[44] That is a useful implementation template for tabular fraud models, but it creates a larger request-path and operational surface.

KServe’s `StorageContainer` manages model storage, not retrieval of online inference features.[155][37]

Feature-store overhead has two forms. First is runtime overhead: request parsing, entity-key extraction, network call, serialization, online-store lookup, and feature assembly. Second is engineering overhead: feature references, schema evolution, image builds, credentials, observability, and co-location. Gojek’s Feast account explains why this is worth doing: before Feast, engineers had to set up database clusters, ingestion jobs, and monitoring per model, project, environment, and region; feature transforms had to be rewritten from Python for online serving, consuming extra time and creating training-serving skew.[8]

Judgment — **For this workload, a standardized feature-store interface is more important than a platform-native connector.** The startup should use one versioned feature contract across training, offline validation, and online scoring; KServe’s Feast Transformer is attractive when the company already runs Feast, while the other platforms can call the same service through application code.

KServe does not supply a defensible mixed CPU/GPU cost per million without a workload benchmark. The formula is the same as for the other candidates, but its inputs differ by mode: Standard mode carries warm Deployment replicas and HPA behavior; Knative mode adds queueing/activator components, can retain minimum instances, and may incur cold-scale events if configured to zero. The most meaningful comparison is therefore a measured bill under a fixed 579-TPS baseline and 5,787-TPS burst profile.

Derived — In KServe’s one-replica Iris queue-proxy benchmark, doubling offered load from 500 to 1,000 requests/s increased p99 from 5.642 ms to 3.691 s, a **654.2×** increase.[40]

Derived — In the same Iris workload through the raw Kubernetes Service path, raising load from 500 to 1,000 requests/s increased p99 from 2.205 ms to 20.307 s (**9,210×**) and reduced success rate from 100% to 93.72%, a **6.28-percentage-point** decrease.[40]

In serverless mode, KServe rolls a canary back by setting `canaryTrafficPercent` to 0, returning 100% of traffic to the previous good model revision and 0% to the current model revision; Standard mode does not support canary deployment and requires another weighted traffic mechanism. KServe’s documented Feast Transformer supplies feature retrieval, not drift detection; monitor feature and score distributions with external observability and detector tooling.[43][70][44]

Standard-mode KServe can use Gateway API traffic splitting even though it lacks KServe’s native Knative canary rollout; the two routing mechanisms should not be treated as the same revision-rollback feature.[68]

Initial KServe effort is Kubernetes and InferenceService setup, framework-specific predictors, InferenceGraph aggregation logic, and—when used—the Feast, Redis, Transformer configuration, and separate images. Ongoing maintenance is HPA or Knative autoscaling, revision and canary management, feature-store operations, graph changes, and external drift-monitoring operations.[36][44][41]

At 1,000 requests/s in KServe’s Iris benchmark, the raw Kubernetes-Service path had a p99 5.502× the queue-proxy/activator path, an absolute p99 gap of 16.616 seconds.[40]

KServe’s published graph example directly matches this model shape: feature enrichment precedes an Ensemble that runs XGBoost, a deep-learning model, and a rule engine in parallel, then a weighted-average service produces the final result. Its Predictor runtime list includes Triton, XGBoost, and LightGBM; Transformer and Predictor can scale independently when preprocessing is CPU-intensive and inference is GPU-intensive.[71]

KServe lists Triton Inference Server, XGBoost, and LightGBM among its built-in Serving Runtimes.[72] KServe does not provision GPU nodes itself, so GPU capacity requires a node autoscaler.[73] The KServe Controller Manager supports KEDA alongside HPA, enabling GPU-metric-based custom scaling configurations using components such as DCGM Exporter, Prometheus Adapter, and KEDA.[72]

KServe Transformer and Predictor components can scale independently. This supports a CPU-intensive preprocessing Transformer and a GPU-intensive Predictor, each with its own scaling policy.[71]

KServe supports automatic request batching. Through its Knative integration, it also supports autoscaling from CPU utilization, requests per second, or concurrency, plus scale-to-zero with rapid activation.[74]

KServe lists an XGBoost ModelServer for saved `.bst`, `.json`, and `.ubj` models and a LightGBM ModelServer for saved LightGBM `.bst` models; both expose HTTP v1/v2 and gRPC v2 support.[37]

KServe documents ML Monitoring capabilities for drift detection, outlier detection, and explainability. Configure the drift detector with the fraud feature baseline and score baseline, route its alerts to the incident process, and use the existing canary rollback path when drift coincides with decision-quality degradation.[75][42][43]

KServe’s current platform description adds three operational capabilities to the ensemble design: standardized inference protocols across frameworks; inference-graph Sequence, Switch, Ensemble, and Splitter nodes; and serverless CPU/GPU inference for XGBoost, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and pluggable custom runtimes. KServe also identifies S3, GCS, Azure, HuggingFace, and PVC as model-storage options.[76][75]

KServe provides production monitoring with payload logging and outlier, adversarial, and drift detection through integrations with AI Fairness 360 and the Adversarial Robustness Toolbox.

### BentoML

BentoML is application-oriented model-serving software. A Service is a class-based unit that can perform preprocessing, inference, or other logic. Services can depend on one another through `bentoml.depends()`, which lets one Service call another’s API methods as if they were local functions.[46] Distributed Services run in separate containers, and BentoML handles service discovery, routing, serialization, and deserialization.[45]

This supports a clean mixed-framework ensemble:

- an XGBoost or LightGBM Service on CPU;
- a neural-inference Service on GPU;
- a feature/preprocessing Service that calls the online store and builds model inputs; and
- an aggregator Service that invokes the branches concurrently where possible, then returns the final fraud decision.

BentoML supports dependent Services through `bentoml.depends` and parallel calls through `asyncio.gather`, alongside resource declarations and independent replicas for CPU/GPU separation.[156][45][157]

BentoML directly supports saving XGBoost and LightGBM model instances in its model store and wrapping them as Services. Model saves receive generated version tags; LightGBM tags use the `name:version` form.[47][48] BentoML also documents support for major frameworks including PyTorch, TensorFlow, XGBoost, and LightGBM, and describes serving models from different frameworks through one endpoint with combined predictions.[77]

BentoML’s distributed-Service documentation explicitly calls out CPU/GPU pipelining, independent Service scaling on different instances, asymmetric GPU requirements, and sequential, parallel, or composed workflows.[45] This is a practical fit where a tree model does not need expensive GPU capacity but the neural model does.

The principal performance caveat is inter-Service orchestration. BentoML says a synchronous endpoint in one Service calling a batchable endpoint in another has default concurrency one; to allow concurrent calls and batching, set `threads=N` on the Service decorator.[49] That means a superficially parallel ensemble can unintentionally serialize behind a single intermediary worker.

**Conclusion — BentoML’s decomposition gives good control over mixed CPU/GPU placement, but the startup must explicitly configure concurrency and benchmark each Service hop.** Otherwise the aggregation or feature Service can become the limiting stage even when model inference is fast.[45][49]

An illustrative, unmeasured BentoML model estimates **20–45 ms p95** and **35–80 ms p99** for in-process or colocated Services; separating components remotely adds a network hop and is not covered by that estimate.[45][158]

BentoML adaptive batching dynamically groups requests and adjusts batch size and window based on traffic. At high traffic it tends to form larger, more frequent batches; at low traffic it prioritizes lower latency with smaller batches.[49] It is disabled by default and must be enabled per endpoint with `batchable=True`.[49]

In BentoCloud’s documented autoscaling example, two replicas configured for concurrency 32 do not scale until simultaneous requests exceed 64; concurrency therefore affects the scaling trigger as well as request handling.[159]

This is useful for a GPU neural stage, but it is a latency-throughput trade-off rather than proof of 5,787-TPS capacity. If a Service cannot process requests quickly enough and exceeds `max_latency_ms`, BentoML returns HTTP 503; the documented remedies are increasing the latency bound or adding resources such as memory or CPUs.[49] Increasing the limit may reduce error responses but worsens authorization delay; it is not a fix for insufficient warm capacity.

With BentoCloud scale-to-zero, the request that triggers scaling waits in a queue while a replica starts, so its response includes startup delay.[159]

A study of a BentoML system reported that adaptive batching improved throughput under moderate-to-high traffic, while emphasizing sensitivity to configuration parameters and recommending workload-specific tuning.[50] That study used a RoBERTa Base sentiment-analysis model on a server with two Xeon Gold 6230 CPUs, eight Quadro RTX 8000 GPUs, and 693 GB RAM, with only one GPU used.[50] Its traffic and model are not comparable to payment fraud inference.

Conclusion — Adaptive batching is a valuable GPU-efficiency tool for BentoML, but it should be applied selectively to the neural branch and tuned against the end-to-end p99, not assumed to meet the 5,787-TPS burst.[49][50]

BentoML’s versioning strength is reproducible packaging. It can package model environments, dependencies, and versions together and generate reproducible Docker images.[78] Its build configuration uses model tags and locks package versions and dependency graphs into exact-version requirements.[79] This is useful when tree and neural dependencies otherwise create Python, CUDA, and ABI conflicts.

For variant routing, BentoCloud documents Canary Deployments from BentoML 1.4.17 onward. It can allocate traffic to selected Bento versions—for example 10% to one version, 30% to another, and the remaining 60% to the main version—then promote a version by raising its traffic share to 100%.[4] For self-managed Kubernetes, a third-party comparison describes BentoML plus Yatai as relying on Ingress-level traffic splitting across separate BentoDeployments, with no operator-native promotion or rollback workflow.[51] This distinction matters: BentoCloud’s canary controls should not be assumed to exist unchanged in a self-managed BentoML deployment.

BentoCloud Gateways support cross-region routing policies and failover, as well as bursting from committed to elastic GPU capacity.[150]

Feature-store integration is application-level. BentoML can declare an external deployed Service as a dependency either by deployment name or URL, and BentoML 1.4 extends `bentoml.depends()` to external deployments while abstracting network communication and serialization.[45][52] That makes a Feast, Redis-backed feature API, or internal feature gateway easy to invoke, but it does not remove the need to design timeout, caching, feature freshness, schema checks, or failure handling.

BentoML cold-start mitigation is concrete. Version 1.4 downloads models during image build rather than at Service startup, then caches and mounts them into containers so they are available when the Service starts. BentoML says this reduces cold-start time and improves scaling performance.[52] It also supports parallel safetensors loading, particularly for large models.[52] Neither statement provides a millisecond startup result for this fraud ensemble.

BentoCloud documents keeping critical Services warm and using `/readyz` during warming.[159]

BentoML model references declared at class scope enable model download during the build, followed by caching and mounting at service startup.[160]

Scheduling, image pull, process initialization, and GPU initialization can still contribute to a new BentoML deployment’s cold-start latency.[160]

Conclusion — BentoML is attractive where the team values reproducible, framework-specific packaging and Python-first orchestration, especially if it can keep model weights and dependencies in the image. It is less turnkey for self-managed production traffic control and feature serving than its service code can initially suggest.[78][52][51]

BentoCloud, rather than self-managed BentoML, keeps Deployment revisions and permits rollback to any retained revision; it can run multiple Bento versions simultaneously, shift traffic gradually, roll back rapidly, and monitor real-time performance across deployed versions. Self-managed BentoML can use Yatai’s Kubernetes rolling deployments, standard health endpoints, and stored Bento-service versions to restore an earlier version. Data-drift detection is an external integration for both deployment forms.[80][4][81]

Initial self-managed BentoML effort is Service composition, image and dependency packaging, CPU/GPU placement, concurrency configuration, Kubernetes deployment, and surrounding traffic control. Ongoing maintenance is Service-hop performance, adaptive-batching configuration, dependency and model-version retention, external feature and drift services, and Kubernetes rollout operations. BentoCloud changes this operating model by providing managed canary deployment and revision controls, and its managed-product charges must remain separate from self-managed Kubernetes costs.[45][78][49][4]

A single Bento service can make the ensemble artifact and rollback atomic, but couples CPU and GPU scaling. Splitting CPU tree and GPU neural services improves resource utilization while requiring explicit compatibility testing, distributed rollback coordination, and traffic control.

The platforms divide into two architectural families. Seldon Core with Triton puts heterogeneous model execution into an inference-server DAG and adds pipeline/experiment control; this is the most direct documented path for XGBoost or LightGBM plus neural networks on mixed CPU/GPU hardware. Ray Serve and BentoML instead make the ensemble a composed application of independently scalable Services or deployments, giving more flexibility but exposing aggregation and inter-Service behavior as tail-latency risks. KServe uses Kubernetes resources and InferenceGraph composition, with particularly explicit canary controls and a documented Feast Transformer pattern.[1][17][23][24][2][36][45][46]

**Conclusion — Choose Seldon Core with Triton as the first benchmark candidate when the immediate requirement is the lowest-complexity mixed-framework serving path; choose Ray Serve when independent stage scaling and fractional heterogeneous resources are decisive; choose KServe when Kubernetes-native canary control and Feast integration dominate; and choose BentoML when reproducible Python packaging and service-level composition outweigh self-managed traffic-control work.** All four must be tested under the common 579-TPS baseline and 5,787-TPS burst with the complete feature path, because their published examples do not establish the client’s p95, p99, throughput, or cost per million predictions.[24][12][32][39][50]

Conditional ranking — For a team prioritizing customizable CPU/GPU ensemble orchestration and explicit burst scaling over minimal platform operations, the conditional order is **Ray Serve, BentoML, KServe, then Seldon Core with Triton**. Ray Serve independently configures and scales ensemble stages with CPU/GPU reservations; BentoML independently scales distributed CPU/GPU Services; KServe Standard Deployment uses standard Kubernetes resources for production; and Seldon Core 2 pipelines support Triton while Triton FIL serves XGBoost and LightGBM.[2][45][68][22][23]

BentoML reduces initial model-packaging work relative to a more elaborate Kubernetes serving stack, whereas KServe becomes more attractive when the team already operates Kubernetes-native model-serving infrastructure. BentoML supports straightforward single-Service deployment, reproducible Docker images, and local development before Docker or Kubernetes deployment; KServe Standard Deployment uses standard Kubernetes resources and is recommended for most production environments.[45][78][68]

Acceptance testing should include warm steady state, sustained 5,787-TPS burst while autoscaling, cold deployment or scale-from-zero behavior, and a production rollback drill; report rejected, queued, and incorrect responses as well as latency. Although the workload is shared across three countries, monitor latency, feature freshness, drift, and fraud outcomes by country, payment instrument, merchant segment, and deployment region because these slices can differ materially.

## Production incidents, monitoring, and engineering effort

A fraud-serving platform is production-ready only when it can move traffic away from a bad model without creating a second incident, expose the signals needed to detect a bad decision regime, and keep those controls operable as models, frameworks, and infrastructure change. Deployment cold start is separate from steady-state inference latency.[13][55][52] The relevant distinction is between **serving telemetry**—availability, latency, queueing, resource use—and **model monitoring**—changes in features, scores, and, when labels arrive, fraud outcomes.

Judgment — For this workload, every platform should retain a known-good, already-warmed version; send model and feature telemetry to a durable store; and make rollback a traffic-routing action first, not an artifact mutation. Replacing model files during an authorization-path incident adds avoidable failure modes.

### Seldon Core with Triton Inference Server

Seldon Core 2 provides the outer control plane for composable serving and traffic management. Its Models can represent core models, feature transformations, drift detectors, outlier detectors, explainers, and adversarial detectors; those components can be joined into a Pipeline. This supports an ensemble arrangement in which preprocessing, a gradient-boosting score, a neural-network score, an aggregator, and monitoring branches are separately deployable components.[53][51] Seldon also exposes models through an internal Envoy gateway.[82]

For a production incident, Seldon Core 2 Experiments can split HTTP traffic between models or inference pipelines and can mirror a percentage of requests to a model or pipeline without changing the response returned to the user. This enables a practical rollback sequence: first change the live traffic split back to a retained stable pipeline; second, preserve the failed version and its logs for diagnosis; third, repair or replace the failed candidate off the authorization path.[22][82] Seldon’s documented traffic controls are therefore useful for both canary deployment and shadow evaluation, rather than only for a final binary production cutover.[22][51]

Triton supplies the model-artifact lifecycle below that routing layer. Each Triton model can have multiple numerically named version directories, and its version policy can expose all versions, a specified number of latest versions, or an explicit list; by default it exposes the latest one version. Adding or removing version directories can change the version used by later requests.[55][59] This makes version retention feasible, but it is not by itself a safe incident-routing mechanism: removing a version in `POLL` mode allows in-flight requests to finish but causes new requests for that removed version to fail.[83][84]

Triton’s Model Repository Index reports each model’s state and reason; operators can require `ready=true` before routing payment traffic to a newly loaded version.[161][162]

Triton has three model-control modes: `NONE`, `EXPLICIT`, and `POLL`. `NONE`, the default, loads repository models at startup and ignores later repository changes. `EXPLICIT` requires model load and unload actions through Triton’s model-control protocol; reloading an already-loaded model requires an unload before load. `POLL` watches repository changes and can replace a successfully reloaded model without loss of availability, while retaining the existing model if reload fails.[84] Triton explicitly advises against `POLL` in production because polling can observe a partially updated repository state.[83][84]

Triton Load/reload accepts model-configuration overrides, while Unload can remove dependent ensemble models. Model-control actions are per-model rather than atomic across an ensemble, and backend-library changes require unloading and reloading dependent models.[84][161]

**For a fraud incident, use Seldon traffic routing to return to a known-good pipeline, then use Triton `EXPLICIT` model management for artifact lifecycle; do not make unsynchronised `POLL` repository mutation the rollback mechanism.** This combines Seldon’s controlled traffic allocation with Triton’s explicit control APIs and avoids the documented failure mode in which new requests to a removed version fail.[22][84][83]

Triton reduces update interruption in some cases by loading new models in the background while continuing to serve existing models; its model-load thread count defaults to four.[83][84] It also supports model warmup: Triton does not mark a warmed model ready until configured warmup requests have completed, avoiding the slow first requests that can result from deferred backend initialization.

The trade-off is that warmup makes updates less responsive, and its effect depends on the backend.[55] ONNX model loading can be significantly slower when TensorRT optimization is enabled, for which NVIDIA also recommends model warmup.[26][85] A remote Triton model repository is copied to temporary local storage by default and is deleted at server shutdown; Triton does not persistently cache cloud-storage files unless the operator implements caching through a repository agent.[59]

Judgment — For a continuously available fraud scorer, maintain a non-zero floor of warmed replicas and pre-stage both the stable and candidate artifacts locally or in node-adjacent storage. Scale-to-zero and artifact download at the moment of a payment burst optimize idle cost at the expense of an authorization-path latency risk.

Seldon’s data-science monitoring path is comparatively integrated. Core 2 treats saved Alibi Detect drift models as Models, and its examples construct a TensorFlow predictor, an SKLearn feature transformation, and Alibi Detect drift and outlier detectors before composing the components into a Pipeline.[53][86] Seldon recommends running drift detectors asynchronously rather than on the synchronous output path; its example reads drift alerts from the drift model’s Kafka topic.[86] Core 2 also logs the input and output of every pipeline step and the pipeline itself to Kafka by default, although direct REST or gRPC model requests are not automatically logged unless they travel through a Pipeline.[87]

Alibi Detect defines expected run-time (ERT) as the average number of time steps before a false drift detection when drift is absent; increasing ERT reduces false alarms but generally delays detection.[86][163]

Alibi Detect calibrates drift-detection thresholds with bootstrap simulations, which can be computationally expensive; GPU execution can reduce that calibration cost.[86][163]

Seldon exposes inference metrics for Prometheus scraping and describes observability across data pipelines, models, and deployment environments.[82][88] Those signals should be joined with pipeline payload records, feature-store records, and subsequent payment outcomes.

A feature-distribution shift is useful when chargeback or manual-review labels are delayed, but it is a proxy for performance decay, not proof of it. When labels arrive, fraud teams can measure model quality and business outcomes directly; a data-quality failure can also resemble drift and should be investigated before retraining or changing a production model.[89]

Judgment — Establish a country-, payment-rail-, merchant-segment-, and customer-tenure-specific reference baseline rather than one global baseline across India, Indonesia, and the Philippines. Monitor feature missingness and freshness first, then feature distributions, model-score distributions, approval/decline rates, and delayed fraud outcomes. A drift alert should create an investigation or a segment fallback decision; it should not automatically retrain or automatically roll back a model.

Initial deployment effort is substantial because Seldon and Triton each impose configuration work. Seldon Core 2 can be installed with Docker Compose or Kubernetes, but the production framework is Kubernetes-native.[82][88][90] Every Triton model requires a configuration containing platform or backend, maximum batch size, and input and output tensors.[55] Triton can create instances on GPU or CPU, including CPU execution when GPUs are available, allowing the tree-model component and neural component to be placed separately.[55] The model repository can be local, Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, or Azure Storage.[59]

Seldon’s deployment graph can place stages in separate pods for independent CPU/GPU scaling; a single-pod graph reduces hops and requires one Istio sidecar.[164]

The multi-framework cost appears in environment compatibility. NVIDIA’s example requires the Treelite version used to serialize a model to match the version inside the Triton container. Python preprocessing or postprocessing may require a custom Python backend stub when its required Python version differs from the Triton container, with `conda-pack` used to capture the environment in that example.[17] Alibi Detect’s TensorFlow, PyTorch, and KeOps backends are not installed by default.[91] Capacity planning also remains an operating responsibility: Seldon recommends running each replica 10–20% below saturation throughput to preserve latency and absorb traffic while server and model replicas start.[12]

Seldon/Triton requires x86/SSE support for some dependencies; Triton FIL requires a Pascal-or-newer NVIDIA GPU and identifies T4, V100 and A100 hardware.[151][146]

Initial Seldon–Triton setup also requires GPU drivers and toolkit, runtime images, and repository credentials; those credentials and GPU infrastructure remain ongoing maintenance responsibilities.[151][146][59][84]

A February 2024 user report for a `TRITON_SERVER` deployment on Kubernetes Cluster Version 27.7.1, using Seldon Core executor and operator images version 1.17.1, described Triton metrics that could not be exposed because of an apparent `/metrics` and `/prometheus` endpoint mismatch; the reporter worked around it with a custom deployment.[92] Seldon Core 2 documentation separately states that its metrics are Prometheus-scrapable.[92][82] These are different versions and configurations, so the practical requirement is to test the exact Seldon–Triton metrics path in the production chart before relying on it during an incident.

The result is a flexible but operationally broad stack: Seldon handles pipelines, experiments, Kafka data flows, and monitoring components, while Triton adds repository policy, model control, warmup, backend, CPU/GPU placement, and runtime-compatibility obligations. That effort is justified when the ensemble needs independently managed framework components and explicit hardware placement; it is a poor fit for a team unable to own those controls continuously.[90][55][17]

Seldon Core 2 multi-model serving can overcommit a shared server: it retains highly used models in memory, evicts others to disk by least-recently-used order, and reloads an evicted model before forwarding a request. The default `SELDON_OVERCOMMIT_PERCENTAGE` is 10%, permitting registered model memory up to `MEMORY_REQUEST × 1.1`; model and server memory requirements are user-supplied.[93]

For an evicted model, Seldon caches the artifact on disk rather than fetching it remotely, but documents a request-time reload latency lower bound of approximately 100 ms. For a payment authorization path, do not overcommit the stable ensemble, its rollback target, or any high-traffic country/rail segment: this mechanism creates a request-triggered cold reload even though the model remains registered and ready.[93]

On Kubernetes, Seldon documents that its CLI cannot modify underlying manifests and does not recommend the CLI for control-plane operations. Manage production pipeline, experiment, resource, and rollback configuration through Kubernetes manifests and the Kubernetes control plane.[94]

Seldon Core 2 Pipeline routing supports weighted splits, conditional routing based on request headers, and shadow mode that sends a copy of a request to a new model without affecting the response; this flexibility requires more YAML.[51]

Seldon Core inference graphs can include custom transformations, a router, and a combiner for an in-deployment ensemble. When Triton Server or MLServer is used, graph transformations are unavailable; keep required feature transformations in a separately served component or preprocessing path rather than expecting this graph capability in the Triton deployment.[74]

In Seldon’s CIFAR-10 image-classification example, the `cifar10-drift` model is outside the output path and drift alerts are read from that model’s Kafka topic.[86]

Treat the feature schema, preprocessing, tree model, neural model, score combiner, and routing configuration as one compatibility unit for rollback. Log only privacy-controlled fraud features and apply PCI/PII retention and access controls to Kafka payloads and monitoring records.

Treat each Triton numeric version directory as an immutable artifact and promote a complete immutable model repository or image beside the current instance. Validate it before a small traffic shift, and retain the prior image, repository version, deployment manifest, model configuration, and performance evidence because rollback must restore both the artifact and its configuration.[95]

Seldon Core 2 has used the Business Source License 1.1 since January 2024; production use outside non-profit education requires a paid licence. This licence and its sales-quoted price are recurring platform-selection inputs in addition to Kubernetes and GPU operations.[73]

### Ray Serve

Ray Serve’s principal strength is programmable composition. It models each stage as a Python deployment and lets teams express chaining, parallel fan-out, ensembles, and dynamic dispatch in Python rather than hand-written YAML. For a fraud ensemble, a request router can concurrently invoke an XGBoost or LightGBM deployment and a neural deployment, then send their scores to an aggregation deployment.[96] Inter-deployment calls should use `await`, not `.result()`, because `.result()` blocks the deployment while the remote call finishes.[2]

Ray Serve provides rolling-update and lightweight-update mechanisms, but its rollback behavior depends on what changed. A production Serve configuration is deployed with `serve deploy`, which is idempotent: the running application honors the latest successfully deployed configuration.[97][62] Lightweight changes—including replica counts, autoscaling configuration, `user_config`, request limits, shutdown settings, and health-check settings—can modify running replicas without restarting them.[97] `user_config` can be applied to existing and future replicas without restarting a replica; Ray explicitly lists changing model weights or versions, traffic splits, feature flags, A/B tests, and hyperparameters as uses, provided the deployment implements `reconfigure`.[62]

Changes to `ray_actor_options`, placement-group settings, application `import_path`, or `runtime_env` restart replicas or all deployments.[97] For large code changes, Ray recommends deploying the updated application to a new Ray cluster and then switching traffic from the old cluster, rather than changing graph import paths and runtime environments in place.[97] That distinction matters for incident procedures: model weights may be reconfigured in place, but dependency, CUDA, Python, or serving-runtime failures should be isolated by a separate-cluster deployment.

KubeRay RayService provides the clearest documented rollback route for cluster-level changes. An incremental upgrade requires Gateway API CRDs, a Gateway controller, a `GatewayClass`, and the Ray Autoscaler enabled in the RayCluster specification.[63] During the upgrade, KubeRay creates a pending cluster at 0% target capacity, initially routes all traffic to the active cluster, waits for the pending pods to become ready, and then shifts traffic in configured increments. The documentation warns that worker creation can temporarily reduce requests per second.[63]

KubeRay v1.7.0 or later supports rollback during an ongoing incremental upgrade. Reverting to the original RayCluster specification cancels the upgrade, gradually shifts traffic back to the original cluster, restores it to 100% target capacity, reduces the pending cluster to 0%, and deletes it.[63] If a third specification is submitted during an upgrade, KubeRay first rolls back to the original known-good cluster before beginning a fresh upgrade, keeping the resource ceiling at 100% plus `maxSurgePercent` but extending migration time.[63] A `serveConfigV2` change, however, updates the running deployment in place and does not create a pending cluster or traffic migration.[63]

**For a Ray Serve model incident, restore the prior pinned Serve application configuration and model artifact through `serve deploy` or the applicable `serveConfigV2` update path, or switch the gateway to an already-running prior application.** `serve deploy` is idempotent and preserves the latest successfully deployed configuration; `user_config` can reconfigure running replicas for model weights, model versions, traffic splits, feature flags, A/B tests, and hyperparameters when the deployment implements `reconfigure`.[97][62] **Reserve RayService incremental rollback for a `rayClusterConfig` upgrade:** reverting to the original specification cancels that upgrade and gradually returns traffic to the original cluster, whereas an in-place `serveConfigV2` edit updates the running deployment without creating a pending cluster or traffic migration.[63] **For high-risk model-runtime or infrastructure changes, deploy the revised application to a new Ray cluster and switch traffic only after it is ready; use the already-running old cluster as the immediate gateway rollback target.** The trade-off is capacity: KubeRay’s older full-new-cluster strategy could require 200% of the original cluster resources, while incremental upgrades reduce duplication through staged capacity movement.[97][63]

Ray Serve supplies extensive serving telemetry. Prometheus-format metrics include successful and failed requests and end-to-end HTTP and gRPC latency histograms; the latency measurement runs from proxy receipt to response sent.[29] Routing metrics expose queued queries, ongoing replica requests, and router queue-wait time, with high queued queries indicating backpressure.[29] Autoscaling metrics expose target replicas, desired replicas, total queued plus in-flight requests, and target ongoing requests per replica.[13] Ray also exposes status through the CLI and dashboard, including application deployment states such as `DEPLOYING`, `RUNNING`, and `DEPLOY_FAILED`, and deployment states including `UPDATING`, `HEALTHY`, `UNHEALTHY`, and `DEPLOY_FAILED`.[13]

Ray’s lifecycle telemetry is particularly useful for new-version cold starts. `ray_serve_replica_startup_latency_ms` measures replica creation to readiness, including node provisioning, image pulling or package installation, Ray actor scheduling, and constructor execution; a separate histogram measures constructor-only initialization.[29][13] Ray logs a slow-startup warning after 30 seconds by default, but that threshold is an alert configuration, not a startup-time guarantee.[13] For multiplexed deployments, Ray exposes model-load and unload latency, the number of loaded models, model IDs, and cache-hit inputs.[13][29]

Ray Serve does not provide native data-drift detection. Its documentation describes it as not being a full ML platform and says it lacks model-lifecycle management and model-performance visualization.[27] It can export telemetry to Arize, but the integration requires adding Arize client code directly to the Serve deployment.

Arize can surface drift, data quality, and data consistency issues.[13][29] Ray itself does not provide native metric storage; users must operate or procure metrics storage, and Ray does not start Prometheus for them. For production, its documentation recommends placing Prometheus outside the Ray cluster so metrics remain available if the cluster is down.[98]

Judgment — Treat Ray telemetry as the service-health layer and add a separate model-observability pipeline. Log selected features, component scores, final score, decision, model version, and delayed labels to a governed analytical store; use a drift system for distribution and data-quality alarms, and use outcome monitoring for fraud recall, false-positive cost, manual-review rate, and approval impact.

Initial deployment can be comparatively fast for a Python team: Ray states that an application can move from a laptop to a Kubernetes cluster with minimal or no code changes.[27] Production, however, should package application code in a custom Docker image rather than a `runtime_env`.[33] KubeRay/RayService then adds a Ray cluster, a Serve controller, proxies, worker nodes, autoscaling, Kubernetes networking, and—if using incremental upgrade—Gateway API components and an autoscaler.[99][63]

For reproducible Ray releases, pin container digests, use content-addressed model artifacts, and declare feature schemas and application versions alongside deployment configuration.[148][62]

Ongoing maintenance is significant. Ray autoscaling acts independently per deployment by default, although application-level policies can coordinate deployments with dependencies or shared resources.[32] It supports delays, factors, and min/max replicas; external scaling is alpha and cannot be combined with built-in autoscaling for the same application.[32] Custom autoscaling policies with dependencies require those modules in the target image and require Python, `cloudpickle`, and library-version parity.[32] Open-source Ray recovers failed workers and replicas but not head-node crashes, which makes head-node fault tolerance a production design requirement rather than an optional refinement.[3][100]

Ray’s default metric path also has an operational timing detail: Serve batches updates for 100 ms by default, while Ray Core reports metrics to the scrape agent every 10,000 ms by default; lowering only the Serve interval does not make data appear sooner in Prometheus.[13][29] Histogram bucket changes require Serve-actor restarts, so percentile precision should be configured before production load testing rather than changed reactively during an incident.[29]

Ray Serve therefore lowers ensemble-composition friction but does not eliminate platform ownership. It is strongest when the fraud team values Python-native graph logic, coordinated CPU/GPU scheduling, and custom scaling behavior—and can operate Kubernetes, Ray fault tolerance, capacity policy, external observability, and a disciplined separate-cluster route for high-risk changes.[96][32][63][29]

Ray Serve autoscaling is a two-layer capacity system: the Serve autoscaler creates replica actors from demand, then the Ray Autoscaler requests CPU or GPU nodes when the cluster lacks placement resources, and the cloud provider adds those nodes. A 10× payment burst therefore requires pre-sized node capacity or a tested queueing policy; replica autoscaling cannot remove node-provisioning time.[64][65]

A manually configured Ray Serve autoscaler has a default `max_replicas` of 1 unless the team sets a larger value; `num_replicas="auto"` instead applies a default maximum of 100 replicas. Set explicit maxima separately for the CPU tree-model stage, GPU neural stage, and aggregation stage rather than relying on either default.[65]

Ray replica scheduling can leave a replica pending indefinitely when its requested accelerator labels have no matching cluster capacity.[148]

Ray recommends keeping a 20–50% gap between its default target of two ongoing requests per replica and maximum of five, rather than treating those defaults as a tested payment-burst policy.[32]

RayService head-node resilience does not eliminate client retry design. In the documented head-Pod deletion test, which allows at most one request in flight, a request can fail when it is in the head HTTPProxyActor; Ray recommends retry logic in Ray scripts.[101]

For independent deployment risk, place separately releasable ensemble components in separate Ray Serve applications: each application has its own endpoint and upgrade lifecycle, whereas updating one component of a composed application updates the other parts as well.[102]

Keep an operator-controlled kill switch outside the candidate application’s own router so that a defective router or combiner cannot prevent rollback. Load-test the router and aggregation deployment for backpressure because it can become the ensemble’s tail-latency bottleneck under burst traffic.

Label Ray metrics with model name and version in Prometheus so that latency, errors, queueing, resource use, and rollout health can be compared by variant. Place CPU-heavy tree scorers and GPU-heavy neural scorers deliberately: resource contention between multiple heavy models on a node increases latency and can cause evictions.[103]

For incident investigation after a cluster is deleted, KubeRay v1.7's beta History Server is an independent deployment that preserves access to RayCluster, RayJob, and RayService logs and events. Ray 2.56.0 Platform Events also surface Kubernetes lifecycle events for KubeRay custom resources in the Ray Dashboard, including node-level causes such as GPU XID errors.[104]

When a workload reports a generic CUDA or task failure from underlying node infrastructure such as a GPU XID error, the Kubernetes Event associated with the node can identify the root cause.[104]

The RayService high-availability example uses Redis to make the GCS fault tolerant and includes a three-node RayCluster with a simple Ray Serve application.[101]

KubeRay describes incremental upgrades as beta and recommends conservative parameter settings to minimize upgrade risk.[63] Changes to the Ray-autoscaler-managed `replicas`, `minReplicas`, `maxReplicas`, and `scaleStrategy.workersToDelete` fields do not trigger a zero-downtime upgrade and are not propagated from RayService to RayCluster custom resources.[99]

Ray production guidance calls for retrying transient failures a handful of times, applying exponential backoff, setting a timeout on every retry, and setting an end-to-end timeout in the Serve application so slow requests do not bottleneck replicas.[33] Ray Serve `BackpressureConfig` can reject overloaded HTTP requests with either 503 or 429 and can send a rounded-up `Retry-After` header through `retry_after_s`; its gRPC overload response is `RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED`.[33]

### KServe on Kubernetes

KServe separates a control plane that manages inference-service lifecycle, Kubernetes resources, and autoscaling from a data plane that executes inference.[68] An `InferenceService` can use predictor, transformer, and explainer components, with independent Horizontal Pod Autoscalers in Standard mode.[41] This maps naturally to a fraud-serving path in which a transformer fetches or computes features, predictors host model components, and a custom predictor or transformer combines scores.

KServe also offers ModelMesh mode for high-density serving on shared runtimes, alongside Standard deployment and Knative revision modes.[155][68][109][37]

KServe’s strongest documented incident rollback mechanism is the serverless canary rollout. Serverless canaries retain the last revision that completed rollout at 100% traffic. `canaryTrafficPercent` sends a specified share to the new revision and the remainder to the last rolled-out revision.[42][69] Setting the current model’s canary percentage to zero routes 100% of traffic to the previous good revision and zero to the new model; tag-based routing can explicitly direct test requests to either old or new revisions.[43]

**KServe’s production rollback is traffic-based: retain the previous healthy revision, then pin traffic back to it when the candidate fails.** This is safer for an authorization path than replacing the candidate artifact in place because the prior revision remains a named routing target.[42][43][105] After a successful promotion, however, the earlier revision’s pods can scale down to zero, so revision-retention and minimum-capacity policy should be deliberate if rapid reversal is required.[43][106]

This canary capability is confined to KServe serverless mode. KServe’s Standard Kubernetes installation is lighter weight but does not support canary deployment or request-based autoscaling with scale-to-zero.[70] Standard mode uses Kubernetes HPA rather than Knative Pod Autoscaler, does not scale down to zero, and supports CPU or memory scaling; concurrency and requests-per-second scaling are available through Knative Pod Autoscaler.[36][42][43][41] The choice is therefore operationally consequential: serverless mode adds Knative but provides canary routing and request-driven scaling, while Standard mode removes the Knative dependency and keeps a warm baseline at the cost of losing the documented canary workflow.[70]

Gateway API can provide traffic routing in KServe Standard mode, while Knative traffic splitting serves that role in Knative mode.[68]

KServe exposes a direct data-drift path through Alibi Detect. Its integration monitors the distribution of incoming requests with detector components.[107] KServe also captures inference requests and responses through inference logging for model-performance monitoring, debugging, prediction auditing, and data collection.[108] It offers basic logging, request-header metadata logging, TLS-enabled logging, payload logging through Knative Eventing, and blob-storage logging, each with distinct requirements.[108] The documented outlier architecture combines payload logging with asynchronous Knative processing.[107]

Prometheus metrics provide a separate service-health view. KServe provides default Prometheus metrics for service performance and health, and the documented example exposes preprocessing, prediction, and postprocessing duration histograms.[39] These are not drift detectors.

Drift detection requires a baseline, a selected statistical or representation-based method, alert thresholds, segmentation, and a response playbook. Feature distributions can remain stable while the feature-to-fraud relationship changes, so delayed-label quality monitoring is necessary for concept drift.[89]

KServe supplies integrated hooks for request capture and Alibi Detect, but fraud-specific drift governance remains an application responsibility. The team should monitor data validity before drift, treat drift as an investigation signal, and use delayed fraud labels to decide whether a version rollback, segment fallback, retraining, or no model action is appropriate.[89]

Initial KServe effort includes a Kubernetes cluster, KServe installation, and a reachable ingress gateway.[44][108] Serverless canary capability requires Knative, while the documented detector notebook requires Knative Eventing version 0.18 or later.[70][107] The Feast feature-store example illustrates the integration surface: it uses a feature-store initializer, separate Feast server and Transformer images, Redis, and the InferenceService. A Transformer’s default predictor call is HTTP; a gRPC predictor requires overriding `predict` for the gRPC call.[44]

For predictable KServe CPU/GPU placement, runtime pinning can be combined with node selectors, affinity and tolerations.[68][109]

A KServe deployment must also account for the selected ServingRuntime’s image, artifact protocol, GPU scheduling, and storage alongside its Gateway API or Knative networking configuration.[155][68][109][37]

Ongoing maintenance requires explicitly pinning `runtimeVersion` in production to avoid unexpected serving-runtime changes.[37][109] It also requires configuring each scalable component rather than assuming automatic burst capacity. In Standard mode, a component without autoscaling configuration defaults to `minReplicas: 1`, `maxReplicas: 1`, CPU scaling at 80% utilization—so it does not actually scale.[41] Predictor, transformer, and explainer can each have distinct min/max replicas and policies, which is useful but increases component-specific sizing, alerting, and capacity work.[41]

Knative revisions are point-in-time snapshots of code and configuration, support progressive rollout and rollback, and can be garbage-collected after inactivity.[106]Judgment — For fraud inference, set revision-retention and minimum-replica policies so that the prior stable revision remains deployable within the incident recovery objective; do not rely on a retained revision that must first pull large artifacts and initialize under live payment load.

KServe is the most directly opinionated option here for Kubernetes-native revisions, canaries, request logging, and detector integration. Its price is a larger control-plane surface in serverless mode; Standard mode reduces that surface but gives up documented canary rollback and scale-to-zero.[43][70]

KServe also has an InferenceGraph `Splitter`, which distributes requests among graph targets by configured integer weights that sum to 100; each request goes to exactly one target according to that probability distribution. This is graph-target routing, not serverless revision rollback. The documented previous-revision rollback instead belongs to serverless canary rollout: KServe tracks the last revision rolled out at 100% traffic, and setting `canaryTrafficPercent` to 0 routes 100% of traffic to that previous good revision.

KServe’s Grafana dashboard templates make incident triage stage-specific. ModelServer dashboards use Prometheus histogram quantiles for preprocessing, prediction, postprocessing, and explanation latency in milliseconds; the Triton dashboard separately plots input, inference, output, internal-queue, and total latency, plus GPU-memory use.[110]

In serverless mode, KServe directs operators to inspect Knative queue-proxy delay, gateway latency percentiles against the SLO, and observed concurrency; high in-flight requests or high CPU/GPU and memory pressure call for more resources or replicas. For Triton, queue latency and GPU utilization identify a model that cannot keep up. If server dashboards meet the SLO, inspect client-side metrics for network latency.[110]

Where the transformer obtains online features from Feast, monitor Feast request latency at p50, p95, and p99, online-store read duration, request throughput and errors, and feature freshness. These signals separate a stale or slow feature path from predictor latency.[111]

Version and restore the InferenceGraph, feature contract, and every component runtime together with the ensemble. Use immutable model URIs or artifact digests: Kubernetes deployment history does not restore a prior model if a mutable artifact location has been overwritten.

A KServe operational guide reports that GPU models scaled from zero can cold-start in tens of seconds to a few minutes. In its 2 GB S3-weight example, the first request exceeded 60 seconds because download and GPU-memory loading exceeded Knative's 30-second default timeout; its mitigations are LocalModelCache, a 600-second `progressDeadline`, and `minReplicas: 1` where cost permits.[71]

KServe v0.15 LocalModelCache pre-downloads weights to node-local storage. The documented configuration uses a 50 Gi NVMe volume on each node labelled `gpu-type: a100` and changes cold start from minutes to seconds by loading from local disk rather than S3. A three-tier cache uses node-local NVMe as the fastest tier, a PVC as shareable network storage, and object storage as the slowest but most durable origin.[71]

GPU-node placement is part of the deployment specification: GPU nodes commonly carry taints, and the InferenceService must specify both tolerations and a node selector or its Pod remains Pending.[71]

### BentoML

BentoML packages serving code, model artifacts, dependencies, and runtime configuration into a versioned Bento. Each built Bento receives a unique version, while model tags identify a model name and version; LightGBM model saves similarly return a generated `name:version` tag.[112][79][48] BentoML also locks Python package versions and dependency graphs by default, generating exact-version requirements; Conda versions are not automatically locked and should be explicitly specified.[79]

These artifact identifiers support reproducibility, but they are not an incident-routing system. BentoML’s `get` API can retrieve a tagged model or resolve a string through a model-alias mapping, yet the documentation does not describe aliases as an atomic production traffic switch.[47] For managed deployment rollback, BentoCloud retains Deployment revisions and permits roll-forward or rollback to any retained revision without deleting prior revisions.[80] Operators can roll back to a specific Deployment revision from the deployment interface.[113]

BentoCloud also supplies a managed canary path. It can run multiple Bento versions simultaneously, gradually split traffic between them, support rapid rollback, and show real-time performance metrics across deployed versions.[4] Each version can be tested independently in the Playground, and the Monitoring tab displays per-version metrics.[4] CLI or Python-SDK canary configuration requires BentoML 1.4.17 or later.[4] By default, canary versions mirror the main Deployment’s instance type, scaling policy, and replica counts, but they can be customized with different instance types, scaling policies, and environment variables.[4]

BentoCloud can route canary traffic by request header, query parameter, or randomly assigned percentage.[4]

**For a production incident, BentoCloud provides a documented version-and-traffic recovery path: restore a prior Deployment revision or take traffic away from a canary.** This is materially different from changing a local model tag, which identifies an artifact but is not documented as a traffic rollback mechanism.[80][4][47][79]

For self-managed BentoML on Kubernetes, traffic management depends on the deployment layer. Yatai describes Kubernetes rolling deployment, standard BentoML health endpoints, and rollback to a stored earlier Bento service.[81] A separate comparison describes BentoML plus Yatai as relying on Ingress-level traffic splitting between separate BentoDeployments, with routing managed by the selected ingress controller rather than an operator-native promotion and rollback workflow.[51] Thus, self-managed rollback is viable, but its mechanics depend on the Kubernetes deployment controller and ingress layer selected by the team.

BentoML provides both operational telemetry and an inference-data collection API. `bentoml.monitor` creates a named monitoring session that can log features, predictions, and targets.[114] Service health endpoints include `/livez`, `/readyz`, and `/healthz`.[114] BentoCloud deployment monitoring reports response-time percentiles including P95, request volume, success rate excluding 4xx and 5xx responses, in-progress requests, CPU and memory use, plus GPU utilization, GPU memory use, and GPU memory-bandwidth use.[113]

BentoML’s native monitoring collection is not automatically a statistical drift detector in the open-source workflow. By default, monitoring records go to rotating files, with retention behavior configurable.[114] They can be exported through OTLP and Fluent Bit to cloud services, Kafka, data stores, or monitoring tools; on Kubernetes, BentoML documents mounting a persistent log volume and forwarding records with a Fluent Bit DaemonSet or sidecar.[114] BentoML also provides an Arize plugin, which must be installed in the deployment runtime environment.[114]

A documented BentoML drift-analysis example aggregates monitoring logs, compares recent records with a baseline such as a prior week or training subset, and applies a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test using SciPy.[115] BentoML separately describes Bento Cloud as provisioning resources to continuously monitor service quality and alert on drift.[115] An Arize integration can monitor distribution drift, data quality, and model performance, and can alert through email, Slack, OpsGenie, or PagerDuty when a monitor crosses a configured or automatic threshold.[116]

BentoML is strongest as a portable packaging and inference-data collection layer; its open-source drift workflow requires exported data plus a comparison-and-alerting system, while BentoCloud adds managed version, canary, and monitoring controls. For payment fraud, monitor feature distributions and score distributions as early warnings, then join predictions to delayed labels for actual fraud-performance measurement; performance drift depends on later ground truth and may be costly or slow to establish.[114][115][4]

Initial deployment work is concentrated in service packaging. A Bento build specifies the Service module and class, runtime Python version and packages, model tags, and optional GPU resources; the Bento can then be containerized for Docker-compatible environments.[79][112] BentoML 1.4 moves model download to image build time, caching and mounting models into containers so they are available at service startup; this reduces cold-start time and improves scaling performance.[52] It also supports parallel loading of safetensor weight parts, particularly for large models.[52]

BentoML class-scope model references allow models to be downloaded during image build, cached, and mounted for Service startup rather than downloaded on the startup path.[160]

For an ensemble with a tree model on CPU and a neural model on GPU, BentoML can use distributed Services. A single Service is simpler for one model and simple API logic; multiple Services run in different containers.[45] Yatai’s Runner abstraction can deploy preprocessing and each model runner as separate autoscaling groups, which permits hardware-specific scaling but requires the service to be decomposed into specialized components.[81] This is useful when the neural model’s GPU queue and the tree model’s CPU queue have different saturation points.

A consolidated Bento Service has lower initial packaging effort than a distributed arrangement, but splitting the CPU-tree, GPU-neural, and aggregation Services adds service discovery, retries, tracing, compatibility checks, and timeout budgets to ongoing operations.[156][45]

Ongoing maintenance includes revision retention, deployment configuration, scaling ranges, dependency locks, log persistence and shipping, monitoring schemas, and drift thresholds. BentoCloud updates are either patches, changing specified fields only, or `apply` operations that reconcile the whole configuration and may reset omitted fields to defaults.[80] Distributed-service configuration can specify separate instance types, minimum and maximum replicas, and `RollingUpdate` strategy per Service.[45] BentoML deployment hooks perform one-time setup before Service workers spawn, while shutdown hooks provide cleanup points for connections and resources.[46]

The self-managed Kubernetes path has a maintenance qualification: a 2026 comparison describes Yatai’s last tagged release as October 2023 and characterizes it as stable but not actively evolving.[51] That makes BentoML itself attractive where a team already has a mature Kubernetes deployment and ingress stack, while BentoCloud is the clearer managed choice for teams that want first-class revision and canary controls rather than building them around Bentos.

A single BentoML Service makes the complete ensemble an atomic release; separately deployed CPU tree and GPU neural services permit independent scaling but add a network hop and release-coordination risk. Feature-store access is application code plus network, identity, and secret management rather than an inherent BentoML integration.

Rollback of separately deployed CPU-tree, GPU-neural, and aggregation BentoML Services requires an ordered change that preserves compatibility between the versions still receiving traffic.[156][45]

BentoML documents four Deployment strategies. `RollingUpdate` gradually replaces the old version and can temporarily mix versions; `Recreate` kills existing replicas before creating new ones, can cause downtime, is fast, ensures only one application version runs at a time, and is the default rollout strategy; `RampedSlowRollout` provides more control over rollout speed; and `BestEffortControlledRollout` adapts rollout speed to deployment success.[117] During a Deployment restart, a continuous-load study observed a rapid increase in errors and response time while a replacement Pod became ready, then a second RPS drop and error increase when that Pod was terminated and replaced; failures fell to zero and RPS stabilized once the application was fully operational.[50]

BentoML Services default to one homogeneous worker. Multiple workers avoid the Python GIL limitation for CPU-bound Python work, but every worker loads its own copy of the model into memory. Size worker count and memory separately for the CPU tree scorer and any service that hosts the neural scorer; a single homogeneous Service cannot independently tune those execution pools.[118]

Self-hosted BentoML requires an assembled observability stack of Prometheus, Grafana, traces, and data-driven alerts. It also relies on the deploying team's IAM, network segmentation, data-handling policies, RBAC, audit logs, and policy enforcement; these are continuing responsibilities for a payment-data service rather than capabilities supplied by the packaged Bento.[119]

Judgment — The four options differ less in whether they can host models than in where they place incident and monitoring responsibility. Seldon plus Triton provides the most explicit composition, model-control, and detector-building blocks, but also the broadest artifact and runtime surface. Ray Serve gives the most programmable ensemble and rich serving telemetry, while leaving model lifecycle and drift observability to surrounding systems. KServe provides the clearest Kubernetes-native serverless canary rollback and integrated Alibi Detect path, but its strongest rollout controls require Knative. BentoCloud offers the simplest managed revision-and-canary recovery path; self-managed BentoML depends more on the chosen Kubernetes and ingress operations.[22][84][29][69][107][80][4]

1. Use traffic rollback to a retained, warmed stable version as the first incident action; investigate artifacts only after authorization traffic is protected.
2. Keep fraud drift monitoring separate from synchronous scoring, and require data-quality validation plus delayed-label performance checks before model replacement.
3. Choose KServe serverless or BentoCloud when managed revision traffic control is the deciding need; choose Seldon–Triton or Ray Serve only if the team can own their broader operational controls.

The operational responsibilities are parallel but not interchangeable: the incident actor controls traffic recovery, while the detector and feature-capture path supplies evidence for investigation rather than an automatic model change.

## Published fraud detection infrastructure experiences

Judgment — Published company accounts are useful for identifying the surrounding operational problems of real-time fraud detection: stateful features, rule changes, traffic control, and coordination between data science and platform engineering. They are not interchangeable benchmark reports. In particular, a deployment workflow measured in minutes, a system decision measured in milliseconds, and a scale-from-zero cold start are different measurements and should not be used as substitutes.

### Razorpay

Razorpay has published the most detailed India-specific account of a streaming fraud stack. **Described activity period:** Razorpay acquired Thirdwatch in August 2019; Thirdwatch is described as an AI-powered real-time fraud-detection solution.[5][120] Razorpay’s Mitra data platform powers Thirdwatch for real-time fraud prevention by serving machine-learning models at scale.[5] Customer data enters through APIs, SDKs, and plugins; the core engine uses Apache Flink for data augmentation, validation, feature generation, identity clustering, and velocity profiling; outputs go to dashboards, APIs, Kafka topics, and downstream operators.[5]

Mitra’s published capabilities put feature computation around the model at the center of the design. Razorpay described prediction results within 200 milliseconds in a distributed environment, hundreds of features generated on the fly through more than 60 operators, and results served from multiple deployed ML models. Its Flink application consumed both a data stream and a control stream carrying control signals, models, and rules; it performed eight to ten complex-event-processing conditions plus joins and enrichments to generate hundreds of real-time ML features for training and serving.[5]

Razorpay also described a material production bottleneck: model serving had to scale with low latency while generating dynamic features, but training could consume maximum resources and bulk network capacity, causing serving requests to fail. It separated training and serving clusters so they could receive resources and scale independently according to their loads. This is a directly relevant design lesson for a 50-million-transactions-per-day fraud service: GPU training or batch backfills must not contend with the online CPU/GPU inference and feature path.[5]

A separate Razorpay system, ADA—Anomaly Detection and Alerting—is a configurable, multi-tenant engine for real-time anomaly detection and fraud prevention. ADA uses Amazon MSK as its event backbone and Apache Flink for stateful stream processing; Razorpay reports detection of transaction anomalies in under 30 seconds, thousands of merchant-level alerts, and approximately 80% lower monitoring costs. Before ADA, ThirdEye queried a 21-day lookback at query time and enforced a minimum 1–2 minute SLA. That is an anomaly-detection pipeline measurement, not the latency of an individual synchronous payment decision.[121]

Razorpay says ADA processes payment, payroll, and banking events through Amazon MSK and Apache Flink.[121]

Razorpay describes ADA’s processing path as Kafka ingestion, Flink event-time and window processing, asynchronous ClickHouse baseline retrieval, and threshold, machine-learning, and complex-event-processing rules. Its example sequence detector identifies five consecutive card declines followed by a success as a card-testing pattern.[121]

Razorpay reports that ADA’s asynchronous ClickHouse baseline lookup supports 1,024 concurrent requests.[121]

In an account of its earlier detector, Razorpay said database polling recalculated payment success rate every two minutes.[165]

Razorpay reports approximately 15 workflow events per payment, of which three are needed to establish final payment status.[165]

ADA provides useful evidence about operational control. Rule updates are serialized to Kafka and applied by Flink as broadcast-state updates, propagating to running pipeline instances without redeployment. MSK decouples payment-event producers from detection consumers, allowing Razorpay to deploy, scale, or roll back detection logic without changing production payment flows. The architecture is also described as plugin-compatible with ETS/Prophet forecasting, Flink CEP sequence patterns, and custom root-cause-analysis strategies.[121]

Razorpay says AdaDSL rule updates hot-reload through an Amazon MSK snapshot topic and Flink broadcast state, reaching running pipeline instances without a restart.[121]

Razorpay reported MSK Serverless connection imbalance, broker-recovery issues, and throttling. During the Asia Cup 2025 surge, pod scaling and excessive GoCloud Pub/Sub connections contributed to reaching its roughly 3,000-active-TCP-connections-per-broker IAM limit.[166]

After switching to franz-go, Razorpay published figures it marked `[VERIFY]`: connections fell from about 1,100 to 300 per broker, and publish latency fell from about 250 ms to 10 ms.[166]

Razorpay has separately described fraud management as a supervised binary-classification problem using fraud/not-fraud labels, with a model outputting a fraud probability and a business-selected cutoff for action. Its published Cybersource case study also says Decision Manager ran alongside Razorpay’s existing fraud-management solution for the first few months before integration into merchant-facing products. That is a parallel evaluation, but it does not document a formal randomized A/B test or a model-serving version workflow.[122][123]

In an April 29, 2021 account, Razorpay described progressive canary releases using Kubernetes, Kong Gateway, Netflix Spinnaker, and Kayenta. Canary traffic starts at 2.5% and can rise to 10%; automated analysis checks errors and latency, while removing the canary Kong target immediately withdraws its traffic.[167]

For checkout shadow analysis, Razorpay described a traffic allocation for a service above 500 requests per second of 98% base, 1% canary, and 1% baseline or mirrored traffic.[168]

The company’s more recent Vulcan account points toward model consolidation rather than public evidence of multi-framework ensemble operations. Razorpay describes Vulcan as a transformer-based payment foundation model trained on about 3 trillion data points across 4 billion payments; components were running in real time for routing, fraud, and risk decisions. Razorpay contrasts Vulcan with separate specialized routing, fraud, risk, and checkout models that do not share signals, and says the model learns from roughly 3,000 signals per transaction.[124]

In its August 2026 Vulcan announcement, Razorpay claimed an 8–10% payment-success improvement, eight times more international-card fraud stopped, and five times more fraudulent or disputed transactions identified without extra alerts; the announcement did not specify a period, denominator, or baseline for those claims.[169]

**Infrastructure —** Razorpay’s published architecture makes fraud inference a streaming-systems problem as much as a model-serving problem: stateful Flink features, CEP conditions, control streams, rules, and independently scalable serving capacity surround the model call.[5] The published figures must remain separate: Mitra’s within-200-ms result is a distributed prediction-result statement, while ADA’s under-30-second result is anomaly-detection timing; neither is a p95/p99 benchmark or deployment cold-start measurement.[121][5] Razorpay has not published a cold-start latency for a newly deployed model, nor an account quantifying the operational burden of simultaneously operating XGBoost, LightGBM, and neural-network frameworks; serving multiple deployed models does not identify their frameworks.[5][124]

Razorpay’s newer Vulcan account adds a different form of cold start: sparse payment history rather than container or model-startup delay. Razorpay says broader payment patterns can inform a decision for a new credential, a one-time-seen instrument, or activity distributed across merchants; its launch results were measured on merchants already sending traffic.[125] Fraud decisions occur before payment authorisation, whereas confirmation may arrive only when a dispute appears weeks later, and some fraud is never reported.[125] Razorpay says card-testing attempts that appear ordinary to individual merchants can become detectable through related instruments and tight timing across the network.[125]

Vulcan is a shared payments foundation model in which a reusable backbone is combined with task-specific context and decision layers.[125] Razorpay says its earlier routing, fraud, delivery-risk, and checkout decisions each had separate models and separate views of a payment; the foundation approach reuses a payment representation.

Razorpay says every new use case still needs relevant data, a clear label, careful evaluation, and a safe serving path.[125] This is a model-consolidation architecture rather than a published XGBoost/LightGBM-plus-neural-network serving ensemble.[125]

Razorpay states that Amazon SageMaker AI handled model training and auto-scaling inference for its foundation-model programme, while NVIDIA accelerated computing and AWS cloud infrastructure underpin the system.[126] Razorpay says the model is in production for network-level fraud protection and routes every transaction through one continuously learning intelligence layer.[126] Razorpay reports handling almost 4 billion customer-to-merchant payments annually.[126]

Thirdwatch’s published merchant cases add operational detail around the fraud decision. Razorpay says Thirdwatch combines seller-specific rule-based algorithms with machine-learning algorithms; in the KaratCart case, its risk engine analysed more than 200 parameters in milliseconds and assigned each order a red or green flag based on return-to-origin probability.[127] KaratCart’s Shopify installation began capturing and flagging orders within minutes, while Thirdwatch experts spent the first week evaluating the merchant’s business model, analysed about three months of historical data, incorporated seller-specific learnings into the global model, and used feedback from actual delivery attempts to improve accuracy.[127] This is merchant integration and adaptation work, not a model-deployment cold-start measurement.[127]

Razorpay’s 2026 payment-gateway guide lists Thirdwatch Fraud Prevention as AI-powered transaction monitoring with customizable risk scoring and says developer-friendly APIs integrate fraud prevention into checkout workflows.[128]

Razorpay’s platform maintains 99.99 percent uptime for over 500 million transactions per month.[121]

Razorpay’s prior architecture monitored thousands of merchants at approximately $500K per year: $250K in licensing fees and $250K in infrastructure, with fundamental scalability limits.[121]

Razorpay’s ADA architecture combines Flink for sub-minute, stateful detection with ClickHouse for deterministic baseline computation and historical context.[121]

Razorpay’s data platform processes more than 5 billion events daily.[121]

Razorpay’s published serving-pattern material lists static in-memory model integration; a standalone static-model service using a web service or socket connections; an online-learning service with integrated training; and a parameter server with integrated model updates. It names Kubeflow, Airflow, MLflow, and H2O as tools.[129]

Razorpay’s Kafka-and-Flink presentation recommends implementing an A/B-testing scenario on live data.[129]

### Gojek

Gojek provides the clearest published example of a company platform supporting several model frameworks. Its Merlin ML platform supported XGBoost, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and user-defined models implemented with arbitrary Python code. Merlin used Kubernetes in production, with KFServing, Knative, and Istio; MLflow managed model artifacts and Kaniko built Docker images.[6]

Gojek described its ML platform as supporting hundreds of millions of monthly orders across more than 20 products in four countries, including payments, fraud, and trust.[170]

Merlin was designed as a notebook-first, self-service deployment environment. Gojek said the target was less than 10 minutes from a pre-trained model to a web-service endpoint, while another company account described the deployment process as taking a few minutes and being observable through the ML Platform console. These are deployment-workflow statements, not first-request cold-start latency.[6][130][131]

Gojek’s account makes the operational complexity explicit. Before an internal platform abstraction, data scientists familiar with TensorFlow, XGBoost, GBM, H2O, Python, or R still had to navigate Docker, Kubernetes, Jenkins, nginx, REST, protobuf, ELK, and Grafana.[6][131] They commonly partnered with software or infrastructure engineers to create build and deployment pipelines, monitoring and alerting systems, microservice wrappers, and other automation.[6][131]

Merlin addressed release safety through traffic management. Gojek documented canary, blue-green, and shadow deployments; Istio could direct a small traffic percentage to a candidate version or atomically switch traffic between blue and green environments. Model artifacts and versions were tracked in MLflow, and model-serving metrics were automatically exposed to Gojek’s internal monitoring platform.[6][130]

Turing obtains ensemble configuration before models run and dispatches most traffic asynchronously to all models, including a simple fallback. If a model times out or fails, the router can immediately return the fallback result.[171]

The company’s published fraud-specific account is GoSage, a graph-neural-network system published in Gojek Product + Tech on December 4, 2024. GoSage represents customers and merchants as graph nodes, with transactions and shared resources such as devices as edges. It uses PyTorch Geometric and multiple GNN layers to capture direct and neighbor relationships; Gojek reports qualitative improvement in fraud detection, fewer false positives, and better detection of organized fraud networks.[7]

Gojek’s JARVIS fraud case study covered April 2017 through September 2018 and addressed GPS spoofing and ride-sharing incentive fraud by generating trip attributes, classifying trips, and automatically banning drivers. Its stated goal was to replace detection taking at least 30 minutes with real-time inspection and intervention.[172]

The largest relation in the published GoSage evaluation dataset comprised 7,895,616 merchant–customer transaction edges.[173]

Gojek’s feature-store work explains another source of operational burden. Before Feast, engineers had to set up database clusters, ingestion jobs, and monitoring for each model, project, environment, and region. Feature transformations were rewritten from Python for online serving, increasing duplicated work and creating training-serving skew. **Described activity period:** Gojek introduced Feast at the start of 2019 to let teams define, manage, discover, and serve features, and later described a common feature stream available to multiple Feast serving deployments.[132]

In the described Feast serving flow, each prediction request first retrieves selected feature values from Feast and then passes them to the model. Gojek said this removed the need to redevelop feature transformations in the serving layer and standardized feature retrieval through one API. Feast monitoring tracked ingested stream and batch data, row counts, distributions, and reads from the store; fraud teams could log served features back to a warehouse to validate what reached models and check for drift.[132][133]

**Infrastructure —** Gojek’s Merlin combined Kubernetes, KFServing, Knative, Istio, MLflow, and Kaniko for model deployment, while Feast standardized online feature retrieval and reduced repeated feature-serving infrastructure and training-serving inconsistency.[6][132] **Simultaneous-framework operation —** Merlin supported XGBoost, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and arbitrary Python models, but Gojek did not publish a single fraud service operating XGBoost or LightGBM together with neural-network frameworks, or quantify the added maintenance burden of that combination.[6][7] **New-model cold start —** Merlin’s stated path from a pre-trained model to a web-service endpoint was less than 10 minutes, and Gojek separately described deployment as taking a few minutes; these are deployment-workflow descriptions. Knative scaled idle services to zero and scaled them up when requests arrived, balancing cold-start latency and cost efficiency.[6][131][130]

Gojek’s broader Indonesia security accounts place fraud controls across the payment ecosystem. On 28 May 2020, Gojek said Gojek SHIELD applied to consumer, merchant, and driver applications, with layered protection through PIN verification and AI-based chat intervention to prevent social-engineering scams.[134] GoPay’s ISO 27001 announcement states that its information-security management system covers account registration, verification, suspension, payment, peer-to-peer transfer, balance top-up, cash withdrawal, and support processes including risk & fraud, legal, compliance, and technology operations.[135] GoPay also identifies biometric identification for payment-partner authorisation and user-to-user transfers as part of Gojek SHIELD.[135]

Gojek reported that platform transactions increased 1,100%, or twelvefold, from June 2016 to June 2019.[136] In the 17 May 2021 GoTo combination announcement, the group reported over 1.8 billion transactions in 2020 across an ecosystem combining e-commerce, on-demand, and financial services; GoTo Financial encompassed GoPay and the group’s merchant and financial-services offerings.[136][137]

Publication and activity dates — Gojek Product + Tech published 《GoSage: How We Detect Fraud Syndicates at Gojek with Graph Neural Networks》 on December 4, 2024.[7] Gojek Product + Tech published 《Feast: Bridging ML Models and Data》 on February 11, 2020; the account says Gojek introduced Feast at the start of 2019.[132]

Gojek said that operating Merlin’s Kubernetes and cloud-native stack with a service mesh required substantial platform-engineering expertise.[130]

Gojek’s feature infrastructure supported both inference-time feature retrieval and reuse of model outputs as downstream-model features: prediction requests retrieved selected Feast values before passing them to the model, while model outputs could be sent to a stream, read into the feature store, and reused as features.[133][132]

### PayMongo

PayMongo publishes a managed Philippines fraud product rather than the internals of a self-hosted ML serving platform. Its Protect service evaluates every payment processed through PayMongo in real time before completion. The machine-learning engine assigns a 0–1000 risk score, applies configured rules, and determines whether to allow, review, or block the transaction. It evaluates behavioral patterns, velocity checks, anomalies, and device and identity signals.[9][138]

Protect couples scoring to operational decisioning. Merchants can define allow, block, or review rules using risk score or level, card and billing country, payment amount, IP address, and billing email. Medium-risk scores of 500–799 and high-risk scores of 800–1000 enter the review queue by default; reviewers can inspect transaction details and risk drivers before closing a case. PayMongo also says its dashboard shows the signals contributing most to a transaction score.[9]

For payment-platform customers, PayMongo says scores are produced automatically as part of the normal payment workflow, without an explicit Fraud Scores API call. The separate Fraud Scores API is aimed primarily at third-party platforms and integrators scoring transactions outside PayMongo’s payment system.[139]

PayMongo says most merchants can activate fraud scoring from the dashboard within minutes and that every payment receives a score immediately after activation. This is a managed-product enablement time, not a model deployment, container warm-up, model-load, or scale-from-zero cold-start measurement.[138]

Sift’s published PayMongo customer case reports that, during PayMongo’s early stage, the company encountered fraud attacks and a 4% dispute rate, selected Sift Payment Protection after an assessment, and used Sift for transaction monitoring, case management, workflow rules, and payment routing. PayMongo risk analyst Cha Tecson described Sift’s integration and implementation as easier and more efficient than other third-party tools used by PayMongo; the Sift case says PayMongo could handle 10–20 times more card transactions safely and securely.[140]

Datadog’s published PayMongo case study describes a different operational issue: as PayMongo’s transaction volumes rose nearly 25 times over 18 months, teams accumulated distinct toolsets, creating a more complex operating environment. The Datadog case says centralized observability let engineers trace a payment through its journey, reducing investigations from hours to minutes; it reports that 75% of incidents were detected and resolved before customer impact and mean time to restore fell from hours to minutes.[141]

**Infrastructure —** PayMongo’s Protect product performs pre-completion, real-time scoring of payments processed through PayMongo, exposes score drivers, applies merchant-configured allow, review, and block rules, and routes flagged transactions to manual review.[9][138] **Simultaneous-framework operation —** PayMongo has not published its model-serving stack, framework mix, or the operational complexity of managing multiple model frameworks simultaneously.[9][142] PayMongo’s published operational complexity concerns disparate team toolsets as transaction volumes increased, and its risk analyst described Sift’s integration and implementation as easier and more efficient than other third-party tools.[141][140] **New-model cold start —** PayMongo says most merchants can activate fraud scoring within minutes and that payments receive scores immediately once enabled; PayMongo has not published cold-start latency for a new model deployment.[138][9][142]

Derived — PayMongo’s reported nearly 25× transaction-volume growth over 18 months implies approximately **19.6% compound monthly growth**.[141]

PayMongo adds published risk-engine outcome and monitoring detail. It says its machine-learning risk engine runs 24/7, evaluates and scores each transaction against thousands of data combinations, links similar data points across the PayMongo network, uses device fingerprinting, and evolves from transaction-pattern analysis.[143] PayMongo reports a fraud rate below 0.1%, a dispute rate below 0.1%, and a 98% transaction-approval rate for its licensed Philippine processing.[143]

PayMongo directs merchants to monitor score distributions to tune thresholds and to investigate unusually high scores or unexpected risk drivers as indicators of new fraud tactics.[144] This is operational score and risk-driver monitoring; PayMongo also exposes the factors that most influenced each scored transaction in its dashboard.[144] Merchants can create fraud rules in the dashboard or through the API, using risk-score thresholds and transaction attributes to approve, flag, or block payments.[145]

PayMongo recommends regularly monitoring the overall risk profile of payment traffic, using score distributions to tune thresholds rather than relying solely on the default medium/high split, and investigating unusually high scores or unexpected risk drivers as possible new fraud tactics.[144]

Protect currently supports cards; e-wallets—GCash, Maya, and GrabPay—QR Ph, and other methods are listed as coming soon. Protect requires account configuration before use, and PayMongo directs users to contact the company for access.[9]

Judgment — The three accounts support different decisions. Razorpay is the strongest example of fraud serving embedded in stateful streaming and online feature generation; Gojek is the strongest example of platform abstractions for multiple frameworks, staged traffic release, and reusable feature serving; PayMongo is the strongest example of the managed fraud-product interface seen by a payment customer. Razorpay’s 200-ms statement, Gojek’s minutes-to-endpoint statement, and PayMongo’s minutes-to-activation statement measure different things and cannot rank cold starts.[5][6][138]

1. Treat first-request cold start as a release-gating benchmark to measure in the startup’s own CPU/GPU cluster, not as a metric supplied by these company accounts.
2. Separate online feature computation, training, and inference capacity from the first production release; Razorpay’s contention experience makes this a reliability requirement. [5]
3. Use staged routing and shadow evaluation before decisioning changes affect authorization outcomes; Gojek’s canary, blue-green, and shadow patterns provide the operational model. [6][130]

**Conditional ranked choice —** On one self-managed mixed CPU/GPU cost basis, rank **1. Ray Serve, 2. BentoML, 3. KServe, 4. Seldon Core with Triton** when Python ensemble composition and independent CPU/GPU scaling decide the choice. Ray Serve separates preprocessing, inference, and post-processing into independently configured and scaled deployments with CPU/GPU reservations; BentoML distributed Services separately scale CPU and GPU processing and modularize sequential, parallel, and multi-model workflows.[2][32][45] Seldon Core with Triton moves ahead when a Triton ensemble DAG is the required composition boundary and the team accepts model/container compatibility work, including matching Treelite versions and custom Python-backend environment packaging.[1][17] BentoML moves ahead of Ray Serve when its Service-based CPU/GPU pipeline structure is the simpler operating model for the ensemble.[45]

Release gate — Run a matched test at **5,787 TPS** covering warm p95 and p99 end-to-end latency with online features, new-version readiness, and traffic rollback to a warm prior version. Test XGBoost or LightGBM, the neural-network component, and the ensemble path under the same mixed CPU/GPU resource allocation; Ray Serve and BentoML both support independent resource configuration and scaling for these component stages.[2][32][45]

The exact p95 and p99, sustainable 5,787-TPS throughput, cold-start time for new model versions, and cost per million successful predictions remain workload-specific for all four configurations. They are settled by exercising the same tree and neural artifacts, feature-store calls, regional topology, burst profile, and rollback procedure on the intended CPU/GPU node mix. Published Razorpay, Gojek, and PayMongo accounts must be kept distinct from platform capabilities where they do not report model-deployment cold starts or the operating burden of multiple model frameworks.
