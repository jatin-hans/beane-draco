Relocating to Germany is not the cash-take-home choice on the stated 2024 facts. With statutory German insurance and foreign-tax-credit relief for regular U.S. income tax, modeled German-resident take-home is $124,924 on $220,000 salary versus $161,394 in the U.S., a $36,470 or 22.6% reduction.

With $200,000 salary and a $30,000 net long-term listed-security gain, it is $135,019 in Germany versus $172,100 in the U.S., a $37,081 or 21.5% reduction.

The recommended course is to relocate only where non-tax compensation or personal considerations justify roughly this annual cash-cost range, while arranging German payroll, social-insurance coverage, U.S. foreign-tax-credit reporting, and FATCA compliance before the move.[1][2]

### alternative capital-gains scenarios

The calculated alternatives are a zero-gain case, a $30,000 **short-term capital gain** on an ordinary security, and a $60,000 **long-term capital gain** on an ordinary security. A long-term capital gain is a gain on an asset held more than one year; a short-term capital gain is a gain on an asset held one year or less.[51] For a 2024 US single filer, the long-term-capital-gain bands are 0% through $47,025 of taxable income, 15% from $47,026 to $518,900, and 20% above that.[25][50] Net investment income tax (NIIT), a 3.8% surtax, applies to the smaller of net investment income and modified adjusted gross income above the applicable threshold; the single-filer threshold is $200,000.[31][50]

The second alternative treats it as a **short-term capital gain**, meaning an asset held one year or less. Short-term gains generally use ordinary-income rates rather than the preferential long-term schedule, so the incremental US regular-tax exposure is higher at this income level than for a conventional long-term gain.[51][50] Losses change that result: US capital losses offset capital gains, and a net excess loss of up to $3,000 may reduce ordinary income, with the remainder carried forward.[51]

Stocks and bonds held as investments are generally U.S. capital assets, and their disposal generally produces a capital gain or loss.[121]

The third alternative is qualifying **section 1202 qualified small business stock (QSB stock)**, held more than five years. The US exclusion may be 50%, 75%, or 100% according to acquisition date and statutory conditions; QSB stock must, among other requirements, be originally issued by a qualifying domestic C corporation and meet asset and active-business tests.[51] The fourth is an eligible gain invested in a **qualified opportunity fund (QOF)**: the taxpayer may elect to defer recognition until sale or exchange of the QOF investment or 31 December 2026, whichever occurs first.[51]

For Germany, the comparable assumed case is an ordinary investment-security gain: securities disposals are capital income rather than private-sale transactions, taxed at 25% Abgeltungsteuer, Germany’s flat capital-income tax, plus solidarity surcharge and potentially church tax. Changing scenario B’s holding period from long-term to short-term generally does not alter that German capital-income treatment. A single taxpayer’s Sparer-Pauschbetrag, the saver’s allowance, is €1,000.[30][66][67] The asset, acquisition date, holding period, losses, church-tax status, and German euro gain must be confirmed before treating this as the taxpayer’s actual result.

A conventional US long-term-gain alternative also requires identification of the asset. A long-term collectible gain has a maximum 28% rate, and the unrecaptured section 1250 component of a real-property gain has a maximum 25% rate.[68][69]

For Germany, EStG section 20(2) classifies gains from disposing of corporate shares, specified derivatives and capital claims as income from capital investments (Einkünfte aus Kapitalvermögen).[67] A German resident’s worldwide investment income, including gains from selling shares, is subject to 25% tax plus solidarity surcharge and church tax where applicable; the standard annual deduction is €1,000 for a single taxpayer.[70] The 25% tax plus the 5.5% solidarity surcharge produces a 26.375% rate where church tax does not apply.[71]

The German alternatives are asset-specific. A seller who directly or indirectly held at least 1% of a corporation during the preceding five years has business income rather than the 25% flat-rate treatment; it is taxed at the individual rate and receives a 40% exemption.[70] For non-business property other than shares, gains are generally untaxed after the required holding period, generally one year; gains below €600 in the calendar year are tax-free even where that period has not elapsed. Real-property gains are taxable where held for ten years or less, subject to the principal-residence exception.[70]

An equity fund has a 30% investor-level partial exemption and a mixed fund a 15% partial exemption; gains on shares acquired before 1 January 2009 remain tax-free.[71]

When determining income from capital assets (Einkünfte aus Kapitalvermögen), EStG section 20(9) deducts a €1,000 Werbungskostenbetrag, called the Sparer-Pauschbetrag; deduction of actual Werbungskosten is excluded.[67] A Freistellungsauftrag, submitted to the bank, permits the allowance to be used in withholding.

Germany applies the single taxpayer’s €1,000 Sparer-Pauschbetrag to qualifying capital income after applicable loss offsets.[67][100]

It may be divided among financial institutions, but assigned allowances in total cannot exceed €1,000 or €2,000, as applicable.[67][72] Without it, a bank withholds 26.375% from the first euro of investment income; the taxpayer may recover excess withholding through the annual return using Anlage KAP.[71]

A loss alternative differs across the two countries. US losses first offset gains, then a net loss up to $3,000 can offset other income, or up to $1,500 for a married taxpayer filing separately; the remainder carries forward with its short-term or long-term character.[51][68] German investment losses cannot offset salary or other income, carry into later years against investment income, and share-disposal losses offset only share-disposal gains.[67]

For QSB stock, the IRS instructions require stock in a C corporation originally issued after 10 August 1993, with stated asset, acquisition and business-activity tests; during substantially all of the holding period, at least 80% of the corporation’s asset value must be used in the active conduct of one or more qualified businesses.[51] Stock acquired after 27 September 2010 can qualify for an exclusion of up to 100% if the section 1202 conditions are met.[51]

## Key regulatory/compliance risks

A move to Germany changes the country that will usually tax day-to-day employment and investment income, but it does not switch off U.S. tax or reporting duties for a U.S. citizen. Four separate questions must be answered: U.S. worldwide taxation; German domestic residence; financial-account reporting under FATCA and related U.S. forms; and treaty allocation and double-tax relief. The answers determine whether the German-relocation take-home result is a genuine net result rather than two gross tax bills added together.

### US citizenship-based taxation

The United States taxes a U.S. citizen’s worldwide income regardless of where the citizen lives. A citizen who becomes resident in Germany therefore remains subject to U.S. income-tax filing requirements on German employment income and investment income, including realized taxable capital gains. Living abroad does not itself make the person a U.S. income-tax nonresident.[1][64][76]

The U.S.–Germany income-tax treaty preserves this result through its **saving clause**—the treaty rule reserving the United States’ power to tax its own citizens under domestic law. The treaty says that, except for listed benefits, it does not affect U.S. taxation of U.S. citizens and residents.[36][38][52] The treaty’s saving clause also applies despite source-country limits on gains, dividends and interest that might otherwise benefit a German resident who was not a U.S. citizen.[39][52]

**A U.S. citizen’s German residence changes the foreign tax system that applies, but does not replace the U.S. system: both countries can have income-tax claims.** The practical U.S. residual is determined by exclusions and foreign-tax-credit calculations, income item by income item, rather than by treating the two countries’ headline income-tax bills as cumulative deductions.[1][36][39]

For the U.S. federal return, the key relief mechanism is the **foreign tax credit (FTC)**: a credit against U.S. income tax for qualifying foreign income taxes paid or accrued. Article 23 requires the United States to allow a resident or citizen a credit for German income tax paid or accrued, subject to U.S. law’s limitations. The treaty also treats certain income that Germany may tax under the treaty as German-source income for FTC purposes.[36][52] German Einkommensteuer is among the German taxes treated as an income tax for this purpose.[52]

The FTC is not automatically dollar-for-dollar against all U.S. tax. Under the treaty explanation, the U.S. limitation generally caps the credit at U.S. tax on net foreign-source income in the relevant FTC category. U.S. expense-allocation rules remain relevant even when treaty rules re-source gross income.[39][52] Thus, German tax paid on salary, gains, dividends, or other investment income must be matched to its U.S. tax category and source treatment.

A special treaty coordination rule applies where a U.S. citizen is resident in Germany and receives certain U.S.-source income that would be exempt from U.S. tax or subject to a reduced U.S. rate if received by a German resident who was not a U.S. citizen. Germany allows credit only for U.S. tax that the treaty permits other than tax imposed solely because of citizenship; the United States then credits German income tax after that German credit, and may treat the relevant income as German-source to the extent needed to relieve double taxation.[36][38][39][52] This is an ordering rule, not permission to deduct the same German income tax twice.

For wages, the **foreign earned income exclusion (FEIE)** is a separate U.S. domestic-law relief method. The 2024 maximum FEIE is $126,500. A taxpayer qualifies only by meeting the foreign tax-home test and either the bona fide residence test or the physical-presence test.[64] The IRS’s 2024 Form 2555 instructions require at least 330 full days in one or more foreign countries during a consecutive 12-month period.

For the physical-presence test, a full day means 24 consecutive hours beginning and ending at midnight; travel days to or from the United States generally do not count. [123]

That period must contain 365 days, or 366 days in a leap year, and include part of 2024; it may begin or end in a calendar year other than 2024.[64] The bona fide residence test requires uninterrupted bona fide foreign residence that includes an entire tax year, with the taxpayer’s actual intention and conduct relevant to the result.[64]

A 2024 mover may qualify through a 12-month physical-presence period beginning or ending outside calendar year 2024, but qualification depends on actual travel days and foreign tax-home facts. In the first year, the taxpayer may need either a special filing extension or a timely return without the exclusion followed by an amended return after qualifying.[64] The overseas extension generally permits a qualifying calendar-year taxpayer to file on June 15 rather than April 15, but interest on unpaid federal income tax runs from April 15.[1]

A qualifying U.S. citizen abroad may request a further filing extension on Form 4868, but that extension does not extend the time to pay; unpaid tax may incur interest and penalties. [124][125]

The FEIE has a material trade-off. A taxpayer cannot claim an FTC or deduction for German income taxes allocable to income excluded under the FEIE; if only part of income is excluded, the corresponding part of German tax is also unavailable as a credit or deduction.[1][64] Moreover, tax on nonexcluded income is calculated using the U.S. tax rates that would have applied without the exclusion.[64] This means the FEIE cannot simply be modeled as removing the first $126,500 of salary and applying low tax brackets to the balance.

For German wages, the FEIE can reduce U.S. taxable earned income, but it trades away the FTC for German tax attributable to excluded wages and does not address investment gains. The salary-only and salary-plus-gain cases should therefore compare an FEIE election with an FTC approach rather than assume one is invariably better.[64]

Capital gains are not foreign earned income for FEIE purposes. The treaty’s Article 13 generally assigns ordinary gains to the seller’s treaty residence, but the saving clause permits the United States to tax a U.S. citizen notwithstanding otherwise applicable treaty limits, subject to Article 23’s special FTC rules.[41][39] A German-resident U.S. citizen may therefore owe U.S. tax on a realized gain even if Germany is the treaty residence state and taxes the same sale.

For the $30,000 gain in scenario B, the treaty does not generally shield a U.S. citizen from U.S. taxation through the normal residence-state rule. Tax overlap can remain where the countries recognize different taxable events, cost bases, sources, or timing; an illustrative German-corporation case describes German tax on an actual sale alongside U.S. treatment that left no U.S. capital gain against which to use the German tax as an FTC.[39][58]

The nature of the gain is therefore consequential. A sale of U.S. or German real property, a substantial shareholding, interests connected with a permanent establishment, a fund interest, or a security portfolio can fall under different treaty and domestic-law rules. The “taxable capital gains” label in scenario B is insufficient to establish a final credit outcome without the asset type, acquisition basis, holding period, source, and sale date.

A U.S. citizen abroad also remains subject to normal U.S. filing and estimated-tax rules. The taxpayer normally files Form 1040; U.S. employers generally must withhold U.S. income tax from wages paid to citizens working abroad unless foreign law requires foreign withholding, although withholding may be reduced where it is reasonable to believe the employee will qualify for the FEIE or foreign housing exclusion.[1][76] Additional Medicare Tax remains relevant: for a single filer, the 2024 rate is 0.9% of Medicare wages and self-employment income above $200,000, and the instructions state that no special rule applies to U.S. citizens living abroad.[27]

The compliance burden extends beyond the income-tax return. A U.S. person with specified connections to foreign corporations, partnerships, transfers to foreign corporations, or foreign disregarded entities may have Form 5471, 8865, 926, or 8858 reporting duties.[1] A direct or indirect shareholder of a **passive foreign investment company (PFIC)**—a foreign pooled investment vehicle subject to special U.S. rules—may have to file Form 8621 after a distribution, a recognized disposition gain, elections, or an annual reporting trigger.[1] Late or incomplete international information returns can trigger penalties, extend or suspend the Form 1040 assessment period, and reduce an allowable FTC.[1]

The citizenship-based-tax risk is therefore both a residual-tax risk and an administration risk. The client should retain German tax assessments, wage statements, work-location records, investment tax records, and foreign-entity documentation in forms that support the U.S. return, FTC calculation, and any FEIE election.[1][59]

Renouncing citizenship is not a short-cut to be assumed in this comparison. The IRS’s 2024 Schedule D instructions state that a person who gives up U.S. citizenship may be treated as selling all property at fair market value on the day before doing so; the rule also applies to long-term U.S. residents who cease to be lawful permanent residents. The instructions direct taxpayers to Publication 519 and Form 8854 for the deemed-sale details, exceptions, and reporting rules. The 2024 covered-expatriate exclusion amount for that deemed-sale income was $866,000, and covered-expatriate status included an average annual net-income-tax test above $201,000 for the preceding five years, absent an exception.[51][14] Those rules concern expatriation, not an ordinary move to Germany while retaining U.S. citizenship.

U.S. capital gains are generally reported on Form 8949 and Schedule D with Form 1040; eligible German income tax may be addressed on Form 1116.[77]

For deciding whether a U.S. income-tax return is required, the IRS’s 2024 Publication 54 includes in gross income amounts that the taxpayer may exclude as foreign earned income or as a foreign housing amount.[1]

IRS Publication 54 states that U.S. citizens remain subject to U.S. tax on worldwide income and may be able to claim a foreign tax credit for foreign taxes on foreign-source income; Form 1116 calculates the allowable credit.[1]

The no-state-income-tax assumption must be independently verified: retaining domicile or sufficient ties to a U.S. state can leave a taxpayer exposed to that state’s income tax despite living in Germany.

### FATCA

**FATCA**, the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act regime, is principally an information-reporting system for foreign financial accounts and institutions; it is not an extra payroll or income-tax deduction in the take-home calculation. Under the U.S.–Germany FATCA intergovernmental agreement (IGA), a U.S. citizen is a U.S. Person.[78]

Germany must obtain information on U.S. Reportable Accounts held at Reporting German Financial Institutions and exchange it automatically with the United States annually under treaty Article 26. The reported data include the account holder’s name, address, U.S. taxpayer identification number, date of birth, account number, account balance or value, income, and the reporting German financial institution’s name and identification number; relevant account reporting also includes gross interest, dividends, other income, and gross sale or redemption proceeds.[78][79] The competent authorities intend to exchange the described information automatically within nine months after the end of the calendar year to which it relates.[80] Reporting German Financial Institutions identify U.S. Reportable Accounts and report annually to the German competent authority, which then exchanges the information under the IGA.[78][79]

For a new individual account, a Reporting German Financial Institution must obtain self-certification of whether the holder is U.S. tax resident. Under the IGA, a U.S. citizen is considered U.S. tax resident for this purpose even if also tax-resident elsewhere. If the self-certification establishes U.S. tax residence, the institution treats the account as U.S. Reportable and obtains a U.S. TIN, commonly through Form W-9 or a comparable form.[78] If changed circumstances make the certification unreliable and the institution cannot obtain valid replacement documentation, it must treat the account as reportable.[78]

For pre-existing accounts, institutions review electronically searchable records for U.S. indicia, including U.S. citizenship or residence, U.S. birthplace, U.S. mailing or residence address, U.S. telephone number, standing transfer instructions to a U.S. account, U.S.-address power of attorney, and certain hold-mail or in-care-of addresses.[78] German financial institutions also use customer due diligence and self-disclosures to determine tax residence.[79][81]

Relocation to Germany creates recurring visibility and reconciliation risk, not a new individual tax rate. German bank and investment-account information can reach the IRS through annual automatic exchange, while the taxpayer separately reports income, accounts, and assets on required U.S. forms.[78][79][81]

The FATCA withholding consequence under Internal Revenue Code section 1471 principally concerns institutions, not the individual employee’s salary or personal capital gain. A Reporting German Financial Institution satisfying IGA conditions is treated as compliant and not subject to section 1471 withholding; a significantly non-compliant institution can ultimately be treated as nonparticipating and face 30% withholding in the stated circumstances.[78][80] This institutional consequence should not be entered as a 30% tax on scenario A or B income.

The individual must separately test two U.S. foreign-account reporting regimes:

The higher “living abroad” Form 8938 thresholds do not arise merely because the taxpayer moved. They require a foreign tax home, satisfaction of a bona fide residence test including an entire tax year or a 330-full-day foreign-presence test ending during the year, and no applicable exception.[83] A mover who arrived during 2024 may instead fall under the lower U.S.-resident thresholds if those conditions are not met.

For an unmarried individual living in the United States, the stated thresholds are more than $50,000 at year-end or more than $75,000 at any time.[83]

Form 8938 and FBAR are overlapping, not substitute, regimes. The Form 8938 instructions state that filing Form 8938 does not relieve a taxpayer otherwise required to file FinCEN Form 114, Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR); filing the FBAR likewise does not relieve a Form 8938 obligation.[83][84] The distinction matters for a German brokerage account holding U.S. stocks: the foreign account itself may be reportable through both regimes even though the account’s individual contents generally are not separately listed on either report.[82][84]

Foreign mutual funds and foreign-issued cash-value insurance or annuity contracts are listed as reportable on both Form 8938 and FBAR, subject to the forms’ respective rules.[82] Directly held foreign real estate is not reportable on either form, whereas an interest in a foreign entity holding real estate can be a Form 8938 asset.[82][84] A foreign pension or deferred-compensation interest can be reportable on Form 8938 if the threshold is met, while rights to foreign-government social-insurance benefits are not specified foreign financial assets.[83][84]

A FATCA IGA exclusion is not the same as an individual Form 8938 exclusion. The IGA lists specified German employer retirement arrangements, qualifying private pensions with annual contributions no greater than €50,000, specified escrow accounts, and qualifying housing-savings contracts as not being U.S. Reportable Accounts under the agreement.[78] Form 8938 instructions nonetheless require reporting of certain retirement, pension, and nonretirement savings accounts when Form 8938 is required, regardless of an IGA exclusion.[83]

The Form 8938 penalty regime is substantial. A required but incomplete or incorrect Form 8938 may produce a $10,000 penalty. After IRS notice, continuing failure beyond 90 days can cause an additional $10,000 for each 30-day period or part-period, up to a $50,000 additional penalty; a tax underpayment involving an undisclosed specified foreign financial asset may also carry a 40% accuracy-related penalty.[83] Failures can lead to criminal penalties and can keep the assessment period open until three years after Form 8938 is filed; omission of more than $5,000 of income associated with specified foreign financial assets can produce a six-year assessment period.[83]

The practical FATCA decision is to reconcile every German account, fund, pension arrangement, and signatory authority annually against FBAR, Form 8938, PFIC, and income-reporting tests. This creates a compliance cost, but avoids treating bank information already exchanged with tax authorities as though it were invisible.[78][76][83][1]

For FBAR and Form 8938 valuations, the required exchange rate is the December 31 quarter-end U.S. Treasury exchange rate.[85]

Form 8938 is required only where the taxpayer must file a U.S. income-tax return as well as meeting the applicable asset threshold. A specified foreign financial asset reported on Form 3520, 3520-A, 5471, 8621, 8865, or 8891 need not be detailed again as an asset on Form 8938, but the taxpayer must identify the relevant forms in Part IV and must include the asset’s value in testing the Form 8938 reporting threshold.[86][87]

For Form 8938 valuation, the taxpayer generally reports a reasonable estimate of the asset’s highest fair-market value during the tax year. Periodic account statements issued at least annually may be used for a financial account’s maximum value, and a non-account asset’s year-end value may be used where it reasonably approximates the maximum. Foreign-currency assets are converted using the U.S. Treasury Bureau of the Fiscal Service exchange rate on the last day of the tax year, or another publicly available rate if Treasury has no rate for that currency.[86]

The FBAR is due April 15 following the calendar year for accounts over which the filer had a financial interest or signature authority during that year, is filed electronically through FinCEN’s BSA E-Filing System, and receives an automatic six-month extension to October 15. Form 8938 is attached to the annual return and is due on that return’s due date, including applicable extensions.[86][82]

German banks, brokers, insurers, and investment providers may request a U.S. Social Security number or other U.S. tax-residency certification and may restrict products, decline an account, or review or close an account when documentation is incomplete.

For 2024, a taxpayer with foreign financial assets may have to file Form 8938. A taxpayer who had a foreign account, or who received a distribution from, was a grantor of, or was a transferor to a foreign trust, must complete Part III of Schedule B and may also have to file Form 3520.[25]

German financial institutions collect customers’ residential address, every country of tax residence, and the respective tax-identification numbers through self-disclosure, and customers must notify relevant changes.[81] For customers resident abroad for tax purposes, institutions report this information annually to the Federal Central Tax Office (BZSt), which forwards it to the tax authority of each country of tax residence; the reported data include interest, dividends, capital gains from financial assets, country of residence, and tax-identification number.[81] U.S. citizenship and a U.S. address are separate FATCA indicators requiring a review for U.S. tax liability.[81]

### German residency rules

German domestic residence is determined by facts, not citizenship. Under section 1(1) of the Einkommensteuergesetz (EStG), an individual who has either a German **Wohnsitz** (domicile or residence) or **gewöhnlicher Aufenthalt** (habitual abode) is subject to **unbeschränkte Einkommensteuerpflicht**, meaning unlimited German income-tax liability.[40]

A Wohnsitz exists where the taxpayer has a dwelling in circumstances indicating that it will be retained and used.[55][88] A rented flat, owned home, house, or furnished room can qualify as a dwelling when the taxpayer has actual control and uses it with some regularity; no statutory minimum number of annual days is required for this analysis.[55] Registration with local authorities is evidence of residence but does not itself create a Wohnsitz.[55]

A gewöhnlicher Aufenthalt is a stay in circumstances showing that the person is present other than temporarily.[89][55] Under section 9 of the Abgabenordnung (AO), a continuous stay of more than six months is treated as a habitual abode from the beginning of the stay, and short interruptions do not break continuity.[89] The six-month period need not fall within one calendar year.[55]

**The key German timing risk is factual: acquiring and retaining a usable German home can establish unlimited German tax liability without waiting for the more-than-six-month habitual-abode rule.** A person who arrives with a leased apartment for an indefinite relocation can become German-resident before the six-month rule would independently apply.[55][89]

Unlimited liability generally reaches worldwide income, subject to applicable treaty allocation and relief rules.[56] For the take-home comparison, the date German unlimited tax liability begins therefore affects whether 2024 salary and realized gains enter Germany’s worldwide-income system.

A taxpayer without a German Wohnsitz or gewöhnlicher Aufenthalt may instead be subject only to **beschränkte Einkommensteuerpflicht**, or limited German tax liability, on qualifying German-source income.[40]

The six-month statutory rule has an exception for stays exclusively for visiting, recreation, medical treatment, or similar private purposes that do not exceed one year.[89] That exception does not describe an employee relocating to work in Germany.

A short business trip and hotel stay generally do not establish residence or habitual abode on their own, but a related extended assignment can cause the continuous habitual-abode period to begin with the earlier trip.[55]

For a move during calendar year 2024, Germany’s income tax is an annual tax, but a shift between limited and unlimited tax liability produces separate factual periods. A single assessment under unlimited liability can include income from the earlier limited-liability period, yet the taxpayer is not treated as unlimitedly taxable for the entire calendar year and the periods remain separately determined.[56] This prevents a simplistic assumption that all 2024 income is automatically German-resident income merely because the move occurred in 2024.

The German calculation therefore needs the actual move date, German housing availability date, workday locations, departure from any U.S. home, and dates of realized gains. Those facts determine the taxable period before applying German rates, social insurance, or treaty relief.[40][55][89][56]

The competent German tax office is generally determined by the taxpayer’s Wohnsitz or, if there is none, gewöhnlicher Aufenthalt; with multiple German residences, jurisdiction normally follows where the taxpayer predominantly stays. [126][89][127]

A German domicile is a fact-based home or dwelling owned or rented by the taxpayer and held under circumstances showing that it will be retained and used; actual control and regular use are relevant, while address registration is evidence rather than determinative.[55][88] Short interruptions, including holidays, trips home, and business travel, do not break a continuous habitual-abode period.[70]

Address registration is a separate administrative obligation: the Taxes for Expats guide states that a person must register the German address at the Einwohnermeldeamt or Bürgeramt within two weeks of moving into the residence.[90]

Family circumstances and any continuing U.S. home should also be documented because they can bear on the factual residence analysis and, if dual residence arises, the treaty centre-of-vital-interests test.

A person who is not German-resident may elect treatment as a German resident if at least 90% of worldwide income is subject to German taxation, or if income not subject to German taxation does not exceed €11,604 in calendar year 2024.[21]

German domestic residence and treaty residence answer different questions. A German Wohnsitz can coexist with a home or habitual abode abroad, and German unlimited tax liability does not depend on the centre of vital interests in that dual-residence situation.[56][91] Treaty residence instead determines treaty entitlement and allocation of taxing rights; German unlimited tax liability can continue when the treaty assigns residence to the other contracting state.[56][91]

### treaty provisions

The 1989 U.S.–Germany income-tax Convention applied to other income taxes for taxable years or assessment periods beginning on or after January 1, 1990. The 2006 Protocol entered into force on December 28, 2007, and its amendments for other income taxes applied to taxable years on or after January 1, 2008; both were therefore applicable in 2024.[38][80] The treaty allocates taxing rights and provides relief; it does not itself create a domestic tax where domestic law imposes none.[52]

**Article 4 — Residence** is the starting point. Treaty residence means liability to tax in a country because of domicile, residence, place of management, or a similar personal connection; taxation only on local-source income does not qualify.[36][38] If both countries treat an individual as resident under domestic law, the treaty applies the following tie-breaker sequence: permanent home; centre of vital interests, meaning closer personal and economic relations; habitual abode; nationality; and competent-authority mutual agreement.[38][39]

For this treaty, U.S. citizenship alone is not enough to make someone a U.S. treaty resident. Germany is to treat a U.S. citizen or green-card holder as U.S.-resident for treaty purposes only if the individual has substantial presence, a permanent home, or a habitual abode in the United States.[38][36][54] This treaty-residence determination matters for treaty allocation but does not erase the U.S. citizen’s domestic-law worldwide tax exposure because of the saving clause.[36][39]

Article 15 — Dependent Personal Services governs ordinary employment remuneration. Salary is taxable in the employee’s treaty residence state unless the employment is exercised in the other treaty state; where work is physically exercised in the other state, that other state may also tax the remuneration attributable to that work.[41][39] The physical location where services are performed matters more than the employer’s payment account or corporate headquarters.[59]

The Article 15(2) short-stay exception requires all three conditions: the employee is present in the work state for no more than 183 days in the calendar year; the employer is not resident in that state; and remuneration is not borne by a permanent establishment or fixed base there.[41][39] The 183-day condition alone is never sufficient.[59] Presence days for this threshold differ from workdays used to allocate salary between countries, and both should be documented with travel calendars, flight records, hotel invoices, calendars, and employer records.[59]

For scenario A and the salary component of scenario B, a genuine German relocation and German work location make Germany central under Articles 4 and 15. A temporary cross-border arrangement may retain residence-state-only taxation under Article 15(2), but only where every one of its three conditions is satisfied.[38][41]

Article 13 — Gains governs capital gains. Gains from immovable property may be taxed where the property is located, including specified indirect interests in real-estate-rich entities.[38][39] Gains from movable property connected with a permanent establishment or fixed base may also be taxed where that establishment or base is located.[39] Subject to these and other stated exceptions, gains from property not covered by Article 13’s earlier paragraphs are taxable only in the seller’s treaty residence state.[38][39]

Article 13 also has targeted departure and former-residence rules. Where departure-country law treats a person as having sold property on cessation of residence and taxes that deemed sale, the individual may elect for the other treaty country to treat the property as sold and reacquired at fair market value immediately before residence changed.[38][36] A former resident state may tax gains on at least a 25% interest in a company resident there if the sale occurs within ten years after residence ends, but only to the extent gain accrued during the former residence period.[41][39]

For an ordinary portfolio gain, Article 13(5) points to the seller’s treaty residence; real-property, permanent-establishment, departure-tax, and at-least-25% former-residence-shareholding exceptions can produce a different result. The $30,000 scenario-B gain must therefore be classified by asset, ownership percentage, location, acquisition basis, and sale date before applying the treaty.[38][41][36]

Article 23 — Relief from Double Taxation is the key provision preventing simple double counting. The United States allows a U.S. citizen or resident an FTC for German income tax paid or accrued, subject to U.S. law’s limitations.[38][52] Germany generally exempts certain income that the treaty permits the United States to tax, with progression, but gives a credit rather than exemption for specified categories, including certain dividends and defined gains.[38][36]

For a U.S. citizen resident in Germany, Article 23’s special ordering rule applies to the specified U.S.-source income described above. Germany first credits only U.S. tax imposed consistently with the treaty rather than citizenship-only tax; the United States then credits German income tax after that German credit, with treaty re-sourcing to the extent necessary to prevent double taxation.[36][38] **The treaty thus coordinates overlapping income taxes; it does not justify adding full U.S. and German income-tax liabilities as two deductions from the same income.**[36][39]

The treaty does not resolve payroll social-insurance coverage. Article 2 excludes U.S. Social Security taxes from covered taxes, and Article 18 addresses taxation of social-security benefits rather than employee or employer contributions.[38][36] The applicable social-security system is governed separately by the U.S.–Germany Social Security Agreement, not Article 15’s employment-income allocation.[59]

Article 25 — Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) provides a formal route where the taxpayer believes one or both countries have produced treaty-inconsistent tax. The case must be presented within four years after notification of the assessment producing the claimed double or treaty-inconsistent taxation.[38][39] Qualifying unresolved cases can proceed to arbitration under stated conditions; binding arbitration is expressly available for unresolved natural-person residence disputes and specified permanent-establishment, business-profits, associated-enterprises, and royalties matters unless the authorities find a particular case unsuitable.[36]

Article 26 — Exchange of Information and Administrative Assistance permits exchange of tax information needed to administer the Convention and covered domestic taxes. The competent authorities’ information-gathering powers include information held by financial institutions, nominees, fiduciaries, and legal-entity ownership information.[38][36][52] Article 26 is distinct from FATCA, although the U.S.–Germany FATCA IGA uses Article 26 as the treaty basis for automatic account-information exchange.[45][78][80]

Article 28 — Limitation on Benefits conditions treaty benefits on qualified-person status and other applicable conditions. An individual is a qualified-person category, although residence and the conditions of the particular substantive treaty provision still matter.[36][38] This is usually less problematic for an individual employee than for corporate structures, but it remains relevant to treaty claims involving investment structures or beneficial ownership.

Article 24 — Nondiscrimination prohibits either country from imposing on the other country’s nationals, in the same circumstances, taxation or connected requirements that are more burdensome than those imposed on its own nationals; it applies even where a person is not resident in one or both countries. The Protocol expressly says this rule does not require the United States to give a nonresident German national the same tax regime that it gives a nonresident U.S. citizen.[45]

Article 29 — Refund of withholding tax means that treaty limits on source-country withholding do not prevent the source country from withholding first at its domestic-law rate on dividends, interest, royalties, or other income. The taxpayer must apply for a refund of withholding above the treaty-permitted amount within four years after the end of the calendar year in which the income was received.[45]

Under **Article 26 — Exchange of Information and Administrative Assistance**, Germany may exchange information with the United States with or without request under the stated German-law framework.

Where Article 25 arbitration applies, the arbitration board determines only the amount of income, expense, or tax reportable to the two countries. It applies the Convention first, then agreed country commentaries or explanations, then non-conflicting domestic laws, and then relevant analogous OECD materials. Its determination binds the countries, gives no rationale, has no precedential value, and each concerned person must notify the competent authority within 30 days of receipt whether that person accepts it.[45]

Article 2’s covered-tax list includes German Einkommensteuer, corporation tax, trade tax and capital tax and U.S. federal income taxes; it does not list U.S. state income taxes, German real-estate transfer tax, or annual German property tax. The Convention itself sets no tax rate: tax cost arises under the underlying domestic taxes.[57]

Germany applies the economic-employer approach in interpreting OECD treaty Article 15. There is no fixed minimum number of days under that approach: even an assignment of three months or less is not a firm de minimis exception, and integration into the host company’s business operations is material.[70]

German domestic law has a separate private-sale rule for German land: § 23 EStG applies where no more than ten years elapse between purchase and sale. Property used exclusively as the owner’s residence from purchase to sale, or in the sale year and the preceding two years, is outside that rule.[57]

The Bundeszentralamt für Steuern states that double-taxation agreements are published in their current language on the Federal Finance Ministry website.[92]

A taxpayer taking a treaty position that requires disclosure files Form 8833; a U.S. foreign-tax-credit claim is made on Form 1116.[75]

Article 1 — General Scope contains the saving clause: except for specified benefits, the Convention does not affect U.S. taxation of its citizens and residents. The preserved benefits include Article 13(6), Article 23, Article 24, and Article 25.[38][36]

OECD TaxBEN is the OECD Tax-Benefit model for comparing tax liabilities and benefit entitlements; it is not the requested OECD tax treaty database. This section establishes no OECD-database treaty-status result.[4]

The U.S.–Germany Social Security Agreement, rather than the income-tax treaty, determines whether 2024 employment is covered by U.S. Social Security or German social insurance. A certificate of coverage may be needed to document the applicable system and support exemption from the other country’s contributions.

A German move can make Germany the main country taxing salary and ordinary investment income under its domestic rules and treaty Articles 4, 13 and 15, but U.S. citizenship preserves U.S. worldwide-income taxation. Article 23 and domestic FTC rules are therefore essential to the German-relocation net-income calculation; simply adding both countries’ gross tax liabilities overstates the burden where credit relief applies, while assuming complete relief understates residual risk where sourcing, timing, basis, or credit limitations differ.[36][39]

FATCA, FBAR, Form 8938, PFIC reporting, and foreign-entity forms create a parallel compliance obligation. German residence is fact-led and can begin with a usable German dwelling before the six-month habitual-abode rule. The common planning requirement is a dated record of homes, travel, workdays, salary allocation, accounts, asset basis, and German taxes paid.[55][89][76][83][1][59]

## Actionable recommendation

### stated assumptions

Judgment — The calculations should be read as a comparison of two mutually exclusive full-year cases, not as a literal model of a move made partway through 2024. That distinction matters because the location where employment services are physically performed determines wage sourcing, and a relocation year can require the salary to be divided between U.S. and German workdays.

For the U.S. comparator, assume the taxpayer is single, is a U.S. citizen, is resident and working in the United States for all of calendar year 2024, earns all W-2 wages for services performed there, has no state income-tax liability, takes no itemized deductions or tax credits beyond those built into the comparison, and has single-person health coverage. This removes foreign-workday allocation: when an employee performs services in more than one country, U.S. foreign-tax-credit sourcing generally allocates compensation on a time basis.[32] The U.S. result is therefore a domestic 2024 baseline, rather than a result available to the person after relocation.

For the Germany-relocation comparator, assume the taxpayer establishes a dwelling and German unlimited income-tax liability on 1 January 2024, remains there through 31 December 2024, and receives the stated salary amounts as compensation for employment performed in Germany. Whether a U.S. employer continues to issue a Form W-2, whether payroll is instead operated through a German employer or affiliate, and whether a certificate of coverage preserves U.S. social-insurance coverage are unresolved personal facts. Compensation is sourced by where services are performed, rather than by the employer or payment account.[32][60][1][2] Assume also that the taxpayer is treaty-resident only in Germany for the year.

Under German domestic law, an individual with a German residence or habitual abode is subject to unlimited income-tax liability. The treaty first asks whether each country treats the person as resident under domestic law, then uses permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality and, if necessary, competent-authority agreement to assign treaty residence.[40][38][54]

The Germany case further assumes that the person has no U.S. permanent home, no closer centre of vital interests in the United States, and no U.S. workdays after the move. These assumptions are consequential, not cosmetic. Article 15 provides that employment remuneration is generally taxable in the residence state unless the employment is exercised in the other state; the short-stay exception requires all three conditions—no more than 183 days’ presence in the work state during the calendar year, a nonresident employer, and no remuneration borne by a permanent establishment or fixed base in that work state.[41][54]

Judgment — Under those full-year assumptions, the salary comparison should use physically U.S.-performed wages in the U.S. case and physically Germany-performed wages in the Germany case. A real 2024 move changes the model: salary must be allocated by actual workdays, rather than assigned merely by the employer’s country, W-2 reporting, payroll location or bank account. The U.S. rule expressly treats the location of services as the source of salary even when a U.S. employer pays the wages into a U.S. account.[1][60]

The $30,000 in Scenario B is assumed to be a net, realized capital gain: sale proceeds less documented tax basis and all relevant basis adjustments. Basis is the taxpayer’s investment in property for tax purposes and is usually acquisition cost, but records may require adjustments for commissions, improvements, depreciation, nondividend distributions and stock splits.[51] The model should therefore not treat $30,000 of cash proceeds or portfolio appreciation as equivalent to a $30,000 taxable gain.

For the base-case German treatment in Scenario B, assume the gain is from ordinary portfolio shares acquired after 31 December 2008, held as a private investment rather than business property, and is not from real property, a partnership or permanent-establishment asset. The available German guidance states that gains on such post-2008 shares are fully taxable and subject to a 25% withholding tax regardless of holding period.[21] It also assumes the shares do not represent an interest of at least 25% in a U.S.-resident company.

Article 13 contains a special ten-year former-residence rule for that substantial shareholding, and it cannot be applied to an unspecified ordinary portfolio gain.[41][39]

The treaty assumption for ordinary gains is that the asset falls within Article 13(5), rather than one of the treaty’s exceptions. That paragraph assigns gains from property not covered by the preceding paragraphs to the alienator’s state of residence. It does not decide the domestic tax base, rate, withholding mechanics or U.S. citizenship-based tax consequence.[41][39] Gains on immovable property, interests in real-estate-rich entities, permanent-establishment business property, and the specified substantial corporate holding each follow different treaty rules.[38][36][39]

For U.S. purposes, assume the gain is an eligible taxable capital gain reported on the U.S. return and is not foreign earned income. Investment income is not foreign earned income, even though it must be reported in gross income.[1] The model must also separately consider the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), which applies to a U.S. citizen or resident with modified adjusted gross income above the applicable threshold and net investment income; the single-filer threshold is $200,000.[31] A taxable gain may be net investment income, subject to the stated trade-or-business exceptions and deductions allocable to it.[31]

The U.S. foreign earned income exclusion (FEIE) is not assumed automatically in the Germany calculation. For 2024, the maximum FEIE is $126,500, but it applies only to qualifying foreign earned income and requires both a foreign tax home and either bona fide foreign residence or 330 full days of physical presence abroad during a 12-consecutive-month period.[1][64] A taxpayer moving during 2024 may need to prorate the exclusion or may not have completed a qualifying period by the original return due date.[1][64] If the FEIE is used, foreign taxes attributable to excluded wages cannot also be fully used as foreign tax credits (FTCs), the credits that offset U.S. tax with qualifying foreign income taxes.[1][60]

Judgment — For a high German tax-rate employment case, the preferred U.S. double-tax-relief approach will often be an FTC analysis rather than a reflexive FEIE election, because the FTC can preserve relief for German wage tax while the FEIE prevents a credit for tax allocable to excluded wages. This is a planning judgment, not a universal result: the answer depends on actual German tax, foreign-source income, deductions allocated to that income, other U.S. income, the timing of tax payment or accrual, and whether the taxpayer qualifies for the FEIE.

The FTC calculation must separate income categories rather than net all German tax against all U.S. tax. Wages and salary are generally general-category income, while non-business capital gains are generally passive-category income; separate Forms 1116 may be needed.[93][32] The credit limitation also requires foreign gross income to be reduced by deductions definitely related to it and a ratable share of other deductions, including the standard deduction for a non-itemizer.[32] A German-resident result must therefore show German tax once and then subtract only the final residual U.S. tax after available relief; it must not add the two countries’ headline taxes as if both were independently payable in full.

For social insurance, the base calculation assumes German coverage. Whether that treatment applies depends on the actual employer, hiring arrangement, posting duration and any certificate of coverage: an employee working in Germany is normally covered by Germany, while a U.S. employer that sends the worker to Germany for five years or less leaves coverage in the U.S. system.[2] Under the U.S.–Germany Totalization Agreement—the bilateral agreement intended to prevent social-security tax on the same earnings in both countries—an employee working in Germany is normally covered by Germany.

A U.S. employer that sends the employee to Germany for five years or less leaves coverage in the U.S.; a posting beyond five years, a German hire, or a non-U.S. employer produces German coverage.[2]

The German calculation should assume employee contributions to the general statutory pension scheme, unemployment insurance, statutory health insurance (gesetzliche Krankenversicherung, GKV), and social long-term-care insurance (soziale Pflegeversicherung). The 2024 employee pension rate is 9.30%; unemployment insurance is 1.30%; statutory health insurance is 7.30% plus half of the applicable supplementary contribution; and long-term-care insurance is 1.70% plus a 0.60% employee-only surcharge if childless.[94] For an ordinary employee in western Germany, the Beitragsbemessungsgrenze der Renten- und Arbeitslosenversicherung—contribution assessment ceiling for pension and unemployment insurance—is €90,600 annually and €7,550 monthly in 2024.

Statutory health-insurance contributions are capped at €62,100 annually and €5,175 monthly from 1 January 2024; this is the maximum earnings base, not a premium or contribution rate.[94][62]

The German base case uses the official 2024 average GKV supplementary contribution of 1.70%, split equally between employee and employer, solely as a modelling assumption. The payroll rules specify that the employee’s actual sickness-fund rate, not the average rate, is used in calculating that employee’s contribution.[33][34] The base case assumes the person is childless, not a church-tax member, and voluntarily GKV-insured rather than privately insured. Each of these assumptions has a direct cash effect.

The 2024 German payroll assumptions use Steuerklasse I. [110][25][111][112][95][67][113][74][100]

At the assumed income level, the employee is above the 2024 annual health-insurance compulsory threshold of €69,300 and may choose private health insurance (private Krankenversicherung, PKV) or voluntary GKV coverage.[62][95] The statutory ceilings still cap the GKV and care contributions, but private insurance uses an individual contract and cannot be modelled accurately without the insurer’s quotation, age, health status, tariff, deductible and employer subsidy. The German comparator therefore uses GKV only as a transparent standardised insurance arrangement, not as a claim about the individual’s actual policy.

For the domestic U.S. comparator, assume employer-sponsored single coverage and show $1,368 as a health-premium benchmark rather than a personal premium. KFF reports a 2024 average annual worker contribution of $1,368 for single coverage and $6,296 for family coverage.[96][97] KFF’s 2024 employer survey reports average single-coverage annual premiums of $8,951 and an average worker contribution of $1,368, with the worker contributing 16% on average.[10][96] The survey covers employer-sponsored plans rather than every U.S. employee and cannot capture all features of a particular plan, including varied premium contributions, drug benefits and wellness incentives.[10]

The comparison currency is an analytical presentation choice, separate from tax-return conversion. The IRS 2024 annual-average table gives €0.924 per US$1 and directs users to multiply dollars by that rate to convert to euros.[11] Thus, the side-by-side table may use €0.924/US$1, equivalently US$1.08225/€1, for a common-currency annual comparison.

The IRS nevertheless requires U.S. tax-return amounts to be expressed in dollars and generally translated at the exchange rate prevailing when income, expenses or taxes are received, paid or accrued.[11][98]

**This assumption set makes the comparison decision-useful: it distinguishes a full-year location choice from an actual split-year move, avoids double-counting U.S. and German tax, and identifies the insurance, gain and residence facts that can change the answer.**

The German GKV base-case employee health-insurance deduction is 8.15% of covered earnings: 7.30% is the employee half of the 14.6% general rate and 0.85% is the employee half of the 1.70% average supplementary contribution, subject to the €62,100 annual ceiling. Deutsche Rentenversicherung’s rates effective from 1 January 2024 show the 1.70% average contribution split 0.85% to employee and 0.85% to employer, but state that the average rate applies only to specified groups, for example Bürgergeld recipients and trainees. The OECD records that the supplementary contribution depends on the individual sickness fund.[94][4]

The employee take-home calculation excludes statutory occupational accident insurance because it is employer-funded. It also excludes employer shares of pension, unemployment, health and care contributions: German social-insurance contributions are generally shared between employer and employee and subject to contribution ceilings.[4]

German wage tax (Lohnsteuer) withheld monthly is treated as a prepayment of the employee’s annual personal income tax, not as a second income-tax deduction in addition to the final German income-tax liability.[4]

For U.S. foreign-tax-credit reporting, the wage case does not meet the simplified election to claim a foreign tax credit without Form 1116: that election requires all foreign-source gross income to be passive income, all income and foreign taxes to be reported on a qualified payee statement, and no more than $300 of creditable foreign taxes for a non-joint filer or $600 for Married Filing Jointly.[47] Foreign wages mean that the all-passive-income condition is not met.[47]

For the German employee social-care-insurance assumption, childlessness means a 4.0% total nursing-insurance rate, split between a 1.70% employer contribution and a 2.30% employee contribution. An employee with one child pays 1.70% employee-side; reductions for a second through fifth or later child apply only through the end of the month in which each child turns 25, and Saxony has special rates.[70]

The U.S.–Germany Totalization Agreement has been effective since 1 December 1979. It covers Social Security taxes, including the U.S. Medicare portion, and, since 1 May 1996, taxes financing German sickness insurance and long-term nursing care; its purpose includes preventing both countries’ Social Security taxes from applying to the same earnings.[2]

The German health-insurance choice follows the general Jahresarbeitsentgeltgrenze (JAEG), the annual earnings threshold: for most employees it is €69,300 in 2024. An employee earning above that threshold is no longer compulsorily insured and may choose voluntary GKV coverage.[95]

For the German capital-income assumption, the Sparer-Pauschbetrag (saver’s allowance) is €1,000 for a single person. The €2,000 maximum applies only to spouses or registered partners making a joint exemption order who satisfy the stated conditions for joint assessment.[99][100][72]

For 2024, a U.S. citizen or resident alien living and working abroad remains taxed on worldwide income and may qualify to exclude up to $126,500 of foreign earnings under the FEIE, in addition to qualifying foreign-housing amounts.[1]

The model also assumes no business income, retirement contributions, stock compensation, carried interest, rental income, tax credits, itemized deductions, dependants, or other income unless expressly shown. It excludes cryptocurrency, PFIC interests, collectibles, partnership interests, special investment funds and other assets whose U.S. or German tax character differs from an ordinary private portfolio-share gain.

The 2024 comparison also assumes no HSA contributions, bonus-timing effects or non-cash benefits. [110][25][111][112][95][67][113][74][100]

### data-quality flags

The tax table should be treated as a standardised estimate, not a personal tax assessment. The German Federal Ministry of Finance states that its income-tax calculator computes tariff income tax under section 32a of the Income Tax Act from taxable income (zu versteuerndes Einkommen), while the actual assessment considers individual circumstances and current law. It expressly warns that taxable income is not the same as gross wages, receipts, income categories or income in the ordinary sense.[101][18]

This matters most for the German salary line. Statutory employee social-insurance contributions, deductible insurance expenses, pension-contribution treatment, payroll class, church-tax status, other income, deductions and the exact period of German unlimited tax liability can all alter taxable income. Statutory pension contributions are among the retirement expenses recognized as special expenses in 2024, subject to the interaction with the tax-free employer contribution.[94] The BMF also states that its wage-tax calculator is not a gross-to-net calculator and does not compute individual net income after social contributions.[101][102]

A midyear arrival creates a second material limitation. German income tax is an annual tax, but a change between limited and unlimited tax liability during the calendar year requires distinct periods to be determined even where there is one annual assessment. Foreign income earned before German unlimited tax liability may also affect the tax rate through the progression clause (Progressionsvorbehalt), a rule that can use exempt foreign income to set the rate applied to German taxable income.[56] A simple full-year German wage calculation should therefore not be substituted for a 2024 arrival-date calculation.

The German domestic-residence date is itself fact-dependent. A German residence exists where a person holds a dwelling under circumstances indicating that it will be retained and used; registration alone is only an indicator.[88][55] A continuous stay of more than six months is treated as habitual abode, and the period may cross calendar years; shorter stays can also require review where the original plan was longer.[55] The move date, lease, availability of U.S. and German homes, travel pattern and intended duration therefore determine whether the person is German-resident for all or part of 2024.

The capital-gain base in Scenario B is not established by the label $30,000. The taxpayer must confirm the asset, acquisition date, purchase price, commissions, basis adjustments, disposition date, holding period, whether there are offsetting losses, and whether the amount is net of all recognised adjustments. U.S. tax treatment can differ between short-term and long-term gains, while Germany’s treatment can differ by asset class and acquisition history.[51][73][21] The base-case 25% German treatment is therefore appropriate only for the stated assumption of a private post-2008 share gain.

German capital-income taxation also has a surcharge and allowance layer. The BZSt states that capital income is generally subject to 25% withholding tax plus solidarity surcharge of 5.5% of the capital-income tax; this is a 26.375% combined rate before church tax.[103] A single person can use a €1,000 saver’s allowance (Sparer-Pauschbetrag), provided the conditions and relevant exemption order or assessment treatment are met.[100][99]Derived — Under the assumed €1,000 allowance and no church tax, the German base-case capital-income charge is 26.375% of the gain translated into euros less €1,000, not 26.375% of the full dollar amount. The conversion date and the exact taxable gain remain transaction-specific.

The available treaty material supports the residence-state allocation for ordinary gains, but the treaty does not erase U.S. citizenship-based taxation. The treaty technical explanation describes the saving clause as preserving U.S. taxation of a U.S. citizen who resides in Germany, subject to the treaty’s special foreign-tax-credit rules.[39] The proper Germany take-home calculation is therefore German tax plus the residual U.S. tax after the actual FEIE/FTC analysis, not German tax alone and not German tax plus unrelieved U.S. tax.

The U.S. FTC result cannot be inferred from German tax paid. Foreign-source wages must be sourced by services performed; if services occur in both countries, the ordinary allocation is foreign workdays divided by total workdays.[60] Personal-property gains have their own sourcing rules, which can depend on foreign tax home, whether foreign tax of at least 10% of gain is paid, asset type and exceptions for inventory, intangibles, depreciable property and foreign-office sales.[60] The actual gain’s source may therefore differ from the assumed German place of residence.

The FEIE is equally fact-sensitive. It is limited to foreign earned income and does not apply to investment income.[1] A taxpayer qualifying for the exclusion must use the tax rates that would have applied without it when calculating tax on nonexcluded income, and must reduce the creditable foreign taxes for tax allocable to excluded income.[1][60] This interaction is why a comparison should not show both a full FEIE and a full FTC against the same German wage tax.

Health insurance is a consequential but unavailable individual input. KFF’s $1,368 is an average worker contribution for U.S. employer-sponsored single coverage, not the employee’s observed contribution.[96] On the German side, GKV’s 1.70% average supplementary contribution is not an insurer-specific rate, and official payroll guidance requires use of the relevant fund’s individual rate.[33][34][65] A change from voluntary GKV to PKV may change the employee’s cash cost and the employer subsidy without changing gross salary.

The employee’s actual U.S. health-premium deduction election is unknown; it can affect both cash take-home and taxable income, so the modeled after-tax premium treatment may not match the employee’s plan. [119]

The social-insurance coverage assumption also requires documentary support. A certificate of coverage issued by the United States or Germany proves exemption from the other system’s social-security taxes on the same earnings, and the SSA advises requesting it before the cross-border work begins.[2] If the employer is a U.S. employer that has sent the worker temporarily to Germany for five years or less, the assumed German contributions may be wrong because U.S. coverage could continue.[2] Conversely, work in Germany for a German or non-U.S. employer ordinarily points to German coverage.

The exchange rate needs two separate treatments. For the comparative table, €0.924 per US$1 is a disclosed 2024 annual-average conversion convention.[11] For U.S. federal tax reporting, the taxpayer uses transaction-date rates in most cases, while foreign taxes claimed as paid or accrued can use different translation timing rules.[93][32][1] The annual conversion should therefore never be used to reconstruct Form 1116, German withholding, asset basis or gain on a particular sale.

The official treaty text issue also needs to remain visible. The Federal Foreign Office’s displayed English treaty text identifies itself as unofficial and directs readers to the official German version published in Federal Tax Gazette 2008, Part II, page 611.[38] The treaty provisions used here are suitable for planning the questions to resolve, but a treaty position on residence, capital gains or relief from double taxation should be checked against the official text and the taxpayer’s precise facts.

The comparison excludes state income tax by instruction, but U.S. state exposure remains a fact to verify before a real move. The IRS states that states may tax income sourced within their borders and that some states do not honour treaty provisions.[104] The no-state-tax assumption should therefore be supported by the taxpayer’s prior domicile, state residency-termination facts, deferred compensation, equity awards and any continuing state-source income.

**These flags rule out treating the displayed net figures as a filing-ready answer; they support using them as a disciplined location comparison while the specific inputs are confirmed.**

The German taxable-income estimate must reflect the statutory deduction structure as well as gross wages. The OECD describes deductible social-security and pension contributions as Sonderausgabenabzug (special-expense deduction): pension relief starts with both employee and employer contributions, is limited to €27,566 in 2024, and is then reduced by the tax-free employer contribution; statutory-health contributions excluding sickness benefit are modelled at 96% of the health contribution, and mandatory long-term-care contributions are deductible.[4]

For Scenario B, German gain measurement uses transaction-date euro conversion, not the annual comparison rate: for gains covered by EStG section 20(2), proceeds are converted into euros at the disposal date and acquisition cost at the acquisition date, after directly related disposal expenses.[67]

The €1,000 Sparer-Pauschbetrag (saver’s allowance) is a flat allowance for investment-income expenses. Actual investment-income expenses generally cannot additionally be deducted, even with records.[72]

A foreign-tax-credit calculation remains open until the German liability is final. The IRS describes a change in foreign tax liability affecting a claimed credit as a foreign tax redetermination; in most cases the taxpayer must redetermine U.S. tax and file Form 1040-X with a revised Form 1116 and supporting documents, and failure to notify can produce a failure-to-notify penalty.[105]

Form 1116 reporting uses U.S. dollars and, for foreign taxes paid or withheld, the exchange rate in effect on the payment or withholding date. Form 1116 also has seven foreign-income categories and requires a separate form for each category and each country within that category.[47]

The OECD TaxBEN framework covers main employment-income taxes and employee and employer social contributions, but it does not include wealth taxes, indirect taxes such as VAT, or in-kind benefits such as free health care. Its selected Germany 2024 output concerns a two-adult family with two children, not the single taxpayer in this comparison.[4]

The BZSt’s stated 26.375% rate is a withholding rate on income from capital: 25% capital-income tax plus a solidarity surcharge equal to 5.5% of that tax. The BZSt section 50c(3) refund procedure applies to specified German-source capital income subject to withholding, not to an unspecified capital gain merely because it is realized by a German resident.[106] The procedure is for non-resident taxpayers receiving German-source capital income; eligibility depends on being the beneficiary when the income accrues.[106]

Where a pre-relocation or other non-resident German-source capital-income case does qualify for BZSt relief, the application requires a certificate of residence and relevant tax certificate or certificates. The residence certificate must be verified by the tax authority competent when the income accrues; the tax certificate evidences the income type and amount and the capital-income tax and solidarity surcharge withheld.[106] The application deadline is four years after the end of the calendar year in which the income accrued, no earlier than one year after tax payment and subject to any later treaty deadline.[106] BZSt states that processing can exceed 20 months and that an application cannot be processed until complete documentation is submitted.[106]

The U.S. health-premium benchmark excludes medical cost sharing. KFF reported that 87% of workers with single employer-sponsored coverage had a general annual deductible in 2024 and that the average deductible among those workers was $1,787.[97] The take-home comparison counts the $1,368 employee premium benchmark, not a deductible or realized out-of-pocket medical spending.[97]

The U.S. result also lacks information on prior-year foreign-tax-credit carryovers, estimated-tax payments, employer equity arrangements, deferred compensation and other foreign taxes. These items can change the residual U.S. liability even when the German income-tax and social-insurance calculation is unchanged.

The request does not supply the German federal state: in 2024, the monthly contribution ceilings for pension and unemployment insurance were €7,550 in western Germany and €7,450 in eastern Germany.[62]

### Decision and next checks

Judgment — On the modelled 2024 cash take-home alone, remain in the no-income-tax U.S. state in both Scenario A and Scenario B. The model also leaves the United States ahead at the −10% and +10% Scenario B endpoints: at −10%, U.S. net take-home is $155,514.30 (€143,695.21) versus Germany’s €113,232.57 ($122,546.07), and at +10%, it is $188,190.30 (€173,887.84) versus Germany’s €136,984.45 ($148,251.57). The annual comparison uses €0.924 per US$1.[11][25][21][14][9][6][7][10][23][31][26][33][35][27] The calculated zero-gain and short-term-gain cases also favour the United States; long-term gains use distinct U.S. rate brackets.[8] This recommendation is on cash take-home only and uses the $1,368 U.S. single-coverage worker-contribution benchmark.[10]

For Scenario A, salary only, the deciding variable is the final residual U.S. tax after German tax and available relief, alongside the social-insurance system actually applicable. A German-resident U.S. citizen remains subject to U.S. worldwide-income taxation, but qualifying foreign earnings may be eligible for FEIE and/or foreign-tax-credit treatment.[1][76] Germany normally covers an employee working there, while a qualifying short U.S. posting may retain U.S. Social Security coverage.[2] The recommendation therefore changes if the certificate of coverage changes: a Germany net calculation built on German Sozialversicherung cannot be used for an employee validly remaining in the U.S. system.

For Scenario B, salary plus gains, Germany is more attractive only if the gains are the assumed ordinary portfolio-share gains, German residence applies when the sale occurs, and U.S. residual tax after the passive-category FTC and NIIT analysis does not consume the apparent difference. Article 13(5) assigns ordinary residual gains to the seller’s residence state, whereas a U.S. citizen remains within the treaty saving clause for U.S. taxation.[41][39] The gain cannot be treated as exempt from U.S. tax merely because the taxpayer has become German resident.

The recommendation reverses under the capital-gains sensitivity where any of the base assumptions fail. It can reverse if the $30,000 is short-term U.S. gain, a gain subject to a different German regime, a gain on real property or real-estate-rich interests, an interest of at least 25% in a U.S. company, business-property gain, or a gain realised before German treaty residence begins.[38][39][60] It can also reverse if a large U.S. residual NIIT or uncreditable U.S. tax remains after foreign-tax-credit limitation rules are applied.[31][60]

The ±10% salary sensitivity should not be interpreted as a universal 10% change in net income. Social-insurance contribution ceilings mean that, above the relevant ceilings, part of a salary increase does not increase German pension, unemployment, health or care contributions. The 2024 ceilings are €90,600 for western-Germany pension and unemployment insurance and €62,100 for health and care insurance.[94][62]Derived — At the income levels modelled in the comparison, a ±10% salary change principally changes income tax and the U.S. or German residual cross-border tax outcome; it does not proportionally change every German social-insurance deduction once the applicable ceiling has been reached.

The direction of the salary sensitivity is therefore clear even before the recalculation: a +10% salary case may widen or narrow the location gap depending on marginal income-tax rates and residual U.S. tax, while a −10% case can make capped German social contributions a larger share of pay. It does not, by itself, determine a location reversal. The location recommendation changes only if the recalculated common-currency net figure crosses over after using the same work, residence, insurance and relief assumptions in both scenarios.

The alternative-gain sensitivity should be run in at least three defined cases:

- **Base portfolio-share gain:** The gain is a realised, documented net gain on private shares acquired after 31 December 2008, with German residence at sale, no church tax and the applicable saver’s allowance. Germany’s stated treatment is 25% withholding, and the BZSt states a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that capital-income tax. [21][103][100]

- **No taxable gain case:** The apparent $30,000 is not a realised taxable gain after basis, loss offsets, asset classification or holding-period rules are applied. Basis and adjustments determine whether gain exists, while certain other German assets may be untaxed after a holding period exceeding one year. [51][21] This case tests whether the Scenario B difference is driven by taxable investments rather than salary.

- **Exception-asset gain:** The gain is from real property, a real-estate-rich entity, permanent-establishment business property, or a substantial 25% corporate interest. These assets are outside the ordinary Article 13(5) assumption and require a separate country-allocation and domestic-law calculation. [38][36][39]

Judgment — The decision should not change solely because gross income moves ±10% or because a gain is relabelled as capital gain. It should change when the recalculation changes a legally relevant threshold, a contribution ceiling, the applicable insurance system, the U.S. relief mechanism or the asset’s tax classification.

The first practical step is to establish the relocation calendar: German lease and registration date, U.S. home availability, all workdays by country, travel days, employer legal entity, payroll entity and whether any German permanent establishment bears the cost. The treaty residence test is sequential, and Article 15’s 183-day condition is not enough by itself.[38][41] Travel calendars, flight records, hotel invoices, personal calendars and employer records can support both presence days and workday allocation.[59]

Second, obtain written employer, insurer and cross-border return projections before accepting the Germany net estimate. The employer should confirm whether the stated Germany-performed compensation remains on a U.S. Form W-2, is paid through a German employer or affiliate, or is a U.S. posting; it should also obtain a certificate of coverage where an exemption is claimed.[2][1] The insurer should provide the actual U.S. payroll premium and German GKV fund rate or PKV quote, replacing the health-insurance deductions in the table; the German payroll calculation uses the employee’s insurer-specific supplementary contribution rate.[33][34] The cross-border return projection should quantify the residual U.S. tax after foreign-tax-credit or FEIE treatment rather than adding the two countries’ headline income taxes.[32][60]

Third, prepare the capital-gain file before sale or relocation: brokerage statements, purchase and sale confirmations, basis records, corporate ownership percentage, asset classification, acquisition date, residence at sale and any capital-loss certificates. The IRS requires records supporting basis and adjusted basis.[51] This step decides whether Scenario B is an ordinary securities-gain case or an exception case requiring a different recommendation.

Fourth, model U.S. double-tax relief before filing. The taxpayer should compare FEIE, foreign housing exclusion where relevant, and FTC outcomes; separately calculate general-category wage income and passive-category investment income; and use transaction-appropriate currency translation.[1][93][32] A 2024 overseas filer generally has an automatic extension to 16 June 2025 if the stated foreign-residence and tax-home conditions apply, but interest on unpaid tax runs from the regular due date.[64]

Fifth, maintain U.S. foreign-asset reporting discipline after opening German accounts. FBAR applies if aggregate foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000 at any time in the year.[76][82] Form 8938 may also apply; for an unmarried person living abroad who meets the foreign-tax-home and presence conditions, the stated thresholds are more than $200,000 at year-end or more than $300,000 at any time.[83] Form 8938 does not replace FBAR, and German banks obtain U.S.-tax-liability self-certification for new accounts and treat a U.S.-liable customer’s account as reportable.[82][81]

Sixth, preserve German and U.S. assessments, proof of taxes paid or withheld, Form 1116 workpapers, coverage certificates and treaty-residence documentation. Article 25 permits a person facing treaty-inconsistent taxation to present a case to the competent authority within four years from notification of the assessment that creates the double taxation. The competent authorities must endeavour to resolve a justified case by mutual agreement; arbitration is possible only if both authorities agree.[41]

**The actionable recommendation is therefore to remain in the no-income-tax U.S. state on the modelled 2024 cash result, while replacing the four standardised inputs—residence/workday pattern, social-insurance coverage, health-insurance cost and gain classification—with the actual facts. Reassess the location decision only if a fully specified recalculation crosses the two net take-home amounts, while preserving an Article 25 route if the two systems produce treaty-inconsistent double taxation.[11][25][21][14][9][6][7][10][23][31][26][33][35][27]**

The Scenario B recommendation changes in two additional defined asset cases. A pre-1-January-2009 share gain is treated as tax-free in the stated German rule, while dividends on those shares remain taxable; an equity-fund gain has a 30% investor-level partial exemption and a mixed-fund gain has a 15% partial exemption.[71] These cases require a fresh German taxable-gain calculation rather than the assumed full ordinary-share gain.[71]

Where treaty-limited withholding is imposed, Article 29 permits domestic-law withholding first and a refund application for the treaty-limited excess. The application period is four years from the end of the calendar year in which the income was received.[45]

The relocation decision changes if higher substantiated German employment expenses replace the €1,230 employee expense allowance, including qualifying work-related moving costs. For eligible persons, the stated work-related moving-cost lump sums from 1 March 2024 are €964 for the eligible person, €643 for each additional person, and €193 for unfurnished accommodation.[3][15]

For the alternative-gain sensitivity, a BZSt withholding-refund amount is not available cash at the sale date: BZSt states that processing can exceed 20 months.[107] A case involving German-source capital income while the taxpayer is non-resident should therefore treat the initial withholding separately from any later permitted refund and retain the residence certificate and German tax certificates required for the application.[107][106]

The recommendation under the sensitivity cases changes in two defined ways. It changes from the full-year Germany model to a split-year calculation if the taxpayer has U.S. and German workdays, because employee compensation is generally sourced by time worked and fringe benefits by geographic location.[47] It also changes from the assumed ordinary German-resident portfolio-gain result to a withholding-and-refund cash-flow analysis only if the gain instead produces covered German-source capital income while the taxpayer is non-resident.[106]

Before accepting a German assignment, request an employer tax-equalization arrangement or a compensation adjustment if the employer will not bear the additional German social-insurance, health-insurance, payroll and compliance costs. If the gain is elective, defer the sale until its asset classification, residence date and U.S. foreign-tax-credit result have been modelled.

The Scenario B Germany-versus-U.S. net-take-home gap changes by $6,970.50 across the corrected joint salary-and-gain −10% and +10% sensitivity cases.[25][21][11][14][9][6][7][10][23][31][26][33][35][27]

Use two advisers for the final implementation: a German tax adviser (Steuerberater) and a U.S. tax preparer experienced in foreign-tax-credit relief.[57]

Before German work begins, document the home and host employer relationship, the residence or non-residence structure, and cost sharing between the entities. Germany applies an economic-employer approach in interpreting Article 15 and has no firm short-assignment de minimis rule, including for assignments of three months or less; this documentation establishes facts relevant to the Article 15 employment-tax analysis.[70]

Obtain written German work authorization before work begins. U.S. citizens may enter Germany without an entry visa and apply for a work and residence permit within 90 days of entry, but visa-free entry does not authorize employment before written work authorization is issued. A U.S. employer without a German permanent establishment or permanent representative cannot withhold, declare or pay German income tax; where it does not perform the applicable obligations, the employee performs them.[70][108] Work authorization and responsibility for German tax payments must therefore be settled before work starts.[70][108]

Apply the income-tax treaty and the Totalization Agreement as separate tests. Article 15 determines where employment income may be taxed, including its three-condition short-stay exception, while the separate Totalization Agreement determines social-security coverage; satisfying one test does not determine the other.[109]

Judgment — The comparison supports a conditional location choice, not an automatic tax-arbitrage conclusion. The U.S. case is a domestic baseline; the Germany case is valid only as a full-year German-residence and German-work model with German social coverage, actual German insurance costs and U.S. tax reduced—but not silently eliminated—through the applicable FEIE or FTC rules. The salary sensitivity generally changes income-tax exposure more than capped German social contributions, while the gain sensitivity can reverse the result when the asset is not an ordinary private portfolio-share gain.[94][62][60][21][39]

The practical direction is firm: verify residence, workdays, employer coverage, insurance and basis before acting; then choose the country with the higher recalculated common-currency net amount. If assessments still produce treaty-inconsistent double taxation, use the treaty mutual-agreement procedure within its four-year presentation period.[38][2][51][41]

The actual German health-insurance fund and its additional contribution rate, the employee’s U.S. health-plan premium, German residence start date, physical work locations, employer structure, social-security certificate of coverage, and the gain asset’s legal classification remain open. Those facts would determine the final payroll deductions, possible split-year treatment, country of social-insurance coverage, foreign-tax-credit calculation, and whether the modeled German 26.375% capital-income rate applies.
