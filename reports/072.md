# Comparison of Gig-Platform Trust Mechanisms in Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil

## Executive summary

Adopt a common trust pattern across Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil, but localize its gates: mutual contextual ratings, progressive identity checks, protected payment release, and a human-recoverable dispute path. Do not use customer ratings or opaque composite scores as automatic determinants of workers’ income, scheduling, insurance, or deactivation.

Argentina needs strong newcomer protections because digital reputation can substitute for missing formal credentials but sparse histories leave workers exposed. Colombia needs document and biometric flows that include eligible migrants and clearly separate customer feedback from work access, since account sharing and migration-status friction make hard gates counterproductive.

Brazil can support the strongest continuous account-integrity model by combining CPF and own-name payment matching with liveness and device checks, provided automated restrictions have intelligible reasons and review. Across all three markets, prior experience of informal work and platform scoring encourages adoption when the system creates a portable route to earnings and discourages it when it feels like unexplained, irreversible loss of livelihood.

## Rating systems

**Definitions —** A rating is reciprocal feedback on a completed interaction, such as Uber’s anonymous 1–5-star rider–driver evaluation. A composite performance score combines customer feedback with platform-defined operational inputs, such as acceptance, completed orders, punctuality, cancellations, tenure, or scheduled attendance. A legal identity requirement establishes the identity document a person must hold; a platform identity check validates that document or identity for account activation or remote service access. Conditional held funds are funds deposited in guarantee before work and released when the stated delivery condition is met; payment processing is the execution of the payment itself. A documented practice is a stated product, policy, or legal mechanism; measured effectiveness is an observed user, worker, safety, service, or adoption outcome.[1][2][3][4][5]

Judgment — Ratings are a trust signal only when users understand what the score represents and workers can recover from an adverse score. In these markets, the same interface often serves two different functions: it helps a rider or customer assess a stranger, but it also governs workers’ access to jobs, schedules, earnings, and sometimes continued account access. That dual role is the central design risk in high-informality contexts, where platform reputation can substitute for formal credentials but platform exclusion can remove an important income channel.

### Which rating systems work effectively in Argentina

Uber Argentina provides the most clearly contextualized consumer-facing model: a rolling 500-trip average, mutual anonymous ratings, a required reason for scores below five stars, and exclusion of traffic-related low ratings. The design exposes a concise pre-ride signal while reducing the effect of at least one cause outside a driver’s control.[1]

Delivery ratings are more consequential and less legible. Workers reported systems ranging from a 1–100 score to a 1–5 score or a total ranking, with customer ratings potentially combined with acceptance, completed work, punctuality, and tenure. In the Argentine delivery-worker survey, 76.4% believed their score affected the amount of work received and about 60% believed it affected job characteristics.[2] Glovo workers said lower scores delayed access to weekly scheduling and prevented access to the best time slots; Glovo said order assignment instead depended on courier–merchant–consumer distance and that scores did not affect order quantity or quality.[2]

In Argentine platform work, negative customer ratings and rejected orders can trigger temporary suspension.[9][97]

Ratings can also enable upward mobility. Argentine freelance designers described taking simple, low-priced projects to build a reputation, then using completed projects, positive reviews, and good scores to access more attractive and better-paid work.[8] Digital reputation can compensate for missing formal credentials, but a high existing rating disadvantages informal workers whose prior customers supplied referrals rather than platform reviews.[22][6]

Research on Argentine freelancers also identifies portfolios and client networks, alongside completed projects, scores and reviews, as ways to reach better work and partly substitute for university, family or employer contacts.[8]

**The effective Argentine pattern is a contextualized two-sided reputation signal, not a composite score that silently converts customer feedback and availability metrics into allocation or discipline.** The key cost is newcomer vulnerability: Uber says averages fluctuate during the first 50–100 trips, while freelancers may need to accept low-paid work to build the history that makes better work available.[1][8]

In the Argentine delivery-worker survey, the share who believed scores affected work volume exceeded the share who believed they affected job characteristics by 16.4 percentage points (76.4% versus about 60%). This shows that workers most clearly experience rating consequences through the amount of work offered, so allocation effects require especially visible explanation.[2]

Workana gives Argentine clients a project-linked freelancer reputation: they can consider the freelancer’s proposal, price, ranking, and rating history, and, after project completion and payment release, rate work quality, communication, and punctuality from one to five stars, with a profile-visible comment. Both sides then rate each other; in a reported Workana project, the freelancer was rated after funds were released.[6][7]

Uber Black in Argentina makes rating a category-access requirement: from June 2024, all Uber Black driver-partners must maintain a rating of 4.8 or above, and new driver-partners need an average rating of at least 4.80. When an average drops below the minimum, the driver receives notice and the next 80 trips are rated; recovering the threshold preserves trip requests, while failing to do so stops Uber Black requests until the threshold is reached.[23]

Uber Argentina may partially or fully remove a driver-partner’s platform access for ratings below the minimum average in that city. It says it notifies the driver and provides suggestions to improve ratings from users or restaurants.[24]

Argentina’s Rappi courier terms give the courier a reciprocal task-level action: at completion, they may rate both the consumer and the commercial partner. Uber Pro instead makes ratings one input in a three-month composite level with points and cancellation rate. These designs distinguish reciprocal conduct feedback from a multi-input rewards classification.[25][26]

### Which rating systems work effectively in Colombia

Colombian platforms commonly use ratings as part of performance management rather than solely as a customer-choice aid. The UN’s Colombia platform-work account says delivery-worker ratings generally combine completed orders, acceptance rate, and customer scores, normally on a 1–5-star scale. Uber’s Colombia article dated 17 July 2018 described a mutual post-trip 1–5-star system, and Rappi’s terms allow couriers to rate both the customer and merchant at order completion.[12][11][14]

Rappi illustrates the design’s material stakes. Its courier score includes customer ratings, completed orders, and cancellations; the score affects visibility, order access, and income. A poor score can reduce assignments or lead to suspension. The score’s bronze, silver, gold, and diamond tiers also determine accident coverage or death-benefit amounts for workers’ families.[13][12] Workers identify the weight of customer ratings in classification and resulting assignments as a negative aspect, while ratings, comments, and observations are controlled by the platform rather than the courier.[12][15]

**For Colombia, delivery ratings become less reliable measures of a courier’s conduct when the composite score also reflects restaurant delays, traffic, or customer expectations; mutual task-level feedback works best when it remains distinct from opaque gates to work, income, suspension, and insurance protection.** A platform should retain operational measures such as completion or punctuality, but show the separate inputs, the consequence attached to each, and a remedy path; otherwise, a customer score becomes an unchallengeable employment-status proxy.[12][13]

### Which rating systems work effectively in Brazil

Brazil has mature reciprocal-rating patterns in ride-hailing and a more composite delivery-score model. Uber’s Brazil-facing guidance describes anonymous mutual 1–5-star ratings after each trip, a driver average from the latest 500 passenger ratings, rider visibility of that average before and during the trip, and optional comments on ratings below five stars.[16] 99 likewise uses reciprocal ratings.

Its terms, last updated 20 April 2022, define a driver classification as the average of the latest 100 ratings; a separate 99Pop article, last updated 22 April 2022, describes displayed points based on an average taking the latest 500 trips into account.[17][18]

iFood’s Score is explicitly composite: levels 1–3 use delivered orders, cancellations, punctuality, evaluations, and scheduled attendance in cities with Planejamento. iFood says performance directly affects earnings, and the Score affects how many future scheduling days couriers can view: four days at Score 1, five at Score 2, and six at Score 3.[20][21] Brazilian delivery research describes scoring tools, reputational evaluations, and automatic blocks as persistent features of work; workers also describe deactivation as a threat to their income source.[27][28]

In Brazil, iFood Score also controls access to its no-fee *Antecipação de Repasses* feature: eligibility requires an active account, at least 28 available app hours and one completed delivery in the preceding 28 days, and excludes Score 0km couriers.[98]

Uber’s feedback visibility is more actionable than 99Pop’s approach, which does not let drivers or passengers inspect their own ratings. Transaction-linked Brazilian ride and delivery ratings can reassure first-time customers, but when they influence access to jobs they may penalize workers for factors outside their control; Uber excludes excessively negative, biased, and out-of-control ratings, while iFood excludes store- or customer-caused delays from its Score.[16][18][20][29][19][21] **Brazil’s strongest design is an Uber-like trust layer—anonymous mutual feedback, a counterparty-visible aggregate, and structured low-score context—kept separate from automated allocation and exclusion decisions.**[16]

### Synthesis

Argentina most clearly shows reputation as a route into better work, but also shows that opaque score consequences can constrain schedules and account access. In Colombia, Rappi’s courier score affects visibility, access to orders, and income; poor scores can reduce assignments or lead to suspension, and bronze, silver, gold, and diamond score tiers determine accident coverage or the amount assigned to a courier’s family on death. Brazil combines familiar reciprocal ratings with composite Scores that affect earnings and scheduling.[8][2][13][12][20][21]

Judgment — Build one common rating interaction across the three markets: mutual post-completion feedback, an aggregate rather than identifiable individual ratings, and a required reason for materially adverse feedback. Localize the governance: in Argentina, protect sparse histories; in Colombia, separate customer experience from safety-net eligibility; in Brazil, do not let composite performance scores silently control earnings or access. A rating can build trust for customers in all three markets, but it discourages worker adoption when it becomes an opaque and irreversible gate to livelihood.

## Identity verification requirements

Identity verification must do two jobs in gig work: establish that the account holder is a real, eligible person, and keep that link intact when an account is used, recovered, or flagged. The effective requirement is not the most documents; it is the least burdensome combination that connects person, account, payment, and—where relevant—vehicle entitlement, with a route to fix correctable failures.Judgment — In high-informality markets, an opaque hard gate can move identity substitution off-platform rather than remove it.

### Which identity verification requirements work effectively in Argentina

Argentina has a strong authoritative-source option: RENAPER offers organizations remote identity validation using DNI data, fingerprints, or facial photographs; its listed fingerprint and face-photo validation services place liveness detection outside their scope. Access is subject to data-protection obligations, a legitimate-interest assessment, and a Convenio Único de Confronte de Datos processed through TAD. One RENAPER SID mobile-verification route combines front-and-back DNI photographs, a liveness selfie with two random gestures, document-authenticity assessment, and a query of RENAPER facial biometrics; a separate route uses a liveness selfie and the entered DNI number and gender without requiring the physical DNI card.[3][35]

Argentina’s SID can validate identity remotely and in real time against RENAPER through DNI images and facial biometrics, using an app or integrated SDK.[99][35]

This is materially stronger than reading a document image alone. A document photo can extract data but does not establish that the uploader is the holder, that a live person is present, or that the details match an authoritative record. Argentina’s DNI ecosystem also requires format resilience: five DNI generations coexist, including an electronic DNI introduced from 2026 that uses a QR code rather than a barcode.[42] A production flow therefore needs document-format detection and a defined exception route: the described DNI coverage excludes foreign documents and Argentine passports.[42]

Existing platform practice is mostly document-led. Rappi Argentina requires adult couriers to have an original valid DNI, bars use of third-party identities, duplicate accounts, and account transfers, and may query public or private databases. It also requires annual or requested updates of personal, commercial, and financial information with supporting documentation.[30] Yet Rappi also says it does not guarantee the accuracy of courier-provided personal data.[30] That distinction matters: unique-account rules deter sharing but do not, by themselves, prove the person currently using the account is the registered person.

Uber requires professional licence, vehicle registration, insurance proof, and a face-visible driver photo, then states that support reviews the documents in 1–2 days. DiDi publishes role-specific requirements and a 24–72-hour document-approval window; taxi onboarding additionally requests a criminal-record certificate.[31][39][32] Argentina’s Law 25.326 requires collection to be adequate, relevant, non-excessive, accurate, purpose-limited, and retained only while needed; sensitive data cannot be compelled, while criminal-record data may be processed only by competent public authorities under the applicable framework.[43] The DiDi taxi requirement and that statutory restriction create a live compliance question that needs legal resolution before adopting criminal-record checks outside a narrowly required category.[32][43]

The adoption cost is operational, not abstract. Argentina’s public digital-validation guidance requires an account, app, internet connection, camera access, natural light, a clear background, an unobscured face, and steady handset use. It identifies lighting changes, a dirty lens, phone movement, and lost connectivity as failure causes, and provides a Punto Digital in-person fallback.[44][45] DNI is the identity document; CUIT/CUIL are tax or labour identifiers.

Argentina’s SID verification flow can require a front-facing camera of at least two megapixels and at least GSM connectivity.[100]

Monotributo is the tax registration through which delivery workers invoice services; reported onboarding also includes proof of residence and orientation.[30][45][10] Repeated document requests therefore add to an already formal transition for workers entering from informal work.[10][30]

**Argentina’s effective requirement is a tiered flow: verify DNI identity first and collect tax or role-specific documents when needed.** RENAPER-backed DNI-plus-liveness escalation reassures clients through an authoritative person-to-record link without demanding tax documentation before an informal worker can explore or apply for suitable work.[30][35][10]

### Which identity verification requirements work effectively in Colombia

In Colombia, a cédula de ciudadanía—or, for a foreign resident, a cédula de extranjería—is an identity document; RUT is tax registration, not identity proof. Colombia’s documented courier practice also accepts PPT and combines identity documentation with migration status and vehicle evidence.[14] Soy Rappi makes activation contingent on successful identity validation and accepts current cédula de ciudadanía, cédula de extranjería, or PPT; couriers must be adults and maintain legal migration status.[4][14] Motorized couriers must also submit a current driving licence, vehicle ownership card, and SOAT. Colombian ride and delivery platforms use worker identity checks; a driving licence and vehicle documents are additional, role-specific checks for motorised delivery and passenger trips.[4] This split avoids imposing motor-vehicle paperwork on bicycle couriers while connecting a motorized worker to road-use eligibility.[4][11]

Rappi’s Colombia terms tie access to a personal account and prohibit account sharing. Its data authorization states that it collects identity-document details, image, biometric data, bank-account number, and activity data; biometric processing is characterized as sensitive data requiring prior, express, informed authorization.[14][36] The stated biometric purposes include verifying identity, confirming that the account is used only by its authorized holder, preventing impersonation and fraud, and applying reinforced authentication where an activity’s context or risk level requires it.[36] This supports risk-tiered biometric authentication rather than deploying repeated face checks without explanation.

Rappi Colombia requires a bank account in the courier’s own name and updates to personal, commercial, and financial information at least annually or when requested.[14]

The migration-document design has direct inclusion consequences. The PPT is a free government-issued identity and migration-regularisation document for eligible Venezuelan migrants.[46] Rappi accepts it, but its standard activation page lists only current cédula de ciudadanía, cédula de extranjería, and PPT. By contrast, Colombia’s 2024 financial-access guidance recognizes the PPT, a certificate showing a PPT application is in process, Venezuelan passports—including active, expired, or near-expiry passports under the stated rule—and specified visas for access to financial products and services.[4][47] This is a financial-sector precedent, not a platform mandate, but it shows that a binary “PPT or exclusion” rule is not the only document policy available.

That flexibility matters because the PPT process combines a virtual FUT with an in-person appointment and biometric registration; to check PPT status, a person must have completed RUMV pre-registration and biometric registration, after which Migración Colombia can take up to 90 calendar days to provide status information.[46][47] Venezuelan migrants made up 54%–62% of the two cited Colombian Rappi-worker samples; the study linked their concentration in precarious work to migration-status and formal-employment barriers.[13] A rigid current-document gate therefore requires a migrant-document path before applicants have a realistic route to regularize.[13][4][46]

Account integrity also cannot be assessed only from stated policy. Reuters reported that more than a dozen Colombian Rappitenderos described a black market in accounts, four interviewed workers said they had bought accounts, and Rappi said it had cancelled 5,600 accounts since March, including 1,060 for false-identity issues.[48] These facts show both enforcement and circumvention. The design implication is to make legitimate activation explainable and recoverable, not simply stricter.

Rappi offers an assisted path: in-person activation in Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, and Barranquilla; a declared one-time 30-minute activation; and agent recommendations during activation.[4] A study separately reported that most cited users became active and could begin work in under 24 hours; this is elapsed time to active access, not the same measure as a 30-minute activation session.[13]

Colombia’s mandatory RUNT transit-information system records drivers, licences, infringements, insurance, and accidents; its virtual authentication uses a web form, an email or SMS link, generated questions, and a code sent to registered RUNT or available financial-sector contact details.[101][102]

RUNT virtual-authentication links expire after two hours, errors can require a two-hour wait, and code receipt depends on the operator, coverage, and signal.[102]

**Colombia’s effective requirement is a current accepted identity document bound to a personal account, plus vehicle documents only where the delivery mode creates driving risk, with consented biometrics stepped up by risk and assisted migration-document handling.** It directly addresses identity substitution without making documentary regularisation an unexplained exclusion mechanism.[14][36][4]

### Which identity verification requirements work effectively in Brazil

Brazil’s distinctive identity anchor is CPF, the Federal Revenue register containing personal information such as name, birth date, and address. CPF is available to Brazilian and foreign individuals, whether resident or non-resident, and each number is unique and permanent.[49][50] CPF is an identifier and matching input for an onboarding record; iFood separately requires an identity document and a bank account in the courier’s own name linked to CPF. CPF is not proof that the person presenting it is the legitimate holder: CPF is widely requested, not confidential, and static identifiers alone can be compromised.[49][33][51]

Brazilian courier platforms therefore use layered evidence. iFood requires a document with identification and a bank account in the courier’s own name linked to CPF; motorcycle couriers need CNH A and car couriers CNH B. Matching a Brazilian courier’s verified identity to an own-name, CPF-linked payout account reduces impersonation and payment-diversion risk; iFood may use a symbolic deposit to confirm that account.[33] CNH A or CNH B remains a separate vehicle-eligibility check.

iFood’s Brazilian courier onboarding requests name, CPF, email, phone, bank details, and a CNH or RG/RNE according to delivery mode; it also periodically rechecks faces to deter account rental or lending and uses facial recognition in stolen-account recovery.[103][104][105][21]

Do not demand business registration before it is needed, so informal workers can enter through the identity-and-payout path.[33][37] iFood also requires a selfie during registration, says facial-recognition technology validates it for account security, may recheck documents after registration, and may use a symbolic bank deposit to confirm that the registered bank account is correct.[37] Uber requires a security check for delivery partners, accepts CNH, RG, RNE, and RNM as identity documents, and accepts specified documents as CPF evidence.[34]

Continuous verification is the practical differentiator. iFood asks motorized couriers to resubmit licence and vehicle documents even when they were already supplied at registration, uses in-app facial recognition for temporary restriction, uses CPF plus facial recognition for account recovery, and requires authorisation for a new phone, with up to two devices authorized simultaneously.[41][21] Its stated enforcement controls treat an account as personal and non-transferable; false documents, account lending or renting, unknown devices, route anomalies, and mismatched identification photos can trigger account action or security alerts.[38] This binds the identity record to ongoing use rather than treating onboarding as a once-only event.

Brazil’s 99 starts verification with a phone number and SMS, then CPF; front-camera verification for drivers and passengers checks for a live interaction rather than a replayed static image. An abnormality can trigger repeat verification that prevents continued use until confirmation.[106][107]

The relevant legal frame is LGPD. Biometric data linked to a person are sensitive data; processing may occur without consent when indispensable for fraud prevention and electronic-registration security, while safeguards preserve the holder’s rights.[52] LGPD also requires purpose limitation, necessity, transparency, security, non-discrimination, accountability, and clear information on purpose, duration, controller, sharing, and data-subject rights.[52] People may seek review of solely automated decisions affecting their interests and request information about decision criteria and procedures.[52]

CPF status should not be converted into an indiscriminate exclusion rule. Receita Federal distinguishes a suspended CPF, caused by incorrect or incomplete registration information, from a CPF pending regularisation because an income-tax return was not filed; both can be regularised, whereas “nulo” denotes fraud.[49] A verification UI should therefore distinguish identity mismatch, document expiry, and administrative CPF remediation, each with its own next step.

**Brazil’s effective requirement is CPF and own-name payment-account matching combined with document, selfie or facial matching, targeted rechecks for account, device, or vehicle risk, and in-app document-specific remediation—not CPF-only validation.** This provides person-to-account assurance while preserving a route for workers whose CPF or documents need correction.[33][41][21][52][53]

### Synthesis —

Argentina has the clearest authoritative registry-and-liveness route; Colombia needs document acceptance and assisted activation that reflect migration status; Brazil needs CPF as a matching signal but not as standalone proof. Across all three, non-transferable-account policies are necessary but insufficient because platform evidence and reported workarounds show that workers can still use another person’s credentials.[30][48][38] The common product pattern is progressive verification: use low-friction document and profile checks at entry, raise assurance for higher-risk events, and provide a recovery path for genuine users who fail capture, document, or account checks.[35][44][4][21]

1. **Argentina:** implement DNI-format-aware capture and RENAPER-plus-liveness escalation, with retry and assisted fallback.
2. **Colombia:** accept the platform’s required current IDs while designing a documented exception path for migrant credentials and pending-status evidence.
3. **Brazil:** use CPF and own-name bank matching as inputs, then verify the live person through document and facial checks; expose remediation for non-fraud CPF statuses.

## Payment escrow approaches

Payment escrow means funds are pre-funded and held until an agreed release condition is met. It is different from ordinary payment processing: fast payment or scheduled payout can improve cash flow, but it does not protect either party while work is underway. The appropriate model depends chiefly on whether the task has a reviewable deliverable or is an immediate, location-based service.

### Which payment escrow approaches work effectively in Argentina

Argentina has two distinct patterns. Delivery platforms use ordinary in-app collection and post-completion worker payout: customers pay for product and delivery in the app, couriers are paid for completed deliveries, and Rappi transfers the prior week’s earnings weekly to the courier’s registered bank account. Cash orders make the courier a temporary collector: they receive the full order value and owe Rappi that amount until depositing it through the platform’s channels.[10][30]

Argentina’s Transferencias 3.0 rail is payment processing, not conditional holding: a bank-account, CVU payment-account, or wallet transfer credits the recipient rather than holding funds for a release condition.[54] It credits virtual or bank accounts within 15 seconds, is available 24/7, supports interoperable QR payments from CBU bank accounts or CVU payment accounts without a debit card, and is irrevocable once the merchant receives funds.[54] BCRA recorded 779 million immediate peso push transfers in August 2026; 76.9% had a CVU at either origin or destination.[55]

For fixed-price freelance work, Workana provides actual escrow: the client deposits funds before work begins, funds remain in guarantee until the client reviews and approves the work, and release can be partial by milestone or complete.[5][56][57] For eligible work valued up to USD 300, formal delivery followed by 15 calendar days of client silence triggers deemed acceptance and automatic release.[58][59] Authorized release is immediate and cannot be recovered; requester-paid arbitration costs USD 10, and the 4.5% service fee is not refunded when arbitration closes the project.[56][60]

**Argentine freelance designers value platform intermediation that assures timely payment. For asynchronous, fixed-price work, the common project-work comparator provides pre-funding and a bounded release rule.** Use CBU/CVU/QR for immediate delivery and payout, but reserve milestone escrow for work with a recorded, inspectable deliverable.[8][61][58]

Common project-work comparator — Workana’s published fixed-price escrow model is not a country-specific implementation. The client deposits the full agreed amount before work begins; the funds remain in escrow until the agreed conditions are met, and the client can release a corresponding percentage at stages as work progresses.[61][57] In a payment dispute, Workana may pay the freelancer, refund the client, or retain the payment on deposit pending judicial or other proceedings.[58] Projects below USD 30 enter automatic arbitration, which releases 50% of the disputed amount to each party; requested arbitration costs the requester USD 10, and the 4.5% service fee is not refunded when arbitration closes a project.[60]

### Which payment escrow approaches work effectively in Colombia

Rappi’s Colombian courier flow is transparent scheduled payout, not escrow. Couriers see their earnings before accepting an order, receive prior-week earnings weekly into their registered bank account, and retain 100% of tips regardless of the customer’s payment method.[14] For cash orders, the courier collects the full order amount and incurs a debt to Rappi until depositing it through designated channels. Rappi’s purchase card can fund order purchases, reducing the courier’s need for working capital without holding their earnings in guarantee.[14][62]

Under Rappi Colombia’s courier terms, accepting an order creates a paid mandate; the courier completes it by delivering and marking it delivered in Soy Rappi. The app’s “Mis Ganancias” section displays balance and earnings.[14]

For Colombia, Rappi’s cash collection is payment processing rather than conditional holding: the courier collects the order value and owes Rappi that amount until depositing it through designated channels.[14] A client should request changes before release and verify the concrete deliverable, credentials, permissions, and other access needed to use the work; after release, payment cannot be reversed.[57] Its USD 300/15-calendar-day deemed-acceptance rule gives freelancers a release path after formal in-platform delivery; disputes below USD 30 are automatically split 50/50, while requested arbitration costs USD 10.[58][59][60]

In Colombia, Rappi grants customer compensation voluntarily and case by case as credits or a partial or total refund to the original payment method; bank-mediated refunds can take up to 30 calendar days.[108]

For eligible Colombian card orders, Rappi may offer immediate RappiCuenta credit, a refund to the original method, or Rappi credits; RappiCuenta credit is capped at COP 60,000 per order.[108]

RappiCard refunds return automatically to the card, while Botón Bancolombia and PSE refunds default to Rappi credits. A customer who rejects unused credits can request a bank transfer through Help Center after supplying the requested information; that transfer can take up to 20 business days.[108]

Cash reliance makes an account-only payout design consequential. Banco de la República reported cash as the preferred instrument for 78.6% of Colombian adults in 2023, while its Bre-B instant-payment system began operation on October 6, 2025.[63] As of January 31, 2026, Bre-B’s directory had 99 million aliases linked to more than 33 million customers and 2.8 million merchants; the Bank’s stated priorities are a unified simple experience, fraud prevention, and education.[63]

**For Colombian delivery, lead with visible pre-acceptance earnings and predictable payout. For fixed-price project work, use the common pre-funded escrow comparator with prominent review, change-request, and deadline states.**[14][61][57][58]

### Which payment escrow approaches work effectively in Brazil

Brazil’s documented on-demand pattern is scheduled payout plus exception handling. iFood pays cloud-mode couriers weekly: deliveries from Monday through Sunday are paid to the registered bank account by 20:00 on the following Wednesday, with holiday payment moving to the next business day.[64] Its in-app statement itemizes expected payout dates, amounts, incentives, deliveries, distance, and time worked.[20] Couriers can submit missing or incomplete-payout cases, each stated to receive support handling within 24 hours.[33]

iFood’s free *Antecipação de Repasses* lets eligible Nuvem couriers advance no more than their already-earned current-cycle balance, including on weekends and holidays. Approved funds arrive the same day within up to three hours and are deducted from the following Wednesday’s payment; this advances earned income rather than holding client funds in escrow.[98]

iFood’s *Antecipação de Repasses* lets eligible couriers advance no more than their already-earned current-cycle balance; approved funds arrive the same day within up to three hours and are deducted from the next Wednesday payment. The advance accelerates earned income rather than holding a customer’s payment pending completion.[98]

For cash ride non-payment, 99’s Acordo de Pagamento is a post-trip reimbursement flow rather than escrow. The driver reports an unpaid or partially paid fare, enters the amount received, and asks the passenger to confirm; if confirmation is absent, 99 says its team reviews the occurrence and reimbursement may be expedited. Review may take several hours.[65] iFood may retain or deduct courier funds where fraud is suspected, releasing or returning them if fraud is not found; this is a fraud hold, not protection for routine delivery completion.[37]

99 says its Brazilian ride offer shows a fixed fare before acceptance and that the displayed amount is what the driver receives; drivers receive 100% of a cancellation fee. An approved passenger-nonpayment claim can be submitted in four in-app steps and credited to the driver wallet within one minute.[109][110]

Alongside Brazil’s established on-demand payment practice, Workana documents a fixed-price project-work escrow model with pre-funding, review-gated release, partial releases, and a 15-day automatic-release backstop for eligible work.[5][56][58] iFood requires couriers to have a bank account in their own name and linked to their CPF, and pays weekly earnings to the bank account registered in the courier app.[33][64]

iFood’s *Antecipação de Repasses* gives eligible couriers earlier access to income they have already earned; it is an earnings advance, not customer-payment escrow.[98]

Pix is Brazil’s instant-payment system: a completed Pix transfer moves money to its recipient, whereas escrow retains client funds until a stated release condition is met.[111]

Pix is Brazil’s instant-payment system: it operates continuously, settles transfers in seconds, and makes transferred funds available in real time rather than retaining them for an escrow release condition.[111]

99Pay says Brazilian customer-wallet money is segregated in a Central Bank account or federal government securities and excluded from a potential bankruptcy estate; that protects wallet balances, not payment for an individual job.[112]

Brazil’s Pix Special Return Mechanism (MED) covers fraud, scams, crime, and certain operational errors, not ordinary commercial disputes. A request can be made within 80 days, institutions may assess notifications within seven days, and a return depends on confirmed fraud and available funds.[111][113]

Pix is Brazil’s continuously available instant-payment system: transfers settle in seconds and make funds available in real time, rather than retaining them for a project-completion release condition.[111]

**Brazil’s documented on-demand default is transparent scheduled payout and a non-payment exception flow. For fixed-price tasks with an inspectable deliverable, use the common pre-funded, release-conditioned project-work comparator.**[64][20][65][61]

### Synthesis —

Across the three markets, escrow is most useful where a client can inspect a formal digital deliverable: pre-funding, milestone release, recorded delivery, and a defined silence deadline reduce freelancer non-payment exposure.[5][57][59][58] It is poorly matched to routine ride and delivery work, where the more relevant trust mechanisms are visible earnings, reliable payout schedules, payment-status transparency, and a low-friction route for cash non-payment or payout errors.[14][64][20][65]

1. **Use two labelled modes:** immediate payment/payout for on-demand services; escrow for fixed-price, reviewable work.
2. **Make release consequential but legible:** show milestone amount, review deadline, requested-change state, and the irreversibility of release before confirmation. [57][59]
3. **Do not treat bank payout as universal access:** retain cash-compatible collection and accessible digital payout paths, especially in Colombia and Brazil. [14][66][63]

For projects below USD 30, Workana applies automatic arbitration and releases 50% of the disputed amount to each party.[67] Where requested arbitration is available, the USD 10 requester-paid fee equals 33.3% of a USD 30 project value (USD 10 ÷ USD 30 × 100); make that paid-arbitration cost visible before the user elects it.[60][67]

## Dispute resolution interfaces

Across these markets, the key divide is between an accessible, time-bound, case-linked appeal and a nominal support channel that leaves the affected person with an unexplained block or an impractical route to remedy.

### Which dispute resolution interfaces work effectively in Argentina

In Argentina, Rappi notifies a courier of a suspension in the app; the courier submits the case through the Help Center, uploads images and other evidence, and receives the agent’s decision in the app. Didi provides a communication channel for inquiries and appeals that remains available to penalised drivers.[30][68] Fairwork’s 2022 Argentina assessment identified Didi as the only reviewed platform with a clear communication channel available to all drivers, including penalised drivers, for enquiries and appeals.

It contrasted this with channels available only to workers meeting parameters such as high ratings or acceptance rates.[68] Rappi’s Argentina terms similarly describe an in-app suspension notice, Help Center escalation to a support agent, image and evidence submission, and an in-app decision.[30]

The operational failure is clearest where the remedy itself prevents work. Delivery workers described having to attend platform offices to attempt reinstatement after a block; this displaced working time and income, while reinstatement remained uncertain.[2] In the same Argentine delivery research, 29.5% of surveyed workers had experienced a sanction or deactivation, averaging 5.6 days; 57.3% of sanctioned workers considered it unfair, but only 46% challenged it.[2] Workers distinguished between accepted enforcement for administrative, conduct, or delivery-rule breaches and blocks they saw as arbitrary because of merchant errors in sealed orders, inability to answer support while riding, or closed channels for depositing cash.[2]

For a live delivery problem, a generic ticket queue is too slow. One Rappi courier reported that support replied about 20 minutes after he had raised a rain-related issue, by which point he had already travelled to the merchant.[2] Uber Argentina presents 24/7 support and in-app emergency assistance, but does not describe a formal dispute flow.[31] Argentina also has consumer-facing recourse: the official consumer-protection service allows a complaint to be initiated and offers the SAC chatbot through WhatsApp, alongside links to the national consumer-arbitration system and the Defensor del Cliente. These are consumer remedies, not substitutes for a courier account appeal.[75]

**Argentina therefore needs an asynchronous in-app appeal with reason codes, contextual evidence, visible status, and a decision deadline; requiring an unpaid office visit during a block is the design to avoid.** This matters particularly for economically precarious workers: physical redress directly competes with income, while only 24.5% of surveyed workers said their platforms convened worker meetings through which recurring issues could surface.[2]

### Which dispute resolution interfaces work effectively in Colombia

For consumers in Colombia, Rappi’s always-visible Help Center lets users submit a PQR from the affected order, check its status, request additional information, and track all PQRs in Conversations; its website PQR button issues a case number by email and keeps the support exchange in the same email thread. Separately, Rappi notifies a suspended courier in Soy Rappi; the courier submits a challenge through the Help Center, uploads images and other evidence, and receives the support agent’s decision in Soy Rappi. After contacting the Help Center for an app-use issue, the courier can take an unresolved block, claim, or complaint to the Defensoría del Repartidor as a second instance.[70][14][71] It is described as individual, personalised and objective, with a defender accompanying each case from beginning to end and a second review for complex cases.[71] Rappi’s terms characterize the Defensoría as an autonomous, impartial third party, although it is a Rappi-created mechanism.[14][76]

For a Colombian Rappi customer complaint, the order-linked flow asks the user to select Account or Help, choose the affected order and problem category, describe the impact, and attach photos; the flow differs slightly from app version 8.11 onward.[108]

The access design is materially better than an office-only model. The service is free, virtual nationwide, and in person by appointment in Bogotá, Medellín and Barranquilla.[14][71] During 2026, Rappi reported that the Defensoría had managed more than 16.500 cases nationwide and that about 30% had a resolution favourable to delivery workers.[77] The same reporting says the service tracks cases from start to finish under Rappi’s terms and feeds case information into operations.[76]

However, the escalation requirement creates friction when account access is income-critical. Colombian Rappi terms let a courier challenge a suspension through the Help Center, upload images and evidence, and receive the decision in Soy Rappi, but state no review deadline or escalation criteria.[14] This gap matters because research on Rappi couriers reports frequent temporary blocks with unclear explanations, and 35% of Platlaboral Redlatt interviewees reportedly experienced blocks after refusing a delivery, sometimes without a clear justification.[13] A Bogotá courier also reported receiving only a generic terms-and-conditions message after his account was frozen, without written explanation, warning or email.[48]

**Colombia’s strongest interface is a free human escalation layer, but it must be paired with a first-instance notice that states the specific reason, evidence considered, case status, and decision deadline.** That combination preserves the Defensoría’s useful accompaniment while preventing ordinary support from becoming an opaque gatekeeper. Fairwork’s standard aligns with this: workers should receive notice and reasons for decisions, a documented process to be heard and appeal, and a clear communications channel.[73][78]

### Which dispute resolution interfaces work effectively in Brazil

Brazil provides the clearest examples of case-linked, in-app dispute design. iFood lets couriers contest eligible deactivations through the courier app’s esclarecer o que aconteceu route, attach materials such as screenshots or police reports, and receive a stated response within two business days; the FAQ limits this route to deactivations up to 90 days old.[21] For incorrect or missing payouts, iFood asks for CPF, the relevant period, payment details and, for missing payments, bank-account evidence; it states a 24-hour handling target.[21] Its support architecture also distinguishes ordinary merchant resolution from platform escalation: customers first use merchant chat and then open an order-linked iFood ticket if unresolved.[79]

iFood’s Brazilian deactivation screen states the reason, offers a review request and explanation field, and displays pending-payout information; workers can attach requested evidence and track updates while distinguishing temporary blocks from deactivation.[104][74]

iFood also separates urgent safety reporting from routine support. Couriers can use Contacto com o Suporte during a route and a Central de Segurança SOS button for accidents, harassment, theft or assault; serious-incident reports can be classified in-app and followed through chat or registered email.[21] For customers, critical cases are opened from the affected order’s Help flow, with a narrative and evidence such as images or chat screenshots.[79]

Uber’s Brazil design is similarly notice-led. Drivers request review from the deactivation message in Uber Driver, with separate processes for criminal-record findings, suspected fraud, safety incidents and low ratings.[72] Safety appeals accept additional information and may be reconsidered using objective evidence such as video, in-app audio, judicial decisions or other requested material.[72] Fraud appeals require at least one completed trip in Brazil, permit one request, and must be initiated within 90 days.[72] Criminal-record review is routed to the screening provider named in the notification, also with one request and a 90-day document deadline.[72]

The inclusion trade-off is evidence burden. Screenshots and free-text accounts are comparatively accessible; police reports, CPF, bank documentation, criminal certificates, and externally held records are harder for informal workers to obtain, retain, or upload.[21][72] iFood states that submitting a contest does not guarantee removal of a block.[74] Its published timings also differ: courier terms promise a deactivation-review response within 48 hours, while its FAQ says within two business days.[37][21]

**Brazil shows that an order-, payout-, or notice-linked entry point is more usable than a generic help centre, but the platform must offer low-burden evidence options and an accessible fallback when app access itself is blocked.** Fairwork identifies iFood and AppJusto as Brazilian examples with accessible communication channels and documented appeals; AppJusto is described as having no automatic deactivations and always-human communication.[73]

In Brazil, 99 offers security-incident reporting through its Help Center and free, 24/7 live chat through Security Solutions.[106][74]

iFood’s published deactivation-review target of up to 48 hours is twice its up-to-24-hour payout-ticket handling target. This supports separate urgency design: a payout discrepancy should receive the faster operational queue, while a livelihood-affecting deactivation needs both a stated decision target and interim account-status clarity.[37][21]

### Synthesis —

The markets point in one direction: effective dispute design combines an immediate, case-linked entry point, explanation of the decision, evidence submission, visible follow-up, and a human escalation route. Brazil has the most operationally specific in-app flows and stated service targets; Colombia has the strongest documented accompaniment and second-instance model; Argentina most clearly demonstrates the cost of office-based and slow support.[2][71][21][72] The mechanisms disagree in their access burden: Brazil’s formal evidence and eligibility limits can exclude workers, while Colombia’s prerequisite support stage can delay escalation.[14][21][72] The common product requirement is not simply more channels, but a credible remedy that remains usable when work and income are already interrupted.

1. Make every block, payout discrepancy, and order incident open a case-specific appeal flow from the affected screen.
2. Show the enforcement reason, applicable rule, evidence requested, case status, and decision deadline before asking the user to navigate Help.
3. Accept a tiered evidence set: free-text account, screenshots and location data first; formal documents only where genuinely necessary.
4. Provide a human second-instance review that is free and remote, without requiring office attendance or high-performance eligibility.

## Prior trust-system experiences and platform adoption

Trust mechanisms influence adoption in these markets less as standalone features than as evidence that a platform will provide reliable access to income, customers, and redress. Workers who have experienced opaque scoring, stalled payments, arbitrary blocks, fraud, or inaccessible support will judge a new safeguard by whether it reduces that risk without delaying work or taking away control.

Judgment — For a gig platform, prior trust-system experience has two effects. It creates expectations of what a platform must make visible—who is verified, why a rating changed, where money is, and how a decision can be challenged. It also creates “trust friction”: a new control is interpreted as protection when it is proportionate, explained, and reversible; it is interpreted as another source of exclusion when it is opaque, slow, or threatens earnings.

### How do users’ prior experiences with trust systems affect platform

**Argentina**

Earlier rating, identity, payment, and redress experiences. Argentina’s adoption context is shaped by insecure and intermittent work, making rapid income access more decisive than confidence in a particular trust feature. In Argentina’s delivery-worker survey, 27% said that friends already working for a company motivated their choice of main platform, and 8.2% identified the company’s good reputation; the report links the importance of friends and contacts in entry to the high share of recently arrived migrants among observed couriers.[2] Delivery-platform growth has been associated with easy access, promised schedule flexibility, and the prospect of better earnings than other alternatives.[2] During the pandemic, workers who had lost jobs used delivery platforms while looking for other work, in a context where unemployment insurance had low coverage and insufficient replacement value.

Among 397 Uber drivers surveyed in Greater Buenos Aires in 2021, 61.5% selected needing work or supplementary income as a reason for starting; 18.6% selected having time for other activities.[8][80]

Argentina’s informal and precarious-work conditions make low-friction onboarding valuable, but they also make workers sensitive to conditions that can abruptly stop earnings. In 2019, 42.3% of employed people were in activities classified as precarious, rising to 60.5% among employed people aged 18–29. The definition includes unregistered employees, unpaid family workers, and own-account workers in unskilled or operationally skilled jobs. In delivery, workers commonly enter as monotributistas: they invoice as self-employed workers and bear their own health, social-security, and occupational-risk costs.[8][10]

In Q4 2024, 42.0% of employed people across Argentina’s 31 urban agglomerations were informally employed and 57.8% were formally employed. The informal category includes jobs in formal enterprises, informal productive units and households.[114][115]

Argentina’s informality measures do not assume workers can identify their own formal status: workers may not know whether a productive unit is registered, confuse pension deductions with registration, or associate *en blanco* with salaried work and *monotributo* with commercial contracting.[115]

Workers do not treat platform work as equivalent to secure formal employment. Interviewed Argentine delivery workers commonly described platform work as refuge work and temporary, while associating formal employment with paid sick leave, paid vacation, unemployment insurance, severance, health coverage, and collective rights. One PedidosYa worker contrasted formal employment with being a monotributista whose account could become inactive and lose status after taking time off.[8]

Zolvers-mediated domestic work in Argentina can improve registration relative to traditional arrangements, while a move from salaried employment into platform self-employment can feel more precarious.[97]

The adoption implication is that identity, ratings, payment controls, and appeals must preserve fast access to earning rather than replicate the delays and insecurity workers already experience outside the platform. A safeguard that delays activation or freezes funds without a clear reason may be perceived as a loss of the platform’s principal advantage: immediate, flexible income access.[8][10]

Ratings generate both opportunity and anxiety in Argentina. On freelance-design platforms, interviewees described a clear reputation pathway: new workers take simple, low-paid projects to build completed-project histories, good scores, and positive reviews; those signals later help them reach better-paid and more interesting projects. Reputation here is a positive adoption and progression mechanism because it converts early work into future access.[8]

Argentine freelancers also build portfolios and client networks through simple, low-paid projects; these assets can partly substitute for university, family or prior-employer contacts.[8]

The same mechanism becomes less credible when scores are tied to livelihood but workers cannot understand or contest them. In Argentine delivery platforms, algorithms averaged post-service customer scores and tracked accepted-order proportions. Platforms used scores ranging from 1–100, 1–5, or overall ranking positions. The score could combine customer evaluations with platform criteria such as acceptance rate, completed orders, punctuality, and tenure.[10][2]

Surveyed delivery workers saw ratings as consequential: 76.4% believed scores affected the quantity of work they received, and about 60% believed scores affected the characteristics of the work. In Glovo focus groups, workers said lower scores delayed access to weekly schedule selection and excluded them from the most desirable time slots. Glovo stated that ratings did not affect the number or quality of orders and that assignment depended on distance among courier, merchant, and consumer; the report leaves that conflict unresolved.[2]

Uber’s Argentine driver model illustrates the ambivalence. The thesis states that drivers were expected to maintain ratings between 4.6 and 4.8 out of five and could lose app access if their average fell below the minimum. In the Greater Buenos Aires sample, drivers rated customer-score-based supervision 6.33 out of 10. Yet 15.1% named dissatisfaction with customers rating their work as a possible reason to leave, although that concern was not statistically associated with an intention to leave.[80]

The fairness problem is not the existence of feedback itself; it is feedback functioning as a hidden employment-control system. The Argentine Uber thesis notes that occasional passengers may know little about the driver and may not use objective criteria, creating a perception that effort is judged unfairly. It also cites evidence that ratings can reflect homophily—for example, similarity in age between passenger and driver—rather than service quality alone. Continuous passenger evaluation can create stress, worry, anxiety, and a feeling of excessive surveillance.[80]

Judgment — In Argentina, use ratings as a reputation asset before using them as an access-control tool. A worker should be able to see the rating inputs, distinguish customer feedback from platform-operational metrics, identify which factors are outside their control, and challenge a score before it affects access to work. This preserves the progression value visible in freelance platforms while avoiding the “continuous judgment” experience reported in delivery and ride-hailing.

Identity and data requests require a particularly clear explanation of purpose. Argentina has usable official infrastructure: Renaper offers remote identity-validation services to businesses and public bodies, including DNI-data validation, fingerprint validation, and face-photo validation. Organizations seeking access must comply with Personal Data Protection Law No. 25,326, and data transfers require prior consent, disclosure of the purpose, and identification of the recipient. Renaper’s listed fingerprint and face-photo services do not include liveness detection.[3]

Government identity validation itself shows where digital flows can fail. Mi Argentina requires an account, app, and mobile data or Internet access. It asks users to permit camera access, remain in natural light with a clear background, hold the phone at face height, remove glasses, and avoid sudden movement. The government identifies changes in lighting, dirty lenses, phone movement, and lost connectivity as possible causes of failure, and offers an in-person Punto Digital fallback.[44]

This matters because Argentine drivers have displayed concern about data misuse and duplicate accounts. In a non-probability survey of 397 Uber drivers in AMBA, fielded from 11 September 2021 to 13 March 2022, drivers resisted providing an email address because they feared data theft and illegal “twin” Uber accounts.[80] Repeated explanation that the study was academic and a digital summary of the project partially mitigated the concern.[80]

A fraud case illustrates why generic reassurance is inadequate. In one Argentine false-job-offer case, scammers initially allowed the victim to withdraw earnings two or three times to create trust. They later increased requested deposits from ARS 4,000 to ARS 35,000 and blocked access to accumulated earnings unless the victim paid. The victim made deposits through Mercado Pago and sent proof through WhatsApp; a purported adviser later asked for personal data through Telegram.[81]

Judgment — The relevant design response is not simply stronger verification. It is a consent-and-control pattern: state why each item is needed, identify the verifier, say whether data are retained or shared, show the status of the check, and provide a recovery route if the check fails. Argentina’s official model already demonstrates the value of an assisted fallback; a platform should add a fast in-app recovery route rather than making office visits the only remedy.

Payment assurance can encourage adoption when it is tied to a clear and credible promise of being paid on time. Argentine freelance designers value platform intermediation because it assures timely payment; they also value dollar-denominated earnings in a context of wage depreciation and currency volatility. This is a direct example of payment certainty supporting participation, although it concerns freelance design rather than delivery or ride-hailing.[8]

Delivery workers report the opposite trust condition: unclear rules for allocation and payment. Workers who depended on delivery platforms as a principal or sole income source identified unclear rules about how orders are assigned and paid, alongside low per-delivery amounts, insecurity, and lack of coverage for accidents or theft. Platforms in the cited delivery study paid workers only for orders that reached customers and offered no base salary.[8][10]

Argentine workers face dynamic pricing and limited disclosure of fares, bonuses, and deductions; they also pay for vehicles, devices, and connectivity, making net earnings difficult to calculate.[116]

Argentina’s payment infrastructure can support immediate, legible disbursement. The Central Bank’s Transferencias 3.0 system describes transfers credited within a maximum of 15 seconds, 24 hours a day, all year, between bank and payment accounts. In August 2026, Argentina recorded 779 million immediate push transfers worth ARS 96 trillion; 76.9% had a payment account, or CVU, as origin and/or destination.[54][55]

However, fast transfers are not escrow. Interoperable QR transfer payments are irrevocable once the merchant receives the funds. An escrow arrangement instead holds funds under explicit release conditions. Workana describes its fixed-price “Depósito em Garantia (Escrow)” as a system in which the client deposits the full agreed amount and funds remain held until agreed conditions are met; partial releases can be agreed as work progresses.[54][69]

Judgment — For Argentina’s on-demand work, use instant payout as the default for undisputed completed tasks, and reserve holds for defined risk events. A platform should not import a long-project escrow model into short delivery or ride tasks. The practical trust signal is a visible earnings ledger—accepted task, expected amount, adjustments, payout destination, transfer status, and challenge option—because uncertainty around allocation and payment is already a reported worker concern.[8][55]

Dispute handling is the clearest retention-critical trust experience in Argentina. In the Fairwork assessment discussed in the findings, all evaluated Argentine platforms showed unilateralism in conflict resolution. DiDi Argentina was identified as an exception with accessible communication channels and documented appeal processes.[73]

The difference between having a formal process and delivering usable redress is substantial. In an Argentine delivery-worker survey, 85.7% said their platform had a formal procedure to seek advice, help, or make a complaint. Yet workers and focus groups reported that support responses could be slow, unsatisfactory, or never arrive. A total of 61.8% had made a complaint or requested assistance; common reasons included order cancellations, payment issues, and technical problems, each named by approximately 40%, while 20% named conflict with customers or merchants.[2]

Account sanctions intensify the cost of ineffective support. Among the delivery workers surveyed, 29.5% had been deactivated or sanctioned at least once, with sanction periods averaging 5.6 days. Of workers who had been sanctioned, 57.3% considered the sanction unfair and 46% challenged it. Workers accepted some blocks for administrative, conduct, or delivery-rule violations, but called blocks arbitrary where the underlying issue was a merchant’s mistake, they could not answer support while riding, or cash-deposit channels were closed.[2]

Some reported remedies also consumed the time that workers needed to earn. Workers attempting to reverse blocks had to go to platform offices; the process cost work time and income, and reversal was not guaranteed. In one case, a courier wrote to support because rain prevented completion of an order; support responded about 20 minutes later, after the courier had already travelled to the merchant, and then removed the order.[2]

The 2021 Greater Buenos Aires Uber study links these experiences to intended retention. Drivers scored company response speed at 5.75 out of 10 and handling of complaints or inquiries at 5.49. Dissatisfaction with complaint or request handling was significantly associated with intention to leave (χ2=18,637; p=,000), although the association was weak, with Cramér’s V of ,217.[80] The study gives two prevalence figures for dissatisfaction—29.5% in the narrative and 22.2%, or 88 respondents, in the table—but reports the same association.[80]

The same study found that higher perceived organizational credibility was directly associated with intention to remain: the logistic-regression estimate was b=1.841, Exp(b)=6.303, p=.043. Its credibility construct included perceptions of promised rewards, professional development, earnings, transparent communication, openness to dialogue, coordination, honesty, reliability, and consistency.[80]

The Argentine design decision is to make dispute handling visible, time-bounded, and reachable without removing a worker from earning access. A credible flow should first state the reason for a block or adjustment; second, preserve the evidence attached to the task; third, allow an in-app response; fourth, offer human review for livelihood-affecting decisions; and fifth, show the case status and deadline. This responds directly to the gap between formal complaint availability and reported support quality.[2][80]

Colombia

Colombia’s high-informality context makes platform entry a route to income but also makes trust safeguards easy to interpret as employment gates. From February to April 2024, 12.7 million people, or 55.8% of employed people, were informally employed. Own-account workers comprised 61.8% of informal employment and private employees 23.0%; together they represented 10.8 million people.[82]

Research on Colombian digital consumers identifies competence, honesty, benevolence, and data security as dimensions of trust.[117]

Colombia’s national informal-employment rate was 54.5% in July–September 2026, compared with 40.3% in its 13 largest cities and metropolitan areas and 82.6% in population centres and dispersed rural areas.[118][119]

DANE’s Colombian informality categories include own-account workers in small establishments and employers in enterprises of five or fewer people. Its EMICRON survey separately records financial inclusion, RUT and Chamber of Commerce registration as microbusiness characteristics.[118][119][120]

Platform work commonly enters through economic need and social proof. An OIT survey in Colombia found that the leading reason for choosing delivery-platform work was inability to find other work. Research on Rappi workers reports that many came from prior informal work, saw app work as an income opportunity, and often arrived through friends, relatives, or acquaintances. In interviews and surveys cited in that research, lack of access to decent jobs and scarcity of formal work accounted for 64% of stated reasons for working with Rappi.[12][13]

Personal referrals matter because workers are assessing a platform through people they already trust, not only through product claims. In this setting, referral-led adoption creates a high bar for a new platform: actual treatment of the first cohort will rapidly shape the stories that circulate through peer networks.Judgment — In Colombia, referrals should be treated as a trust channel. Onboarding, first payout, and first appeal are the moments most likely to become peer evidence for or against the platform.

A 2021 survey of 413 platform users in Bogotá, Cali and Medellín found average trust of 5.29 out of 7; its competence, honesty and benevolence components averaged 5.64, 5.12 and 5.09, respectively.[117]

In the 2021 survey of 413 users in Bogotá, Cali and Medellín, trust was associated with electronic word of mouth at a standardized coefficient of 0.670 and an R² of 0.448.[117]

Colombian digital-consumer research identifies the phone as the principal internet device and finds that users expect stronger security and transparency for banking and online procedures than for entertainment and social activity.[121]

The same Colombian research identifies personal-data misuse, misinformation and unclear operations as trust concerns. Shared family, third-party and plan access can ease connectivity barriers while introducing privacy, security and traceability risks.[121]

Ratings. Uber Colombia describes a mutual post-trip rating model using one to five stars. Among current Uber drivers with prior taxi-industry experience, 70% of Colombian drivers reported pride in working with Uber, drivers reported that Uber customers were more respectful than taxi passengers, 46% reported greater independence and flexibility, and 44% reported greater income stability. These are reported views of current drivers with taxi experience.[11][66]

Rappi’s courier score incorporates customer ratings, completed orders, and cancellations. It affects courier visibility and access to orders, so a low score can reduce orders offered and potential earnings. Colombian delivery platforms generally combine completed orders, acceptance rate, and customer ratings, commonly on a one-to-five-star scale.[13][12]

Workers identify the use of customer ratings in worker classification and its effect on assigned orders as a negative aspect. Rappi’s bronze, silver, gold, and diamond tiers also determine accident-coverage percentages or benefits assigned to family in the event of death.[12][13]

Judgment — Colombia should separate customer-feedback signals from entitlement signals. Customer feedback can help improve matching and service quality, but it should not silently determine earnings access, insurance coverage, or high-demand-zone access. When one score changes several livelihood outcomes, the platform must display the factors, their weight, the current consequence, and the available remedy.[13]

Among the two sampled Rappi-worker groups, Venezuelan migrants represented 54%–62%. The research links their participation in precarious work to migration-status barriers, limited access to formal work, and cultural and social integration barriers. An interviewed Venezuelan courier said he feared that formalization would leave him unemployed because he lacked papers to satisfy formalization requirements. Identity design for these groups must account for migrants as well as Colombian citizens.[13]

Colombia provides a formal identity route for eligible Venezuelan migrants through the Permiso por Protección Temporal, or PPT. Migración Colombia defines the PPT as an identity document under the Temporary Protection Statute for Venezuelan migrants, intended to regularize migration status and issued free of charge. Rappi’s courier activation page explicitly accepts a current Colombian national identity card, foreigner identity card, or PPT.[46][4]

The migrant-verification path is not instantaneous for everyone. The PPT process includes RUMV preregistration, a socioeconomic survey, biometric registration, and the PPT application. The financial-sector identity guide says status verification requires the first two registration phases and that Migración Colombia may take up to 90 calendar days to provide status information after those steps. It also lists the PPT certificate in process, Venezuelan passport, V, M, and R visas, and PEP-TUTOR as valid documents for Venezuelan migrants to access financial products and services.[46][47]

This creates a central adoption trade-off: identity controls that accept only final credentials can exclude people for whom app work is an entry route into income, while controls that ignore identity integrity enable account trade and impersonation. Rappi’s stated activation process offers a relatively low-friction route for people with accepted documents: agents validate identity documents, and the company states that activation is performed once and takes 30 minutes. It also offers in-person activation in Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, and Barranquilla.[4]

Colombian users expect greater transparency when a digital activity carries greater risk.[121]

The rapid-activation promise sits alongside a broader report that most users obtained active accounts and could begin working in under 24 hours. That speed supports adoption where lack of alternatives is a principal driver. It does not remove the need for safeguards, because Rappi terms also require legal migration status, personal use of the registered account, ongoing documentation updates at least annually or on request, and verification and security checks.[13][14]

Rappi’s stated identity model includes risk-based biometric use. The company says it uses biometric data when needed to meet applicable legal obligations and protect the ecosystem, users, commercial partners, and their staff. Stated purposes include verifying the courier’s identity, preventing fraud and impersonation, stopping unauthorized access, ensuring that only the authorized account holder uses the account, and applying reinforced authentication or monitoring where the nature, context, or risk level of an activity requires it.[36]

The need for continuing account integrity is visible in actual workarounds. Reuters reported that Rappi had cancelled 5,600 accounts in Colombia since March, including 1,060 for false-identity issues. More than a dozen Rappitenderos told Reuters there was a profile black market, four said they had bought an account, and fake profiles were reportedly offered for as much as USD 160. A Medellín courier separately said that, while blocked, he could keep working using an account bought through social media or WhatsApp.[48][13]

The Colombian identity design decision is to combine fast document-compatible activation with step-up verification when risk changes, not to rely on a single hard gate. The verified account must remain personal, but a worker whose documents are pending, mismatched, or challenged needs a clear status, accepted alternatives, and a route to correct the record. Otherwise a safeguard intended to prevent impersonation can push income-dependent workers toward account sharing or off-platform work.[14][36][13]

Prior rating experience has a mixed effect on adoption. Uber Colombia describes a mutual rating model after every trip, using a one-to-five-star scale. The company says ratings record each side’s view, identify what is going well or needs improvement, and foster respect and cordiality.[11]

For drivers with taxi-industry experience, the comparison with prior work can be favorable. In the 2024 IDB-Uber survey, 58% of drivers with taxi experience reported pride in working with Uber; the share was 70% among Colombian drivers. Across the subgroup, 46% reported greater independence and flexibility and 44% reported greater income stability.[66]

Yet the broader Uber-versus-taxi quality index places Colombia among the lowest-scoring markets, despite high pride among Colombian former taxi drivers. The index combines six items: pride, customer respect, income stability, earnings, independence and flexibility, and feeling secure driving with the app. The apparent conflict is not a contradiction: 70% is one favorable attitude among Colombian drivers with taxi experience, while the index aggregates several dimensions across the relevant comparison group.[66][83]

Rappi’s Colombian courier scoring is more materially consequential than a simple post-service feedback system.

Workers identify this linkage as a negative aspect where it determines access to work. The OIT summary notes the weight of customer ratings in worker classification and resulting effects on assigned orders.

Payment-related trust in Colombia rests more on speed, clarity, and control than on a demonstrated preference for held funds. Rappi states that courier earnings are displayed before the courier accepts an order and that total earnings from the immediately preceding week are transferred weekly to the courier’s registered bank account. It also states that tips belong 100% to the courier and are neither interfered with nor retained by Rappi.[14]

The user experience can still be undermined if pay is not intelligible. Colombian Rappi workers report limited clarity about how total earnings and kilometre rates are calculated. They also report that in-app tips are integrated into total delivery pay rather than reported separately, making it harder to understand the final income composition.[13]

The country’s payment context reinforces the need to offer more than one rail. The Banco de la República says cash was preferred by 78.6% of Colombian adults in 2023, partly because a lack of interoperability among digital-payment providers limited electronic-payment adoption. Bre-B, Colombia’s interoperated instant-payment system, began operation on October 6, 2025; by January 2026 it had 218 participating entities, five fully interoperating systems, and a directory with 99 million aliases corresponding to more than 33 million customers and 2.8 million merchants.[63]

Drivers’ stated willingness to use an optional platform financial feature is stronger in Colombia than in Brazil, but it is not evidence that workers accept payout delays or escrow. In the 2019 IDB-Uber survey, 72% of Colombian driver respondents said they would use automatic savings; in the 2024 survey, 82% said they would use it. In the 2024 survey, 39% selected a 10% contribution of Uber earnings for pension saving and 41% selected 5% for health-insurance saving.[83][66]

The Colombian payment design decision is to make voluntary money-management tools opt-in and keep earned-income availability separate from them. An optional savings, insurance, or reserve feature can build on stated interest, but it must show the amount, destination, withdrawal conditions, and ability to pause. It should never be confused with escrow or used to justify delaying ordinary worker payouts.[83][66][14]

Colombian dispute experience contains both usable platform patterns and reasons for skepticism. Rappi’s terms say a courier may challenge temporary or permanent suspension through the Help Center, submit images and evidence, and receive an in-app outcome saying whether the account has been restored or remains suspended. The terms also describe the Defensoría al Repartidor as an autonomous, impartial third party; it provides virtual appointments nationally and in-person appointments in Bogotá, Medellín, and Barranquilla, but app-use issues must first go through the Help Center.[14]

Consumers have a different but more traceable complaint interface. Rappi’s Colombian consumer flow offers both an in-app Help Center and a website PQRs button. In the app, users can file a PQR from a specific order, check its status, request additional information, and see all complaints in Conversations. On the website, a user receives a case number by email and can continue communication in the same email thread.[70]

The stated process does not erase the legacy of opaque blocks. Research reports that 35% of interviewees in the Platlaboral Redlatt project said refusing a delivery could lead to temporary or permanent blocking, sometimes without clear justification. Reuters reported a Bogotá strike against account blocks and a points system that determined who could work in high-demand areas. The Superintendency of Industry and Commerce had previously found that Rappi failed to comply with orders to improve complaint responses.[13][48]

In 2026, Rappi reported that its Courier Ombudsman had received more than 16,500 nationwide assistance requests to date and that 30% produced favorable outcomes for delivery workers. The figure shows use of a second-instance channel, but the report does not specify the outcomes for the remaining requests.[76]

The Colombian dispute-design decision is to make an appeal a continuation of the task record, not a separate request to “be heard.” The courier should see the original decision, evidence used, policy basis, any payout impact, available evidence upload, reviewer status, deadline, and escalation route. This addresses the specific gap between a documented appeal channel and workers’ experience of unexplained blocks.[14][13][76]

Brazil

IBGE reports that 71.1% of Brazilian platform workers are informal.[122]

Earlier rating, identity, payment, and redress experiences. Brazilian workers are familiar with platform systems, but familiarity does not equal trust. Delivery-worker social networks are established off-platform trust channels: research observed 46 groups, analysed 378 posts with more than 17,000 comments, and found that workers use these spaces for solidarity, information exchange, and mobilisation around earnings, accidents, work strategies, and daily difficulties.[27] In the third quarter of 2025, about 2.0 million people aged 14 or older worked through digital service platforms in either their main or secondary job, equivalent to 1.9% of employed people.[27] Of platform workers in their main job, 86.8% were own-account workers; 62.7% worked exclusively through platforms.[84]

In Q3 2024, about 1.7 million Brazilians worked through digital service platforms in their main job, representing 1.9% of private-sector employment. That was 25.4% above the Q4 2022 figure of 1.3 million.[122]

In Q3 2024, 71.1% of Brazilian platform workers were informal, compared with 43.8% of non-platform workers; 35.9% contributed to social security, compared with 61.9% of non-platform workers. Own-account workers comprised 86.1% of platform workers.[122]

The main stated reasons for platform work in Brazil are flexibility, lack of other work, and the prospect of higher earnings. Among people using service platforms in their main job in 2025, 26.8% cited flexibility, 24.6% inability to find another job, and 20.8% higher expected earnings than other available work. For passenger-transport app workers excluding taxi apps, the corresponding figures were 27.2%, 33.4%, and 24.4%; among delivery workers, they were 26.6%, 26.9%, and 26.6%.[84]

Prior formal-work experiences help explain why autonomy is attractive. Qualitative research with young delivery workers describes previous jobs marked by low pay, nominal benefits, broken agreements, humiliation, extreme subordination, rigid schedules, and limited prospects. Workers value flexibility and absence of direct supervision even while recognizing algorithmic control; their use of autonomy does not mean they experience no control.[85]

The Brazilian adoption proposition is therefore income plus perceived independence, not trust technology alone. A safeguard that resembles another opaque employer control will undermine the very autonomy workers believe they are choosing, even if it improves formal risk management.[84][85]

A Brazilian study finds that adoption intent depends more on trust in the platform, product, and community than directly on trust in an individual peer.[123]

In a study of 942 Brazilian adults, trust in the platform, product and user community positively affected intention to use sharing services, while trust in an individual peer had no direct effect.[123]

Brazilian workers’ prior rating experience is commonly one of continuous scoring and automated consequences. Research on delivery work describes scoring tools, route alerts, reputational evaluations, and automatic blocks as routine. It also describes continuous monitoring of time online, movement, productivity, and behaviour, with algorithms determining order volume, routes, and per-trip amounts.[27]

Workers report an information-asymmetry problem. Delivery workers in the cited research viewed automated decisions as insufficiently transparent, including decisions related to how customer and restaurant fees were allocated. Another study characterizes algorithmic management through continuous tracking and evaluation, automation, and lack of transparency.[27][28]

A Campinas courier gave the practical fear behind these perceptions: a customer complaint could trigger an almost immediate account block, even where the customer simply disliked the worker. A separate driver reported that rejecting more than three Uber rides could result in a block and described deactivation as a threat to a person’s present income source.[28]

Platform practices show that alternative patterns are available. Brazil’s 99 uses bilateral passenger-driver ratings. Its performance-rate calculation compares completed rides with accepted rides over one week. New drivers are exempt from performance evaluation until they complete 50 rides or reach 28 days of use, and city-specific thresholds can lead to temporary suspension of 24–72 hours or permanent suspension after four weeks below the local limit.[17]

99 also makes ratings visible to the other side of the ride: passengers can see driver ratings at request or after the ride, while drivers can see passenger ratings when the ride is requested. The displayed rating is based on the last 500 trips, but users cannot inspect their own score in 99Pop. The company says a high score can bring peak-hour privileges and access to highly rated users, while below-average ratings can lead to bans.[18]

Uber Brazil also uses a rolling 500-trip average and presents ratings as supporting safety, integrity, and good coexistence. The company says high-rated drivers may receive in-app extra amounts and region-specific benefits. These systems offer useful reputation signals, but their use as work-allocation and account-access tools makes explanation and appeal essential.[86]

Judgment — A new Brazilian platform should preserve a novice grace period and avoid making a single composite score the hidden governor of work access. Separate safety incidents, customer service feedback, acceptance behaviour, and fraud signals. Show which category triggered a consequence and provide an evidence-based review route before an irreversible loss of access.

Identity verification begins from an especially familiar identifier: the CPF. Receita Federal describes CPF as a person’s identity record, containing information such as name, date of birth, and address. CPF registration is available to Brazilian and foreign people, resident or non-resident; it has no minimum age, is unique and permanent for each citizen, and is free through federal services.[49]

CPF collection is ordinary in daily life. Mastercard describes CPF as Brazil’s most common form of identification, requested for activities including buying groceries, renting a car, and logging into public Wi-Fi. The same source says CPF is not confidential and that static identifiers are insufficient for robust digital identity verification because readily available deterministic data can be compromised.[51]

A CPF status also cannot be treated as a binary identity verdict. Receita Federal says a “Suspenso” status means information is incorrect or incomplete and can be corrected; “Pendente de Regularização” refers to a missing income-tax declaration; “Cancelado” can reflect a duplicate registration or procedural decision; and “Nulo” indicates fraud.[49]

iFood’s courier onboarding illustrates a more layered model. Registration requires age 18 or older, an identity document, and a bank account in the applicant’s name linked to their CPF. Motorcycle and car delivery add category A or B driving licences, respectively. iFood may recheck documents after registration, may refuse or suspend registration for security reasons, and may make a symbolic bank deposit to confirm the account.[33][37]

There is a material speed risk. Brazilian delivery workers report waiting months for approval, and in some cases as long as two years; the report does not attribute the wait specifically to identity verification. Still, approval delay changes the worker’s experience of platform entry from flexible income access to a queue controlled by an opaque system.[27]

The Brazilian identity design decision is CPF-plus, not CPF-only. Use CPF as a familiar matching field, then add document-to-selfie or device and account-consistency checks where risk warrants it. Explain any exception status in plain Portuguese, distinguish correctable data problems from fraud, and show the activation queue and next action so an applicant does not experience verification as unexplained exclusion.[51][49][37][27]

Brazil’s financial-trust evidence points to caution about platform control over earnings. In the 2024 IDB-Uber survey, only 39% of Brazilian drivers said they would use automatic saving from Uber earnings, while 50% rejected the option. Willingness fell from 76% in 2019 to 39% in 2024. For pension saving, 40% selected no saving; for health-insurance saving, 48% selected no saving.[66][83]

Pix accounted for 24.9% of recorded Brazilian payments in 2023; 73% of surveyed respondents had used it, and 64.9% preferred it, compared with 55.7% who preferred cash.[124][125]

By December 2023, 74% of Brazilian adults registered in Cadastro Único had a Pix key and 72% had made a Pix payment.[124][125]

In Brazilian Pix research, merchants rated receiving a payment easy or very easy at 90.2%, rapid or very rapid at 88.9%, and secure or very secure at 70.4%.[124][126]

Pix familiarity is not universal: 17.3% of respondents had not used it in the prior year, 37.3% of non-users did not know how to use it, and 24.2% of users sometimes chose another payment method because of internet difficulty.[124]

The broader financial condition makes involuntary holds especially risky. Across the IDB-Uber sample, only 27% said they could manage a financial emergency; the report characterizes many drivers as living paycheck to paycheck. In Brazil, platform workers in their main job worked an average of 44.7 hours per week, compared with 39.3 hours for non-platform workers, while earning 12.0% less per hour on average.[66][84]

Brazilian workers also report platform control over payment arrangements. In 2025, 71.3% of delivery workers said the platform fully determined how payment was received, and 78.3% said the platform fully determined payment per task. This creates a distinction that product teams should preserve: workers may accept an app as the route for income, while still resisting the app deciding when, how, or whether income can be accessed.[84]

For Brazil’s Q3 2024 platform workers, apps set the amount received for 91.2% of private-passenger drivers and determined customers for 76.7%; among delivery workers, apps set deadlines for 70.4% and the payment form for 76.8%.[127]

Some existing Brazilian flows address specific payment risks without becoming general escrow. 99’s Acordo de Pagamento appears only after a driver reports receiving no payment or partial payment for a cash trip. The driver enters the amount received, the passenger is asked to confirm the nonpayment or partial payment, and the 99 team reviews the case if the passenger does not confirm. The company says the analysis can take a few hours before reimbursement.[65]

iFood provides payment-dispute routes for missing or incomplete courier payouts. For a missing payout, the courier submits CPF, the unpaid period, and bank-account proof; for an incomplete payout, the courier submits CPF, the relevant week, and the received amount. iFood says these support requests are handled within 24 hours, but that is a response commitment rather than a stated correction deadline.[33]

Judgment — Brazil needs payout autonomy as the default. Offer opt-in saving, reserves, and insurance contributions only after funds are available or through a clearly reversible choice. For payment incidents, use a narrow case flow like 99’s—amount, counterparty confirmation where appropriate, status, reviewer, and stated timeline—rather than broad, unexplained payout holds.[66][83][65]

Prior dispute experiences make human access and review transparency central to credibility. Uber Brazil says driver-partners can seek a review of deactivation for criminal-record findings, suspected fraud, safety incidents, and low ratings. For suspected fraud, the appeal is initiated from the deactivation message in the Uber Driver app; a specialized team reviews it. Eligibility requires at least one completed trip in Brazil, the worker has 90 days to appeal, and may request review once.[72]

For criminal-record results, Uber states that iAudit or Emptor conducts checks. The deactivation notification identifies the provider and its portal, where the driver submits applicable certificates or documents. The process allows one review request per case and requires documents within 90 days.[72]

iFood’s terms state that a courier may request account-deactivation review in the app, explain the reasons, and receive a response within 48 hours. The deactivation screen also displays pending payout information. The terms say couriers are generally informed three days before deactivation, except in suspected criminal cases.[37]

Workers’ accounts show why formal review availability alone is not enough. One commenter said they could not access the app to contest a block. Another said they submitted proof and video but had an appeal denied twice. A third reported that support cancelled an uncompleted delivery after the worker sent photos and live location, yet the worker received a 24-hour block three days later, account health fell, and the worker believed fewer orders followed.[87]

An interviewed driver who had an accident described trying to contact the app and send messages, receiving no information, being unable to speak with anyone, bearing the loss, and being charged for food that could not be delivered. These experiences make an appeal interface credible only when it remains accessible after deactivation, retains the worker’s evidence, identifies the decision basis, and offers a meaningful escalation path.[28]

The Brazilian dispute-design decision is to maintain a reachable review channel even when the account is offline. Do not require the app session that a block has removed; provide web, phone, or assisted access. Show the evidence considered, differentiate immediate safety protection from final sanction, and make account, earnings, and appeal status visible in one case record.[87][28][37]

In the 2019 IDB-Uber survey, 76% of Brazilian driver respondents said they would use automatic savings; in the 2024 survey, 39% said they would use it, a decline of 37 percentage points. These responses concern stated interest in an optional financial feature, supporting an opt-in and reversible design for savings, reserves, and similar money controls rather than a default feature.[83]

In 2024, willingness to use automatic savings was 43 percentage points lower among surveyed Brazilian drivers than Colombian drivers (39% versus 82%). A regional financial-trust feature should therefore not use Colombia’s expressed receptiveness as a proxy for Brazil.[66]

Applying the reported 61.8% own-account-worker share to Colombia’s 12.7 million informally employed people yields an estimated 7.8 million informally employed own-account workers in February–April 2024. This quantifies the scale of the group for whom documentation, payout, and enforcement friction can directly affect access to income.[82]

In the Argentine delivery-worker survey, reported availability of a formal help or complaint procedure exceeded reported use of a complaint or assistance request by 23.9 percentage points (85.7% versus 61.8%). The gap indicates that published availability alone is not the same as a support route workers actually use.[2]

Among Brazilian delivery workers in 2025, the share saying the platform fully determined task pay was 7.0 percentage points higher than the share saying it fully determined the payment-receipt method (78.3% versus 71.3%). The distinction supports separate controls for task-price transparency and payout-method choice rather than treating payment control as one undifferentiated experience.[84]

### The existing gig-platform trust practices and informal-economy conditions in Argentina

The three markets share high reliance on independent and informal work, but the trust systems workers bring with them are not interchangeable. Argentina combines low-friction entry with concern about opaque ratings, payment allocation, fraud, and complaint handling. Colombia combines peer-referral entry and migrant inclusion needs with fast document activation, strong account-personalization rules, and consequential courier scoring. Brazil combines routine familiarity with CPF and app ratings with skepticism toward opaque algorithmic management and platform control over earnings.[8][13][51][27]

The relevant comparison is not whether a market has ratings, verification, payment, or appeals. All three do. The relevant comparison is what workers have learned those mechanisms mean:

Judgment — A shared regional pattern should be implemented as a common trust architecture with local policy settings, not as one uniform flow. The common architecture is: verified identity, recorded task evidence, transparent money movement, explainable rating consequences, and an appeal path. The local settings are document types and fallback channels, payment rails and payout timing, rating consequences, language, and escalation access.

A useful cross-market principle is the **access-preserving safeguard**: a control should become stricter as the risk rises, but it should not block ordinary access to work or earned money without an explicit reason, a visible status, and a route to correction. Argentina’s fraud and data-theft concerns support clear purpose and recovery. Colombia’s migrant documentation context supports accepted alternatives and step-up verification. Brazil’s CPF and algorithmic-management context supports layered verification and explanation of automated decisions.[80][81][4][47][51][28]

The same principle applies to payment assurance. Escrow is appropriate where work has definable scope, deliverables, and acceptance milestones. Workana’s fixed-price flow requires full deposit in guarantee, allows partial release by agreement, uses platform-recorded communications as evidence, and progresses from mediation to arbitration if the parties cannot agree. It also applies an automatic 50/50 split for projects below USD 30, charges USD 10 for arbitration if paid within 96 hours, and does not allow mutual ratings after arbitration.[69][67]

Those conditions make Workana a useful model for project work, not a direct template for time-sensitive delivery and ride-hailing. A USD 10 dispute fee is proportionally significant for low-value work, a fixed 50/50 low-value outcome may feel predictable but may also feel detached from evidence, and a final decision without appeal reduces process complexity while increasing the importance of evidence capture before arbitration.[67][60]

Judgment — For gig tasks, use “micro-escrow” only where there is a genuine completion or quality dispute: retain only the disputed increment, not the whole worker balance; state the release rule before acceptance; and resolve through task-linked evidence. For ordinary completed tasks, the trust-building mechanism is not escrow but immediate, auditable payout.

The additional evidence sharpens the comparison in three ways. First, it documents an Argentine rating-recovery pattern rather than only a rating threshold. Uber Argentina requires Uber Black partners to maintain a 4.8 rating or higher, calculated from the 500 most recent rated trips. A partner falling below the threshold receives notice and has the next 80 trips evaluated to recover it; only after failing to recover does the partner stop receiving Uber Black requests.[23] This supports a rating design in which a consequential threshold has a visible remediation period before access to a premium work category is removed.[23]

Second, Workana shows how a project-market reputation system can combine performance, conduct, recency, and platform participation rather than treating a star average as the sole trust signal. Its levels combine total net income, net income over the prior three months plus the current month, distinct clients giving five-star ratings, average score, and recent non-compliance; its HERO tier additionally requires no connects suspension in the prior three months, no breaches in the prior 12 months, and 95% of projects completed without violations.[90] Workana makes the status dynamic: a freelancer can be downgraded when one requirement is no longer met and upgraded again when all requirements are met, and status changes the number of weekly connects from 2 at Iron to 80 at Hero.[90] This is a useful contrast with delivery scoring: a tier can reward verified project history and recent conduct, but its criteria and the work-access consequence must be inspectable.[90]

Third, Colombia’s reported delivery experience makes the cost of an unexplained automated customer remedy concrete. Fundación Karisma reported a Cali case in which a customer marked an order undelivered, the platform automatically gave the customer delivery credits, and the courier was unpaid and blocked after completing the delivery.[91] In another February 2022 case, a courier helping a colleague injured in a traffic accident was banned for seven days for not collecting the delivery; the injured colleague died.[91] Karisma therefore calls for timely explanations and redress for ratings and blocks, clear reasons for suspensions, the ability to report problems during a trip or delivery, and meaningful contact while blocked or suspended.[91] For an income-dependent Colombian courier, a customer-compensation flow, earnings decision, and enforcement decision must be one case record with an immediate incident-report option and a route to respond before a livelihood-affecting final sanction.[91]

Uber’s partner privacy notice, updated September 1, 2026, adds a reusable identity-and-redress pattern for the three-market architecture while requiring country settings to follow local law. Uber says it may collect government-issued identity documents, profile photos, selfies, and facial-verification information; before going online, driver and delivery partners take a real-time selfie, and facial recognition may compare it with the profile photo where law permits.[92] It says automated systems screen identity documents and profile photos for digital alteration, manipulation, reuse, and mismatch; flagged materials go to specialized human review, and a deactivated user has a right to appeal.[92] The notice also says partners can view their average one-to-five rating in the app and a rating breakdown in the Privacy Center.[92] This supports a common regional pattern of step-up identity confirmation, human review after an automated flag, appeal after deactivation, and inspectable rating information, localized by the laws and document rules of Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil.[92]

Brazil’s current Uber policy specifies safeguards that address part of the reported rating anxiety. Uber says mutual-rating access loss can occur when a driver’s or delivery partner’s average received rating falls below the average for other partners; it says it may remove ratings from excessively negative or biased users and ratings based on factors outside the driver’s control. Uber also states that it warns partners when access is at risk, except in serious cases requiring legal or safety investigation. A Brazilian rating design should therefore distinguish a service signal from a sanction trigger, identify the comparison rule, filter ratings based on out-of-control factors, and notify the worker before ordinary access loss.[29]

Uber Brazil’s identity model adds regular security checks of motor-vehicle records, real-time photo confirmation that must match the profile photo, current and unexpired required documents, and a prohibition on account sharing, impersonation, false data, and third-party documents. This supports a layered Brazilian onboarding and continuing-integrity flow rather than CPF-only verification.[29][51]

99’s passenger-side enforcement provides a graduated rating pattern that is relevant to Brazil. Its Security Center monitors drivers’ ratings of passengers and sends educational communications; a single rating does not trigger a block. Before a passenger block or suspension, 99 sends notice in the app and by email; an educational block lasts one week, followed by continued monitoring, and recurrence results in permanent deactivation. A passenger can seek unblocking through a portal, with the decision sent by email.[89]

By contrast, 99Food permits a restaurant, after a completed order, to select Insatisfeito and then Bloquear entregador parceiro; the named courier then stops receiving orders from that establishment. A Brazilian delivery platform should give the courier notice, the establishment-specific consequence, and a task-evidence response route whenever merchant feedback restricts future work.[93]

### Synthesis

Across Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil, prior trust experiences shape adoption through a common test: does the platform increase predictable access to income while leaving workers able to understand, correct, and contest its controls? Argentina shows the value of timely payment and credible complaint handling, but also data-fraud anxiety and mistrust of opaque ratings. Colombia shows that fast, document-compatible access and referrals can support adoption, while migrant exclusion, account trading, and consequential scoring can undermine it. Brazil shows that familiarity with CPF, ratings, and digital payments does not create trust when systems feel opaque, punitive, or controlling of earnings. The strongest direction is therefore access-preserving safeguards: fast onboarding, clear money status, explainable ratings, and human-accessible appeals; the local difference is which existing experience most needs repair.[8][80][13][66][28]

1. Build one trust architecture, but localize accepted documents, payout rails, rating consequences, and appeal access by country.
2. Keep ordinary activation and earned-income access fast; apply stronger verification or holds only when risk is visible and specific.
3. Make every consequential rating, payment adjustment, suspension, and verification failure explainable in the task record.
4. Provide an appeal route that remains usable after an account is blocked, with evidence upload, status tracking, decision timing, and human escalation.
5. Treat first activation, first payout, and first dispute as adoption moments, because peer networks in all three markets turn those experiences into platform reputation.

Dependable settlement is the payment choice for on-demand work; conditional holding is the payment choice for reviewable fixed-price projects, where a client funds agreed scope, deadline, obligations, and value before work begins. In Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil, workers use platforms to maintain income and may lose earning time through blocks, delayed support, or scheduled settlement.[2][14][84][64][69]

The cross-market rule is simple: account access and pay need an explanation-and-review path before the platform adds more behavioural scoring or more invasive checks. Argentina and Brazil supply the strongest evidence for this order because both show how ratings and enforcement can become material controls over access to work, while Colombia supplies the clearest existing escalation architecture.[2][27][94][14]

#### Argentina: make contestability visible before making entry stricter

Argentina should launch with a **contestability layer**: an in-app system that tells a worker what happened, what evidence was used, what immediate income effect follows, what they can submit, who will review it, and when they will receive a decision. This is the first priority because delivery workers reported that reversing a block required going to platform offices, cost work time and income, and still did not guarantee reactivation. In one reported Rappi case, support replied about 20 minutes after a courier had already gone to the merchant, then removed the order; the support channel existed but did not match the urgency of the work.[2]

The design should distinguish a worker-controlled failure from a merchant, customer, platform, connectivity, cash-handling, or support-access failure. Argentine workers accepted some administrative, behavioural, and delivery-rule violations as grounds for blocking, but described blocks as arbitrary when a merchant made the error, when they could not safely answer support while driving, or when cash-deposit channels were closed. This distinction should appear in the reason code itself, not be hidden in a generic account-status message.[2]

The appeal flow should be mobile-first, asynchronous, and evidence-oriented: select a reason; attach photo, route, merchant, customer, safety, or payment evidence; see whether earnings or access are temporarily affected; receive a dated decision; and escalate a material loss of access to human review.Judgment — For a courier who relies on daily work, an appeal that requires travel to an office is not merely inconvenient; it is an income-costing product requirement. The platform should also offer a safety-safe response window: never require a courier to answer a call or message while riding.

Score transparency is the second Argentine priority. The ILO study found that workers understood their scores to affect the amount of work received and, for roughly 60%, the characteristics of work; scores could include customer evaluation plus acceptance rates, completed orders, punctuality, and tenure. A rating therefore operates as both reputation and labour-allocation control, so a generic five-star label is inadequate explanation.[2]

The platform must say which part of a score is customer feedback, which part reflects operational performance, which elements change access to jobs or schedules, and which do not. It should show the worker a score ledger with date, event type, outcome, and adjustment reason, without exposing another user’s personal data. This protects the useful market function of reputation while preventing workers from having to infer consequences from changes in order volume or access.

There is a specific Argentine conflict that makes this essential. Glovo workers interviewed in Argentina said lower scores delayed access to weekly schedule selection and excluded them from the best hours. Glovo said scores did not affect the number or quality of orders and that assignments depended on distance between courier, merchant, and consumer. The product answer is not to decide which account users should believe; it is to disclose the actual allocation factors, identify whether score affects schedule access, and provide event-level review when an account consequence occurs.[2]

Argentina’s remote identity check should be proportionate rather than an in-person gate. RENAPER offers businesses and public bodies remote identity validation, including DNI data validation, fingerprint validation, and face-photo validation. The state service specifies that liveness detection sits outside the scope of its fingerprint and face-photo services, so a platform that needs proof of current presence must add a separate liveness control rather than assuming that a profile photo or registry match provides it.[3]

A practical Argentine onboarding flow can validate DNI data against RENAPER, capture a profile image or selfie, and route mismatches to review. It must disclose the purpose and recipient of data processing, collect only data that are adequate, relevant, and not excessive, and use an exception route for foreign documents or older DNI formats. Argentina’s data-protection law requires clear prior information about purpose, recipients, whether responses are mandatory, consequences of refusal, and access, correction, and deletion rights.[43]

Payment escrow should not be the first Argentine expansion bet for location-based gigs. The established protected-payment example is crowdwork: clients deposit before work begins and delivery is held until payment occurs. Argentine designers value platform intermediation that assures timely payment, but the source distinguishes virtual crowdwork from physical delivery, where work conditions are governed by the local labour market.[8]

Argentina’s adoption context makes trust credibility especially consequential. Friends already working at a company motivated 27% of surveyed delivery workers to choose their main platform, and 8.2% cited company reputation. Recent migrants were a major reason peer networks mattered to entry. This means a bad unblock experience, unexplained ranking, or unpaid-work story is likely to travel through social proof, not remain an individual support ticket.[2]

Argentina’s product decision: build the appeal and explanation system before increasing verification depth or score-linked incentives; it makes reputation usable without turning it into unchallengeable control.

#### Colombia: operationalize the staged redress path, then keep verification inclusive

Colombia has the strongest documented starting point for a staged dispute-resolution interface. Soy Rappi states that a courier notified of temporary or permanent suspension can use the Help Center, submit images and evidence, and receive an in-app decision after a support agent reviews the case. The terms also describe the Defensoría al Repartidor as an autonomous, impartial third party for situations affecting courier interests or well-being.[14]

The Ombudsman is a second instance rather than a substitute for first-line support. It offers virtual appointments nationwide and in-person appointments only in Bogotá, Medellín, and Barranquilla, with prior appointment required in both cases; app-use matters must first go through the Help Center. In April 2026, Rappi described strengthened channels for unresolved blocks, claims, and complaints, including individualized accompaniment and second-stage review for complex cases.[14][71]

The product priority is to make that sequence legible in the interface. First, notify the person of the decision and the relevant rule. Second, let them upload evidence and see the review state. Third, show the deadline for an initial answer and the conditions for escalation. Fourth, offer the Ombudsman route without requiring a user to discover it outside the case flow.Judgment — A staged path earns trust only if the user can see where their case sits, why the first decision was made, and what escalation changes.

Colombian onboarding should remain document-linked but avoid unnecessary exclusion. Soy Rappi requires couriers to pass verification and security filters, use their own account, be at least 18, maintain legal immigration status, and keep vehicle documents current when delivering by car or motorcycle. The terms list valid identity documents as the Colombian citizenship card, foreigner identity card, or Temporary Protection Permit (PPT).[14]

This is a useful baseline for an individual-account integrity model: verify identity, prevent account sharing, verify vehicle authority where service risk requires it, and recheck documents when risk or expiry warrants it. It is not a basis for treating a stricter check as inherently better. Colombia’s financial-access guidance lists the PPT, the certificate showing a PPT application is in progress, Venezuelan passport, specified visas, and PEP-TUTOR as valid documents for Venezuelan migrants in that context. A platform should therefore create a clearly labelled migrant-document route and avoid designing only for a Colombian national ID.[47]

The PPT route needs careful status design. The Migración Colombia lookup can show approved, in process, or ready for collection; an in-process certificate documents that the application is under study but does not guarantee approval. Correcting document errors can require reissuance, an identity document, and a completed FUT form, while status information can take up to 90 calendar days after required registrations.[47]

Judgment — The onboarding interface should separate “can work now,” “needs additional document,” and “record mismatch under correction.” A binary rejected/approved screen would transform administrative delay into platform exclusion and invite account-sharing workarounds.

Colombian rating design should be third, after redress and inclusive verification. Users rate each courier’s service, while the platform controls ratings, comments, and observations; Rappi is described as assigning higher or lower courier status based on consistency and good service. The platform should disclose the inputs to status, which outcomes it influences, when the status refreshes, and how a courier disputes an incorrect rating or operational event.[15]

For payments, Colombia should prioritize clear earnings and settlement information, not market escrow as a universal answer. Rappi displays courier earnings before order acceptance, pays the prior week’s earnings weekly to the registered bank account, and states that tips belong entirely to the courier without Rappi retention. Cash orders work differently: the courier collects the full amount and owes it until depositing through Rappi channels.[14]

Colombia remains highly cash-oriented: the Banco de la República reported cash as the preferred payment instrument for 78.6% of adults in 2023, even while Bre-B’s interoperated instant-payment system began operations on October 6, 2025. By January 2026, Bre-B had 218 participating entities and five fully interoperating SPBVIs. The platform should therefore support cash-aware exception handling and increasingly interoperable transfers, while showing each party whether payment is pending, received, due for deposit, or under review.[63]

Colombia’s product decision: lead with a timed, visible Help Center-to-Ombudsman escalation path; retain proportionate verification with migrant-document routes; treat ratings and payment displays as transparent operational systems, not as substitutes for redress.

#### Brazil: make ratings and enforcement explainable before adding more control

Brazil should first address the trust problem created when ratings, performance controls, and account access converge. 99 documents a bilateral, post-trip rating system: drivers and passengers rate each other from 1 to 5 stars; passengers see driver ratings at request or afterward, and drivers see passenger ratings as a trip is requested. The platform describes high ratings as potentially bringing peak-hour privileges and access to highly rated counterparts, while below-average ratings can lead to suspension or loss of access.[17][18]

The rating UX is internally conflicted. 99 says its displayed score is an average of the last 500 trips, but another 99 terms page describes driver classification as an average of the last 100 ratings. It also says users cannot consult their own rating in 99Pop, arguing that this prevents artificial behaviour and preserves authentic evaluations.[18][17]

The platform should resolve this for users, not ask them to infer it. It should expose a current score, the rating window used, score movement over time, the categories that influenced it, the platform’s non-rating performance measures, and the consequences attached to each threshold. Where a rating can restrict matching, incentives, or access, it needs an appeal path and a reasoned decision.Judgment — Hiding the score may discourage gaming, but hiding the basis of an income-affecting consequence creates an avoidable fairness and comprehension failure.

This matters because Brazilian workers experience algorithmic controls as routine. Research on delivery work reports scoring tools, route alerts, reputational evaluations, and automatic blocks as constants. IBGE’s 2025 results show that 71.3% of delivery workers said the platform fully determined how payment was received, while 78.3% said it fully determined task payment; IBGE also warned that reliance on one or a few apps can make an account block drastically affect work and income.[27][84]

Brazil has examples of better worker-facing practice. AppJusto is reported to have clear deactivation sections in both contract and interface, no automatic deactivations, and always-human communication. Parafuzo created a channel to dispute absences, fines, and evaluations, with a promised response within two business days. These are appropriate design patterns: notice, reason, evidence submission, human review, response target, and an auditable outcome.[73]

CPF should be the first Brazilian identity anchor because it is broadly available and administratively inclusive. Receita Federal says CPF has no minimum age, is available to Brazilian and foreign citizens whether resident in Brazil or abroad, is unique and permanent, and can be registered, consulted, or updated without charge. For platform participation, the adult requirement belongs in the platform’s service policy; CPF availability should not be confused with eligibility to work.[49]

CPF alone is not sufficient assurance. Mastercard describes CPF as a static identifier and recommends supplementing it with dynamic signals such as phone, email, and IP information; government Datavalid checks registration data against original government databases. iFood’s courier terms combine CPF, bank information, CNH or RG/RNE depending on delivery mode, a selfie, and facial-recognition validation.[51][95][37]

The right Brazilian verification pattern is therefore **CPF-plus-risk**, not CPF-only and not universal biometric escalation. Start with CPF and document consistency; add selfie, liveness, account confirmation, or manual review where service risk, fraud signals, or account changes justify it. Biometric data are sensitive personal data under Brazil’s LGPD, which requires purpose limitation, necessity, transparency, security, prevention, non-discrimination, and accountability.[52]

Brazil lacks a documented in-platform escrow model suitable for ranking against Argentina or Colombia. iFood’s routine system is bank payout after accepted and completed deliveries, with weekly payout for cloud-mode couriers and a different schedule for logistics-operator-linked workers; suspected fraud can trigger a hold that is released or returned if fraud is not found. Payment UX should therefore emphasize earnings breakdowns, scheduled payout dates, fraud-hold status, and a path to challenge discrepancies rather than call ordinary settlement escrow.[37][20]

Brazilian prior experience also makes peer communications central. Delivery-worker social networks share information about earnings, accidents, work strategies, and daily difficulties; one study observed 46 groups, 378 posts, and more than 17,000 comments. In São Paulo, a Fairwork-reported poll found that 7 in 10 residents would stop using delivery and transport apps that failed to provide decent worker pay and conditions.[27][96]

Brazil’s product decision: make ratings, automated enforcement, and payout decisions inspectable and appealable; use CPF as the inclusive starting credential, adding higher-friction checks only under a documented risk rule.

#### Ranked cross-market verdict and transfer rules

Derived — The first-priority mechanism is dispute resolution in all three markets, but for different reasons: Argentina’s priority is access to a usable appeal; Colombia’s is operationalizing an existing staged escalation route; Brazil’s is checking opaque algorithmic enforcement. The shared criterion is whether a person can understand and challenge a decision that affects account access or earnings. This verdict rests on Argentina’s office-based and delayed support experience, Colombia’s Help Center and Ombudsman sequence, and Brazil’s rating/blocking environment and documented appeal examples.[2][14][27][73]

The second-ranked mechanism differs. Argentina needs score and allocation transparency because workers see ratings as affecting both volume and characteristics of work. Colombia needs inclusive verification because document requirements already condition access and the country’s migrant-document ecosystem creates a real onboarding branch. Brazil needs score transparency and appeal because reciprocal ratings can lead to suspension or banning while users may not see their own score.[2][14][47][18]

The third-ranked mechanism is identity verification in Argentina and Brazil, but its form should differ. Argentina has a DNI/RENAPER route constrained by consent, data-protection duties, multiple DNI formats, and foreign-document exceptions. Brazil has a broadly accessible CPF reference, but CPF is static data and biometric processing is sensitive under LGPD. Colombia already has the most explicit operational document checklist, so its main product need is not more checks but a usable inclusive-document and correction path.[3][42][51][52][14][47]

Payment escrow does not receive a universal ranking because the mechanisms are not comparable. Argentina’s established protected-payment case concerns virtual crowdwork; Colombia documents pre-acceptance earnings and weekly payout; Brazil documents post-completion bank deposits and exceptional fraud holds. A fixed-price freelance escrow—full funding before work, defined scope, evidence retained in platform chat, staged release, mediation, then platform arbitration—fits a milestone-based service but should not be transplanted unchanged into instant rides or delivery.[8][14][37][69]

Judgment — Use a service-type rule for escrow. For fixed-price, asynchronous professional work, hold client funds against explicit milestones and preserve platform-native evidence. For rides and delivery, where a task completes in minutes, use authorization, payment-status visibility, narrowly scoped holds, rapid exception resolution, and clear payout rules; a long hold would shift risk onto workers who often depend on immediate earnings.

Prior trust-system experience should shape rollout communications as much as interface design. In Argentina, peer referral and platform reputation visibly influence platform choice, so explain appeal outcomes and rating rules in worker-facing, shareable language. In Brazil, peer networks circulate evidence of earnings, accidents, and treatment, so publish decision policies and response targets that can withstand collective scrutiny. Colombian adoption effects from prior ratings, verification, payment, and dispute experiences are not quantified here; the product implication is to instrument completion, abandonment, appeal, and reinstatement outcomes by document path and city rather than assume the Argentine or Brazilian response will transfer.[2][27]

The priority order should change only when a market-specific mechanism proves better on the decisive outcomes: verification reduces harmful impersonation without raising exclusion or completion time; held funds reduce non-payment disputes without delaying workers’ usable income; or appeals meet defined targets for speed, reason quality, and correct reversal. The documented Argentine, Colombian, and Brazilian practices establish implementation patterns, but not comparative effectiveness results for those thresholds.[3][14][94]

1. **Launch a common contestability layer first:** reasoned notices, evidence upload, case status, human escalation, and dated decisions for blocks, payment holds, and consequential score actions.
2. **Localize the trigger, not the principle:** Argentina needs allocation and block explanations; Colombia needs Help Center-to-Ombudsman routing; Brazil needs rating and automated-enforcement review.
3. **Use progressive verification:** registry/document baseline, risk-based step-up checks, and a manual exception path for migrants, document mismatches, and technical failures.
4. **Separate reputation from hidden labour control:** show score inputs, consequences, and correction routes whenever ratings affect matching, schedules, incentives, or access.
5. **Match payment protection to service tempo:** milestone escrow for fixed-price work; rapid settlement, transparent exceptions, and payout reliability for rides and delivery.

### Open questions

Comparative outcome measures remain open for which specific rating, verification, escrow, and dispute-interface designs produce the highest activation, completed-work, fraud-prevention, payment-confidence, and retention rates in each market. Market-specific usability testing and operational metrics segmented by delivery mode, migration status, tenure, payment method, and connectivity conditions would settle the product-level trade-offs.
