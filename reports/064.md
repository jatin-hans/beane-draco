# UCC Analysis of Paper Delivery, Acceptance, Cure, and Summary Judgment

## Executive summary

On the stated record, neither party should obtain summary judgment that Buyer conclusively rightfully rejected all 500 reams or that Buyer conclusively must accept them subject to Vendor’s proposed cure.

If the purchase order’s letter-size specification was part of the agreement, the legal-size shipment was nonconforming despite its asserted equivalent value, and substantial performance does not displace the UCC perfect-tender rule for a single delivery.

Buyer’s use of 180 reams and printing of its letterhead and logo on the remaining 320 reams strongly support acceptance after an opportunity to inspect, but whether that conduct followed sufficient opportunity and whether Buyer seasonably rejected remain fact-dependent.

Vendor’s five-business-day exchange offer does not automatically undo an acceptance or compel Buyer to accept cure, but it may be a valid cure only if the statutory timing and reasonable-grounds requirements are proved.

Buyer should preserve its alternative damages claim for accepted nonconforming goods, while Vendor should seek adjudication of cure only after resolving the contract terms, timing, and acceptance facts.

## Governing UCC rules and contractual conformity

The supplied factual record begins with Buyer's October 15 purchase order for letter-size paper; Vendor's November 8 delivery of 500 legal-size reams; Buyer's use of 180 reams for internal drafts and overflow projects; Buyer's printing of its letterhead and logo on the remaining 320 reams; and Buyer's November 30 demand for return of payment. Copy paper is movable goods, so the transaction is governed by Article 2.[1] The governing state's enacted Article 2, its official comments, and controlling state case law determine the substantive rules; the forum's summary-judgment rule determines the procedural standard.[1][2]

**The decision path is conformity → rejection → acceptance → cure.** First, the court determines whether the October 15 letter-size specification was an obligation of the contract and therefore whether the November 8 tender conformed. Second, for a nonconforming single delivery, the court determines whether Buyer rejected within a reasonable time and seasonably notified Vendor. Third, it determines whether Buyer's post-delivery conduct instead constituted acceptance. Fourth, only after a rejection does the court determine whether Vendor had a statutory right to cure by exchanging conforming paper.[3][4][5][6][7]

### What is the applicable UCC framework for nonconforming tender

Article 2 defines goods as things movable when identified to the contract. It applies to transactions in goods, subject to statutory exceptions not implicated by an ordinary commercial purchase of office paper. The seller’s core obligation is to transfer and deliver in accordance with the contract, while the buyer’s core obligation is to accept and pay in accordance with that same contract.[8][9]

The UCC defines *conforming* goods as goods or performance that are in accordance with the obligations under the contract. That definition makes conformity a contractual question. The paper’s utility, resale value, or suitability for a different office use may bear on damages or cure, but none changes the initial test: did Vendor deliver what the contract required?[9][10]

For a non-installment sale, UCC § 2-601 supplies the *perfect-tender rule*. In ordinary words, a buyer may reject a delivery when the goods or the delivery tender fail in any respect to match the contract. Subject to the special installment-contract provisions and to any agreed limitation of remedies, the buyer may reject the entire delivery, accept it all, or accept commercial units and reject the remainder.[3][9][11]

The rule is exacting by design. The New Jersey Supreme Court in *Ramirez v. Autosport* explained that Article 2 preserves perfect tender before acceptance: the buyer may reject for any nonconformity, even though the seller’s cure rights temper the consequence of rejection. The court rejected the proposition that a curable defect cannot justify rejection; rejection and cure are distinct stages of the statutory scheme.[10][12][13]

The buyer’s § 2-601 options do not themselves make every physical refusal legally effective. *Rejection* is the buyer’s refusal to accept or retain delivered goods accompanied by notice to the seller that it will not keep them. Under § 2-602, rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery or tender and is ineffective unless the buyer seasonably notifies the seller. To serve as an effective rejection, the communication must also be explicit and unequivocal; a mere complaint is insufficient. Timeliness and the clarity of the communication are separate requirements. Reasonable time is contextual rather than a fixed number of days; it depends on the nature, purpose, and circumstances of the action.[4][13][14][15]

A buyer must also identify a defect that reasonable inspection could ascertain when its omission would prevent the seller from curing the defect seasonably. Section 2-605 can bar reliance on an unstated defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection when Vendor could have cured it had Buyer stated it seasonably. Between merchants, the same consequence follows if, after rejection, Vendor makes a written request for a full and final written statement of every defect on which Buyer proposes to rely and Buyer omits the defect.[16] The relevant defect here is straightforward: delivery of legal-size rather than letter-size paper.[9][11][6]

Inspection is the bridge between delivery and the buyer’s election. Unless the parties agreed otherwise, § 2-513 gives the buyer a right to inspect tendered or delivered goods at a reasonable place, time, and manner before payment or acceptance. When the seller sends the goods to the buyer, inspection may occur after arrival; storage in Buyer’s office is therefore compatible with an inspection period and is not, by itself, acceptance.[17][9][14]

The UCC recognizes limited exceptions to inspection before payment, including C.O.D.-type delivery and payment against documents of title. Neither exception changes the ordinary rule unless the transaction used those payment terms. The legal consequence relevant here is that receipt, custody, and even payment are not conclusive acts of acceptance; the buyer ordinarily may inspect after arrival before making its election.[17][18][6][19]

Section 2-606 defines *acceptance*—a term of art that does not turn on passage of title, as the Kansas Comment states—and § 2-607 addresses its consequences.[20] First, after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, the buyer signifies to the seller that the goods conform or that it will retain them despite a known nonconformity.[11] Second, the buyer fails to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect.

Third, the buyer does an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership, subject to the proviso that a wrongful act counts only if the seller ratifies it.[5][6][20]

The UCC separates the legal status of acceptance from the separate merits of a rejection. A rejection can be effective for acceptance purposes but wrongful as a contract matter; conversely, a nonconforming tender can give a buyer a substantive right to reject, but the buyer can lose that right by accepting. This separation matters because Vendor’s assertion that Buyer accepted does not itself prove that the legal-size tender conformed, and Buyer’s assertion that the tender was nonconforming does not itself prove its November 30 response was an effective rejection.[20][19][11]

Acceptance carries major consequences. Buyer must pay the contract rate for goods accepted; acceptance precludes rejection of those goods; and Buyer bears the burden of proving any breach concerning accepted goods.[9] Acceptance does not eliminate every Article 2 remedy.

For accepted goods, Buyer must notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after it discovered or should have discovered the breach or be barred from any remedy; timely notice preserves the possibility of other remedies and gives Vendor an opportunity to investigate and offer a repair or replacement.[21]

The buyer’s alternative after acceptance is *revocation of acceptance*, not ordinary rejection. Revocation means undoing a prior acceptance. It requires nonconformity that substantially impairs the value of the relevant lot or commercial unit to the buyer, plus one of two specified bases: acceptance on the reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur, or acceptance without discovery of the defect because discovery was difficult or seller assurances reasonably induced acceptance. Revocation also must occur within a reasonable time after discovery or constructive discovery, before a substantial unrelated change in the goods’ condition, and is effective only upon notice to the seller.[9][22][11]

That later-stage standard is materially stricter than the pre-acceptance rule. Before acceptance, a qualifying nonconformity permits rejection under § 2-601; after acceptance, Buyer must prove substantial impairment under § 2-608. The distinction protects a seller from a return remedy over trivial defects after the buyer has treated the goods as its own, while preserving a damages remedy for accepted but nonconforming goods where notice is timely.[6][10][23]

Vendor’s proposed exchange invokes the UCC’s *cure* provisions. Cure is the seller’s statutory opportunity to correct a rejected nonconforming tender rather than treating rejection as an automatic end to the transaction. Under § 2-508(1), where the seller’s performance time has not expired, a seller whose tender is rejected as nonconforming may seasonably notify the buyer of its intent to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time.[7][9][10]

Section 2-508(2) creates a different post-deadline route. If the seller had reasonable grounds to believe that its nonconforming tender would be acceptable, with or without a money allowance, and gives seasonable notice, it may have a further reasonable time to substitute a conforming tender. Reasonable grounds can arise from prior course of dealing, course of performance, usage of trade, or circumstances surrounding formation. In deciding whether the further cure time is reasonable, the surrounding circumstances include Buyer’s change of position and inconvenience, the time Vendor needs to correct the nonconformity, and Vendor’s ability to salvage the goods through resale to others.[7][24][9][10]

A cure offer is therefore conditional, not self-executing. Within an unexpired performance period, the seller’s right to cure is described as unconditional after rejection; after that period, Vendor must show reasonable grounds to expect acceptance, seasonable notice, and a further reasonable time for a conforming substitute. The later sections addressing Buyer’s November 30 demand and Vendor’s five-business-day offer apply those criteria to the chronology.[13][12]

Cure after a valid revocation of acceptance presents a distinct issue. Article 2 expressly specifies cure after rejection but does not directly resolve whether a seller retains the same right after revocation. Courts are divided: some allow a seller the same cure rights after revocation, while the reported majority position described in the authorities does not treat revocation as creating a seller right to cure.[24][6]

This framework means that a size mismatch can trigger § 2-601 without ending the matter: Buyer had to inspect and reject effectively, while Vendor could retain a statutory opportunity to exchange the goods if § 2-508’s conditions were met.[3][4][7]

Tender of delivery requires Vendor to put and hold conforming goods at Buyer’s disposition and provide the notification reasonably necessary to enable Buyer to take delivery. Tender is a condition of Buyer’s duty to accept and, absent a contrary agreement, to pay; where a tender fails to conform sufficiently to permit rejection, risk of loss remains with Vendor until cure or acceptance.[25]

The statutory election to accept commercial units and reject the rest has an important limit: acceptance of part of a commercial unit is acceptance of that entire commercial unit. Whether each ream, the 500-ream shipment, or another grouping is the relevant commercial unit is therefore material to the scope of any acceptance.[25]

After rejection, Buyer may not exercise ownership over a commercial unit. If Buyer possessed goods before rejection and has no § 2-711(3) security interest, it must hold them with reasonable care at Vendor’s disposition long enough to permit removal; after rightful rejection, it has no further obligation regarding those goods. On rightful rejection or justifiable revocation, Pennsylvania U.C.C. § 2711(c) gives Buyer a security interest in goods in its possession or control for payments made and reasonable inspection, receipt, transportation, care, and custody expenses, and permits Buyer to hold and resell them as an aggrieved seller.[16][25][26]

By contrast, an exercise of ownership over a commercial unit after rejection is wrongful against the seller and can support a finding that a buyer who attempted rejection later accepted the goods, or that a buyer who purported to revoke later reaccepted them, when its acts are inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[27][28][29][25]

The remedy consequences turn on the status of the rejection. A buyer that rightfully rejects or justifiably revokes may cancel and recover the price paid, as well as pursue Article 2 remedies for the affected goods; Georgia Code § 11-2-711(1), as applied in *Jacobs v. Metro Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc.*, provides return of the purchase price upon cancellation.[25] A buyer that wrongfully rejects or wrongfully revokes exposes itself to the seller’s remedies; Georgia Code § 11-2-703 includes damages for nonacceptance and, in a proper case, recovery of the price.[25]

Pennsylvania U.C.C. § 2719(a) permits the parties to add to, substitute for, or limit statutory remedies, including return and repayment or repair and replacement of nonconforming goods.[16] A contractual remedy is optional unless the agreement expressly makes it exclusive; Vendor’s proposed exchange therefore does not, merely as an offer, establish an exclusive remedy.[16]

After an effective rejection, Buyer may not exercise ownership over a commercial unit and must hold goods already in its possession with reasonable care at Vendor’s disposal long enough to permit removal. That post-rejection duty distinguishes safeguarding rejected paper from using it as Buyer’s own.[16][30]

Thus, the later acceptance analysis must identify the relevant commercial unit or units—such as an individual ream, a case, pallet, or the lot—rather than assume that use of some reams necessarily accepts all 500.[16][31][32]

A commercial unit is a grouping that the market or trade custom treats as a single whole and whose division would materially impair its character or value; whether the 500 reams meet that definition is consequential to any attempt to separate acceptance of the 180 used reams from the remaining 320.[33][34]

After a rightful rejection, Buyer must hold goods in its possession with reasonable care for a period sufficient for Vendor to remove them, and Buyer may not treat rejected goods as its own by using or reselling them. Buyer should follow Vendor’s reasonable return instructions when Vendor offers to cover the cost.[35][36]

Both § 2-508 cure routes require Vendor to seasonably notify Buyer of its intent to cure before sending replacement goods.[35]

In a cure dispute, Vendor bears the burden of showing that it gave timely notice of cure.[35]

A rejection notice must be explicit and unequivocal; a mere complaint is not an effective rejection. The notice must describe the defect with reasonable detail, and failure timely to give that notice makes rejection ineffective.[15][24]

Seasonably means within the time the parties agreed or, if they agreed on no time, within a reasonable time. A seller’s promise to cure a defect can extend the time otherwise reasonable for Buyer to reject.[35][37]

Whether Buyer had a reasonable opportunity to inspect depends on reasonable place, time, and manner; industry customs and practices inform that determination.[24]

For acceptance based on an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership, the Kansas Comment requires that Buyer know of the defect or be deemed to know it because Buyer failed to inspect.[20] Continued use for a period after Buyer has complained of a known nonconformity can signify an intent to retain the goods under § 2-606(1)(a); Buyer need not know the full extent of the nonconformity when it knows of some defect.[6]

Conduct altering or committing goods to Buyer’s own use can be inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership. In Park County Implement Co. v. Craig, the Wyoming Supreme Court held that installing a hoist and dump bed on a purchased vehicle constituted acceptance on that basis.[38]

In Economy Forms Corp. v. Kandy, Inc., decided March 29, 1974, the court treated the buyer’s receipt of drawings in October 1970 and forms in January 1971, followed by use of the forms for many months without notifying the seller that they were inadequate, as conduct indicating acceptance and leading the seller to believe the goods had been accepted.[39]

A buyer that attempts rejection may later accept through post-rejection conduct, and a buyer that purports to revoke may reaccept through later acts inconsistent with the seller’s ownership. Continued use after an attempted rejection is treated as acceptance, makes the rejection ineffective, and leaves revocation of acceptance as the return remedy.[25] One authority further states that, after a buyer rejects a tender, § 2-606 acceptance does not occur unless the seller retenders or takes positive steps showing that the tender remains open.[6][38]

For a post-deadline cure, the reasonableness of the further cure period depends on the nature of the goods, Buyer’s urgency, and the effort cure requires.[35]

Separately, where a seller is attempting or promising cure, courts have held that the time to revoke acceptance can be extended “almost indefinitely” while the buyer gives the seller an opportunity to remedy the defect. Those timing effects do not themselves establish a statutory cure right: cure before the performance time expires requires seasonable notice and conforming delivery within that contract time, while post-deadline cure requires reasonable grounds to expect acceptance and seasonable notice before a further reasonable substitution time is available.[37][6][16]

The perfect-tender election remains subject to the parties’ agreement and the UCC obligation of good faith; those limits do not replace the rule that a single tender must conform to the contract.

Buyer’s November 30 demand seeks rejection and repayment; it does not expressly invoke revocation of acceptance under § 2-608. The legal effect depends on the substance of Buyer’s conduct and notice, not the label attached to the demand.

Section 2-508 cure addresses a rejected tender. It does not automatically undo an acceptance or compel a buyer that has accepted goods to surrender them for an exchange, although Vendor may propose a voluntary post-acceptance exchange.

### Whether legal-size paper conformed to the purchase order and contract

The October 15 purchase order specified letter-size paper. The parties’ contract also described the product as standard office paper without defining dimensions. Those descriptions create the conformity dispute: Buyer reads letter size as the operative express specification; Vendor reads standard office paper broadly enough to encompass legal-size paper.

A description of goods that becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods will conform to that description; Buyer need not separately prove explicit reliance on the description.[16] Formal words such as warranty are unnecessary.[15] Thus, if the purchase order’s letter-size specification was incorporated into the parties’ agreement, it imposed a contractual obligation that Vendor’s legal-size shipment did not satisfy.[9]

Georgia UCC § 11-2-317 provides that exact or technical specifications displace inconsistent general language of description.[16]  Letter size is a precise dimensional requirement; standard office paper is general language that may describe grade, ordinary commercial use, or a range of paper products.[25][16] If the two cannot reasonably be harmonized, the specific letter-size requirement controls.[9]

**If letter size was an operative contract term, Vendor’s delivery of legal-size paper was nonconforming as a matter of Article 2.** Goods conform only when they accord with contractual obligations, and § 2-601 permits rejection when the goods or tender fail in any respect to conform. Functional equivalence does not erase a failure to meet an agreed dimensional specification.[9][3][14]

Vendor’s strongest position is not substantial performance; it is contract interpretation. Under § 1-303, a course of performance, course of dealing, or applicable usage of trade may give particular meaning to terms, supplement or qualify the agreement, and help ascertain its meaning. A *course of dealing* is a sequence of conduct in prior transactions between these parties that establishes a common basis of understanding. A *usage of trade* is a regularly observed commercial practice that justifies expecting observance in the transaction, and its existence and scope must be proved as facts.[40]

UCC § 1-303(e) directs courts to construe express terms and applicable course of performance, course of dealing, or usage of trade consistently whenever reasonable.[41] If a consistent construction is unreasonable, Georgia UCC § 11-1-303 provides that express terms prevail over course of performance, course of dealing, and usage of trade; course of performance prevails over course of dealing and usage of trade; and course of dealing prevails over usage of trade.[41] This hierarchy permits Vendor to introduce a genuinely applicable commercial practice or established prior dealing to explain standard office paper, but it prevents a general trade understanding from simply contradicting a specific letter-size instruction.[25][41][42]

Under UCC § 2-202 and O.C.G.A. § 11-2-202, terms in a final written agreement may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous agreements, but may be explained or supplemented by course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade, and consistent additional terms unless the writing was intended as the complete and exclusive agreement.[41] This means that the undefined phrase standard office paper does not become self-defining merely because it appears in a written contract.[42][25] Commercial context can matter, but it must explain or supplement rather than contradict an operative express specification.[41][43][9]

The classic interpretive question is therefore objective meaning: what would a reasonable person in the parties’ commercial positions understand the documents to require? A term is ambiguous when reasonably susceptible to more than one meaning. The UCC’s approach rejects a purely dictionary-based reading and directs interpretation through the parties’ language, actions, commercial practices, and surrounding circumstances.[42][44]

That approach leaves room for a harmonizing interpretation. For example, Vendor could seek to prove that, in a relevant office-supply trade and place, standard office paper includes both letter and legal dimensions and that the parties used letter size only as a nonbinding ordering preference. Vendor could also rely on prior transactions in which Buyer accepted legal-size paper under equivalent paperwork. But a trade usage must be proved and timely disclosed to avoid unfair surprise, and a single isolated transaction ordinarily does not establish the repeated transactions necessary for course of dealing.[40][43]

Buyer’s competing argument is stronger on the present stated terms. A purchase order’s express letter-size designation identifies the precise format Buyer requested. It does not merely state a use, quality grade, or general product category. Legal-size paper is a different format, and Vendor’s assertion that it has equivalent value accepts rather than answers the essential question: whether legal paper is the letter-size paper called for by the contractual obligation.[9][10]

In *T. W. Oil*, fuel oil outside the contract’s stated sulfur specification was nonconforming despite evidence of trade equivalence and possible utility.[83]

The controlling comparison is Buyer’s specific letter-size specification against Vendor’s general standard-office-paper description; the specific requirement prevails if the terms conflict and both are part of the agreement. That conclusion favors Buyer on nonconformity, but it does not itself decide the separate formation and interpretation issue of whether the purchase-order term was incorporated, superseded, or reasonably harmonized with the contract description.[9][41]

The lack of a universal state-law designation matters. The UCC is a model code enacted by states, which can revise it and whose courts can interpret comparable provisions differently. This analysis uses the widely adopted Article 2 structure reflected in the cited statutes and cases; the governing state’s enacted text and precedent ultimately control.[1][14]

The 2003 revision of UCC Article 2 was never adopted by any state and was withdrawn in 2011; the candidate’s cited source identifies the 1951 version as the latest official version.[51]

The present issue is thus narrower than whether legal-size paper is commercially useful. Vendor may show that the contractual phrase standard office paper has a trade, course-of-dealing, or other contextual meaning consistent with legal size. Buyer may show that the letter-size instruction was an exact contractual specification. If Buyer establishes the latter and Vendor cannot establish a consistent qualifying meaning, the tender was nonconforming before any question of acceptance, rejection, or cure arises.[40][41][9]

This subsection therefore makes Buyer’s letter-size term the decisive conformity issue and rules out Vendor’s equivalent-value argument as a substitute for proving that legal-size paper met the actual agreement.[9][3]

Under Georgia UCC § 11-2-106(2) and UCC § 2-106(2), goods or conduct, including any part of performance, conform only when they accord with the obligations under the contract; conformity therefore concerns Vendor’s total contractual undertaking rather than quantity, quality, or warranty breaches alone.[25] Thus, paper that is usable and merchantable as legal-size paper can still be nonconforming if the operative undertaking required letter-size paper.[15][25]

That unresolved contractual-meaning issue prevents summary judgment determining conformity, even though the specific letter-size term controls if both descriptions are operative and inconsistent.[25]

The two stated formats are physically distinct: U.S. letter paper is 8.5 by 11 inches, while legal paper is 8.5 by 14 inches. Legal paper is therefore three inches longer, and a document’s required paper size must match the paper used for printing.[45]

The UCC defines the parties’ agreement as their actual bargain, found in their language or inferred from course of performance, course of dealing, or usage of trade. A course of performance requires repeated performance in the transaction that the other party knowingly accepts or acquiesces in without objection; a course of dealing requires prior transactions that establish a common interpretive understanding.[16]

On the stated record, summary judgment cannot conclusively establish conformity or nonconformity. The letter-size purchase-order term favors Buyer if it was incorporated and operative, while the undefined standard-office-paper term leaves open whether the documents were superseded or can be harmonized through admissible evidence of the parties’ agreement, course of dealing, course of performance, or trade usage.[9][16]

The merchantability warranty, if Vendor was a merchant of paper and the warranty was neither excluded nor modified, would require goods to pass without objection in the trade under the contract description and be fit for their ordinary purposes; UCC § 2-106(2) separately defines conforming goods or conduct as those in accordance with contractual obligations.[16] That implied-warranty standard does not displace the parties’ express dimensional obligation: paper can be usable for ordinary office purposes yet fail to conform to an agreed letter-size description.[16][15]

A sales contract does not fail for indefiniteness merely because one or more terms remain open if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for an appropriate remedy.[46]

No separate, explicit reliance by Buyer is required for a qualifying seller description to create an express warranty.[15]

In American Aluminum Products Co. v. Binswanger Glass Co. (1990), the Georgia Court of Appeals upheld a finding that a general contractor’s purchase order, including its plans and specifications, was the contract where the subcontractor did not object to terms differing from its proposal and proceeded to manufacture the ordered parts and send a supervisor to the job site.[25]

Georgia authorities support treating purchase-order specifications as intended requirements when the recipient proceeds without objection, rather than allowing a party unilaterally to abandon them. In *American Aluminum Products Co. v. Binswanger Glass Co.*, the court upheld a finding that a purchase order, including its plans and specifications, was the contract where the recipient did not object and proceeded with performance. *Southern Concrete Services, Inc. v. Mableton Contractors* treats specifications as intended for observance, and *Golden Peanut Co. v. Hunt* holds that a construction allowing unilateral abandonment of specifications is patently unreasonable.[25]

The October 15, 2024 purchase order specified letter-size copy paper.

Vendor delivered 500 reams of legal-size paper to Buyer’s office on November 8, 2024.

The dimensional mismatch is measurable: each legal-size sheet has 25.5 more square inches of paper area than a letter-size sheet, or 27.3% more area, and is 1.273 times as long. Those computed physical differences reinforce that letter and legal are distinct formats rather than identical paper described with different labels.[45]

A contract for the sale of goods may be formed in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties recognizing that a contract exists.[46] An agreement for a goods-sale contract can be sufficient even if the moment when it was made cannot be determined.[46]

For an order or offer to buy goods for prompt or current shipment, § 2-206(1)(b) treats either a prompt promise to ship or a prompt or current shipment of conforming or nonconforming goods as an invited acceptance.[47] A shipment of nonconforming goods is not an acceptance if Vendor seasonably notifies Buyer that the shipment is offered only as an accommodation.[47]

A definite and seasonable acceptance can form a sales contract despite additional or different terms unless it is expressly conditional. Between merchants, additional terms may enter the agreement unless the offer limits acceptance, they materially alter the agreement, or the offeror objects within a reasonable time.[1][84]

### Whether substantial performance changes the rejection analysis

Vendor invokes substantial performance by arguing that legal-size paper has equivalent value and remains usable as office paper. That argument uses a familiar common-law idea in the wrong Article 2 setting. The doctrine of *substantial performance* asks whether performance was close enough to the contract’s requirements that the other party must accept it, ordinarily with damages for the deviation. For a single sale-of-goods delivery, Article 2 instead starts with the perfect-tender rule.[6][12][23]

The UCC does not impose a general substantial-performance test on a one-time delivery. *Ramirez* explains that commercial sales historically left no room for substantial performance and that the Code retains the buyer’s right to reject for any nonconformity before acceptance. The Code moderates the potential harshness through cure and revocation rules, not by rewriting § 2-601 to require a material variance in every single-delivery contract.[12][10][13]

**Vendor cannot defeat a pre-acceptance rejection merely by showing that legal-size paper was valuable, usable, or a substantial performance of the office-paper purpose.** If letter size was a contract obligation, legal size was nonconforming; the relevant limitations are Buyer’s effective-rejection requirements and Vendor’s potential cure rights, not a generalized substantial-performance defense.[12][3]

The UCC does use a substantial-impairment standard, but in defined settings. One is an *installment contract*—a contract that requires or authorizes delivery of goods in separate lots to be separately accepted.[30] For such a contract, a buyer may reject a nonconforming installment if the nonconformity substantially impairs that installment’s value and cannot be cured, or if the defect concerns required documents.

If the installment defect does not amount to breach of the whole contract and the seller gives adequate assurance of cure, the buyer must accept that installment.[48]

The described November 8 delivery is one shipment of 500 reams. On those stated facts, it is analyzed as a single delivery rather than as one separately accepted installment in a multi-lot arrangement. The statutory substantial-impairment-and-adequate-assurance rule would materially strengthen Vendor only if the governing agreement authorized or required separate lots for separate acceptance.[48][9]

The distinction is important because an installment-contract rule does not merely ask whether a buyer dislikes the deviation. It asks whether the deviation substantially impairs the installment and cannot be cured, while expressly requiring acceptance if adequate cure assurances apply in the statutory circumstances. No comparable substantial-impairment threshold appears in § 2-601’s rule for an ordinary single tender.[48][3][23]

Substantial impairment also governs *revocation of acceptance*. Once a buyer has accepted, it may return the goods only if the nonconformity substantially impairs their value to that buyer and the statutory grounds for revocation are met. The inquiry combines a subjective element—the buyer’s particular needs and circumstances—with an objective evidentiary element: Buyer must provide evidence from which a factfinder can infer that the nonconformity left those needs unmet.[6]

Buyer’s claim that the wrong paper size disrupted workflow and required staff to reformat documents is relevant to value and inconvenience. The UCC authorities recognize that inconvenience and the total impact of defects can matter to substantial impairment, even where cure itself may be inexpensive. But an unquantified assertion of wasted staff time, standing alone, does not itself establish the substantial impairment necessary to revoke acceptance.[6]

That later burden should not be projected backward onto rejection. Buyer need not prove that the legal-size shipment substantially impaired the value of the paper in order to show a pre-acceptance right to reject under § 2-601. Buyer would need substantial impairment if its conduct is found to have accepted the paper and its November 30 demand is therefore treated as revocation.[6][23]

The analytical sequence is therefore fixed. First, determine whether letter size was contractually required. Second, if it was, the tender was nonconforming under perfect tender. Third, determine whether Buyer made an effective rejection before acceptance; that question belongs to the next section, which separately assesses use of the 180 reams and customization of the remaining 320 reams. Fourth, only if acceptance occurred does Buyer face the substantially-higher revocation standard.[3][6]

Equivalent value and practical usability may bear on whether Vendor reasonably expected legal-size paper to be acceptable under § 2-508(2) and on the reasonableness of a prompt exchange cure. They do not make legal-size paper conform to an agreed letter-size requirement, and they do not substitute for the substantial-impairment standard that governs a post-acceptance revocation claim.[7][10][3][6]

The distinction between conformity and remedy should remain sharp. Equivalent value may support Vendor’s cure case and may undercut Buyer’s proof of substantial impairment after acceptance. It does not answer the earlier contractual-conformity question, because § 2-601 asks whether the tender complied in any respect with the contract.[3][49][50]

This subsection therefore rules out substantial performance as a general bar to Buyer’s pre-acceptance rejection right, while reserving substantial impairment for an installment contract or a post-acceptance revocation claim.[6][48][23]

Article 2 governs this sale of goods, whereas Georgia’s Article 2A provisions are lease rules: they appear in Part B, Default by Lessor, and address lessor and lessee rights on improper delivery and cure by a lessor.[51] The Article 2 perfect-tender framework—not the parallel lease provisions—therefore governs this copy-paper sale.[25][51]

Fungible goods are units equivalent by nature or trade usage, or goods the parties agreed to treat as equivalent; the record contains no agreement or proved trade usage treating legal-size paper as equivalent to the specified letter-size paper.[16]

Pennsylvania U.C.C. § 2601 provides the single-tender rule: where goods or tender fail in any respect to conform, the buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept commercial units and reject the rest, subject to installment-contract and remedy-limit provisions. Section 2-612 requires substantial impairment of an installment’s value before installment rejection is permitted, and § 2-608 applies substantial impairment to revocation after acceptance; neither establishes a general substantial-performance defense to a single sale-of-goods tender.[16][52]

### Synthesis

If the letter-size specification was operative, the November 8 legal-size tender was nonconforming under the single-delivery perfect-tender rule. Equivalent value and substantial performance do not change that conformity result; the dispositive later questions are effective rejection, acceptance through Buyer's conduct, and any cure right Vendor establishes under § 2-508.[9][3][7][5]

## Rejection and acceptance of the delivered paper

Buyer’s demand for repayment on November 30 can be a rejection only if Buyer had not already accepted the paper and if it rejected within a reasonable time after a reasonable chance to inspect. The distinction matters because Article 2 permits a buyer to reject a nonconforming tender before acceptance, but acceptance bars later rejection of the goods accepted and leaves the buyer to the narrower remedies for accepted goods.[3][49]

### Whether Buyer rightfully rejected the paper after inspection and storage

UCC § 2-513 gives a buyer the right to inspect delivered goods at a reasonable place, time, and manner before acceptance; when the seller sends the goods, inspection may occur after the goods arrive.[17] A reasonable opportunity to inspect is a contextual standard that turns on the place, time, manner, and relevant commercial practice, not a fixed number of days.[24][14][36][34] In *Plateq Corp.

v. Machlett Laboratories, Inc.* (1983), the buyer’s reasonable opportunity to inspect specialized tanks, failure then to reject them or raise specific objections, and subsequent conduct constituted acceptance.[36] In *Miron v.

Yonkers Raceway, Inc.* (1968), a buyer accepted a racehorse by waiting until the day after delivery to inspect an obvious broken leg, illustrating that the reasonable inspection period depends on the goods and circumstances.[36]

The office manager’s November 8 receipt and storage of the 500 reams placed the goods in Buyer’s possession, but possession and temporary storage are not themselves acceptance. Courts and UCC commentators distinguish temporary possession from acceptance because acceptance cannot occur through non-rejection until the buyer has had a reasonable opportunity to inspect.[14][5][6] Storage also can be consistent with the post-rejection duty of a buyer in possession to hold goods with reasonable care long enough for the seller to remove them.[4][9]

Buyer’s November 30 demand came 22 days after the November 8 delivery. UCC § 2-602 requires rejection within a reasonable time after delivery or tender and makes rejection ineffective unless the buyer seasonably notifies the seller.[4][53] Rejection requires affirmative action; silence does not reject goods.[20][19] A rejection also ordinarily must identify a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection, particularly where seasonable identification would permit cure.[9][11][23]

**Buyer has the stronger position that receipt and closet storage did not themselves accept the paper, but Buyer cannot obtain summary judgment that its November 30 demand was a rightful rejection.** The legal-size discrepancy was apparently visible from the goods or packaging, and the subsequent three weeks of use and customization are powerful evidence that Buyer had an opportunity to discover it; yet the reasonable-inspection inquiry remains contextual, and 22 days is not automatically timely or untimely under § 2-602.[24][4][14][53]

Vendor’s contrary theory—that delivery, receipt, and storage alone establish acceptance—fails under the statutory structure. Acceptance by non-rejection occurs only after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, while acceptance by conduct requires a signification of retention or an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[6][5] The ensuing conduct, rather than storage itself, supplies Vendor’s stronger acceptance evidence.

The November 30 demand therefore remains legally consequential only if Buyer had not accepted the relevant goods before giving notice and if its notice was seasonable and sufficiently identified the size objection. That question prevents Buyer from obtaining judgment for a complete repayment based solely on the November 30 demand, while it also prevents Vendor from resting an acceptance judgment solely on initial possession and storage.[4][9][14][20]

Section 2-612’s rule for an installment contract is a substantial-impairment test, not an ordinary substantial-performance doctrine: Buyer may reject a nonconforming installment only when the nonconformity substantially impairs that installment’s value and cannot be cured, subject to the required-document and whole-contract provisions; if subsection (3) does not apply and Vendor gives adequate assurance of cure, Buyer must accept that installment.[48] For a delivery governed by § 2-601 before acceptance, however, the governing rule is perfect tender: any failure to conform to the contract permits rejection, subject to the Article 2 exceptions and contractual limitations.[3][12] After acceptance, the separate § 2-608 revocation rule requires a nonconformity that substantially impairs the goods’ value to Buyer plus the statutory circumstances concerning assumed cure, difficult discovery, or seller assurances.[6]

The Official Comment to UCC § 2-602 adds that even a wholly nonconforming delivery requires affirmative action by the buyer to avoid acceptance; the buyer receives a reasonable time to notify the seller, and the inspection rules must be read with that reasonable-time requirement. Thus, a letter-size mismatch, if established, would create a potential ground for rejection but would not itself make Buyer’s November 30 demand an effective rejection.[27]

Under the reasonable-time and seasonability rule, what is a reasonable time depends on the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances; an action is seasonable when taken within the agreed time or, if none was agreed, within a reasonable time.[39][25] Reasonableness of a buyer’s notice is ordinarily a factual question, although summary adjudication is proper where uncontroverted facts are dispositive.[25]

In assessing whether rejection occurred within a reasonable time, courts consider the nature of the goods, how easily the discrepancy could be discovered, any need for testing or inspection, commercial practices, and prejudice caused by delay.[24][85]

Buyer’s initial closet storage is also consistent with the statutory duty of a buyer that has physically possessed and rightfully rejected goods to hold them with reasonable care at the seller’s disposition long enough to permit removal. By contrast, after rejection, an exercise of ownership over a commercial unit is wrongful against the seller.[26][25]

An effective rejection communication must be explicit and unequivocal; a mere complaint about the paper does not reject it. Buyer’s November 30 demand for return of payment is an affirmative refusal to retain the shipment, but Buyer also had to seasonably state the inspectable size defect if Vendor could have cured it.[15][16]

Vendor’s general awareness of a potential problem would not itself make an untimely or nonspecific rejection notice effective.[24][85]

Payment after tender is a circumstance tending to signify acceptance, but payment alone is not conclusive. Buyer’s demand for return of payment therefore adds a payment circumstance without itself resolving whether Buyer accepted the paper.[54]

The paper-size issue concerns a standard office supply and an observable physical specification; one Article 2 explanation states that a buyer of office supplies that waits weeks to object will almost certainly be treated as accepting by inaction.[16][33]

If Buyer had rightfully rejected the paper, it had to hold the reams with reasonable care for a sufficient time for Vendor to remove them, but it could not treat them as its own property by using or reselling them; beyond safe holding, Buyer had no further obligations and need not bear indefinite storage or return costs.[35]

The October 15 purchase order’s letter-size specification and the contract’s undefined standard-office-paper phrase must be read with the parties’ expressed intent to determine what Vendor promised; an integrated purchase-order contract controls the parties’ conduct, and its terms govern their conduct.[39]

An effective rejection and a rightful rejection are distinct: an effective rejection may still be wrongful and in breach of contract, but an effective yet wrongful rejection does not itself constitute acceptance.[19]

Conformity turns on the parties’ agreement: goods conform when they accord with the contract’s obligations, and an integrated purchase order controls the parties’ conduct according to its terms and expressed intent.[13][39] The court must therefore decide whether the October 15 purchase order’s letter-size specification formed part of the governing agreement notwithstanding the contract’s undefined standard-office-paper language.

If it did, legal-size paper failed the agreement’s size specification and could support rejection before acceptance even if legal-size paper had equivalent value.[39][55][13]

For a single delivery before acceptance, Article 2’s perfect-tender rule, not substantial performance, permits rejection for any nonconformity. A curable nonconformity may thus support pre-acceptance rejection, but rejection does not automatically end the contract because the seller may have a right to cure; within the contract’s performance period, that cure right is unconditional.[13][12] A seller’s cure opportunity can keep the contract in force after rejection, and the seller bears the burden to prove that it corrected the nonconformity.[12][10]

Effective rejection and rightful rejection are separate issues. A rejection that meets the reasonable-time and seasonable-notice requirements can nevertheless be wrongful, and therefore a breach, if the goods conformed to the contract; an effective but wrongful rejection is not acceptance.[19][26]

A seller’s cure response can affect both the time available for rejection and whether the tender remains open for a later acceptance communication. A seller’s promise to cure may extend an otherwise reasonable rejection period; after rejection, the buyer may communicate acceptance under § 2-606(1)(a) only if the seller has re-tendered the goods or affirmatively indicated that it is holding the tender open.[37][54]

In construing the conflicting documents, a specific letter-size requirement ordinarily takes priority over the undefined general phrase standard office paper. Trade usage, course of dealing, and course of performance may establish a specialized meaning for standard office paper only if supported by admissible evidence.

### Whether use of 180 reams after an opportunity to inspect

Buyer used 180 of the 500 delivered reams for internal drafts and overflow projects during the three weeks after delivery.Derived — 180 divided by 500 equals 36% of the shipment consumed before Buyer’s November 30 demand. This was business use, not preservation of goods for Vendor’s retrieval.

UCC § 2-606 defines acceptance in three relevant ways: after a reasonable inspection opportunity, the buyer signifies that it will retain the goods despite nonconformity; the buyer fails to make an effective rejection after that opportunity; or the buyer performs an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[6][5] Conduct, rather than a formal statement, can signify a decision to retain nonconforming goods.[20][19] Courts have treated sale of unrejected goods and use of goods as raw material in manufacturing as ownership-inconsistent acts supporting acceptance.[6]

Buyer’s internal complaints about reformatting were not notice to Vendor of rejection.[4][15] Those complaints support the inference that Buyer recognized the size difference while it continued consuming the legal-size paper.

Continued use after awareness of some nonconformity can signify an intention to retain the goods despite the defect, even if the buyer does not know the defect’s full scope.[6] The Kansas Comment adds that acceptance through an ownership-inconsistent act requires knowledge of the defect or attributed knowledge based on failure to inspect.[20][19]

Vendor consequently has a strong acceptance argument for the 180 used reams. Buyer did not simply take temporary possession; it consumed paper in ordinary business operations after a period in which the size difference could be inspected and after employees experienced the practical consequence of the difference. After a reasonable opportunity to inspect, this conduct strongly supports acceptance by signification of retention despite known nonconformity under § 2-606(1)(a). It also strongly supports acceptance under the separate ownership-inconsistent-act route in § 2-606(1)(c).[6][5][20]

Buyer’s best response is that acceptance remains a fact-sensitive statutory classification. Authorities hold that continued use may leave questions of reasonable notice, revocation, and substantial impairment for the factfinder where practical circumstances make continued use reasonable; however, those authorities concerned goods such as trucks and business equipment that remained usable and returnable, not consumable paper used up in Buyer’s operations.[56] Buyer’s argument therefore has less force as to the reams it consumed than it could have as to goods held intact for Vendor.

Once Buyer accepted the 180 reams, it could not use the November 30 demand as an ordinary rejection of those accepted reams. UCC § 2-607 requires payment at the contract rate for accepted goods and provides that acceptance precludes rejection, although acceptance does not itself eliminate other Article 2 remedies for a nonconforming tender.[9][49] A buyer that accepts goods and provides the required notice may seek damages for loss resulting from nonconformity, including incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[9][23]

Partial acceptance does not automatically decide the status of every ream. UCC § 2-601 permits a buyer to accept some commercial units and reject the rest, but acceptance of part of a commercial unit accepts the entire unit.[3][5][38] A commercial unit is a quantity treated in commercial usage as a single whole whose division materially impairs its character, market value, or use value.[8] The legal consequence for the unconsumed reams thus depends on whether each ream, a carton, or the entire shipment was the applicable commercial unit.

Each separately packaged ream is likely a commercial unit, although the classification depends on the packaging and applicable trade practice.[3][11][19]

The consequence is that Vendor should obtain at least a partial-acceptance ruling for the 180 consumed reams if the factfinder concludes Buyer knew or was charged with knowing of the size mismatch when it used them; Buyer’s remaining remedy for those reams is damages, not rejection.[6][9][20][49]

A Georgia appellate decision provides a closer business-use analogy: continued use of a defective copying machine to keep the buyer’s business operating was reacceptance, precluding recovery of the machine’s full contract price. That rule supports Vendor’s position that consumption of paper in Buyer’s ordinary operations is more than protective possession.[25]

*Linscott* collected acceptance decisions involving vehicle use and repair, travel with a defective trailer, and occupancy and repair of a mobile home.[86]

Buyer’s best factual counterexample is authority holding that a buyer’s use of defective labels was not acceptance after the buyer had effectively rejected them, the seller refused return, and the buyer used the labels under the seller’s instructions while the parties resolved the problem. The supplied facts contain no prior effective rejection, refusal to accept return, or Vendor instruction to use the legal-size paper.[57]

If the 180 reams were accepted, Buyer must give Vendor notice of the asserted breach within a reasonable time after it discovered or should have discovered it, or Buyer is barred from a remedy; Buyer bears the burden to establish breach as to accepted goods. A buyer that accepted goods and gave the required notice may recover ordinary-course loss from the seller’s breach in a reasonable manner; for breach of warranty, the stated measure is the difference at the time and place of acceptance between the value of the goods accepted and the value they would have had if warranted, unless special circumstances show proximate damages of a different amount, plus incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[25]

Use reasonably necessary to evaluate conformity does not automatically constitute acceptance, but use that goes beyond evaluation and extracts the goods’ commercial value is ownership-inconsistent conduct. Buyer used the 180 reams for internal drafts and overflow projects, not solely to evaluate paper size; that use extracted the paper’s commercial value.[33][30]

In *Capitol Dodge Sales, Inc. v. Northern Concrete Pipe, Inc.* (1983), the Michigan court held that a reasonable inspection period must permit an opportunity to put the product to its intended use or test it; the buyer’s employees had used the new pickup for about three days while it repeatedly overheated before telling the seller they would not take it.[31] Buyer’s staff’s reformatting complaints show that intended-use experience disclosed the practical consequence of legal-size paper; the later continued consumption supports Vendor’s position that the use exceeded inspection.[31][6][20][33]

Consumption and modification are recognized examples of acts inconsistent with a seller’s ownership: use of flour to bake bread and cutting delivered steel beams to project size are ownership acts that preclude later rejection of the altered goods.[36] Conversely, in *Jorgensen v.

Pressnall* (1976), buyers’ continued residence in a mobile home did not defeat proper rejection or later revocation because their use was necessary and reasonable after prompt defect notice and repair opportunities.[36] Buyer’s ordinary-business consumption of paper before any stated rejection is an ownership use, not the necessary continued possession recognized in *Jorgensen*.[36]

For any reams Buyer accepted, § 2-607(3)(a) required Buyer to notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered the size nonconformity; otherwise Buyer is barred from a remedy.[34] The November 30 demand must therefore be assessed both as an attempted rejection and, for accepted reams, as notice of breach.[34][21]

The Kansas Comment to § 2-606 (1996) explains that, after an opportunity to inspect, acceptance under § 2-606(1)(a) may be shown by either the buyer’s expression or conduct signifying that the goods conform or will be retained despite nonconformity. The Comment further identifies payment as generally one circumstance bearing on acceptance, rather than conclusive proof of acceptance.[20]

The decision on appeal in *Economy Forms* is reported at 511 F.2d 1400.[19][39][87]

### Whether printing letterhead and logo on the remaining 320 reams

Buyer printed its standard letterhead and logo on the remaining 320 reams. That act customized every remaining ream for Buyer’s own business identity and use. Unlike closet storage, customization changes the goods in a manner that makes ordinary return or resale by Vendor materially less practical.

Under § 2-606(1)(c), a buyer accepts goods when it performs an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership; an act wrongful as against the seller counts as acceptance only if the seller ratifies it.[5][9][58] Courts have treated installation of a hoist and dump bed on a purchased vehicle as acceptance because the alteration was inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[38] Other authorities identify using goods as raw material in manufacturing other products as conduct inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[6]

**Vendor has the stronger acceptance argument for the 320 printed reams because Buyer permanently appropriated all of them to its own branded operations.** Printing Buyer’s letterhead and logo is closer to incorporation or customization than to temporary possession, storage, or a protective measure taken while awaiting return instructions.[6][5][38] It objectively signals that Buyer intended to use the reams itself rather than preserve them for Vendor.

The three-week interval before Buyer’s November 30 demand reinforces that conclusion. Buyer had both actual use experience with 180 legal-size reams and the practical ability to see the dimensions before it printed the remaining 320.[17][24][6] The staff’s reformatting complaints strengthen the inference that Buyer knew the paper was legal size when it directed the customization.[6][20]

Buyer can argue that printing did not expressly state a willingness to keep nonconforming paper. That argument does not answer the separate ownership-inconsistent-act route to acceptance, which turns on what Buyer did with Vendor’s goods rather than on an express acceptance statement.[6][5] Buyer can also invoke the qualification that a wrongful ownership-inconsistent act constitutes acceptance only upon Vendor’s ratification.[5][59] The use and printing strongly support acceptance. If an earlier effective rejection made either ownership-inconsistent act wrongful against Vendor, § 2-606(1)(c) makes Vendor’s ratification consequential.[5][60][61]

Buyer’s November 30 demand cannot ordinarily reject reams that Buyer had already accepted. Acceptance precludes rejection of accepted goods; if acceptance was made with knowledge of a nonconformity, revocation based on that nonconformity is available only when acceptance rested on the reasonable assumption that the nonconformity would be seasonably cured, while acceptance does not itself impair another Article 2 remedy for nonconformity.[9][62][49] The possible revocation route requires substantial impairment of the goods’ value to Buyer and conditions not addressed by the ordinary rejection analysis; it is considered in the later discussion of Vendor’s proposed cure and Buyer’s claimed loss.[22][50]

The commercial-unit question again matters. Acceptance of part of a commercial unit is acceptance of that entire unit. If each printed ream is a commercial unit, Buyer’s conduct accepts that ream; if the printed and consumed reams form one commercial unit, acceptance of part accepts the whole unit.[5][8][38][60] The statutory rule supplies the result only after the factfinder identifies the commercially relevant unit.

The consequence is that the printed reams present Vendor’s strongest factual acceptance theory, but not a conclusive summary-judgment result: a factfinder must determine whether the printing was an ownership-inconsistent act, whether Buyer had a reasonable opportunity to inspect and knew or was chargeable with knowledge of the size discrepancy, whether the act required and received Vendor ratification, and the applicable commercial unit.[60][57][61]

The new authority separates acceptance by ownership-inconsistent conduct from a later revocation analysis. In Alimenta, blanching peanuts was acceptance because it was inconsistent with the seller’s ownership, yet revocation remained effective because blanching had not substantially changed the peanuts. Printing Buyer’s letterhead and logo can therefore support acceptance without automatically resolving whether Buyer could establish the distinct statutory requirements for revocation.[25]

Whether a buyer accepted through later acts inconsistent with the seller’s ownership is a question of fact determined from the evidence in the particular case. The closest acceptance examples—installation of heavy equipment and processing peanuts—support Vendor’s analogy, but do not convert branded printing on paper into acceptance as a matter of law.[63][25][57]

Modification or alteration is conduct that can be inconsistent with the seller’s ownership. Printing Buyer’s standard letterhead and logo altered all 320 remaining reams and made them branded stock for Buyer’s operations.[33][5]

If the November 30 demand is treated as revocation rather than rejection, Buyer must establish that the size nonconformity substantially impaired the value of the relevant lot or commercial unit and that Buyer accepted either on the reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur, or without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances. Revocation must occur within a reasonable time after Buyer discovered or should have discovered its ground, before a substantial change in condition not caused by the goods’ defects, and is ineffective until notice to Vendor; a buyer who revokes has the same rights and duties as after rejection. Buyer’s printing is a change in condition not caused by the paper-size discrepancy.[25][16][15][30]

Derived — 320 divided by 500 equals 64% of the delivered reams customized with Buyer’s letterhead and logo. Whether acceptance of those reams extends further depends on the applicable commercial unit.[5][8]

If Buyer had accepted the paper, Vendor’s exchange offer would not automatically resolve the dispute. Buyer would first have to satisfy § 2-608’s revocation requirements: substantial impairment of value to Buyer and acceptance either on an unfulfilled reasonable expectation of cure or without knowledge because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances.[34] A buyer who validly revokes has the same rights and duties regarding the goods as a buyer who rejected them.[25] Section 2-508 is triggered by rejection, not clearly by revocation after acceptance, and courts disagree whether a seller retains a cure right after revocation.[35]

### Vendor delivered 500 legal-size reams to Buyer’s office on November

The operative events are straightforward: Vendor delivered 500 reams on November 8, 2024; Buyer stored them, used 180, printed on 320, and demanded repayment on November 30. The acceptance analysis must treat the consumed and customized goods separately from initial receipt because Article 2 recognizes acceptance of all goods, partial acceptance by commercial unit, and acceptance through ownership-inconsistent conduct.[3][5]

Judgment — The governing jurisdiction’s enacted version of UCC Article 2, together with any contractual choice-of-law clause, controls the motion. The model-UCC language and the state enactments collected in the authorities use the same core acceptance structure: reasonable opportunity to inspect; effective rejection through timely notice; and acceptance through retention, failure to reject, or ownership-inconsistent conduct.[6][4][5]

The 2025 New York Article 2, Part 6 compilation is titled 《Breach, Repudiation and Excuse》 and identifies § 2-601 as Buyer’s Rights on Improper Delivery and § 2-602 as Manner and Effect of Rightful Rejection; it also links to 2024 and 2023 versions while cautioning that the compilation may not be current.[64] That compilation confirms the statutory topics relevant to this dispute but does not itself select New York law for the November 2024 transaction.[64]

Section 2-606 identifies three acceptance routes: the buyer signifies willingness to assume ownership, fails to make an effective rejection, or acts inconsistently with the seller’s ownership. The factual chronology makes the summary-judgment issue asymmetric. Buyer’s November 30 demand supports an affirmative refusal to retain the paper, but its earlier use and customization support acceptance of the particular reams involved.[20][10][38] The law therefore allows a conclusion that Buyer accepted at least part of the shipment without treating the office manager’s initial storage as acceptance of all 500 reams.[14][1][38]

The consequence is that the court should analyze Buyer’s conduct ream group by ream group or by the applicable commercial unit, rather than treating the shipment as automatically accepted or rejected in a single undifferentiated act.[3][5][8]

The statutory structure also distinguishes rejection from revocation of acceptance. If Buyer accepted a lot or commercial unit, it may revoke only where the nonconformity substantially impaired value to Buyer and Buyer either accepted on a reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur, or accepted without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances. Revocation must occur within a reasonable time after discovery, before a substantial change in condition not caused by the defect, and is ineffective until notice to Vendor.[25]

Article 2 treats rejection and acceptance as legally incompatible statuses for the same accepted goods. After acceptance, Buyer must pay for the goods and bears the burden of proving breach. A buyer that accepted goods and gave the required breach notice may recover ordinary-course loss from nonconforming tender in a reasonable manner, plus incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[15][30][16]

The missing packing slips for four other accounts show incomplete recordkeeping during that period, but do not by themselves establish what Buyer ordered, what Vendor delivered to Buyer, or the meaning of the parties’ agreement. They may bear on the weight of Vendor’s evidence about its general delivery practices if Vendor relies on those practices.

The claimed workflow disruption and reformatting problems are evidence of operational consequences from legal-size paper, but Buyer’s inability to quantify lost staff hours establishes no dollar amount of damages. The complaints may bear on the importance of the letter-size specification, but they do not turn accepting conduct into a timely rejection.

### Synthesis

Buyer’s receipt and storage on November 8 did not itself accept the paper, and the November 30 demand was not automatically ineffective merely because it followed delivery by 22 days. But the 180 reams consumed in business operations and the 320 reams permanently customized with Buyer’s letterhead provide substantially stronger evidence of acceptance than storage does.[4][5][14][38] Vendor therefore has the stronger position that Buyer accepted the used and printed reams, while Buyer retains a fact-dependent argument concerning whether its November 30 notice effectively rejected any still-separable, unaccepted commercial units.[3][5][8][49] The court should deny Buyer’s request for summary judgment declaring a complete rightful rejection and should not grant Vendor judgment solely from receipt and storage; it should instead determine whether the use and printing accepted the relevant commercial units.

The added authorities reinforce that the court should deny summary judgment declaring either a complete rightful rejection or complete acceptance. Buyer’s November 30 notice presents fact questions on reasonable inspection opportunity, seasonability, and the sufficiency of its affirmative rejection; Vendor’s use-and-branding theory presents fact questions on ownership-inconsistent conduct, knowledge, ratification if required, and commercial-unit scope. The uncontroverted consumption of 180 reams gives Vendor its strongest partial-acceptance argument, while the legal effect of printing the other 320 reams remains a fact-bound application of § 2-606.[27][57][61][25]

Vendor’s strongest summary-judgment ground is not mere receipt or closet storage but Buyer’s commercial consumption of 180 reams and alteration of the other 320 after an opportunity to inspect. Those acts are inconsistent with a claim that Buyer preserved the shipment for return, subject to the statutory rule that a wrongful ownership-inconsistent act constitutes acceptance only when Vendor ratifies it.[54][16][30][33]

Section 2-606 recognizes acceptance after a reasonable opportunity to inspect when Buyer signifies that the goods conform or that it will retain them despite nonconformity, when Buyer fails to make an effective rejection, or when Buyer performs an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership; a wrongful ownership-inconsistent act is acceptance only if Vendor ratifies it.[16] Vendor’s five-business-day exchange offer invokes § 2-508 only if Buyer rejected the goods; § 2-508 does not clearly extend to a buyer that accepted and later revokes, and courts differ on cure after revocation.[35] A seller’s promise to cure may extend the otherwise reasonable time for rejection.[37]

## Vendor’s proposed cure and Buyer’s claimed loss

This issue turns on a critical sequence: cure is a seller’s statutory chance to correct a nonconforming tender after rejection, while revocation is a buyer’s much narrower route to undo an acceptance already made. Vendor’s proposed exchange may preserve the transaction if the statutory cure conditions apply; it does not automatically erase Buyer’s claimed disruption or automatically compel Buyer to surrender remedies.

### Whether Vendor’s five-business-day exchange offer was a timely available cure

Judgment — UCC § 2-508 distinguishes two cure routes. The first applies while the contract’s delivery or performance period remains open. The second can apply after that period, but only where the seller reasonably expected acceptance of the nonconforming goods. The distinction matters because the first route is comparatively strong and the second depends on the commercial circumstances.

Under § 2-508(1), when a buyer rejects a nonconforming tender before the time for performance expires, the seller may seasonably notify the buyer of an intention to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time. A timely cure under that subsection gives the seller an unconditional right to correct the tender during the agreed performance period.[7][10] Thus, if the October 15 purchase order or incorporated contract permitted delivery or replacement after November 30, Vendor’s offer to exchange the legal-size paper for letter-size paper could be a valid cure if Vendor gave seasonable notice and actually supplied conforming paper within that still-open period.[7][1]

The original performance deadline might be established by a delivery window, prior dealings, or proved trade usage; it does not automatically equal the November 8 delivery date.[40][88]

The proposed exchange directly addresses the asserted defect: replacing legal-size stock with letter-size stock. An exchange is also a practical form of substituted conforming tender where the defect lies in an objectively verifiable product specification rather than in a service or an irremediable loss. Courts have treated repair of the original goods as an adequate cure even though § 2-508 speaks of substituting a conforming tender; repair is more appropriate for a minor flaw, while a major flaw may require a substituted product. Here, Vendor’s proposed exchange is a substituted conforming tender rather than a repair.[6][7]

If the contractual performance period had expired before Vendor made its offer, New York UCC § 2-508(2) governs: where a buyer rejects a nonconforming tender that the seller had reasonable grounds to believe would be acceptable “with or without money allowance,” the seller may, with seasonable notice, have a further reasonable time to substitute a conforming tender.[7][65] That subsection permits a further reasonable time to substitute conforming goods only where Vendor had reasonable grounds to believe Buyer would accept legal-size paper, with or without a money allowance, and Vendor seasonably notified Buyer of cure.[7][65] Reasonable grounds can arise from prior dealings, course of performance, trade usage, or the circumstances in which the parties made the contract.[24]

In *T. W. Oil*, the court upheld a post-deadline cure where the seller offered substitution one day after rejection, replacement goods were already in transit and expected within a week, and the buyer showed no cognizable prejudice.[89]

Vendor’s private belief that legal-size paper would be acceptable is insufficient to establish reasonable grounds under § 2-508(2). Prior acceptance of legal-size paper, communications permitting substitution, or an established trade practice could supply such grounds.[90][88][6]

The cure examples draw the relevant contrast: in Wilson v. Scampoli, a retailer could reasonably expect a new color television delivered in its factory crate to be accepted and, if defective, could substitute a conforming tender; in Zabriskie Chevrolet, Inc. v. Smith, a dealer had no reasonable right to expect acceptance of a new automobile with serious deficiencies that it knew or should have known about through its claimed inspection.[6] Vendor’s reasonable-expectation argument rests on the undefined phrase standard office paper and legal-size paper’s ordinary office use, but a clear, operative letter-size requirement known to Vendor would instead support the Zabriskie comparison because it would make acceptance of the deviation less reasonably expected.[7][24][6] The argument is weakened, however, if the letter-size requirement in the purchase order was clear, operative, and known to Vendor.

The delivery records and the parties’ surrounding communications bear directly on whether Vendor could reasonably treat legal-size paper as acceptable rather than as a deviation from a specific order term.[7][24]

Five business days is not inherently either timely or untimely: its calculation depends on the contract’s definition of business day, and its reasonableness depends on travel distance, replacement-stock availability, the contract terms, and Buyer’s urgency.[66][32] For post-deadline cure, a further reasonable time depends on the surrounding circumstances, including Buyer’s change of position, the inconvenience to Buyer, the time Vendor needs to correct the error, and Vendor’s ability to resell the returned goods.[12][10][13] Buyer’s application of its own letterhead and logo to the remaining stock is a substantial change of position relevant to that analysis; it makes a simple return-and-exchange more disruptive than it would have been immediately after delivery. The reformatting complaints likewise support some inconvenience, although the scope of that inconvenience remains an issue addressed below.[10][13]

**Vendor consequently has the stronger cure position if the performance period was still open, but a five-business-day proposal after that period carries no automatic right to force an exchange.** Its availability then turns on reasonable grounds, seasonable notice, and a context-specific reasonable time.[7][12][10]

The stated chronology separates the remedies by quantity: Vendor delivered 500 reams on November 8, Buyer consumed 180 reams and printed its letterhead and logo on the remaining 320 during the following three weeks, and Buyer demanded repayment on November 30. UCC § 2-601 permits acceptance of commercial units and rejection of the rest, but acceptance of part of a commercial unit accepts the entire unit; whether a ream, a group of reams, or the 500-ream delivery is the pertinent commercial unit depends on commercial usage and cannot be resolved from the stated facts.[3][5][8]

The UCC’s timing standard is circumstance-specific: an action is seasonable when taken within an agreed time or, if none is agreed, within a reasonable time, and reasonableness depends on the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances. Georgia authority states that a reasonable time does not mean immediately and is ordinarily a jury question under the particular circumstances.[25][16]

The tender rule reinforces that the exchange must actually put conforming letter-size paper at Buyer’s disposition, with the notifications reasonably necessary to enable delivery; tender entitles Vendor to acceptance and payment only according to the contract.[16] Seasonable notice means notice within an agreed time or, if none was agreed, within a reasonable time, and Vendor bears the burden to show its cure notice was timely.[35]

Vendor’s proposed exchange covered only the 320 remaining reams on which Buyer printed its letterhead and logo; it did not and could not replace the 180 reams Buyer had already consumed. The § 2-508 cure question therefore concerns whether Buyer effectively rejected the 320 remaining reams before acceptance. If the 180 consumed reams were accepted, Buyer must pay the contract rate for them, cannot reject them, and must pursue any breach remedy under the accepted-goods rules rather than through § 2-508 cure.[7][49]

An exchange of the 320 remaining reams would also need a workable disposition or credit for the stock Buyer had branded with its letterhead and logo.[91]

The contract may define business day—for example, by days when specified courts or banks are open—and that definition governs how Vendor’s proposed five-business-day period is counted.[66]

The reasonable-time inquiry also includes transit distance, availability of replacement stock, the contract terms, and Buyer’s urgency.[32]

A qualifying post-deadline cure requires reasonable grounds to expect acceptance, seasonable notice, and a further reasonable time for conforming delivery; after rejection, Vendor also bears the burden of proving that it actually corrected the nonconformity.[35][13]

A seller’s promise to cure can extend the time otherwise reasonable for Buyer to reject, and courts have treated a seller’s attempt or promise to cure as extending the time for revocation while Buyer gives the seller an opportunity to remedy the defect.[37][6]

On the stated quantities, no untouched reams remain: Buyer consumed 180 reams and printed its letterhead and logo on the other 320. The proposed exchange therefore concerns goods Buyer has already consumed or customized, not identified unconsumed and unprinted stock.

### Whether Buyer could refuse Vendor’s proposed exchange and demand return

A refund is not the remedy produced merely by receiving nonconforming goods. Under New York UCC § 2-711(1), cancellation and recovery of so much of the price as has been paid follow where the buyer “rightfully rejects or justifiably revokes acceptance.” Accordingly, Buyer’s November 30 demand succeeds as a demand for repayment only if it was a legally effective rejection or a justified revocation of acceptance.[67][9]

If Buyer had effectively rejected before accepting, a valid cure under § 2-508 limits the immediate consequences of rejection. The buyer may reject for nonconformity before acceptance, but the seller’s statutory right to cure means that rejection does not necessarily discharge the contract.[12][13] During an unexpired performance period, Buyer would have to permit a conforming replacement tender after Vendor seasonably gave notice of cure.[7][1] Buyer could still preserve a claim for provable loss caused by the original defective tender, but it could not treat a timely statutory cure as automatically entitling it to terminate and recover the entire price.[23][68]

The analysis changes if, as considered in the preceding section, Buyer accepted some or all of the goods through use, customization, or conduct inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership. Acceptance precludes rejection of the accepted goods. A buyer that accepted with knowledge of a nonconformity generally may not revoke because of that known nonconformity unless it accepted on the reasonable assumption that the seller would seasonably cure it.[49][11] A November 30 demand would then function, if at all, as an attempted revocation rather than a rejection.

Revocation requires more than a known dimensional mismatch. The nonconformity must substantially impair the value of the lot or commercial unit to this Buyer, and Buyer must have accepted either on the reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that did not occur or without discovering the nonconformity because discovery was difficult or seller assurances induced acceptance. Revocation must occur within a reasonable time after discovery and before a substantial, unrelated change in the goods’ condition; it becomes effective only when Buyer notifies Vendor.[9][22][11] Use of paper and printing proprietary letterhead matter to both the acceptance and condition-change inquiries.

The governing law becomes especially important if the November 30 demand is revocation. Courts are divided over whether a seller has the same right to cure after revocation that § 2-508 gives after rejection. Some decisions permit post-revocation cure, while the reported majority view denies a statutory cure right after revocation.[24][6] In an unpublished April 10, 2009 Eleventh Circuit decision, Car Transp.

Brokerage Co. v. Blue Bird Body Co., the motor-coach buyer’s revocation claim under O.C.G.A. § 11-2-608(1)(b) failed because invited error barred it from denying that it had to give the seller an opportunity to seasonably cure nonconformities before revocation.[25] Thus, Vendor cannot rely on § 2-508 as a universal answer once Buyer’s conduct is classified as acceptance and Buyer invokes revocation instead.[25]

Buyer’s strongest position is that the letter-size specification made the delivery nonconforming, that its demand was prompt enough under the commercial circumstances, and that the offered exchange came too late after Buyer had committed the stock to internal operations and customized it. Vendor’s strongest position is that the proposed letter-size substitute would completely correct the dimensional deviation promptly and that Buyer’s conduct had already moved the dispute from rejection into acceptance and, at most, damages. The four missing packing slips establish incomplete recordkeeping for other accounts, but without a connection to this transaction they do not create a material factual dispute over this tender, the parties’ agreement, Vendor’s reasonable grounds for cure, or the reasonableness of five business days. A party must identify record material supporting a genuinely disputed fact, and only a dispute that could affect the outcome under the governing law precludes summary judgment.[2][69][70][71]

**Buyer cannot obtain a refund merely by refusing the exchange, and Vendor cannot compel acceptance merely by offering an exchange; the decisive issue is whether this was still a rejection case or had become an acceptance-and-revocation case.**[7][67][11]

The remedies must also be separated by quantity. The 180 reams already consumed cannot be physically exchanged. If Buyer accepted those reams, it must pay the contract rate for them and cannot reject them; its remaining route is a timely notified and proven breach claim for accepted-goods damages, unless it establishes justified revocation under the stricter rules.[49][62][72][11] Vendor’s proposed exchange addresses only the 320 remaining, logo- and letterhead-printed reams.

That offer neither substitutes the goods nor itself compels Buyer to surrender them: its legal effect depends on whether those reams were accepted or effectively rejected and, in a rejection case, whether Vendor satisfies § 2-508’s cure conditions.[7][9][49]

If Buyer had rightfully rejected while in physical possession of the paper, it had to hold the goods with reasonable care at Vendor’s disposition long enough to permit removal, unless it held the statutory security interest. Once rejection was rightful, Buyer otherwise had no further obligations concerning the rejected goods.[26][25] Thus, a rightful rejection would require preservation for Vendor’s removal; it would not itself require Buyer to accept Vendor’s replacement tender unless Vendor established an available cure right.[26][25]

If Buyer accepted the paper, it must establish both breach and timely notice of breach to obtain an accepted-goods remedy. Acceptance does not itself foreclose a breach-of-warranty claim, but it shifts the dispute to the notice, proof, and damages rules for accepted goods. Section 2-714 applies to breach claims regarding accepted goods, not rejection, revocation, or rescission claims, and Georgia authority states that it applies only after the time for revocation has passed.[39][25]

Those records instead leave disputed proof on the separate questions of Vendor’s reasonable grounds, notice, and the commercial circumstances governing cure.[25]

If the 500 reams are one commercial unit, acceptance of any part is acceptance of the entire commercial unit. Thus, Buyer’s use of 180 reams may carry consequences beyond those individual reams, but whether this shipment was one commercial unit is a factual classification not supplied by the stated terms.[16]

A buyer also must state, in connection with rejection, a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection when the seller could have cured it if timely stated. The legal-versus-letter-size mismatch appears inspection-ascertainable, so the content and timing of Buyer’s November 30 notice matter independently of whether the notice was called a demand for return of payment.[16]

If Buyer establishes rightful rejection or justifiable revocation, it has a security interest in goods in its possession or control for payments made and reasonable inspection, receipt, transportation, care, and custody expenses, and may hold and resell the goods as an aggrieved seller. That remedy concerns the remaining goods; it does not make a refund automatic without rightful rejection or justifiable revocation.[16]

If any contract price remains unpaid, Buyer may, after notifying Vendor of its intention, deduct all or part of the damages resulting from Vendor’s breach from any part of the price still due under the same contract. That setoff remedy applies to an unpaid balance, not recovery of a price already paid.[16]

Under § 2-607, Buyer must pay the contract rate for accepted goods; acceptance bars rejection of those goods, and acceptance with knowledge of a nonconformity bars revocation unless Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption that the nonconformity would be seasonably cured. Acceptance itself does not impair another Article 2 remedy for nonconformity.[11]

If Buyer rightfully rejected or justifiably revoked, it may cancel and recover the price paid. The 1980 article states that recovery should be reduced by the fair value of Buyer’s use of the goods, making the value of the 180 reams used relevant to any repayment calculation.[16][6]

Post-acceptance notice both gives Vendor an opportunity to investigate and address the reported breach, including through repair or replacement, and is a condition of Buyer’s preserving remedies: after accepting a tender, Buyer must notify Vendor of breach within a reasonable time after discovery or when it should have discovered the breach or be barred from any remedy.[21]

Unlike notice of rejection, notice of breach after acceptance need only alert Vendor that the transaction is troublesome; whether it was given within a reasonable time depends on the circumstances.[92][93]

The classification of the reams controls two distinct monetary consequences: Buyer must pay the contract rate for any reams it accepted, while a recovery of purchase price after rightful rejection or justified revocation may be diminished by the fair value of Buyer’s use of the paper.[25][6]

### What does Buyer’s unquantified staff-time loss establish about disruption and

Buyer’s evidence of reformatting and employee complaints supports a real operational inconvenience from receiving paper in the wrong dimensions. In a revocation analysis, substantial impairment asks about the particular buyer’s needs and circumstances, not the preferences of an abstract average buyer. But the inquiry also has an objective component: the buyer must offer evidence from which a factfinder can infer that its particular needs were not met.[6]

The claimed disruption therefore matters in two ways. First, it supports Buyer’s argument that legal-size paper had less value in a workplace built around letter-size documents, forms, and branded materials. Second, it informs whether five additional business days for cure would be reasonable, because buyer inconvenience and change of position are relevant to the further-reasonable-time inquiry.[6][12][13]

Unquantified staff time does not supply a damages number. A buyer that accepted goods and gave notice of breach may recover losses resulting in the ordinary course of events, measured in any reasonable manner; in an appropriate case, it may also recover incidental and consequential damages.[9][72] Incidental damages include reasonable expenses incurred in inspection, receipt, transportation, and care and custody of rightfully rejected goods; commercially reasonable charges, expenses, or commissions connected with cover; and any other reasonable expense incident to delay or breach.

Consequential damages include losses from general or particular requirements and needs Vendor had reason to know about when the parties contracted and that Buyer could not reasonably have prevented by cover or otherwise.[16][9][68]

Buyer can support the fact and scale of workflow loss through firsthand testimony from employees or managers, contemporaneous reformatting records, payroll or timekeeping information, job logs, document-production records, or other business evidence. Summary-judgment evidence must be admissible at trial and based on personal knowledge rather than hearsay or unsupported opinion.[69] A damages presentation must also connect the claimed loss to the wrong-size paper rather than ordinary workplace inefficiency or unrelated causes.[73]

The absence of an hour total or dollar valuation does not make the operational disruption irrelevant or necessarily bar recovery. Buyer must nevertheless present evidence sufficient to permit an intelligent and reasonable estimate of the loss; consequential damages additionally require foreseeability, reasonable certainty, and mitigation. On the stated record, the unquantified reports establish inconvenience, not a calculable damages award or substantial impairment as a matter of law.[74][6] The same evidence can still matter to a factfinder evaluating Buyer’s particular paper needs, the commercial reasonableness of Vendor’s proposed cure period, and the credibility of Vendor’s assertion that legal-size paper had equivalent value.[6][10]

In *Campbell*, Article 2 permitted a remedy despite acceptance, and the court reinstated a $30,000 verdict for the buyer.[93]

**Buyer has the stronger position on the existence of some workflow disruption, while Vendor has the stronger position on the presently unproved magnitude, causation, and legal substantiality of staff-time loss.**[6][68][73]

Additional labor expense can qualify as incidental damages when it is shown to result from the seller’s breach. Buyer must still establish causation: Vendor may show that no causal connection exists between the asserted deficiency and the claimed damage, or that another factor was the sole proximate cause.[25]

Uncertainty about the amount of loss is not, by itself, the central bar to damages; Georgia’s rule against vague or speculative damages concerns uncertainty as to cause. But Buyer must provide competent proof from which the claimed workflow loss can be ascertained with reasonable certainty and linked to the wrong-size delivery. There is no bright-line test separating consequential or indirect damages from direct damages; consequential or indirect losses are not directly related to the breach but naturally flow from it and would not necessarily be incurred by every injured party suffering that breach.[25][74]

On summary judgment, Buyer cannot rely solely on allegations or denials of disruption after a properly supported motion; it must identify specific facts showing a genuine issue for trial. Proof that paper was the wrong size, without admissible proof of actual loss, does not itself establish accepted-goods damages.[75][71][25]

Buyer must connect any workflow loss to the paper-size nonconformity. A breach-based claim requires a causal relationship between the breach and injury, and Vendor may contest causation with evidence of supervening causes or Buyer’s own actions. Buyer also must act reasonably and timely to mitigate damages; reasonable mitigation expenditures may themselves be recoverable.[15][74]

If the paper was accepted, Buyer’s damages route requires breach notice within a reasonable time after it discovered or should have discovered the breach, and Buyer bears the burden of proving breach as to accepted goods. Proper notice and proof preserve ordinary-course loss damages measured in a reasonable manner, with incidental and consequential damages in a proper case.[16]

For accepted goods, the usual breach-of-warranty measure is the difference, at the time and place of acceptance, between the value of the goods as accepted and the value they would have had as warranted; special circumstances may establish proximate damages of a different amount.[76]

### Synthesis

Vendor’s five-business-day proposal is a potentially effective cure, not a self-executing answer to Buyer’s November 30 demand. If Buyer effectively rejected while contractual performance time remained open, Vendor’s seasonably noticed conforming exchange would carry substantial force. If performance time had expired, Vendor must establish reasonable grounds for expecting acceptance, seasonable notice, and a further reasonable cure period that accounts for Buyer’s operational disruption and customized stock.[7][12][13]

If Buyer had already accepted the paper, the dispute shifts to revocation and accepted-goods damages. That shift forecloses ordinary rejection of accepted reams and strengthens Vendor’s argument against a wholesale refund, but it leaves Buyer’s damages claim available if Buyer gave required notice and proves breach-caused loss. The 180 consumed reams cannot be returned through the proposed exchange; if accepted, they remain payable at the contract rate subject to any proven accepted-goods damages. The exchange proposal concerns the 320 remaining printed reams only, and cannot require their exchange unless Vendor establishes an available cure right after an effective rejection. Buyer’s unquantified staff-time evidence supports inconvenience and contextual reasonableness, not a fixed recovery or substantial impairment as a matter of law.[7][49][11][72][68]

If the paper is classified as accepted, Buyer’s November 30 communication must also satisfy the post-acceptance notice requirement, which gives Vendor an opportunity to investigate and address the reported breach and is required to preserve Buyer’s remedies. Buyer bears the burden to prove breach concerning the accepted goods. Its available remedy then is ordinary-course loss determined reasonably, plus incidental or consequential damages in a proper case, rather than an automatic return of the price.[21][39][25]

## Summary-judgment disposition

The dispositive question is not simply whether legal-size paper was a defective tender. It is whether Buyer preserved a rejection remedy before it consumed or irreversibly customized the shipment. On the stated undisputed record, Buyer’s three-week consumption of 180 reams and irreversible branding of the remaining 320 reams constitute acts inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership after Buyer had an opportunity to inspect, and therefore establish acceptance of both groups before the November 30 demand.[5][9][19] The unresolved contract-meaning question independently controls whether the legal-size tender conformed and may preserve a breach or damages claim even if particular reams were accepted.[3][4][5][6][9][49][76]

If federal procedure governs, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a) permits summary judgment only when no genuine dispute exists as to a material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The movant initially bears the responsibility to demonstrate the absence of a genuine issue of material fact. A fact is material only if it can affect the result under the governing UCC rules, and a dispute is genuine only if the evidence permits a reasonable jury to find for the nonmovant. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248, 255 (1986). The court credits the nonmovant’s evidence and does not weigh credibility.[2][77][70]

### What do Vendor’s incomplete delivery records and missing packing slips

The missing packing slips concern four other accounts, not Buyer’s 500-ream shipment. They establish incomplete recordkeeping for the period, but do not establish what Vendor shipped to Buyer, alter the October 15 purchase order, or establish an agreement that legal-size paper met a letter-size requirement.[2][69][70]

The missing slips create a genuine material dispute only if admissible evidence connects them to Buyer’s shipment or to a term of the parties’ agreement. The material UCC facts are the agreed size requirement, the paper delivered, Buyer’s opportunity to discover the size, its use and customization of the goods, its notice to Vendor, and the elements of rejection or revocation. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 248; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1).[2][69][70][78]

In federal court, Rule 56 requires a party asserting or disputing a fact to cite particular record materials—such as documents, deposition testimony, declarations, admissions, or interrogatory answers—or to show why the opposing party cannot produce admissible evidence.[2] Affidavits and comparable materials must rest on personal, firsthand knowledge rather than hearsay or unsupported opinion.[2][69] Thus, Vendor cannot use generalized assertions about incomplete delivery files to prove that Buyer accepted a different contractual size term.

Conversely, Buyer cannot defeat a supported motion merely by pointing to an administrative gap; the nonmovant must set out specific facts from which a reasonable jury could find in its favor.[70][71]

Buyer’s assertion that legal-size paper disrupted workflow and wasted staff time can support damages or revocation only through admissible, firsthand evidence of the disruption and its consequence. The stated record supplies no quantified hours or other evidence from which a factfinder can infer substantial impairment of value to this Buyer. The revocation standard considers the buyer’s particular needs but requires evidence beyond its assertion that those needs were unmet. UCC § 2-608; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1); Anderson, 477 U.S. at 248.[6][9][2][69]

Buyer’s unquantified loss claim may still matter in two limited ways. First, if Buyer accepted the goods and gave timely notice of breach, Article 2 permits damages for loss resulting in the ordinary course from a nonconforming tender, determined in a reasonable manner. Second, evidence of actual disruption could bear on whether the wrong size substantially impaired the paper’s value to this Buyer for revocation purposes. Neither proposition converts an unmeasured allegation into proof that Buyer could return the purchase price for all 500 reams.[76][50]

Vendor’s equivalent-value argument likewise does not make the missing packing slips material. For a single delivery, the Article 2 perfect-tender rule asks whether the goods conformed to the contract, not whether the delivered substitute had roughly equal market value. Substantial performance is principally the rule associated with installment contracts; it does not replace the conformity inquiry for the stated one-delivery transaction. The unresolved meaning of the purchase order’s letter-size specification and the contract’s undefined phrase standard office paper can therefore be material to breach, but the unrelated missing packing slips are not.[3][24][9][12]

The practical result is that the court should disregard the four other accounts’ missing slips as a basis to infer a dispute about this delivery unless a witness, document, or other admissible proof supplies the missing link. That ruling leaves the contract-interpretation issue and any properly supported damages evidence to be assessed on their own merits.[2][69][70][71]

The Supreme Court in Anderson v. Liberty Lobby (1986) held that only disputes that could affect the outcome under the governing substantive law are material, and that a properly supported summary-judgment opponent must identify specific facts rather than rely on allegations. Thus, the missing slips and the asserted workflow loss matter only if competent evidence permits a reasonable finding that they bear on the contract, this delivery, acceptance, revocation, or legally recoverable damages.[75][71]

Georgia’s Court of Appeals held in Ficklin v. Buyer may prove ordinary-course loss, incidental damages, or consequential damages by a reasonable method if it gave the required notice, but must connect the loss to Vendor’s breach and provide evidence from which the amount can be determined with reasonable certainty.[25]

If Vendor relies on delivery records as business records, it must supply foundation testimony for them. The absence of packing slips, without that foundation and without evidence tying the absent slips to Buyer’s shipment, does not itself establish a competing account of the delivery.[79][80][81]

Buyer can obtain additional discovery rather than an immediate adverse ruling only by submitting an affidavit or declaration specifying why it cannot present facts essential to oppose summary judgment. On that showing, Rule 56 permits the court to defer or deny the motion, allow time to obtain declarations or take discovery, or issue another appropriate order.[80]

The stated record identifies no jurisdiction whose particular Article 2 enactment or controlling decisions govern this dispute.[40]

If Vendor relies on its incomplete delivery files as affirmative proof of a fact about Buyer’s shipment, the gaps may prevent summary judgment on the fact those records are offered to establish. Missing records concerning four other accounts, however, do not create a triable dispute about Buyer’s transaction without evidence connecting them to it.

### Whether the summary-judgment record supports rightful rejection

Buyer has a substantial threshold argument that legal-size paper was nonconforming if the letter-size term in the October 15 purchase order controls the parties’ agreement. Section 2-601 allows a buyer to reject all goods, accept all goods, or accept commercial units and reject the rest when the goods or tender fail in any respect to conform to the contract. That is the perfect-tender rule: for a single delivery, even a nonmaterial mismatch can support pre-acceptance rejection.[3][82][12][23]

The phrase standard office paper does not erase the letter-size specification as a matter of commercial common sense. Standard office paper describes a category of commodity paper; letter and legal are distinct dimensions within that category. If the parties agreed upon letter size, legal-size paper does not conform merely because it is usable paper of equivalent market value. At the same time, the conflict between a specific purchase-order dimension and an undefined general contractual phrase presents a contract-interpretation issue that can matter to whether Vendor breached at all.[3][9][1]

**The contract-meaning dispute prevents summary judgment that Vendor made a conforming tender, but it does not preserve Buyer’s right to reject after Buyer accepted the goods.** Acceptance and conformity are separate Article 2 inquiries. A buyer may accept nonconforming goods, and acceptance does not automatically extinguish all remedies for breach; it precludes rejection of the accepted goods and shifts the buyer toward notice and damages rules.[14][49][76][23]

Buyer’s office manager’s receipt and closet storage do not alone establish acceptance. Article 2 requires a reasonable opportunity to inspect before acceptance can arise through failure to make an effective rejection, and temporary possession alone does not equal acceptance. Storage may also be consistent with holding goods pending inspection or, after a proper rejection, storing them for the seller’s account.[5][6][14][38] The storage evidence therefore does not decide the case by itself.

The later conduct does. Under UCC § 2-606(1)(c), a buyer accepts goods by doing an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership; if the act was wrongful against the seller, it is acceptance only if the seller ratifies it. The Kansas Comment further describes knowledge of the defect, or knowledge attributed through failure to inspect, as relevant to acceptance through ownership-inconsistent conduct.[5][20] Paper size is readily observable from the ream packaging and the paper’s dimensions, and Buyer’s staff encountered the mismatch while using the product.

The use of 180 reams for internal drafts and overflow projects is conduct strongly consistent with ownership. Buyer depleted those reams in its own operations, leaving Vendor unable to recover, resell, or exchange them. The staff complaints about reformatting demonstrate that the use was not an accidental or purely custodial handling of the paper; Buyer used legal-size sheets while aware of the practical difference from letter size. Continued business use of goods has been treated as conduct supporting acceptance or reacceptance in other Article 2 settings, although the cases turn on their circumstances.[5][20][56]

Buyer accepted the remaining 320 reams by printing its standard letterhead and logo on them. That irreversible customization made the reams Buyer-specific inventory and is conduct inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership under UCC § 2-606(1)(c). Park County Implement Co. v. Craig, 397 P.2d 800 (Wyo. 1964), is illustrative: installation of a hoist and dump bed on a purchased vehicle constituted acceptance because it was inconsistent with the seller’s ownership.[5][38]

This conclusion does not depend on treating all 500 reams as one commercial unit. Section 2-606(2) provides that acceptance of part of a commercial unit is acceptance of the whole unit, but whether the shipment as a whole, a carton, or each ream is the pertinent commercial unit may depend on the transaction.[5][38] Here, Buyer used or branded every ream: 180 were consumed and 320 were customized.

The acceptance conclusion thus follows from Buyer’s conduct as to each portion, not from an assumption that all 500 reams form a single commercial unit.

Buyer’s November 30 demand for return of payment occurred after those acts. A rejection must be made within a reasonable time after delivery or tender and requires seasonable notice to the seller. The Code supplies no fixed number of days; reasonableness depends on the action’s nature, purpose, and circumstances.[4][14][53] Three weeks may not invariably be unreasonable for every kind of goods, but it is not a period during which Buyer merely inspected and preserved the paper. Buyer put the whole delivery to productive or customized business use before demanding repayment.

Acceptance precludes rejection of accepted goods. Buyer must pay the contract rate for accepted goods, although acceptance does not itself eliminate every remedy for nonconformity. If Buyer timely notified Vendor of the breach, it may seek damages for an accepted nonconforming tender; Buyer bears the burden to establish the breach as to accepted goods.[49] Buyer’s November 30 demand cannot operate as an ordinary rejection of any reams already accepted, but it can remain an ordinary rejection as to a ream only if Buyer proves that it had not previously accepted that ream and that no earlier effective rejection or other material timing fact changes the analysis.[4][5][6][9][49]

Buyer’s November 30 demand can be considered only as a claimed revocation of acceptance, not ordinary rejection. UCC § 2-608 requires Buyer to prove a nonconformity substantially impairing value to it and either acceptance on a reasonable assumption of seasonable cure that failed or acceptance without discovery because discovery was difficult or Vendor gave assurances. Revocation also must occur within a reasonable time after discovery, before a substantial change in condition not caused by the defect, and upon notice to Vendor.[9][22][50]

Buyer’s stated facts do not support revocation as a matter of law. Buyer’s employees discovered the size problem during the three-week period, complained about reformatting, nonetheless consumed 180 reams, and branded the other 320 before demanding repayment. The record described in the request contains no fact that Buyer accepted on an earlier assurance that Vendor would cure, or that paper size was difficult to discover. Its unquantified workflow complaint also does not, standing alone, establish substantial impairment.[6][9]

By contrast, *Campbell* involved cabinet defects that were difficult to discover and materially interfered with appliance use, followed by unequivocal, timely notice and an opportunity to cure.[92][93]

Continued use does not always foreclose revocation. Georgia decisions treat effective revocation, reasonable notice, substantial impairment, and continued use as ordinarily fact-dependent where a buyer offers practical reasons for continued use, such as inability to obtain replacement transportation during a dispute.[56] Those decisions do not assist Buyer on these facts because Buyer does not identify necessity, a preservation effort, prompt complaint coupled with unavoidable use, or an inability to obtain letter-size substitute paper. Buyer instead exhausted or altered all 500 reams before its first stated demand.[6][5][20]

Vendor’s five-business-day exchange offer neither reverses Buyer’s acceptance nor independently compels an exchange. UCC § 2-508 governs cure following rejection: before the performance time expires, Vendor may give seasonable notice and cure within the contract period; afterward, Vendor must show reasonable grounds to believe the tender acceptable, seasonable notice, and a further reasonable time to substitute conforming goods. Whether five business days is reasonable depends on Buyer’s change of position, Buyer’s inconvenience, and the time Vendor needed to correct the tender.[7][12][13]

The proposed cure therefore cannot supply an independent basis for judgment that Buyer had to accept a replacement after it accepted the goods. Courts are divided over cure after revocation: some extend the rejection-stage cure right, while the reported majority view does not.[24] But that split is not outcome-determinative here.

Buyer’s ordinary rejection fails because acceptance occurred first, and Buyer has not shown the prerequisites for revocation on the stated facts. The exchange offer remains relevant to damages mitigation and to any claim that Buyer accepted in reliance on a seasonable cure, but it neither erases Vendor’s possible size breach nor restores Buyer’s already-lost rejection remedy.[49][50][23]

Accordingly, the court should deny Buyer summary judgment seeking a rightful rejection of all 500 reams and a full refund, and grant Vendor partial summary judgment that Buyer accepted the 180 consumed reams and the 320 branded reams before November 30. The court should leave open conformity under the parties’ agreement, post-acceptance notice of breach, and any provable damages for accepted nonconforming goods. UCC §§ 2-607(3)–(4), 2-714.[9][62][72][49][76]

The contract-meaning issue remains independently material. In Economy Forms Corp. v. Kandy, Inc. (N.D. Ga. 1974), the court treated an integrated purchase order and the parties’ expressed intent as controlling the promised goods; Georgia authority also recognizes trade usage as admissible to construe a contractual term and treats a genuine dispute over governing specifications as a jury issue. The stated record identifies no trade-usage evidence that legal size means letter size. Thus, the specific letter-size purchase-order term remains Buyer’s principal evidence of nonconformity, while the undefined phrase standard office paper creates a genuine interpretation issue rather than an equivalent-value defense.[39][25][3][9]

Whether later conduct is an act inconsistent with the seller’s ownership is ordinarily determined from the evidence in the particular case. O’Shea v. Hatch (N.M. Ct. App. 1982) states that rule, while Woodridge USA Properties, L.P. v. Southeast Trailer Mart, Inc. (11th Cir. 2011) upheld judgment as a matter of law where the buyer acted inconsistently with the seller’s ownership by undertaking to resell the delivered trailers. Here, no reasonable characterization of the undisputed conduct treats depletion of 180 reams for Buyer’s operations and printing Buyer-specific letterhead and logo on the other 320 reams as preservation of goods for Vendor’s disposition.[57][63][5][61]

The statutory qualification for an ownership-inconsistent act does not alter this result. Section 2-606(1)(c) makes a wrongful act acceptance only if the seller ratifies it; Vendor’s position that Buyer accepted the shipment and its proposed exchange of the remaining stock supplies the required ratification if Buyer’s use or branding is characterized as wrongful against Vendor.[60][61][39]

Continued use after an asserted revocation can be fact-dependent when the buyer has a practical reason to continue using indispensable goods while attempting to resolve the dispute. Franklin v. Augusta Dodge, Inc. (Ga. Ct. App. 2007) involved a truck buyer with no other transportation who repeatedly sought a replacement or refund, and Griffith v. Stovall Tire & Marine, Inc. Buyer identifies none of those circumstances: it did not preserve any reams, seek a replacement before consuming and branding the goods, or show that letter-size paper could not be obtained.[63][25][6][5]

Vendor’s exchange offer must also be assessed under cure’s context-specific requirements, not treated as self-executing. Pennsylvania’s Article 2 enactment, which tracks UCC § 2-508, permits cure within an unexpired contract-performance period after rejection and permits additional reasonable time only where the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the tender acceptable and seasonably notified the buyer. In Car Transportation Brokerage Co. v. Blue Bird Body Co. (11th Cir. Apr. 10, 2009), applying Georgia law in a revocation dispute, the court treated the opportunity for seasonable cure as relevant and rejected a claimed futility exception lacking supporting evidence. A five-business-day exchange could therefore be a commercially reasonable proposed cure only upon the required factual showing; it does not compel acceptance or itself establish that Buyer validly revoked.[16][25]

If Buyer does not pay for goods it accepted, Vendor may invoke the seller remedies for wrongful rejection or failure to pay, including damages for nonacceptance and, in a proper case, the price. Conversely, a buyer who rightfully rejects or justifiably revokes may hold a security interest in goods in its possession for payments and specified reasonable expenses. Those remedial consequences confirm why the acceptance and revocation determinations, rather than the unrelated missing slips, are dispositive.[25]

A reasonable opportunity to inspect is contextual, not a fixed number of days. It may include trial use needed to put goods to their intended use, as the Michigan court recognized when a buyer used a pickup for about three days before rejecting it for repeated overheating. That principle supports Buyer’s position that initial handling and limited testing did not alone constitute acceptance, but it does not characterize three weeks of consumption and customization of the entire shipment as mere inspection.[31]

If Pennsylvania law governs and the agreement contains a repair-or-replacement or return-and-repayment remedy, the agreement may add to, substitute for, or limit Article 2 remedies. Such a remedy remains optional unless the parties expressly agreed that it is exclusive. Nothing in the stated standard-office-paper term makes Vendor’s proposed exchange an exclusive contractual remedy.[16]

Vendor bears the burden to show that it gave timely notice of cure. Thus, Vendor’s five-business-day proposal can support a cure defense only if Vendor proves seasonable notice and, after the performance period, reasonable grounds to believe the legal-size tender would be acceptable and a further reasonable time for substitution.[35][34]

Rule 56 permits a court to grant summary judgment on a claim, defense, or part of either, and requires the court to state its reasons for granting or denying the motion. If Buyer accepted the paper, UCC § 2-607(4) places on Buyer the burden to establish that the accepted goods breached the contract. The court should therefore grant Vendor partial summary judgment on ordinary rejection and preserve the distinct conformity, notice-of-breach, and damages issues that the acceptance ruling does not resolve.[80][34]

Rule 56 also permits the court to identify individual material facts as undisputed for later proceedings without disposing of every claim or determining the amount of damages.[2]

Recommended disposition. Deny Buyer’s request for summary judgment declaring a rightful rejection of all 500 reams and ordering a full refund. On the stated, undisputed record, grant Vendor partial summary judgment that Buyer accepted the 180 reams it consumed and the 320 reams it irreversibly customized before the November 30 demand; therefore the demand was not an ordinary rejection of either group. The ruling does not decide whether legal-size paper conformed to the agreement, whether Buyer gave timely notice of breach after acceptance, or whether Buyer can prove damages. UCC §§ 2-601, 2-606, 2-607, 2-714; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a).[3][5][9][2][49][76]

An effective rejection and a rightful rejection are separate questions. A buyer’s rejection can be effective for acceptance purposes yet wrongful because it breaches the contract; an effective but wrongful rejection does not itself constitute acceptance.[19] Before acceptance, when rejection is at issue, Vendor bears the burden to establish that its tender conformed; after acceptance, Buyer bears the burden to establish breach as to the accepted goods.[6][34]

UCC § 2-606(1) identifies three independent routes to acceptance: after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, Buyer signifies that the paper conforms or that it will retain it despite nonconformity; Buyer fails to make an effective rejection after a reasonable opportunity to inspect; or Buyer does an act inconsistent with Vendor’s ownership. If the ownership-inconsistent act is wrongful against Vendor, it constitutes acceptance only if Vendor ratifies it. Thus, if, after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, Buyer indicated that it would keep the paper despite the size difference, that indication would be acceptance and would preclude ordinary rejection, while leaving other Article 2 remedies for nonconformity available.[31][49]

A rejection notice must identify the defect with reasonable specificity. Specific notice that the defect was the legal, rather than letter, size gives Vendor an opportunity to cure; failure to identify a reasonably ascertainable defect may bar Buyer from relying on it to justify rejection or establish breach where seasonable, more specific notice would have permitted correction.[6][9]

A seller’s promises to cure can extend the reasonable time for rejection. In Jones v. Abriani, the buyers repeatedly complained after taking possession of a nonconforming mobile home, the seller repeatedly assured them that the defects would be cured, and the court held that rejection about one year after possession was within a reasonable time.[37]

A timely rejection does not necessarily end the contract. Within the contract performance period, Vendor’s right to cure is unconditional after seasonable notice of its intent to cure and a conforming delivery within that period. After that period, Vendor has a further reasonable time only if it had reasonable grounds to believe the legal-size tender would be acceptable, seasonably notified Buyer, and substitutes conforming paper within a reasonable further time. An offer to exchange is not itself a completed cure, and Vendor bears the burden to prove that it corrected the nonconformity if it relies on cure.[9][13]

A refund of the price depends on rightful rejection or justified revocation. Even then, the fair value of Buyer’s use of the goods reduces the amount recoverable; therefore, use of the 180 reams would reduce a price recovery even if Buyer otherwise established a return remedy.[67][6]

Those omissions prevent a ruling that the proposed exchange was a practically adequate conforming cure of the entire 500-ream delivery.

### Synthesis

**Vendor’s position is stronger on acceptance and rejection: Buyer’s undisputed use of 180 reams and irreversible branding of 320 reams establish ownership-like conduct after an opportunity to inspect, so the November 30 demand cannot be an ordinary rejection.** The letter-size term may still establish nonconformity despite Vendor’s equivalent-value position, because Article 2’s single-delivery rule is perfect tender rather than substantial performance. That dispute supports a possible accepted-goods damages claim, not repayment through rejection.[3][5][9][12][49][76]

The missing packing slips for four unrelated accounts do not create a genuine material dispute without admissible evidence tying them to Buyer’s delivery or contract. Buyer’s unquantified workflow claim may bear on damages, but it does not establish substantial impairment or revive a rejection remedy. Vendor’s five-business-day exchange offer does not automatically resolve the case because cure after acceptance or revocation is not automatic and depends on governing jurisdiction and factual context.[6][56][2][69][24]

### Open questions

Open questions remain whether the letter-size term was incorporated into and controls the parties’ agreement, whether an applicable course of dealing or trade usage gives standard office paper a consistent broader meaning, when Buyer discovered or reasonably should have discovered the size mismatch, whether Buyer gave Vendor notice before November 30, and whether the five-business-day exchange was seasonable and supported by reasonable grounds to expect acceptance. The missing packing slips may matter only if they bear on those contract, delivery, notice, or commercial-context issues. Buyer’s claimed workflow disruption remains open as to amount and as to whether it substantially impaired the paper’s value if Buyer must proceed by revocation rather than rejection.
