FutureMeds is a credible clinical-research-services platform for proprietary-equity diligence, but its public record does not support a fully underwritten PE investment case. It had 28 clinical sites across seven European countries by October 2025 and hybrid or virtual-trial coverage in 18 countries, giving it meaningful operating scale in a fragmented site-management market.[1][2][3] Phoenix Equity Partners’ July 2025 investment provides institutional validation, while FutureMeds’ owned-site, recruitment and decentralised-trial model offers plausible cross-sell and consolidation potential.[3][4] The available disclosures do not establish consolidated revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, cash generation, debt, customer concentration, CAC, LTV, contract backlog or a transaction valuation.

An investable thesis therefore depends on confirming that site utilisation, sponsor retention, study-level contribution margins and working-capital conversion can turn the reported footprint into durable cash earnings.

This profile uses only information publicly available on or before 17 November 2025; developments after that date are not treated as cutoff-date facts.

## Company profile of FutureMeds

FutureMeds operates in the clinical-research services market rather than in drug discovery or development. Its role is to provide the infrastructure, patients, investigators and operational delivery needed for pharmaceutical companies, biotechnology companies, trial sponsors and contract research organisations (CROs) to run clinical trials. That distinction matters because the business is exposed principally to study starts, recruitment execution, site utilisation, quality performance and sponsor demand—not to the binary approval outcome of a therapeutic asset.

FutureMeds’ stated proposition is a European dedicated research-site network combined with decentralised clinical-trial services. The company presents the combination as a way to control site delivery while extending access to patients beyond a physical clinic estate. As at the 17 November 2025 cutoff, its reported scale was material, but the public record uses several different measures—physical sites, listed locations, country operations, home-care countries, healthcare-professional partners and prospective-patient reach—that should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Business and customers

FutureMeds is an independent European site-management organisation (SMO): a company that manages dedicated clinical-research sites and supports sponsors’ trial execution. Phoenix Equity Partners described it in July 2025 as a group of research sites providing patient recruitment and clinical-trial management services across Europe, while HGL Private Capital called it the leading independent clinical site-management organisation in Europe. Its customers are pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies, sponsors and CROs.[3][5]

The business model is B2B clinical-research delivery. FutureMeds does not develop medicines or operate as a software-only vendor. It sells the ability to recruit, screen, enrol, retain and monitor trial participants; conduct protocol visits; provide investigators and clinical staff; generate trial data; and deliver related site-management and decentralised services. The company describes its offering as patient recruitment, clinical-trial execution and hybrid or virtual trial services for major global pharmaceutical sponsors and CROs.[3][6][7]

FutureMeds’ UK legal operating entity is FUTUREMEDS LTD, company number 12521988, a private limited company. Companies House records its incorporation in England and Wales on 17 March 2020, while FormationData lists 16 March 2020. Its registered office is Bridle Road Clinic, Bridle Road, Bromborough, Wirral, CH62 6EE, England. Its registered SIC classification is 74909, Other professional, scientific and technical activities not elsewhere classified. The entity’s 2022 financial statements identify its principal activity as clinical-trial activities.[8][9][10][11]

The company itself gives a slightly earlier business origin. It says FutureMeds was founded in late 2019 by Dr Radoslaw Janiak, Chief Executive Officer; Marcin Gondek, Chief Financial Officer; and Paul Chambers, Chairman. The stated purpose was to build a fully owned, pan-European research-site network capable of conducting trials at scale while retaining local service. The late-2019 date is therefore a reported business-founding date, while 17 March 2020 is the incorporation date of FUTUREMEDS LTD; they describe different events.[12][8]

The company’s core asset is its Dedicated Research Site model. A dedicated research site is a clinical location whose teams and facilities focus on running clinical studies rather than fitting research around ordinary healthcare delivery. FutureMeds says its sites are independent, fully owned and dedicated to research, with centralised and integrated operations. It presents sole focus on trial delivery as enabling faster study start-up, more consistent operating processes, greater control of data quality and patient experience, and adoption of new technologies and methodologies.[13][14]

The stated ownership model is strategically relevant. In a conventional fragmented site market, a sponsor or CRO often coordinates separate sites with different systems, recruitment practices and capabilities. A network that owns the site operations can standardise feasibility, contracting, regulatory processes, training, patient engagement, quality assurance and client reporting. FutureMeds says its operating structure includes full-time research staff, a centralised quality-assurance team, global project management, patient recruitment, feasibility and business-development support.[15][14]

**FutureMeds is a clinical-research delivery platform whose proposition combines controlled dedicated-site operations with patient-access, recruitment and decentralised-trial capabilities; it is neither a biotechnology developer nor a pure technology vendor.**[3][13][4][7]

The physical-site model is supplemented by FutureMeds @home, its decentralised clinical-trial (DCT) solution. A DCT is a trial design in which some or all study activities occur remotely or at a participant’s home rather than entirely at a research clinic. FutureMeds offers hybrid trials, combining clinic visits with remote-friendly visits at a patient-selected location, and fully virtual trials, in which all visits are remote and are supported by home nursing and principal-investigator oversight.[4]

FutureMeds @home is broader than a nurse-visiting service. The company lists a DCT framework that includes a homecare-provider network and central project team; telemedicine and electronic consent; patient-support centres; principal-investigator and sub-investigator networks; wearable-device training; patient-engagement and social-media campaigns; home sampling and direct-to-patient investigational-product supply; electronic medical records, electronic source verification and digital reporting; and a mobile research unit called FutureMedsGO.[4]

The company also describes protocol-design and trial-delivery support. Its DCT specialists can assist from early protocol development through delivery, drawing on site and investigator experience, local regulatory knowledge and homecare execution. FutureMeds says it has advised sponsors on DCT elements and protocol amendments to accelerate delivery, but it does not quantify the frequency, financial value or measured time savings of those interventions.[4][16]

The acquisition of emovis was central to the development of this service line. In July 2023, FutureMeds announced the acquisition of Berlin-based emovis, described as a dedicated study site and provider of pan-European homecare visits and DCT services. FutureMeds subsequently introduced FutureMeds @home in September 2023 as a European DCT solution comprising a DCT framework and toolkit, proprietary mobile home-health services and trial-design consultancy.[17][16]

emovis added operating history rather than only a new product concept. FutureMeds said emovis had operated Phase Ib–IV clinical-trial services since 2001, launched Homecare Visits in 2012, and had recruited more than 9,000 patients in more than 250 studies across 120 indications and 14 therapeutic areas. FutureMeds also said the acquired business had reached promised recruitment numbers in 90% of those studies and passed more than 30 audits, although those are historical emovis claims, not post-acquisition FutureMeds-wide KPIs.[18][17]

FutureMeds’ DCT model is intended to work both with its own sites and with external sites. The company states that FutureMeds @home has supported more than 1,850 non-FutureMeds sites, using country coordinators, local experts, proprietary systems and a single point of contact to manage delivery without additional sub-vendors. This creates a potentially important distinction for commercial analysis: the DCT/homecare business can generate service activity beyond the capacity of the owned-site estate.[4]

The company’s patient-recruitment proposition is integral to its site model. Clinical trials frequently fail or slow because sites cannot identify eligible participants, enrol them fast enough, or retain them through the required visits. FutureMeds says it addresses those constraints through targeted patient-centric engagement, pre-pooling of potential participants before site activation, local healthcare-provider expertise and healthcare-professional networks.[19][20][21][22]

Pre-pooling is the company’s term for identifying, educating and engaging potentially suitable patients before a site is activated for a study. FutureMeds says the approach is intended to make engaged patients available immediately following activation, reduce time to recruitment targets and improve engagement through pre-education. These are operational objectives rather than published, network-wide measured outcomes.[21][22]

Its patient-acquisition channels include healthcare-professional relationships, principal investigators, hospitals, general practitioners, patient communities, advocacy groups and direct-to-patient engagement. FutureMeds says its dedicated model connects investigators, physician partners and patient advocacy groups, while its UK referral programme uses GP practices to identify potentially eligible patients. In that model, a GP practice receives study criteria, contacts suitable patients and refers them to FutureMeds; the study is then conducted at FutureMeds sites, not at the referring GP practice. Participating practices are compensated for searches and other work.[15][23]

The UK model therefore separates participant identification from protocol delivery. This can allow FutureMeds to access community patients through trusted primary-care channels while retaining control over trial conduct, data collection and investigator oversight at dedicated sites. FutureMeds says its UK teams work with the NHS, patient communities, trusted GPs and local communities; it does not disclose the number of NHS arrangements, GP practices, referrals, referral conversion rates or revenue attributable to those relationships.[24][13][23]

Other country models use different access routes. FutureMeds describes Spain as having dedicated research sites in established hospitals, able to support complex studies in secondary-care settings. It describes its Bulgaria operation as collaborating with hospitals, medical experts, local physicians, pharmacists and specialists. It says Poland has a strong patient database and robust recruitment, and that Romania offers large treatment-naïve patient populations supported by specialist networks and referral pathways.[13]

The business serves a broad therapeutic mix. The company’s website identifies cardiovascular, endocrinology and metabolic, immunology, vaccines and infectious diseases, and oncology as therapy areas. It describes its DCT solution as having supported 330-plus trials across 25 therapeutic areas and more than 140 indications, including rare diseases, paediatric populations and challenging risk groups.[20][4]

FutureMeds has also sought to develop oncology capability. In 2024, it said it increased oncology readiness at seven network sites; three sites had been activated for their first lung- and breast-cancer trials, and three additional sites were prepared for the same indications in 2025. It also reported a soft launch of a community-based ambulatory oncology programme, combining dedicated-site infrastructure with decentralised elements, although it reported only qualitative early feedback rather than financial or clinical outcome metrics.[25][26]

The group’s service offer includes specialised capabilities at acquired sites. CPS Research in Glasgow, acquired in August 2023, provides Phase II–IV trial activity, consultancy, endoscopies, gynaecological procedures and PK/PD-related services. The latter refers to pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic work: measuring how a treatment moves through the body and how it produces effects. These services may allow the network to address more complex studies than a basic recruitment-only site model, but FutureMeds does not separately disclose their utilisation or economics.[27]

FutureMeds has made a number of operational and technology investments intended to support scale. It reported consolidating operations and implementing a single Clinical Trials Management System across all Dedicated Research Sites in 2022. A clinical-trials management system is the operating system used to coordinate studies, site workflows, patient tracking, recruitment, documents and reporting. FutureMeds said the system was intended to improve speed, timeline protection and patient experience; it did not quantify a realised productivity or cost effect.[28]

Judgment — For a private-equity reader, the important commercial distinction is that FutureMeds has several revenue-bearing service layers: owned-site delivery, patient recruitment, decentralised visits, home nursing, feasibility and trial-design support. The model can support cross-selling and a larger addressable study mix, but it also requires close operational integration because site, nurse, investigator, referral and data workflows must all meet protocol and regulatory standards.

FutureMeds has reported selected evidence of recruitment performance. It says its average time from activation to first patient in is 24 hours. First patient in, usually abbreviated FPI, is the date on which the first participant is enrolled or begins protocol activity after a site becomes active. The company does not state the observation period, sample, therapeutic mix or whether this metric applies to all sites and studies.[22]

The company reported that two Polish sites enrolled 7.5% of the global target population for a Lyme disease vaccine study in 10 weeks. In a separate vaccine-trial example, it said it had an initial target of 1,060 participants aged 65 or older across nine sites in three countries, equal to 13.25% of an 8,000-person global target, and ultimately recruited 1,559 participants, contributing 20% of the global target. One site exceeded its initial target within nine business days. These are study-specific execution examples and cannot be read as network-wide recruitment conversion, utilisation or margins.[29][30]

A third example concerns a German flu-vaccine study. FutureMeds @home was engaged with other providers to support a nationwide study across Germany, supplying home-care visits to 12 research sites. It said a team of 18 qualified professionals was selected from a database of more than 13,000 registered healthcare professionals, and that it had completed 40 visits as of 26 February 2025 with a 100% completion rate for scheduled visits and no cancellations. One completed visit occurred on 31 December 2024 within 24 hours of the request, and another required a journey of more than 700 kilometres to a Baltic Sea island. These facts demonstrate ability to execute complex home visits under specific study conditions, not the typical cost-to-serve or network-wide service level.[31]

FutureMeds presents patient experience as an operational input into retention. Its sites provide participant monitoring and specialised medical care, and may reimburse participants for time and travel when eligible under a study. The company says dedicated teams educate participants, answer questions and address concerns throughout the patient journey. Patient testimonials on its website describe satisfaction with home visits, communications and investigational-product management; they are qualitative experience evidence rather than a measured net promoter score, retention rate or satisfaction survey.[24][14][4]

The company’s operating message is therefore clear: use owned-site control, local patient access, pre-pooling and decentralised delivery to reduce recruitment bottlenecks and participant burden. FutureMeds says this should accelerate timelines, lower client costs, improve data quality and increase retention. Its public operating record supports the presence of the component capabilities and examples of execution, while the claimed programme-level effects are not quantified across the network.[27][32][14][16]

This business model supports conventional site-centric trials, hybrid trials and fully remote trial components. It also allows FutureMeds to provide DCT services to sites it does not own. That breadth is strategically important because it can reduce dependence on the utilisation of any single physical clinic and allows the network to address protocols requiring geographically dispersed participants, home visits, rare-disease access or paediatric participation.[4][33]

The business-and-customer profile therefore supports a commercial thesis centred on service quality, recruitment speed, geographic reach and sponsor relationships. It does not, on its own, establish contract duration, client renewal, backlog, sponsor concentration, pricing, contribution margin, CAC, LTV or revenue mix by service line; those financial and customer-underwriting issues are addressed in Financial strength and investment attractiveness.

FutureMeds also has a patient-facing acquisition pathway. Its UK patient website invites prospective participants to register interest through a study form, speak to the FutureMeds team to check eligibility, and arrange a visit with the site team. It presents participation as access to specialist healthcare-team support and investigational medicines or therapies not available on the market.[34]

Phoenix Equity Partners described FutureMeds on 29 July 2025 as providing end-to-end services comprising patient recruitment, clinical-trial execution and hybrid or virtual trials for major global pharmaceutical sponsors and CROs.[35]

FutureMeds @home is led by Managing Director Gemma Whiteley and delivers decentralised and home-based clinical-trial services across Europe. The company identifies continuity of home-trial provision as material to trial delivery: disruption can cause lost visits, protocol deviations and participant drop-outs, while requiring home-visit-dependent participants to return to a site can reduce compliance and increase drop-out.[36]

The Polish network grew through acquisition and greenfield development. Its advanced medical suite has seven medical rooms, six nursing rooms and a local laboratory.[32] FutureMeds said the site started moving at the end of September 2023 and received its first volunteer at the new location on 9 October 2023. This is facility-specific capacity, not a network-wide average.[32]

Germany has at least two named locations: Berlin and Frankfurt. FutureMeds said Frankfurt was its second German site, and in February 2025 appointed Ninon Armbrust as Managing Director of FutureMeds Germany. Germany’s importance derives partly from emovis. The July 2023 emovis acquisition added the Berlin site and pan-European homecare/DCT capability. FutureMeds said the Berlin site was expected to attract 30% more trials through the network’s commercial and business-development capability in the coming years; that was a forward-looking expectation, not a recorded post-acquisition result.[17]

Spain has four listed locations: Madrid DKF, Madrid, Seville and Cadiz. FutureMeds says its Spanish dedicated research sites are located in well-established hospitals, provide access to a large network of experienced medical professionals and can support complex studies in secondary care. Ukraine remains part of the disclosed footprint despite an unusually high operating-risk environment. FutureMeds lists Kyiv, Zhytomyr, Vinnytsia and Lviv among Ukrainian network locations. FutureMeds says Ukraine is one of the only private networks still operating there and that it actively engages pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies to bring trials back to the country. The DCT and homecare layer has broader European reach than the owned-site platform. After acquiring emovis, FutureMeds initially described HomeCare Visits in 16 countries and high DCT readiness across its network in six countries. By October 2023, it described home-nursing solutions in 18 countries. In July 2025, Phoenix said the group had hybrid or virtual solutions in 18 countries.[18][27][48][3]

This distinction matters. An 18-country DCT footprint does not mean FutureMeds owns a research site in each of those countries. It means the business represented that it could provide hybrid, virtual or homecare delivery in those markets. The physical dedicated-site estate was disclosed as seven countries, while the DCT layer supports site mixes across 18 countries through homecare providers, local coordination and remote-trial infrastructure.[3][4]

The size of the homecare network has also been stated differently over time. emovis’ dedicated homecare-nurse database was said to have exceeded 1,100 clinical-trial-trained nurses in early 2023, and FutureMeds later reported more than 1,400 trained homecare providers across Europe. These counts are consistent with network expansion or different definitions, but they do not establish active utilisation, employment status or available capacity on a particular study date.[18][4][38]

FutureMeds reports broad patient reach: 70 million-plus prospective patients across Europe, access to 60 million-plus patients through strategically located sites and Europe-wide homecare/DCT solutions, and more than 32,000 patients enrolled across the network. Prospective access, potential catchment population and historical enrolment are materially different concepts. Prospective-patient reach is a market-access estimate; enrolment reflects participants actually brought into studies.[19][22]

The group reports more than 2,320 studies conducted by its sites, while the DCT service reports more than 320 projects across more than 120 indications since 2012 and more than 330 trials across 25 therapeutic areas and 140 indications. The different counts have different stated scopes—network-site studies, DCT projects, and DCT trials—and should not be added together.[19][4]

FutureMeds reported that more than 150 of the more than 320 trials supported by FutureMeds @home over the preceding ten years involved rare diseases.[33]

FutureMeds has also reported a substantial workforce build. In March 2025, it said its team had grown from five people at the early Wroclaw site to more than 500 members. Its 2022 UK statutory accounts disclosed an average of 18 employees, including directors, compared with three in 2021. The two figures are not comparable: the more-than-500 statement concerns the wider reported team and does not state whether it includes all group employees, contractors or provider-network members, while the accounts relate to FUTUREMEDS LTD.[37][11]

The company has sought quality and compliance credentials to support a multi-country sponsor offering. It reported that during 2024 it passed three inspections, 10 sponsor audits, one BVMA Germany audit and two client audits in its DCT department with no critical findings. It also cites relationships with the NHS in the UK and Viamed Salud in Spain, and participation in IQVIA Prime Site Network, Medpace’s Flagship Site Program and Fortrea’s Site Advisory Board.[25][12]

These relationships may enhance credibility, patient access and trial flow, but they should not be mistaken for disclosed revenue commitments, exclusivity arrangements or customer-concentration evidence. The business-profile implication is that FutureMeds has built a network embedded across commercial sponsors, CROs, health systems, hospitals, investigators, referral partners and homecare providers; the economics and contractual durability of those relationships belong in Financial strength and investment attractiveness.[12][25][23]

The company’s expansion has been acquisition-led as well as organic. Judgment — The footprint is large enough to support a platform model, but the principal operating question is not simply the number of locations. It is whether FutureMeds can make country sites, hospital arrangements, referral channels and DCT providers function as one commercial and quality system while preserving local investigator relationships. That is the mechanism through which scale becomes higher utilisation, better feasibility conversion, lower central overhead per trial and stronger sponsor retention.

The disclosed footprint enables FutureMeds to pitch cross-border studies, geographically dispersed recruitment, rare-disease programmes and home-based trial components. It also creates exposure to site-start-up execution, country regulatory variation, healthcare-partner dependence, local investigator capacity, IT integration and the inherently uneven DCT maturity of sites. FutureMeds itself noted that DCT readiness, experience and capability can vary materially even within the same network.[4][18]

The operating-footprint conclusion is therefore clear. FutureMeds has evolved from a small Polish research-site base into a seven-country European network with a broader 18-country DCT/homecare layer. The public site count is not fully reconciled—24, 27, 28 and 29 are all disclosed at different points or under different definitions—but the direction and multi-country scale are well established.[37][3][1][2][40]

FutureMeds’ announced growth strategy combined additional sites, deeper healthcare-system relationships, broader therapeutic capability and expansion of FutureMeds @home. The later location navigation showing four Romanian locations—Brasov, Bucharest, Galati and Craiova—signals a potentially broader Romanian network, but does not establish opening dates, dedicated-site status, ownership or trial activity at each location.[40][26]

Romania is commercially relevant because FutureMeds describes it as offering treatment-naïve patient populations, experienced investigators, specialist networks and referral pathways. Its planned Brasov hospital model and Galati outpatient-centre model suggest an asset-light or partnership-led route to local capacity, potentially reducing greenfield lead time while leveraging existing patient flows. The public record does not disclose commercial terms, exclusivity, capex, site economics or sponsor demand supporting those additions.[13][26]

Germany is another stated growth market. Following the February 2025 appointment of Ninon Armbrust as managing director, FutureMeds said it would expand geographically in Germany and strengthen therapeutic-area capability, including oncology. The Gooseberry arrangement is a partnership, not evidence that FutureMeds had established US-owned sites or a US operating network. No revenue target, operating commitment, exclusivity provision or financial contribution was disclosed.[33]

FutureMeds’ own stated expansion priorities include greater patient recruitment, increased trial volume, broader therapeutic expertise and geographic reach. The platform has already demonstrated an ability to add locations through transactions. HGL said it provided growth capital in 2021 to support organic growth and a buy-and-build programme, followed by additional funding in 2023 for a management cash-out and further buy-and-build opportunities. The later acquisition history—emovis, CPS Research, Pulse and Hera—shows that management executed acquisitions across Germany, Scotland, Ukraine and Bulgaria.[5][17][27][48][49]

The principal potential value-creation levers implied by the expansion plans are as follows:

- **Network density and country build-out:** Adding sites in existing markets can improve sponsor coverage, reduce fixed central cost per site, expand patient catchments and make the network more relevant for multi-site protocols. The disclosed late-2025 focus included Romania, Germany and wider European expansion. [26][47][12]

- **DCT penetration:** FutureMeds can potentially attach homecare, remote visits, telemedicine, eConsent, home sampling and direct-to-patient logistics to trials run at its own sites and external sites. The DCT model is already represented as available across 18 countries, materially wider than the seven-country site estate. [4][3]

- **Patient-engagement productivity:** The company’s pre-pooling, GP-referral, healthcare-professional and community-engagement methods aim to improve speed to first patient and reduce recruitment delays. A scalable recruitment engine could improve site utilisation and win rates, but the current disclosures do not report funnel conversion, CAC or recruitment gross margin. [23][21][22]

- **Clinical specialisation:** Oncology, rare disease, paediatric and complex secondary-care protocols can create differentiation if FutureMeds can demonstrate high-quality execution and access to appropriate patients. The company has increased oncology readiness at seven sites and has a partnership targeting rare-disease and paediatric decentralised trials. [25][33]

- **Integration and centralisation:** Standardised feasibility, proposal, IT, quality, finance and operational systems can make acquisitions more productive and preserve service consistency. FutureMeds has implemented a single CTMS and planned further standardisation after emovis, but it has not published realised synergy savings. [28][52]

Judgment — The strategic direction is coherent for a site-network platform: build density in attractive local markets, buy or partner for specialised patient access, centralise the back office, and use DCT capability to convert a clinic network into a broader European delivery network. The return case depends on disciplined site selection and integration because each expansion adds staffing, regulatory, recruitment and sponsor-utilisation requirements before it adds recurring contribution.

The announced plans also create execution exposure. Opening a research site requires suitable investigators, clinical staff, ethics and regulatory readiness, patient-engagement activity, sponsor feasibility wins and study activation. A location announced as part of a network is not economically equivalent to a mature site with contracted studies, high recruitment conversion and recurring sponsor activity. The Romanian plans thus demonstrate growth ambition, not realised capacity or revenue as at 17 November 2025.[26]

FutureMeds’ DCT expansion also faces adoption and standardisation constraints. The company noted that DCT readiness can vary significantly among sites, including within the same site network, and estimated that most sites were three to six years away from adapting DCT approaches, elements, technology, platforms and processes. That is a company estimate, not a forecast of FutureMeds’ financial performance, but it identifies why DCT rollout may be commercially valuable and operationally demanding.[18]

The company has reported a commitment to cultural and people development as part of scaling. In early 2024, it launched Progress P.A.C.T., a framework based on People, Access, Collaboration and Truth. In June 2025, it said more than 80% of colleagues would recommend FutureMeds as a great place to work and described leadership-development, cross-country knowledge-sharing, structured feedback and recognition initiatives. These are relevant to the retention of specialised research personnel, but they are company-reported culture measures, not proof of productivity or reduced attrition.[12][61]

The expansion plans give a PE owner identifiable operating workstreams: mature recent acquisitions, standardise country operating systems, increase site-density and DCT attach rates, develop specialty protocols, improve patient-engagement conversion, and use hospital and health-system partnerships to secure differentiated patient access. The operating evidence demonstrates the ingredients for those levers; the outcome depends on a reconciled baseline of site maturity, active studies, investigator capacity, conversion, contribution margin and cash generation, which belongs in Financial strength and investment attractiveness.[52][28][25]

FutureMeds stated in 2025 that it was expanding with two sites in Romania, in Lviv in Ukraine and in London in the UK.[12]

## Private-equity investment thesis

FutureMeds presents a credible operating-platform story for private equity: a European site-management and decentralised-clinical-trial (DCT) provider with an established acquisition history, a multi-country footprint and a recent sponsor-to-sponsor ownership transition. Scottish Business News reported revenue of £27m for the year ended 31 December 2024, up from £15.5m in 2023, and employee headcount of 397 in 2024, up from 287 in 2023. Transaction value, gross margin, EBITDA, cash conversion, debt and customer concentration have not been disclosed.[57][18][62]

**Investment thesis — FutureMeds is a plausible European clinical-trial-site consolidation platform, but entry price, leverage and returns must be determined from diligence-verified earnings quality rather than public information or the reported prior sponsor return.** The key opportunity is to convert site density, patient access and DCT capability into higher site utilisation, broader sponsor relationships and central-cost leverage, while controlling recruitment, integration and clinical-governance risk.[5][1][43]

### potential value-creation levers

First, FutureMeds can densify its European dedicated-research-site network and add therapeutic capability. HGL described the business as operating 19 sites across six countries, including the UK, Poland, Germany and Spain; management’s July 2025 growth priorities were broader site footprint, recruitment of more diverse patient populations and expertise in new and complex therapeutic areas. Romania illustrates the model: FutureMeds announced two dedicated sites in Brasov and Galati, expected to become operational in Q4 2025–Q1 2026, with oncology, haematology, CNS disorders and metabolic disease priorities; it also planned at least one further Romanian site in 2025 and further expansion during 2026–2027. Germany’s stated plan similarly included geographic growth and oncology capability after opening a second site.[5][62][26][47]

The Romanian expansion thesis is based on access to hospital and outpatient-centre infrastructure, investigators and patient populations. The proposed Brasov site was to be embedded at St. Constantine Hospital, with access to hospital facilities, imaging, specialists and patients, especially in oncology and haematology; Galati was to be located within Terra Clinique. Management characterises Romania as a cost-efficient, patient-rich clinical-research environment versus some Western European countries, but it has not quantified FutureMeds-specific cost savings or contribution margins.[26] **The immediate value-creation question is whether each new site earns an attractive return after start-up, investigator compensation, recruitment spending and country-level overhead—not whether the market rationale is appealing.**[26]

Second, FutureMeds has a potentially differentiated hybrid/DCT layer that could increase revenue per sponsor programme and utilise both owned and external sites. Its acquired DCT service was described as having a 12-year record, availability in 18 European countries and the capability to launch in a country within four weeks. FutureMeds @home was described in September 2025 as using more than 1,400 trained home-healthcare professionals, integrating with FutureMeds-owned and external sites, and offering sponsors a single-vendor model with dedicated project management.[5] The Gooseberry Research partnership, announced on 10 September 2025, adds a potentially asset-light U.S.–Europe route for home-based, decentralised and hybrid trials, focused on rare-disease and paediatric trials; Gooseberry contributes U.S. home-nursing capability, so this is not evidence that FutureMeds owns U.S. sites.[33]

Third, recruitment productivity may support win rates, pricing and site utilisation if it is reproducible at network scale. FutureMeds combines targeted engagement, pre-pooling, local expertise and healthcare-professional networks in its recruitment offer. It says pre-pooling patients before site activation enables rapid recruitment after activation, and it combines patient pathways, direct-to-patient recruitment, site operations, profile analysis and community engagement to generate participant leads with a high likelihood of retention.[19] It also cites relationships with NHS organisations, trusted GPs and communities in the UK, an established referral network, and a Poland mobile unit with capacity to reach up to 15,000 additional patients annually.[21][24][28][14]

One disclosed operating example is material but narrow: two Polish FutureMeds sites enrolled 460 participants—287 in Krakow and 173 in Wroclaw—equal to around 7.5% of the approximately 6,000-person global target for one Lyme-disease vaccine study, within 10 weeks. FutureMeds attributed the outcome to its patient database, physician engagement, referrals and word of mouth. This demonstrates a case of concentrated recruitment performance, not a network-wide enrolment rate, retention rate, gross margin or proof of superior CAC. **Patient-access assets are potentially more valuable than undifferentiated site capacity because recruitment delay is a sponsor’s critical bottleneck, but FutureMeds must demonstrate repeatable time-to-first-patient, enrolment, screen-failure, retention and cost-per-randomisation performance by site and indication.**[90][29]

Fourth, operational integration and selective buy-and-build are the clearest prospective EBITDA levers. HGL’s 2021 investment funded organic growth and acceleration of FutureMeds’ buy-and-build programme; HGL supplied further funding in 2023 for a management cash-out and additional buy-and-build opportunities.[5] FutureMeds acquired emovis in 2023 and subsequently identified potential synergies from consolidating study solutions, aligning workflows, improving communication and building an integration roadmap.[5] It planned standardised feasibility and proposal processes, IT implementation and staff upskilling, efficiency audits, and client meetings on the combined proposition.[52]

FutureMeds also reported consolidating operations and implementing a single clinical trial management system (CTMS) across all dedicated research sites in 2022. Sector analysis identifies accounting, HR, patient recruitment, budgets, contracting, business development and a common CTMS as the core integration domains through which site networks can reduce duplication and improve service efficiency.[28] Cross-country transfer of therapeutic and operational expertise, plus standardised feasibility and contracting, should improve responsiveness to multinational sponsors if implemented effectively.[95] However, FutureMeds’ own emovis integration workshop identified employee concerns, IT integration, learning curves and new matrix communication flows as operational challenges.[52]

Judgment — A sponsor should pursue a “density before breadth” strategy: acquire or develop only those sites that deepen local referral pathways, therapeutic expertise and patient pools, then centralise non-clinical processes without disrupting investigator relationships. Broad geographic expansion without trial volume creates fixed-cost dilution rather than scale economics.

Sector structure supports this approach. Bourne Partners estimated in 2024 that more than 80,000 clinical-trial sites exist globally and fewer than 10% belong to broader networks; its top ten dedicated networks collectively accounted for fewer than 600 sites and less than approximately 25% share. It described the market as fragmented and early-stage, while identifying a PE opportunity to buy small sites at single-digit EBITDA multiples and sell scaled platforms at mid-teens multiples. These are industry estimates, not FutureMeds valuation evidence.[94] The value-creation plan is therefore credible in mechanism, but its investability turns on whether FutureMeds’ acquired sites can be integrated without impairing recruitment throughput or clinical quality.[94]

FutureMeds’ strengthened leadership structure creates a defined operational agenda for this plan. The COO’s priorities are digitising and automating patient-engagement processes, supporting site development and coordinating expansion in existing countries; the CFO’s priorities are process automation and financial infrastructure for acquisitions and operational growth; and the Chief Growth Officer’s remit covers geographic and operational expansion, oncology and CNS, new service lines and FutureMeds @home in rare-disease trials. FutureMeds intends these changes to harmonise European delivery while maintaining a lean executive structure, service reliability, alignment across sites and support teams, delivery predictability, and more efficient recruitment and operational workflows.[97] Paul Maddison, appointed CFO, brings 25 years of financial leadership and M&A experience, more than 10 years in healthcare and life sciences, and advisory work with more than 40 NHS organisations; Iwona Tongbhoyai has more than 18 years of site and clinical-trial-management experience and more than seven years in consultancy covering start-up readiness, transformation, M&A, strategic planning and decentralised-trial delivery.[97]

Technology investment priorities are cross-site patient-database integration and recruitment tools.[58] Therapeutic specialisation is planned to deepen in high-margin oncology and neurology.[58] At sector level, oncology, CNS and rare-disease trials are strategically valuable and often command premium multiples; multi-phase capabilities, inpatient support and curated patient registries strengthen strategic positioning, while patient data assets are acquisition catalysts.[98]

Converting episodic study awards into preferred-provider and repeat-site relationships can improve pipeline visibility, reduce selling cost per awarded study and lessen sponsor concentration. This requires consistently strong feasibility, activation, enrolment, data-quality and retention performance.

Judgment — Add-on selection should favour local density and complementary therapeutic, investigator and patient-access capabilities over headline site count. Standardised processes, centralised data and analytics, and add-on density are recognised roll-up levers; operational standardisation across newly acquired sites is the stated mechanism for margin improvement.[99][58]

The proposed margin mechanism is to standardise newly acquired sites and prioritise add-on density where it accelerates margin expansion, rather than treating headline acquisition size as the value driver.[58][99]

A buyer should establish whether FutureMeds has strategic sponsor-network relationships and portfolio-level allocations, because these arrangements move the commercial model beyond study-by-study contracting and can create more enduring demand.[100]

Applied Clinical Trials proposed strategic sponsor-network relationships with comprehensive patient-enrolment KPIs and allocation of portions of a sponsor portfolio to site networks instead of a “study-by-study” approach. It also proposed risk-sharing contracts under which networks meet recruitment and retention goals across a protocol or programme at a relatively fixed cost; comprehensive enrolment KPIs could reduce sunk costs. This is a proposed model rather than FutureMeds’ current contracting model.[100]

The market backdrop supports investment in network capability. Transparency Market Research valued the global clinical-trial investigative-site-network market at US$9.2 billion in 2025 and identifies global research activity, drug and biologics investment, chronic and complex disease prevalence, personalised medicine, efficient recruitment and retention, hybrid/decentralised models, and standardised high-quality cross-geography data as growth drivers. It further identifies digital infrastructure supporting remote monitoring and real-world-evidence capture as a route to better network performance and sponsor appeal for faster, lower-cost trials.[101]

### likely exit routes and timeline

The clearest evidenced exit route is a secondary private-equity transaction. HGL Private Capital initially invested in FutureMeds in 2021 and subsequently sold its stake to Phoenix Equity Partners; HGL reported a 10x money multiple on its initial 2021 investment. FutureMeds announced on 29 July 2025 that Phoenix had become a strategic shareholder to support the next phase of growth. Neither party disclosed the purchase price, ownership percentage, investment amount or company valuation.[43][1][62]

A subsequent sponsor sale is plausible if Phoenix and management can demonstrate that FutureMeds has become a consistently integrated, cash-generative European platform. Applied Clinical Trials forecast in October 2024 further consolidation of independent clinical-site networks over the following few years, most likely by private-equity firms and CROs developing strategic site-network capabilities.[100] The clinical-research-site sector is fragmented, and private equity has used roll-up strategies to build larger site groups for resale.

A comparable institutional signal came on 14 August 2025, when THL announced an agreement to acquire multinational site network Headlands Research from KKR, subject to customary closing conditions.[102][103]

A strategic exit is also plausible. Sector analysis identifies larger acquisitive research sites, CROs seeking vertical integration, diversified strategics and PE firms as consolidators. CROs seek site ownership to improve control over patient identification, enrolment, retention, protocol execution and quality; an acquirer could therefore value FutureMeds’ patient-engagement, dedicated-site and DCT capabilities as a route to greater operating control.[95] This strategic rationale is supported by FutureMeds’ stated model of providing patient recruitment and clinical-trial-management services to pharmaceutical, biotech and CRO customers.[95][5][2]

Judgment — An illustrative holding period is four to six years from a new sponsor’s entry, rather than a promised timetable. The first 18–24 months should prove site-level recruitment economics, integration and common systems; the following period should establish revenue density, margin expansion and cash conversion sufficient for a sponsor or strategic buyer to underwrite. An earlier exit would require unusually rapid evidence of these outcomes, while a longer period may be necessary if country launches, DCT adoption or integrations lag.

An illustrative exit window of 2029–2031 would be four to six years after Phoenix’s 2025 entry, allowing time for acquisition integration, local density, DCT cross-selling, a KPI history and deleveraging.[1][5]

The exit decision should be milestone-based: sell when FutureMeds can show sponsor diversification, repeat programme volume, standardised site performance, positive cash conversion and a credible pathway for the buyer to continue consolidation. **The prior HGL-to-Phoenix sale makes a further sponsor exit an evidenced route, while a CRO or strategic sale offers potentially greater strategic value if FutureMeds can demonstrate controlled patient access and consistent trial delivery at scale.**[43][1][95]

Strategic acquirers represented nearly 90% of site-related M&A from 2021 through Q2 2025 and drove the bulk of consolidation; private-equity sponsors were increasingly active in building site platforms in 2025.[98] This makes strategic buyers the leading evidenced sector exit route alongside a secondary sponsor sale.[98]

An initial public offering is a less certain exit route than a strategic sale or secondary buyout unless FutureMeds achieves substantially greater scale and audited consolidated earnings.

A merger with a complementary research-site or patient-recruitment platform is another potential route to greater scale and service breadth. Its value depends on compatible governance, quality systems and site economics.

A continuation vehicle is another prospective exit route if integration, EBITDA and cash conversion have been demonstrated.[1][62][3]

A three- to seven-year period is the conventional private-equity capital-return horizon. For FutureMeds, this supports a milestone-based timetable: establish cross-border standardisation, retain principal investigators, prove enrolment delivery and cash conversion, and then market the asset as an integrated platform. Cross-border standardisation is identified as the principal scaling challenge, and principal-investigator retention directly affects site quality and client relationships.[104][58]

Strategic buyers are the most evidenced sector buyer category: Objective Investment Banking & Valuation reports that strategic acquirers represented nearly 90% of site-related M&A from 2021 through Q2 2025, while financial sponsors were increasingly building site platforms in 2025. Acquirer rationales include participant-data access, disease-state or geographic density, and vertical integration into clinical research.[98][105]

### valuation considerations

No public FutureMeds transaction valuation is available through 17 November 2025. Phoenix’s investment amount, ownership stake and valuation were not disclosed. HGL disclosed its 10x money multiple but not HGL’s entry value, exit proceeds, enterprise value or the return on its later 2023 funding.[62] A Finux page displays an estimated 2023 value of PLN0, marked “closed” with 100% confidence; this is a closed-company model output, not a transaction price or an indicator that FutureMeds had zero economic value.[1][73]

Scottish Business News reported £27m of revenue for the year ended 31 December 2024, compared with £15.5m in 2023. Public materials provide no disclosed gross margin, EBITDA or adjusted EBITDA, cash flow, burn rate, debt, CAC, LTV or customer-concentration figures.[57] Accordingly, neither an EV/revenue valuation nor an EV/EBITDA valuation can be responsibly calculated from public data.[18] This places FutureMeds in a diligence-led valuation process: the buyer must construct normalised EBITDA from legal-entity accounts, management accounts and site-level data, then reconcile it to cash conversion and net debt.

Judgment — Normalised EBITDA should exclude non-recurring acquisition, integration, country-launch, restructuring and sponsor-specific mobilisation costs only where evidence shows they will not recur. It should not exclude recurring recruitment expenditure, investigator support, home-nursing delivery costs or central technology costs merely because management categorises them as growth investment. The underwriting model should separately test booked revenue, contracted backlog, pipeline conversion, trial cancellation, bad debt, site utilisation and working-capital timing.

Industry valuation context is useful only as a boundary condition. Bourne Partners’ view is that smaller sites can be acquired at single-digit EBITDA multiples and consolidated platforms may exit at mid-teens multiples, while Provident states that roll-ups often target freestanding sites with at least $5–15 million of revenue and that larger, well-run sites command higher multiples. These are sector observations, not FutureMeds-specific transaction comparables.[94] Weaver’s analysis covers publicly available healthcare EBITDA transaction multiples from January 2016 through September 2021 and presents the median, lower and upper quartiles, and lower and upper extremes in a box-and-whisker plot.[95][106] Healthcare EBITDA transaction-multiple dispersion varies materially by segment, so valuation should not mechanically apply a median or mean multiple.[106]

Debt capacity should follow verified earnings and cash flow, not market leverage availability. DC Advisory reported that stronger UK private-credit unitranche borrowers were priced in the mid- to high-400 basis-point range in Q3 2025 and that upper-mid-market leverage was 5.0x–6.5x EBITDA; the figures came from an October 2025 survey of 98 European banks and direct lenders and are not definitive market data.[71] With FutureMeds’ EBITDA, debt and lease liabilities undisclosed, this range cannot be translated into borrowing capacity.[71]

If relevant FutureMeds entities use UK FRS 102, lease-accounting revisions effective from 1 January 2026 may raise reported EBITDA by moving rental expense into depreciation and interest while also recognising lease liabilities on balance sheet. That can alter leverage, covenant, affordability, earn-out and distributable-profit calculations; valuation and debt documents should therefore specify a consistent pre- or post-change lease basis.[107] **The valuation conclusion is simple: pay for demonstrated, cash-converting site-network earnings and give no entry credit for claimed scale, DCT opportunity or the prior sponsor’s 10x return until those claims translate into measurable operating results.**[107][1][94][106]

The reported revenue increase of £11.5m from 2023 to 2024 was accompanied by a 110-person increase in headcount, from 287 to 397. This establishes growth in reported revenue and workforce, while normalised EBITDA and revenue per employee remain unavailable from public information.[57]

Working-capital underwriting should explicitly test receivables and holdbacks: many study sites have average receivables collection periods exceeding 90 days after costs are incurred, excluding holdbacks that can take years to collect on a long study.[108] Patient-data value also depends on consent rights: lack of appropriate pre-existing consent has often prevented buyers from contacting past clinical-trial subjects at scale to obtain access to subject data in a licensing transaction or sale.[105] Sector valuation guidance identifies sponsor/CRO concentration and investigator stability as material factors: dependence on a single sponsor or CRO elevates risk, diversified client portfolios are rewarded, overreliance on one principal investigator constrains value, and diversified investigator networks demonstrate sustainability.[98] FutureMeds has not publicly disclosed its sponsor/CRO concentration, investigator concentration or collection profile.[18]

A discounted-cash-flow valuation should model study milestones, customer concentration, capital expenditure, quality and compliance investment, tax, receivables, protocol liabilities, contingent consideration and country-level cash repatriation. It should also adjust for pass-through revenue, owner remuneration, share-based compensation, capitalised development, deferred revenue, working-capital seasonality, lease liabilities and other debt-like items.

Where historical earnings quality or trial performance cannot be fully substantiated, the purchase-price structure should use deferred consideration, earn-outs, indemnities or regulatory and quality holdbacks.

The equity-value bridge should separately define net debt, working-capital adjustments and other agreed closing items: Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt ± Working Capital Adjustment ± other agreed closing items. Diligence rejection of an EBITDA add-back reduces accepted EBITDA and enterprise value, while net-debt or working-capital findings separately reduce equity value. Debt capacity depends on accepted EBITDA, free cash flow, leverage tolerance, collateral, working-capital needs, capital expenditure and downside protection.[109]

The equity bridge must be explicit: enterprise value equals adjusted EBITDA multiplied by the purchase multiple, while approximate equity value equals enterprise value less debt plus cash; debt-like items, working capital, cash treatment and purchase-agreement terms determine the final bridge. EBITDA should be normalised for one-time items and identified consistently as LTM, NTM or FY+1.[110][99]

This site-operator range should remain separate from broader pharma-services and CRO references. As of June 2025, Objective Investment Banking & Valuation reported median and average recent pharma-services transaction multiples of 19.8x and 21.6x EBITDA respectively, with top-quartile transactions above 27x; public CRO comparables had a 17.8x median EBITDA multiple and 1.8x revenue multiple. These higher figures reflect different business populations from a dedicated-site network.[98]

The buyer should build the multiple from scale, awarded-but-not-initiated-study backlog, profitability, compliance record, therapeutic specialisation, geographic reach, technology enablement, sponsor diversification and investigator stability. Awarded but not yet initiated studies provide forward revenue visibility; dependence on one sponsor or CRO, or one principal investigator, reduces value. CTMS, eSource and other digital platforms are indicators of operational maturity and scalability.[98]

Awarded but not yet initiated studies are a critical valuation driver because they provide forward revenue visibility.[98] The valuation model should therefore identify FutureMeds’ awarded-but-not-initiated studies, their expected start dates, revenue and conversion into active trials.[98]

Objective Investment Banking & Valuation states that mid-market clinical-research-site operators generally transact at 6x–12x EBITDA, with larger and more differentiated networks occasionally achieving low-teens multiples; standalone sites below $5 million EBITDA are generally valued at 4x–6x, and mid-sized operators with $5–10 million EBITDA may justify 7x–9x.[98]
For a specific acquisition or add-on transaction, the relevant financing measure is the incremental cost of the capital required for that transaction rather than the weighted average cost across all capital sources.[108]

A buyer should make price and leverage conditional on a quality-of-earnings and working-capital review that reconciles legal-entity accounts to site-level revenue, contribution margin, aged receivables, holdbacks, backlog, sponsor/CRO concentration, investigator dependence and net debt. Revenue quality, customer concentration, cash conversion, management continuity, specific growth plans and substantiated earnings adjustments determine the company-specific multiple.[110][98][108]

### top 5 risks

1. **Opaque group financials and leverage capacity:** Public materials do not disclose consolidated revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, cash flow, debt, CAC, LTV or customer concentration. That prevents a public assessment of earnings quality, balance-sheet resilience, debt service capacity and downside liquidity. [18] [62]

2. **Multi-country expansion, standardisation and investigator-retention execution:** FutureMeds’ growth depends on adding sites, countries, therapeutic areas and acquisitions. Its emovis integration work identified employee concerns, IT integration, learning curves and new matrix communication flows; the company’s planned Romanian and German expansion creates start-up and ramp risk. Cross-border standardisation is identified as the greatest scaling challenge for a pan-European site network, while principal-investigator retention directly affects site quality and client relationships. [52] [26] [26] [47] [58] [58]

3. **Recruitment, enrolment and retention performance:** Recruitment is central to the customer proposition but vulnerable to sponsor decisions, staffing and participant engagement. Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development research cited by FutureMeds states that 11% of selected clinical-trial sites fail to enrol a single subject, 37% under-enrol, and 89% of studies eventually meet enrolment goals, often after doubling the original timeline. [29] FutureMeds states that four out of five clinical trials experience delays costing $600,000–$8 million daily, and that about 50% of selected sites endanger trial timelines through poor patient-recruitment performance. [29] [29] FutureMeds notes that enrolment pauses can put site teams and patient marketing on hold, cause staff to move to other studies and lead prospective participants to lose interest; it states willingness can fall by up to 20–30% and marketing cost per randomisation can rise. [30] [30] Delayed recruitment-cap approvals can also delay investigational-product, lab-kit and electronic-device supply, compressing the remaining enrolment window. [30]

4. **DCT, home-nursing, clinical-governance and data-control risk:** DCT readiness, experience and capability can vary materially even within a network. FutureMeds says ethics committees and principal investigators have historically had reservations about third-party DCT solutions when guidance was unclear, while sites may fear loss of control, role redundancy, quality problems and protocol-compliance or data-collection risks when activity occurs outside direct supervision. [18] [18] [111] Engagement, retention and investigational-medicinal-product adherence can also depend on person-to-person contact in some indications. [18]

Multi-country site and home-trial delivery exposes FutureMeds to source-data and pharmacovigilance risks; a severe failure could suspend studies, require remediation and damage its reputation across the network.[4][13][33]

5. **Customer concentration, sponsor-spending and adoption risk:** Dependence on a single sponsor or CRO elevates risk, while diversified client portfolios are rewarded; FutureMeds has not publicly disclosed its sponsor/CRO concentration. [98] In the CRO sector, 2024 study-award decisions for bidding CROs extended significantly, while biotech funding had been suppressed for several years. [112] DCT use accelerated during COVID-19 but FutureMeds said recent trials showed fewer DCT elements than at pandemic levels. [18] It also cited a Tufts adoption benchmark of approximately six years for one company to adopt trial-execution innovation and more than 20 years for broad industry adoption; FutureMeds estimated most sites remained three to six years from adapting DCT approaches, technology, platforms and processes. [18] [18] In the CRO sector, 2024 study-award decisions for bidding CROs extended significantly, while biotech funding had been suppressed for several years. [112] Wider clinical-trial disruption is an additional downside: during COVID-19, 86% of tracked sites had ceased enrolment as of 10 April 2020 and global enrolment was down 74% as of May 2020. [95]

**The risk profile favours a conservative-capital-structure transaction: the principal downside is not drug-approval risk, because sites are paid for trial services regardless of whether a drug is approved, but volatility in sponsor trial activity, enrolment execution, integration and the timing of cash collection.**[95][30]

Clinical quality, audit and investigator-stability risk: Sponsors and CROs routinely audit clinical-trial sites, and FDA findings can be devastating to sites; potential audit findings and their valuation implications require review.[105] Diligence must assess Good Clinical Practice adherence, quality-assurance coverage, current standard operating procedures, staff training, audits and audit readiness; unresolved FDA findings or compliance-framework gaps can materially affect value and transaction dynamics.[105][98]

Clinical-trial patient availability remains a challenge for CROs; recruitment in global trials in Russia and Ukraine had effectively stopped, except for some western parts of Ukraine, and Greece experienced increased clinical-trial activity as large pharmaceutical firms investigated it as an alternative.[112]

### Investment conclusion

FutureMeds is a conditional platform investment. At Phoenix Equity Partners’ 29 July 2025 entry, the business operated 27 clinical sites in seven European countries and offered hybrid or virtual solutions in 18 countries; Phoenix’s Q3 2025 LBO used a unitranche facility. HGL’s 2021 investment subsequently exited to Phoenix with a reported 10x money multiple. These facts establish operating scale, sponsor appetite and acquisition financing, but not transaction value, earnings quality, cash conversion or debt capacity.[3] Phoenix’s July 2025 investment and stated plans to expand geography, recruitment, trial volume and therapeutic expertise reinforce the strategic direction.[71][1][43][62][3]

The central investment case is a scale-and-density thesis: selective site acquisitions and de novo development can broaden patient access and therapeutic reach, while common systems, central recruitment, feasibility, contracting and back-office functions can create operating leverage. FutureMeds’ DCT layer can extend the offering beyond owned sites and support a single-vendor proposition for sponsors.[33][95][2]

The investment should proceed only at a price that preserves returns under conservative assumptions for site ramp-up, integration costs, DCT adoption, recruitment productivity and leverage. Management’s claims of faster recruitment, stronger retention, lower cost and improved data quality are strategically relevant, but they are not yet published as network-wide financial or operational evidence.[18] The principal diligence decision is whether granular data substantiate the reported £27m 2024 revenue, sponsor diversification, site-level contribution and cash conversion.[18][14][57]

Futuremeds Zdrowie Spółka z Ograniczoną Odpowiedzialnością, the Polish entity with KRS 0000458985, reported 2023 net revenue of PLN2,274,703, net profit of PLN810,276, total assets of PLN4,440,486 and equity of PLN4,191,882.[73]

FutureMeds has the ingredients of a credible European clinical-trial-services platform: site scale, buy-and-build experience, patient-access infrastructure, DCT capability and demonstrated sponsor appetite through HGL’s exit to Phoenix. The sector remains fragmented, and centralising site-network functions is a recognised route to scale economies and strategic relevance.[1] The same strategy carries material execution risk because new-country launches, acquisition integration, recruitment continuity and DCT governance all determine whether nominal network scale becomes profitable scale.[43][94][95][52] The evidence therefore supports a growth-oriented PE thesis, but not a leverage-led or multiple-led underwriting case before consolidated earnings, cash conversion, customer exposure and net debt are verified.[30][18][71][106]

1. Validate site-level revenue, contribution margin, recruitment productivity, backlog and cash collection before setting entry EV or leverage.
2. Tie management incentives to enrolment quality, sponsor retention, integration milestones, cash conversion and patient-safety/compliance metrics.
3. Prioritise site density and therapeutic specialisation over broad country expansion.
4. Underwrite DCT as an incremental service and utilisation lever, not as a guaranteed straight-line market-growth assumption.
5. Preserve strategic-exit optionality by demonstrating a standardised, auditable network valuable to both CROs and subsequent sponsors.

Open items are consolidated historical revenue, gross margin, EBITDA or adjusted EBITDA, operating cash flow and burn, cash and debt, funding-round amounts and valuation, revenue and gross profit by country and service line, site and investigator utilisation, study backlog and conversion, sponsor concentration and retention, contract duration and pricing, CAC, LTV, patient-referral conversion, and the economics and liabilities of acquired entities. These would be settled by entity-level statutory accounts and management reporting that reconcile the group perimeter, revenue recognition, profitability, cash flow, leverage, client exposure and cohort economics.
