# CME Group Cash Conversion, Debt and Refinancing Risk

## Executive summary

CME’s Q1 2025 cash generation and capital structure support sustainable growth and financial flexibility, although quarterly cash conversion benefited partly from timing-sensitive working-capital movements. Operating cash flow rose $223.9 million, or 25.1%, to $1,116.6 million, and OCF conversion increased to 116.8% from 104.4%, alongside higher revenue, operating income and trading volume.[1] Receivables absorbed an additional $103.4 million of cash, while income taxes payable supplied an additional $129.5 million, so the improvement in reported conversion was not solely a recurring operating gain.[1][2] CME had $3.450 billion of fixed-rate note principal, no short-term debt, approximately $2.3 billion of unused revolver capacity, and maturities beginning only in 2028, which limits near-term refinancing pressure while preserving capacity for corporate and strategic needs.[1][2][3]

### What is the operating cash flow conversion rate?

Judgment — This analysis defines the operating cash flow conversion rate, also called the operating-cash-flow-to-net-income or earnings-quality ratio, as net cash provided by operating activities divided by net income for the same reporting period, expressed as a percentage. It tests whether accounting earnings are supported by operating cash, rather than measuring free cash flow after capital expenditure, dividends or debt service.

This orientation is important because some market sources express the inverse ratio—net income divided by OCF—which reverses the numerical interpretation.[20] Under the OCF-to-net-income convention used here, a result above 100% means operating cash flow exceeded reported net income for that period.[20][21]

**The conversion measure is a diagnostic of the relationship between earnings and cash, not a pure measure of collections or operating profitability, because it also includes non-cash expenses and movements in operating assets and liabilities.** Accordingly, the ratio should be read alongside the cash-flow reconciliation rather than treated as an independent measure of sustainable cash generation.[1][21]

Under the common indirect cash-flow presentation, cash from operating activities begins with net income and adjusts for non-cash items and changes in operating assets and liabilities. Typical adjustments include depreciation, amortization, stock-based compensation, deferred taxes, impairments, gains or losses, receivables, inventory, payables and deferred revenue.[22] There is no universal threshold for interpreting operating-cash-flow-to-net-income ratios across industries.[22]

### Synthesis

CME’s Q1 2025 OCF increased 25.1%, materially faster than revenue and net income, while OCF conversion increased from 104.4% to 116.8%.[1] Higher trading volume, revenue and operating income establish genuine positive business momentum, and CME itself linked the OCF increase largely to higher trading volume.[1]

At the same time, the conversion improvement was not entirely operational: receivables became a $103.4 million larger cash drag, while income taxes payable became a $129.5 million larger cash source and selected current-account movements swung favorably by $110.1 million.[2] **The appropriate conclusion is that cash generation strengthened alongside underlying activity, but the quarter’s unusually strong cash conversion includes timing-sensitive working-capital support and should not be extrapolated mechanically.**[1]

## Debt and available liquidity

CME’s debt position at March 31, 2025 combined long-dated fixed-rate funding with a sizeable undrawn corporate revolver. The relevant distinction is between **debt principal**, the contractual amount repayable at maturity, and **carrying value**, the accounting amount recorded on the balance sheet after issuance discounts and costs. The former is the appropriate basis for maturity and refinancing analysis; the latter is the reported balance-sheet debt measure.

It excludes purpose-specific clearing facilities and restricted customer collateral because those resources are not ordinary funding for acquisitions, buybacks, or discretionary investment.

At March 31, 2025, CME had approximately $2.3 billion of excess borrowing capacity under its multi-currency revolving senior credit facility. The facility could be used for general corporate purposes, for clearing-house liquidity in certain circumstances at CME’s discretion, and as a backstop for commercial paper. CME reported no borrowings under the facility.[2][3]

The facility matured in April 2030 and could be increased to $3.3 billion, subject to the consent of the agent and lenders providing the additional commitments.[2] The Senior Credit Facility matures on April 23, 2030 and permits voluntary prepayment without a premium or penalty.[1][26] The $1.0 billion accordion feature is potential capacity rather than committed unused capacity at March 31, 2025.

An increase in commitments requires, among other conditions, a certificate from a CME senior officer confirming the required corporate approval, the accuracy in all material respects of representations and warranties, and that no Default has occurred and is continuing immediately before or after the increase takes effect.[11]

CME also maintained $1.0 billion of committed repurchase facilities, secured by non-cash collateral and designed to provide cash when clearing firms fail to promptly discharge obligations. Those facilities were subject to annual renewal and had no borrowings outstanding. CME’s clearing-related cash balances are large but restricted. At March 31, 2025, CME held $108.0 billion in a Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago cash account included in performance bonds and guaranty fund contributions. CME classified cash performance bonds and guaranty fund contributions as restricted cash and restricted cash equivalents in its cash-flow statement.[2][3]

The operational scale behind those restricted balances is substantial. During Q1 2025, CME’s clearing house transferred approximately $7.0 billion per day through its clearing systems to settle gains and losses between clearing firms. These settlement flows demonstrate the central-counterparty role of CME Clearing, but they are not corporate operating cash flow and are not deployable as general investment cash.[2][3]

CME’s regulated operations require it to retain resources. As a CFTC-regulated Derivatives Clearing Organization, CME must maintain regulatory capital equal to at least one year of projected operating expenses and cash, liquid securities, or a line of credit equal to at least six months of projected operating expenses. Its Designated Contract Markets have comparable financial-resource requirements, and CME reported compliance with both the DCO and DCM requirements at March 31, 2025.[2][3]

**CME’s Q1 2025 capital structure supported sustainable growth and strategic flexibility because the company combined strong cash generation, an undrawn $2.3 billion general-purpose revolver, investment-grade ratings, covenant compliance, and a debt ladder with no listed fixed-rate maturity before 2028.** That flexibility is real but bounded by regulatory-resource requirements, the holding-company structure, and substantial shareholder distributions.

CME Group’s parent-company funding position is distinct from the resources held by its subsidiaries. CME Group has no operations of its own and relies on subsidiary dividends for shareholder dividends; absent guarantees, subsidiaries have no obligation to pay parent-company debt or make funds available to the parent, and subsidiary creditors have prior claims on subsidiary assets.[38][36]

On March 1, 2022, CME Group’s Form S-3 registration statement and related prospectus covered debt securities, Class A common stock, preferred stock and warrants that CME may issue and sell from time to time pursuant to Rule 415. The registration statement incorporated CME Group’s Annual Report on Form 10-K for the year ended December 31, 2021.[36] Unless specified otherwise in offering materials, the prospectus states that net proceeds may be used for general corporate purposes, including acquisitions and debt repayment.[36]

The indenture framework does not limit the aggregate principal amount of debt securities CME may issue, and permits additional securities of a series unless an offering supplement provides otherwise. This gives CME a contractual route for future financing alongside internally generated cash and committed revolving capacity.[36][32]

Cash and cash equivalents declined from $2.9 billion at December 31, 2024 to $1.4 billion at March 31, 2025, while performance-bond and guaranty-fund contributions held increased from $98.9 billion to $120.7 billion.[2] CME stated that its retained cash balance reflects anticipated or possible short-term cash needs, prevailing interest rates, its corporate investment policy, and alternative investment choices.[12][2]

The senior credit facility is unsecured and is not guaranteed by subsidiaries. Its covenants include a minimum consolidated-net-worth requirement and customary limitations on liens, subsidiary indebtedness, fundamental changes involving CME or significant subsidiaries, and specified dispositions of consolidated assets or voting stock of specified exchange subsidiaries.[25]

As of December 31, 2024, pro forma for the March 2025 notes offering and use of proceeds, the parent notes would have been structurally subordinated to approximately $1.3 billion of subsidiary indebtedness and other liabilities, including trade payables. That amount excluded $98.9 billion of clearing-member cash performance bonds and guaranty-fund contributions with equal and offsetting assets, and $5.2 billion of deferred tax liabilities.[25]

CME identified its ability to continue generating funds and manage indebtedness to continue investing in the business, variability in earnings on cash accounts and client collateral, and the ability to meet margin and liquidity requirements of the BrokerTec matched-principal business as performance considerations.[10]

CME also identified debt service as a potential claim on a significant portion of operating cash flow, reducing cash available for capital expenditures, acquisitions or investments, dividends, and general corporate purposes.[27]

CME reported cash and cash equivalents of $1.4 billion at March 31, 2025, compared with $2.9 billion at December 31, 2024. CME stated that a majority of this balance was invested in money-market mutual funds investing only in U.S. Treasury securities, U.S. government agency securities, U.S. Treasury-security reverse repurchase agreements, and short-term bank deposits.[1]

Together, the reported figures show that corporate cash and cash equivalents declined from $2.8924 billion at December 31, 2024 to $1.4053 billion at March 31, 2025 even as performance-bond and guaranty-fund contributions held increased from $98.9 billion to $120.7 billion. The collateral increase should not be mistaken for an increase in deployable corporate liquidity.[12][2]

CME reported that cash provided by financing activities was higher in Q1 2025 than in Q1 2024 because cash performance bonds and guaranty-fund contributions increased.[1]

Contractual debt service takes priority over discretionary uses of cash such as acquisitions, share repurchases, and dividends; clearing-related financial resources also cannot be freely redirected to those purposes.

### What features of CME Group’s exchange and clearing business

CME’s core business is an exchange-and-clearing model in which revenue is substantially derived from clearing and transaction fees. The company also generates market-data and information-services revenue and other revenue. A majority of clearing and transaction fee revenue is linked to trade execution, with fees generally assessed and recognized when a trade is successfully executed.[30][39]

The model has strong operating leverage. Operating leverage means that revenue can rise faster than costs when activity increases because many costs are relatively fixed in the short term. CME stated that its cost structure is generally fixed in the short term while operating cash sources are largely dependent on contract-trading volume.[15]

That dynamic was visible in Q1 2025. As covered in **Operating cash flow and cash conversion**, CME’s operating cash flow increased to $1.1166 billion from $892.7 million in Q1 2024, while cash conversion—operating cash flow divided by net income—improved to 116.8% from 104.4%. CME attributed the increase in operating cash flow largely to higher trading volume.[2][1][3]

CME reported 1.816 billion total futures and options contracts in Q1 2025, up 13% from 1.608 billion in Q1 2024. Average daily volume rose to 29.768 million contracts from 26.360 million, and electronic volume rose to 93% of total volume from 91%.[2][3]

The activity increase was broad based. Q1 2025 average daily volume exceeded Q1 2024 volume in interest rates, equity indexes, foreign exchange, energy, agricultural commodities, and metals. Broad participation across product lines is more supportive of a durable activity signal than growth limited to one product complex.[17]

CME reported that market uncertainty remained high in Q1 2025, including uncertainty related to tariffs and Federal Reserve policy, and said it believed those factors contributed to increased contract volume. Its business model tends to benefit when customers seek to hedge or speculate on market volatility.[2][30]

Revenue and profitability strengthened with volume. Q1 2025 total revenue was $1.6423 billion, compared with $1.4879 billion in Q1 2024; clearing and transaction fees were $1.3373 billion, compared with $1.2089 billion; and market-data revenue was $194.5 million, compared with $175.4 million.[2]

CME’s operating margin increased to 67.5% in Q1 2025 from 64.5% in Q1 2024. Net income increased to $956.2 million from $855.2 million while total expenses increased only 1%, to $534.3 million from $528.3 million.[2][3]

The average rate per contract declined 1%, to $0.686 from $0.695, in Q1 2025. CME attributed the decline primarily to higher volume-tier incentives and greater micro equity contract volume, partly offset by product mix and a February 1, 2025 fee increase. Volume growth therefore drove transaction-fee growth despite a modest decline in per-contract realization.[1]

CME’s accounts receivable movement reduced, rather than inflated, Q1 2025 operating cash flow. The cash-flow statement recorded a $196.8 million use of operating cash from accounts receivable in Q1 2025, compared with a $93.4 million use in Q1 2024. The larger receivables use is consistent with more cash being tied up temporarily in amounts billed but not yet collected.[40][14]

CME’s income-taxes-payable change added $280.9 million to Q1 2025 operating cash flow, compared with a $151.4 million source in Q1 2024. An increase in taxes payable means income-tax expense exceeded cash tax payments in the period, temporarily supporting operating cash flow.[40][18]

The working-capital pattern therefore cuts in opposite directions. Receivables reduced Q1 2025 cash generation by more than they did in Q1 2024, while income taxes payable provided a larger cash benefit. **The stronger Q1 2025 operating cash flow was supported by higher underlying trading activity, while the receivables movement was a cash headwind rather than a source of favorable conversion.**[1][40][14][18]

CME’s collateral-reinvestment activity is another material feature of the cash-flow model. The company reinvests cash performance bonds and guaranty-fund contributions and distributes part of the interest earned back to clearing firms. In Q1 2025, earnings from those contributions were $873.6 million and related interest-distribution expense was $804.8 million, compared with $1.0367 billion and $967.4 million, respectively, in Q1 2024.[2][3]

The collateral-related earnings and distributions should be analyzed together. Their net contribution before other items was approximately $68.8 million in Q1 2025 and $69.3 million in Q1 2024.Derived — Q1 2025: $873.6 million less $804.8 million; Q1 2024: $1,036.7 million less $967.4 million. The gross amounts declined, but the net spread was broadly stable.

CME’s operational cash generation is therefore tied to several distinct drivers: contract volume, fee rates and product mix, market-data subscriptions, collateral-reinvestment income net of distributions, and the timing of working-capital movements. The company’s management attribution identifies higher trading volume as the principal driver of the Q1 2025 year-over-year increase in operating cash flow.[1][3]

The business also carries structural liquidity responsibilities. CME Clearing acts as central counterparty: it becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, marks positions to market frequently, and manages credit risk through performance-bond requirements and guaranty-fund contributions. This structure supports market confidence and transaction volume, but it requires dedicated liquidity arrangements and regulatory capital.[30][27][3]

**The exchange-and-clearing model supports high cash generation when trading volumes rise, but it also requires CME to separate corporate liquidity from customer collateral and clearing-contingency resources.** This distinction is central to judging funds available for acquisitions, product development, and shareholder distributions.

CME’s exchanges offer benchmark products in interest rates, equity indexes, foreign exchange, agricultural commodities, energy and metals, alongside cash and repo fixed-income trading through BrokerTec, cash and OTC FX trading through EBS, and central-counterparty clearing through CME Clearing.[36][41]

CME Group operates the CME, CBOT, NYMEX and COMEX derivatives exchanges, where customers trade futures and options and manage risk.[50][51]

CME defines total contract volume as exchange-traded and cleared contracts plus certain cleared-only contracts. Volume is measured in round turns, meaning a completed purchase and offsetting sale, and average rate per contract equals total clearing and transaction fees divided by total contract volume.[38]

Most clearing and transaction fees are recognized when a trade is successfully executed, completing CME’s trade-matching, novation and clearing activities. Clearing and transaction fees include per-contract charges for trade matching, clearing, electronic-platform trading, portfolio reconciliation and compression, risk mitigation and other services.[41]

Accounts receivable comprise trade receivables and unbilled revenue. The timing of revenue recognition, billings and collections produces both billed receivables and customer advances and deposits; CME also states that receivables in a period primarily result from clearing and transaction fees billed in the final month of that period.[41][42]

CME states that average rate per contract is affected by volume-based incentives, product mix, trading venue, and the mix of member and non-member customers. This explains why growth in trading volume and growth in transaction-fee revenue need not move in the same proportion.[38][42]

CME Clearing stands between clearing firms as an intermediary with equal and offsetting claims to and from firms on opposite sides of each cleared contract. It manages exposure through financial standards, performance-bond requirements, mark-to-market settlement cycles, mandatory guaranty-fund contributions and intraday monitoring.[41]

Cash performance bonds and guaranty-fund contributions may be invested in government securities, Federal Reserve cash accounts, highly rated government securities, money-market funds or CME’s Interest Earning Facility. Interest earned accrues to CME and CME may distribute it to clearing firms; the underlying cash collateral remains restricted and cannot be used for company operations or to satisfy operational liabilities.[41]

In its Form 10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2022, CME stated that “Clearing and transaction fees include electronic trading fees and brokerage commissions, surcharges for privately-negotiated transactions, portfolio reconciliation and compression services, risk mitigation and other volume-related charges for trade contracts,” and that it recognizes the majority of this fee revenue when a trade is successfully executed.[43] In the same filing, CME stated that market-data and information-services revenue is priced primarily by reportable devices and subscribers, generally billed monthly, and recognized monthly as customers receive the service.[43]

CME’s September 2022 Form 10-Q also explained the receivables mechanism: the timing of revenue recognition, billing and cash collection produces billed accounts receivable and customer advances and deposits recorded as contract liabilities; certain transaction, annual-license and other fees are billed upfront before revenue is recognized. This mechanism explains why accounts-receivable movements can affect operating cash flow even when the underlying trade-execution revenue has been recognized.[43][44]

In its September 2022 Form 10-Q, CME stated that its clearing house marks open futures and options positions to market twice daily and cleared-only interest-rate-swap positions once daily; it collects payments from clearing firms with losing positions and pays firms with gaining positions. These clearing-member settlement movements are distinct from CME’s corporate operating cash generation and reinforce the need to separate clearing liquidity from general corporate liquidity.[43]

CME states that the timing of revenue recognition, billing, and collections results in billed accounts receivable and customer advances and deposits recorded as contract liabilities.

CME’s Q1 2025 clearing and transaction fees were $1,337.3 million, up 11% from $1,208.9 million in Q1 2024; market-data and information-services revenue also increased 11%, while other revenue increased 7%.[2]

For Q1 2024, the change in income taxes payable provided $151.4 million of operating cash, compared with $282.5 million in Q1 2023, and income taxes paid were $114.8 million.[18]

Contract liabilities were $46.1 million at March 31, 2022, compared with $15.2 million at December 31, 2021.[44]

Unlike a manufacturing business, CME’s fee-based exchange and clearing model does not require heavy physical-capital investment to generate revenue, although resilient technology, cybersecurity, regulatory compliance, and clearing-risk infrastructure remain essential investments.

Because changes in accounts receivable and income taxes payable are working-capital timing items that drive divergence between operating cash flow and net income, neither Q1 2024 nor Q1 2025 operating cash flow alone measures sustainable cash capacity for strategic investment.[12]

### Capital-allocation commitments and capacity for strategic investments

Capital allocation is management’s use of cash among reinvestment, debt reduction, acquisitions, dividends, share repurchases, and liquidity retention. CME’s Q1 2025 capital allocation shows that the company had substantial cash-generating capacity but also made substantial shareholder distributions.

CME paid $2.5674 billion of cash dividends in Q1 2025, compared with $2.3284 billion in Q1 2024. The Q1 2025 dividend cash outflow exceeded the quarter’s $1.1166 billion operating cash flow.[2][1]

Dividends are financing cash outflows and therefore do not reduce reported operating cash flow.[52]

The dividend payment included CME’s variable-dividend framework. CME stated that it intends to continue paying a regular quarterly dividend targeting 50% to 60% of prior-year cash earnings, while the annual variable dividend is based on prior-year performance and expected cash needs. The board retains discretion over the amount and payment of dividends.[27] On February 6, 2025, CME declared a regular quarterly dividend of $1.25 per share, with an estimated first-quarter payment of approximately $455.0 million assuming no change in shares outstanding.[15][27]

In determining dividends, CME considers earnings, financial condition, capital requirements and indebtedness.[52]

CME declared an annual variable dividend of $5.80 per share in December 2024 and paid it on January 16, 2025. That timing helps explain why Q1 dividend cash use was much larger than current-quarter operating cash flow.[27]

On December 5, 2024, CME announced that its board had authorized a share-repurchase program of up to $3 billion of outstanding Class A common stock. The timing and number of repurchases are at CME’s discretion; the program has no expiration date, does not obligate CME to acquire a particular amount, and may be modified, suspended or terminated at any time.[2]

No Class A shares were repurchased under the publicly announced program in Q1 2025. CME did report 31,624 shares surrendered by employees to satisfy tax obligations on restricted-stock vesting, which is not the same as discretionary market repurchases under the authorization.[2]

The share-repurchase authorization therefore preserved flexibility rather than creating a fixed future cash obligation. Management could prioritize technology investment, liquidity preservation, or acquisitions over repurchases without breaching the authorization’s terms.[2][3]

CME’s direct investment cash outflows in Q1 2025 were modest relative to operating cash flow and dividends. Net cash used in investing activities was $16.0 million, including $14.2 million of net property purchases and $2.4 million of investments in privately held equity investments.[29]

CME expected approximately $90.0 million of 2025 capital expenditures, net of leasehold improvement allowances. The company said technology spending would support system capacity, performance improvements, acquired-platform integration, and office-space improvements.[27]

Technology investment is strategically important for CME’s business model. The company has a 10-year strategic partnership with Google Cloud, announced in 2021, to accelerate its move to cloud infrastructure and support expanded access, real-time data, analytics, product innovation, efficiencies, and market resiliency.[27][15]

CME also identified continued investment needs in systems capacity, technology infrastructure, personnel, facilities, and management controls as part of growth. These needs are ongoing rather than one-time discretionary expenditures because higher transaction volumes require resilient and scalable market and clearing infrastructure.[27][15]

Management’s resource-allocation process explicitly includes liquidity and cash needs, new product development, expansion of existing products, and product discontinuation. CME operates as one consolidated reportable segment, and senior leadership uses consolidated results in making these investment and operating decisions.[2][3]

CME has stated that it intends to explore acquisitions, investments, partnerships, joint ventures, and other strategic opportunities. It may use additional equity or debt financing for such transactions, although incremental debt would increase leverage.[27][15]

The company’s debt agreements do not significantly restrict its ability to incur additional indebtedness. This preserves funding flexibility, but it also means that future acquisitions or partnerships financed with debt could weaken the currently favorable leverage and maturity profile.[15][27]

CME’s capital-return policy is therefore the main near-term call on corporate cash. In Q1 2025, the $2.5674 billion dividend payment was more than twice quarterly operating cash flow, while share repurchases under the new program were nil. The company had capacity to adjust buybacks because the authorization was discretionary, but dividend policy and regulatory liquidity needs remain important competing uses of cash.[2]

CME’s parent-company structure adds another constraint. The parent relies on dividends from subsidiaries to pay its own shareholder dividends, while substantially all operating revenue and assets are located at subsidiaries. Subsidiary cash flows, regulatory restrictions, business needs, and subsidiary creditor claims can affect the cash ultimately available to the parent.[2][30]

**CME’s Q1 2025 financial flexibility was sufficient to support routine technology investment, product development, and measured strategic investment, but the scale of shareholder dividends means large discretionary investments would require active prioritization among cash retention, shareholder returns, and additional financing.**

Q1 2025 cash dividends of $2,567.4 million equaled 229.9% of the quarter’s $1,116.6 million operating cash flow. The quantified gap underscores that the January variable-dividend payment drew on cash already held and/or financing capacity in addition to current-quarter cash generation.[2]

Effective April 23, 2025, CME amended and restated its senior credit agreement. The resulting multi-currency Senior Credit Facility had aggregate commitments of $2.25 billion, comprising $1.98 billion of multi-currency commitments and $270 million of U.S.-dollar commitments; it matures on April 23, 2030. Facility proceeds may refinance amounts under the prior agreement and fund ongoing working capital and other general lawful corporate purposes. CME may request commitment increases up to $3.25 billion, subject to the agreement’s conditions.[11]

CME’s common shareholders receive dividends and other distributions only if declared by the board, subject to preferred-stock rights. This board-declared structure is consistent with CME’s ability to calibrate discretionary capital returns alongside liquidity retention, debt management, technology investment and strategic opportunities.[36][1][3]

CME announced in Q1 2025 that it was piloting solutions for wholesale payments and asset tokenization using Google Cloud Universal Ledger. This is a specific strategic technology initiative alongside the broader cloud partnership and demonstrates a continuing call on capital and management attention for product and infrastructure development.[45]

In Q1 2025, technology expenses increased 11%, while professional fees and outside services decreased 14%, partly because consulting costs for the Google Cloud migration were lower. The expense pattern shows continued technology spending while migration-related consulting expense declined.[12]

The Q1 2025 statement of equity separately reported dividends on common and preferred stock of $456.1 million, or $1.25 per share; this measure is distinct from the $2,567.4 million cash-dividend amount in the cash-flow statement.[2]

Q1 2025 purchases of property, net, were $14.2 million versus $19.8 million in Q1 2024, and investments in privately held equity investments were $2.4 million versus $3.5 million.[1]

At December 31, 2024, CME’s future non-cancellable purchase obligations totaled $1,286.4 million, including $176.5 million due in 2025.[27]

Since implementing its variable-dividend policy in early 2012, CME reported returning $28.6 billion to shareholders through dividends.[10]

CME identified the need to manage risks, control costs and achieve synergies from acquisitions, investments and alliances, including its Google partnership, joint ventures with S&P Dow Jones, and actions by primary business and distribution partners.[25]

CME also identified variable costs from its Google Cloud transition and the potential for duplicative costs while it maintains both its on-premise environment and Google Cloud environment during that transition.[25]

CME’s certificate of incorporation commits it to maintain open-outcry floor trading for a product while that market meets specified liquidity tests. The commitment includes maintaining facilities for conducting business, disseminating price information, clearing and delivery, and providing reasonable financial support for technology, marketing and research for open-outcry markets.[25]

CME paid approximately $2.6 billion of dividends during Q1 2025 while cash and cash equivalents declined by approximately $1.5 billion from $2.9 billion at December 31, 2024 to $1.4 billion at March 31, 2025. The concurrent dividend distribution and smaller cash balance show shareholder distributions alongside a materially smaller cash buffer.[2][17]

The April 23, 2025 Senior Credit Facility provides $2.25 billion of borrowing capacity for ongoing working capital and other general corporate purposes, with potential increases to $3.25 billion. While commitments or covered obligations remain, its consolidated-net-worth covenant is based on December 31, 2024 consolidated net worth and is adjusted for actual share repurchases and special dividends, including annual variable dividends, subject to a $2.0 billion aggregate cap and a 0.65 multiplier. The facility therefore provides general-purpose borrowing capacity, while its net-worth covenant ties some capacity to distributions and repurchases.[26][11]

The indenture framework permits CME and its subsidiaries to incur additional indebtedness, issue or repurchase securities, pay dividends, and engage in affiliate transactions, and it does not limit the amount of indebtedness or securities that CME or its subsidiaries may issue. CME also warns that using funds for numerous purposes may limit funds available to pay the notes. CME’s approximately $2.6 billion of Q1 2025 dividend distributions are therefore relevant to debt-service flexibility.[25][17]

CME Group planned to launch CME FX Spot+ in 2025 as a spot-FX marketplace intended to connect liquidity between spot-FX and FX-futures trading environments.[27] CME Group seeks to grow its futures and options business by expanding and innovating core offerings, launching products and services, and increasing customer participation.[27]

### Synthesis

CME’s fixed-rate-note structure supported financial flexibility at March 31, 2025. Its fixed-rate notes were staggered from 2028 through 2048, with an approximately 11.8-year principal-weighted remaining maturity; its AA-/Aa3 long-term ratings had Stable outlooks, and CME was in compliance with its debt-facility covenants.[1]

The business generated stronger operating cash alongside higher trading volume, revenue, margins, and net income, while receivables were a cash headwind in Q1 2025. The principal limits on deployable funds were not note maturities but the $2.5674 billion dividend payment, regulatory capital and liquidity requirements, and the need to distinguish corporate liquidity from restricted clearing collateral.[2][40][3]

1. Maintain the 2028–2032 maturity ladder as the primary refinancing-monitoring framework, rather than relying on the 11.8-year average maturity alone.
2. Preserve the $2.3 billion general-purpose revolver as corporate liquidity; do not treat clearing-house contingency facilities or client collateral as strategic-investment funding.
3. Fund routine growth investment from operating cash flow while calibrating discretionary buybacks and variable dividends to larger acquisition, technology, or infrastructure opportunities.

CME Group states that interim quarterly results are not necessarily indicative of results for any subsequent period.[3]
