For a 15-person Austin business with both payment-card and health-record exposure, Coalition is the strongest starting quote because its published wording most clearly separates PCI fines and assessments, regulatory defense and penalties, and breach-response services, including a stated five-minute average response time.

Chubb is a close alternative when you value a broad, established incident-response panel and potentially strong payment-card wording, but your quote must confirm the applicable limits, whether regulatory defense is a duty, and any non-panel vendor sublimit.

Cowbell Prime is worth quoting for its small-business fit and 24/7 claims support, but its public materials leave more of the PCI, regulatory, sublimit, and response-time detail to the issued policy.

No policy replaces PCI compliance or pays every HIPAA or card-brand charge, so buy limits only after checking the merchant agreement, HIPAA role, cyber controls, and the schedule of coverage-specific limits and retentions.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7]

Those are not the same: a PCI-DSS assessment usually arises through your merchant-services contract, while a HIPAA civil monetary penalty is a government regulatory matter.

Judgment — Employer-held employment health records generally are not HIPAA PHI. Determine whether the records instead belong to a group health plan or arise from work as a HIPAA covered entity or business associate before treating HIPAA enforcement as an exposure. Keep employee-privacy and breach-notification exposure separate. PCI DSS applies if you accept, process, or transmit payment-card data, regardless of your 15-person size, revenue, industry, or Austin location.[8]

### Chubb

Chubb’s relevant offering is Cyber Enterprise Risk Management, or Cyber ERM. It is aimed at organizations holding sensitive customer or employee information, operating a network, or running a website. That makes it relevant to an Austin business with online sales, payment-card data, and employee health records.[9]

Chubb’s form defines Protected Information broadly enough to include both sides of your data profile: medical or healthcare data, protected health information, and credit or debit card numbers. That definition identifies both data categories as information the policy recognizes, but it does not turn every incident involving either category into a covered claim.[4]

For the payment-card side, Chubb uses the term Payment Card Loss. The definition includes monetary assessments, fines, penalties, chargebacks, reimbursements, fraud recoveries, and card-reissuance costs that the insured becomes legally obligated to pay after an actual or alleged network-security failure or failure to protect payment-card data. It specifically contemplates amounts due under a payment-card processing agreement, a merchant agreement, or a written issuing/acquiring-bank demand related to alleged PCI DSS, EMV, or mobile-payment-security noncompliance.[4][12]

That is stronger wording than a generic promise to cover cyber “fines.” It recognizes that many PCI losses are contractual: the card brand, acquiring bank, or processor may assess a charge against the merchant under the merchant-services arrangement rather than a government agency imposing a civil fine. A payment-card assessment therefore needs to be analyzed against both the insurance wording and the merchant agreement that determines who can assess the charge, what is assessed, and whether it follows a qualifying breach.[4][12][40]

The May 31, 2016 P.F. Chang’s district-court decision illustrates why the specific Chubb insuring agreement and merchant contract matter. MasterCard imposed more than $1.7 million of assessments on the processor after the 2014 breach, largely for fraud recovery involving notification and replacement cards; the processor sought reimbursement from the retailer under its contract. The court held that the policy’s general third-party liability wording did not cover the processor and card-brand liabilities because the processor’s own records were not accessed, and held that contractual-liability exclusions barred the passed-through amounts. It separately held that card-replacement costs fell within that policy’s notification-cost agreement.[41] The decision was an unpublished opinion from one court, and the appeal was dismissed by stipulation after the parties settled during mediation.[41]

Chubb’s Payment Card Loss wording does not provide ongoing PCI compliance insurance. It excludes later PCI DSS, EMV, or mobile-payment-agreement assessments unrelated to a specific claim. It also excludes expenses to upgrade controls beyond the pre-incident standard or simply to achieve compliance with PCI DSS, EMV, or a mobile-payment merchant agreement.[4]

**For your card business, Chubb potentially covers a breach-linked payment-card liability; it does not insure the ordinary cost of becoming or staying PCI compliant.** That distinction decides whether a card-brand charge is a claim expense or your own operational expense.[12][4]

For the health-record side, Chubb distinguishes Regulatory Fines from Payment Card Loss. Regulatory Fines are civil monetary fines or penalties imposed by a government entity in its regulatory capacity because of a Regulatory Proceeding. The policy excludes criminal fines, disgorgement, the multiplied portion of multiplied damages, and amounts uninsurable by law.[4]

A Regulatory Proceeding means a suit, civil investigation, or civil proceeding by a government agency, licensing entity, or regulator based on an alleged or potential violation of Privacy or Cyber Laws resulting from a Cyber Incident. That means a HIPAA-related civil monetary penalty would need to arise from a qualifying regulatory proceeding tied to a Cyber Incident and be legally insurable under the governing law.[4][9]

Judgment — Treat a HIPAA civil money penalty as a separate coverage question from a PCI assessment. The former must satisfy the policy’s regulatory-proceeding and legal-insurability requirements; the latter must satisfy Payment Card Loss requirements under the payment agreement or bank-demand route. A single breach can trigger both paths, but neither path automatically pays the other category.

Chubb describes regulatory proceedings coverage as defense for regulator actions and coverage for fines and penalties where insurable by law. It separately describes Payment Card Loss as contractual liabilities owed to payment-card industry firms after a cyber incident, including card-reissuance costs.[9][33]

That separation matters because a PCI assessment is not necessarily a government fine. It can be a contract-based merchant liability. Chubb’s general contractual-liability exclusion has a stated exception for Payment Card Loss under Insuring Agreement E, preserving the possibility of covered payment-card losses even though the loss arises under a merchant agreement.[4][42][12]

Chubb’s materials also show why policy form and endorsement review matters. Its Cyber Crime endorsement excludes fees, costs, fines, penalties, and other expenses resulting directly or indirectly from access to or disclosure of confidential or personal information, specifically including credit-card and health information. The Payment Card Loss provisions and the Cyber Crime provisions apply to different insuring agreements, so you should not assume that a favorable payment-card definition overrides a restriction in another coverage part.[4][12]

The more useful question for a quote is: Which insuring agreement pays the loss, what is that agreement’s limit, and which endorsement modifies it? Chubb states that the precise coverage depends on the policy as issued, the facts of the loss, and applicable law.[13]

#### Chubb limits, sublimits, retentions, and erosion

A policy limit is the maximum the insurer will pay. A sublimit is a smaller maximum that applies to a particular coverage category, event type, or expense. A retention is the amount you pay before that coverage responds. These three items must be read together.

Chubb’s publicly available materials establish that it uses scheduled limits by coverage category and that some coverage extensions are part of, not additional to, the maximum policy limit. For a Non-Malicious Computer Related Act, one Chubb quotation sets a $1,000,000 each-Cyber-Incident limit, a $1,000,000 aggregate limit for all Cyber Incidents, a $25,000 retention for each Cyber Incident, and a 12-hour waiting period for combined business-interruption loss and extra expense; those limits are part of, not additional to, the applicable declarations limits and maximum single and aggregate policy limits.[12]

Chubb also offers modular coverage and says clients can choose insuring clauses and limits, including full limits on privacy-notification and crisis-management expenses where relevant. Its U.S. application asks applicants to request separate Cyber Expenses and Cyber Liability limits in $1 million, $2 million, $3 million, $5 million, or other amounts.[19][43]

That structure means the number advertised on a brochure is not necessarily the amount available for PCI assessments, regulatory defense, credit monitoring, or a particular category of business interruption. Chubb advertises an aggregate limit of up to $15 million, but its factsheet does not assign that amount to every coverage category.[19]

A useful example concerns response-vendor choice. One Chubb quotation listed a $1 million each-incident and aggregate Cyber Incident Response Team limit, while the Non-Panel Response Provider option was limited to $500,000 each incident and $500,000 aggregate. The quoted retentions were $0 for the panel-team option and $25,000 for the non-panel provider option.[12]

A separate Chubb declaration states that the insured is not required to use the Cyber Incident Response Team, but electing a Non-Panel Response Provider invokes the lower declaration limits. It listed a $500,000 each-incident and $500,000 aggregate limit with a $5,000 retention for that option.[18]

The practical Chubb rule is that vendor freedom can cost coverage capacity. You are not required to use the Cyber Incident Response Team. If you use a Non-Panel Response Provider, the declarations’ lower non-panel limits apply, the maximum rate for Cyber Incident Response Expenses is the rate in the Chubb Cyber Incident Response Team Panel Guidelines, and Chubb pays only expenses it considers reasonable and necessary.[12]

Chubb’s response expenses must be reasonable and necessary. The insurer can pay only expenses it deems reasonable and necessary, and the maximum rate payable is tied to its panel guidelines.[12]

Chubb also uses event-specific restrictions that can constrain payment. If a Widespread Event, Neglected Software Exploit, Ransomware Encounter, or Non-Malicious Computer Related Act could fit more than one insuring agreement or extension, the policy applies only the single lowest applicable limit, with the corresponding retention and coinsurance.[12]

For neglected software exploits, Chubb’s quotation used coinsurance that rises with the time a patch or mitigation was available but not applied: 0% through 90 days, 5% from 91–180 days, 10% from 181–365 days, and 25% after 365 days. The insured-paid coinsurance does not reduce the policy limits; the insurer-paid portion does.[12]

Chubb says weak patch-management practices can lead to a Neglected Software Exploit endorsement. It describes full coverage for the first 45 days after a published vulnerability, followed by progressively greater risk sharing at 45-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day milestones.[9][44]

Judgment — For a 15-person business, this makes patching a financial-control task, not merely an IT task. Keep an owner-approved patch inventory, document exceptions, and require your managed IT provider to report patch status. A breach involving an ignored known vulnerability can create both a claim problem and a coinsurance problem.

#### Chubb incident response and its SLA position

Chubb’s Cyber Incident Response Team has a broad roster of services relevant to both payment-card and health-record events. Its named capabilities include computer forensics, public relations, notification and identity services, call-center services, cyber extortion and ransom services, legal and regulatory communications, and business-interruption services.[4][12]

The response-expense definition is more specific. It includes pre-litigation legal services; forensic firms, including a PCI Forensic Investigator; PR and crisis communications; legal services to identify contractual indemnification rights; privacy-law analysis; notification drafting; regulatory communications; call-center services; and monitoring services.[12]

For a payment-card breach, the PCI Forensic Investigator provision matters. Chubb defines a Cyber Incident for the Cyber Incident Response Fund to include an actual or reasonably suspected failure to handle, manage, store, destroy, protect, use, or otherwise control Protected Information, and an unintentional violation of a Privacy or Cyber Law. Cyber Incident Response Expenses include retaining third-party forensic firms, including a Payment Card Industry (PCI) Forensic Investigator, to determine cause and scope and, if the Cyber Incident is active, assist in containing it.[4]

For a health-record breach, Chubb’s response-expense definition includes legal services to determine what Privacy or Cyber Laws require, legal work for notification and regulatory communication, legally required notification operations, and credit monitoring, freezing, or thawing.[12]

Where notification or monitoring is not required by law, Chubb requires prior insurer consent. With consent, it can include voluntary notification, credit monitoring, credit freezes and thaws, healthcare-record monitoring where available, social-media monitoring, password management, fraud alerts, and identity restoration for affected people who accept the offered services.[12]

Chubb’s U.S. cyber materials state that the incident-response team provides legal consultation and pre-litigation legal services, computer forensics, credit monitoring, fraud consultation, call-center assistance, and public relations.[22]

Chubb’s response service is available 24/7. Policyholders can call the Cyber Incident Response Coach Hotline at 800-817-2665 or use the Cyber Alert app for reporting and response support.[22][45][12]

Chubb’s urgent-response process has an important administrative distinction. Its Crisis Hotline routes urgent messages immediately to an Incident Response Coach, a pre-approved law firm, for initial triage. However, the hotline does not satisfy formal policy notice requirements, and engaging the hotline may be billable at the panel-guideline hourly rate.[23][4]

For Chubb, first call the response hotline to control the event, then give formal claim notice under the policy. Doing only the first can leave you with a notice dispute; doing only the second can delay triage.[23]

Chubb’s U.S. materials use terms such as immediate assistance, immediate triage, instant reporting, and 24/7 availability. They do not state a U.S. Texas-policy maximum time for callback, forensic deployment, or containment.[22][9][12]

Chubb has published more specific response statements outside the United States. Its Singapore material says an Incident Response Manager will contact the client within one hour and an app report will generate a consultant call within one minute. New Zealand material makes the same one-minute app and one-hour manager-contact statements.[26][46]

Those international platform commitments show that Chubb can operate a timed workflow, but they are not the SLA for your Austin policy. For your quote, ask the broker to obtain the U.S. incident-response terms in writing, including initial acknowledgment, breach-coach assignment, forensic engagement, and after-hours escalation.

#### Chubb credit monitoring and legal defense

Chubb includes credit monitoring in its Cyber Incident Response Fund. It lists legal fees, forensics, notification costs, credit monitoring, and public relations as fund components.[9][47]

Chubb also says its incident-response expenses are paid on behalf of the policyholder and include consumer solutions more robust than minimum legal requirements. It does not state a standard monitoring duration, per-person allowance, or separate monitoring sublimit in the cited U.S. product material.[9][44]

For you, that means Chubb can be suitable where a breach may involve both customer card data and employee health records, because the response language addresses credit monitoring and healthcare-record monitoring. The actual duration, population covered, consent requirements, and limit come from the issued declarations and response-fund wording.[12]

Chubb’s defense wording deserves special attention. Its policy gives Chubb the right and duty to defend a covered Claim, including one that is groundless, false, or fraudulent. Chubb retains the right to appoint counsel and direct the investigation and defense.[4]

Chubb can provide meaningful defense for covered privacy, network-security, and regulatory matters, but you should require the quote to identify whether regulatory defense is discretionary or a duty to defend, and whether defense costs are inside the limit.[4][48]

### Coalition

Coalition’s documented Cyber Policy 3.0 is unusually direct about separating PCI exposure from regulatory exposure. Its specimen schedule lists Regulatory Defense and Penalties and PCI Fines and Assessments as separate insuring agreements.[1][2]

Coalition announced its new U.S. surplus-lines Active Cyber Policy on April 9, 2025 and said that all U.S. non-admitted new-business and renewal quotes would use the new form beginning April 15, 2025. The policy is marketed for organizations with annual revenue up to $5 billion and limits up to $15 million.[10]

For regulatory matters, Coalition’s policy pays claim expenses and regulatory penalties the insured is legally obligated to pay as a result of a claim in the form of a Regulatory Proceeding.[1][2][37]

Coalition Cyber Policy 3.0 defines regulatory penalties as monetary fines and penalties, including consumer-redress awards, imposed in a Regulatory Proceeding to the extent insurable under applicable law. It excludes costs to comply with injunctive relief, establish or improve privacy or security practices, and perform audits, reporting, or compliance.[14]

That distinction is central to employee-health-record exposure. A HIPAA-related proceeding is not expressly named in the supplied Coalition wording. A HIPAA civil money penalty can potentially fit only if the proceeding falls within the policy’s definition, arises from the required security-failure, data-breach, or privacy-liability trigger, and the penalty is legally insurable.[1][37][14]

Coalition defines a Regulatory Proceeding as certain government information requests, civil investigative demands, or civil proceedings arising from a security failure or data breach. It also extends to GDPR, CCPA, or similar regulation claims arising from privacy liability.[1][37]

**For employee health records, Coalition provides a conditional regulatory-defense-and-penalty route rather than a named HIPAA penalty promise.** The policy definition, the facts of the breach, applicable law, and endorsements decide the outcome.[37][14]

For PCI exposure, Coalition’s policy is more specific. It says it will pay claim expenses and PCI fines and assessments the insured is legally obligated to pay after a claim for a data breach compromising payment-card data.[1][2]

Coalition defines PCI fines and assessments as direct monetary fines and assessments under a merchant-services agreement for fraud recovery; operational expenses, including card reissuance and cardholder notification; and case-management fees. The charges qualify only when they result from a data breach.[1][2]

Coalition excludes chargebacks, interchange fees, discount fees, and other service-related fees, rates, or charges from that definition.[1][2]

This is an important e-commerce distinction. Coalition can cover a defined breach-driven assessment, but it does not turn the full financial consequences of a card-processing dispute into covered PCI loss. Ordinary card economics, reversal fees, interchange fees, discount fees, and chargebacks remain outside the definition.[1][2]

Coalition’s contractual-liability exclusion has an express exception for PCI Fines and Assessments coverage. Therefore, the fact that a PCI assessment comes through a merchant agreement does not automatically defeat it; the claim still must satisfy the policy’s PCI definition and all other terms.[1][2]

Coalition also includes breach-response legal advice for PCI operating requirements and merchant-services-agreement obligations after a payment-card data breach. This can include a PCI Forensic Investigator if the merchant agreement requires one. It excludes legal proceeding, arbitration, or mediation fees for general PCI compliance advice, except for assessing PCI fines and assessments for a covered data breach or remediating breached systems.[1][2][14]

Coalition’s PCI coverage is most useful when you can show a defined card-data breach, a merchant-services-agreement assessment, and eligible cost categories. It is not a blanket reimbursement program for processor fees, compliance projects, or every payment-card dispute.[1][2]

#### Coalition limits, defense-cost erosion, and separate response capacity

Coalition’s policy makes the declarations schedule especially important. A Policy 3.0 specimen schedule listed a $1 million limit and $2,500 retention for Regulatory Defense and Penalties, and the same $1 million limit and $2,500 retention for PCI Fines and Assessments.[1]

Those numbers are not an offer to your business. They show how Coalition can separately schedule coverage categories. The specimen itself says coverage exists only for insuring agreements for which a limit appears in the schedule.[1]

Coalition’s policy says claim expenses reduce the applicable limits, are subject to retentions, and may exhaust the limits. Its application also states that legal defense and claims expenses reduce the amount available for judgments or settlements and apply against the retention.[14][64]

Coalition’s cited policy materials state that, when one incident triggers several coverage categories, the combined retentions do not exceed the largest applicable retention.[1][2][116][37][34][117]

That means a $1 million regulatory-defense limit should not be read as $1 million for a penalty plus unlimited legal fees. Attorney fees, investigation, defense, and appeal costs use the same applicable limit.[1][14]

Coalition markets Any One Claim Coverage as resetting the full policy limit for each separate incident during the policy period. The Active Cyber Policy wording separately states that the Aggregate Policy Limit is the maximum payable for all covered loss, each insuring-agreement limit and the Per Event Limit are within that aggregate, and only an elected Optional Separate Limit for Breach Response Costs is additional.[10][20]

Coalition also advertises a separate limit for breach response that preserves limits for other coverages. Its coverage checklist says a separate breach-response-cost limit is available on most policies, helping preserve limits for extortion and business interruption.[65][34]

The policy wording says breach-response services are in addition to and do not erode the Aggregate Policy Limit of Liability. That service benefit is separately limited to a maximum of 72 hours after notification.[66][1]

Coalition’s best structural advantage is the potential separation of initial breach-response capacity from liability-limit erosion. Its PCI, regulatory-penalty, and defense payments still require close review because claim expenses can erode the applicable liability limits.[65][66][14]

Coalition has a specific Wrongful Collection endorsement issue relevant to e-commerce. It says its Enhanced Privacy Liability endorsement provides dedicated sublimits for Wrongful Collection, but the cited material does not state the dollar amount or whether the limit is inside or additional to the main aggregate.[67]

Coalition says an Enhanced Privacy Liability Endorsement may remove biometric and pixel-tracking restrictions up to full policy limits, for additional premium and subject to underwriting and review of privacy controls.[118]

For an online seller using analytics, session replay, advertising pixels, chat tools, or similar tracking technologies, this is not a side issue. Coalition reports that 77% of wrongful-collection claims in its analysis originated from website tracking and 73% involved analytics technologies.[68]

Judgment — Ask Coalition to quote Wrongful Collection coverage expressly if your website uses analytics pixels, session-replay tools, advertising conversion tags, embedded video, live-chat software, or social-media plugins. PCI coverage does not solve a website privacy-collection lawsuit.

## Factors that drive premiums

For a 15-person Austin business with $2–5 million in revenue, cyber-insurance price is not set by revenue alone. The insurer is pricing the combination of your online sales operation, customer card-data exposure, employee health-record exposure, technical security posture, prior losses, and the coverage structure you select.

**The practical rule is to treat payment-card exposure and health-information exposure as two separate underwriting tracks, then compare like-for-like limits, deductibles, sublimits, and services before treating one premium as cheaper.**

### Exposure and business operations

Revenue and employee count matter because they are measures of business scale, but both Chubb and Coalition collect more than those headline figures. Chubb’s small-business Cyber ERM application requests prior- and current-fiscal-year global revenue, domestic-revenue percentage, online-revenue percentage, employee count, and the maximum number of unique people or organizations whose protected information could be compromised or stored or transmitted during the policy period. The protected-information count includes employees, retirees, customers, partners, and third parties for whom the applicant is responsible, including data held by contracted providers.[60] Coalition’s application similarly requests employee count and expected revenue for the next 12 months.[14]

Coalition expressly describes industry, revenue, and company size as actuarial rating inputs. It also says its underwriting considers the technology stack—operating systems, hardware, software, and internal controls—because cyber losses can spread across connected systems.[93] Chubb’s application further asks for the business’s class and plain-language description of operations, products, and services; its technology E&O/cyber application specifically lists Credit Card Processing, Healthcare, and Retail E-Commerce as revenue categories.[60][48]

For an e-commerce business, the percentage of revenue earned online is important because website availability, checkout systems, payment processors, customer accounts, and third-party ecommerce platforms create both interruption and privacy exposures. Chubb’s application expressly asks for online-revenue percentage, while its cyber-risk guide identifies retail exposure as including dependence on websites for online sales, critical IT service providers, and sensitive data created by frequent financial transactions.[60][33] Chubb also asks whether the applicant operates as a financial institution, payment processor, or data aggregator, broker, or warehouse, or derives more than 50% of revenue from technology products and services; an affirmative answer requires details.[60]

The scale of data at risk is a distinct driver from revenue. Coalition states that more sensitive information can increase pricing exposure because criminals may steal, resell, or use it as leverage in ransomware demands. Its guide names both personal health information (PHI) and credit-card data as sensitive data types that can be resold or held for ransom.[94] Cowbell reports that Healthcare is among the sectors consistently experiencing some of the highest rates of cyber claims and that industries handling sensitive information typically face higher-than-average ransom demands and longer recovery periods.[30]

**Your $2–5 million revenue and 15-person size are underwriting inputs, but the potentially decisive exposure measures are online-revenue share, annual card transactions, the number of people whose data is exposed, and the business’s dependence on digital operations.** This makes it possible to give each insurer a complete description of the actual loss scenario rather than allowing revenue alone to stand in for risk.[93][60][14]

#### PCI-DSS and HIPAA are separate disclosures

PCI-DSS exposure concerns payment-card handling: whether you collect, process, store, transmit, or can access card data; how many transactions you handle; which payment providers you use; and what card-security controls exist. HIPAA-related exposure concerns protected health information and the safeguards and compliance obligations surrounding it. They can occur in one company, but insurers do not necessarily assess them as one generic data-risk category.

Chubb’s U.S. small-business application asks separately whether the website, computer system, or telephone system captures payment-card information; if yes, it asks whether point-of-sale terminals accept chip-enabled cards and whether the applicant self-attested to PCI compliance during the past 12 months. It separately asks whether those systems request or capture medical records or personal health information and, if so, whether the applicant is compliant with HIPAA and the HITECH Act.[60] Chubb’s broader proposal form asks whether PCI DSS duties are outsourced, the estimated annual PCI transaction count, and the applicant’s or outsourcer’s PCI DSS compliance level: Level 1, Level 2, Level 3, Level 4, or Not Compliant.[55]

Chubb’s application also asks whether payment-card databases and point-of-sale data are encrypted or tokenized, whether card-validation codes are not stored, and whether card data is masked.[8][121][48][122]

Coalition’s application asks whether the applicant collects, processes, stores, transmits, or accesses payment-card information, PII, or PHI other than its own employees’ data. If the answer is yes, it asks for estimated annual card-transaction volume and the number of PII or PHI records.[14][64] That distinction matters to this business: employee health records may be PHI or otherwise highly sensitive employment information, while customer card data is separately tied to payment flows and PCI-DSS responsibilities.

Chubb measures the number of unique people or organizations whose protected information is at risk, not the count of separate records. If one customer has card details and another protected data type, that individual is counted once; the form nevertheless requires inclusion of employees, customers, partners, and responsible third parties.[60] This is the **unique-person count**: a useful number to prepare before requesting quotes.

Dual exposure raises different underwriting questions rather than automatically producing one combined surcharge: payment-card handling brings transaction-volume, processor, terminal, and PCI-attestation questions, while health information brings record-count and HIPAA/HITECH-compliance questions. Accurate separate disclosure lets you test whether each carrier’s quote actually reflects the risks you need insured.[60][14][55]

Chubb’s Protected Information Worksheet makes the e-commerce exposure more concrete. It asks for the maximum foreseeable protected-information exposure, including anticipated growth or expansion; counts each person or business once even if several data types are held; and includes information of employees, retirees, customers, partners, and third parties for whom the applicant is responsible. For electronic sales records, Chubb asks for the B2B/B2C revenue split, average sale size, estimated annual sales transactions, percentage recorded electronically, repeat-customer frequency, years of historical electronic sales records retained or accessible, and customer-retention period.[42]

Coalition’s 2025 Cyber Claims Report identifies industry as shaping both claim frequency and severity and states that businesses handling sensitive financial data or personal health information are often targeted because of the value of that data. This directly describes the combined card-data and health-information profile of this business.[95]

Payment-processing architecture is quote-relevant. A validated point-to-point encryption (P2PE) solution removes the merchant’s systems from PCI scope for those transactions, reducing direct card-data exposure, compliance burden, and risk profile; underwriters may offer premium credits. Outsourcing PCI DSS duties does not remove the need to disclose the annual transaction count and the applicant’s or outsourcer’s PCI DSS compliance level, and a third-party provider does not necessarily remove the original customer-data exposure. Neither arrangement removes the separate health-record exposure.[96][55][97]

Chubb’s proposal form asks about written contracts with payment processors, how incident-response responsibility is assigned, and waivers of recourse.[43]

For a business handling both health and payment-card data, expanding beyond one state adds regulatory complexity to an already regulated-data exposure: PHI and payment-card data each increase exposure, notification-cost laws differ by state, and businesses operating in multiple states or internationally face higher regulatory complexity.[91]

Your 15 employees are an underwriting input, but their account access and security controls matter alongside headcount. Chubb and Coalition collect employee-count information; Coalition identifies exposed employee credentials without MFA as a risk and asks about MFA, encryption, backups, and secondary verification for funds transfers and payroll or other banking-detail changes.[60][14][94]

Coalition’s 2024 claims data shows why health-record exposure can affect underwriting severity: among its policyholders, healthcare claim frequency was 1.38%, 29.0% above the 1.07% reported for businesses under $25 million in revenue, and average healthcare claim severity was $145,000, 83.5% above the $79,000 reported for that under-$25-million group.[95]

In Coalition’s 2025 claims data, ransomware’s $269,000 average loss was 131.9% higher than Coalition’s $116,000 overall average loss. For an e-commerce business holding card and health-related data, this supports buying limits and business-interruption terms based on a serious ransomware scenario rather than a routine low-cost incident.[98]

Payment-card data flow changes the risk even at the same transaction volume: tell underwriters whether checkout is hosted or tokenized, or whether your systems store or process full card numbers, expiration dates, or sensitive authentication data. A hosted, tokenized checkout generally reduces the systems in direct card-data scope, but does not eliminate e-commerce cyber risk.

Employee health information does not automatically make an employer a HIPAA covered entity or business associate; that depends on the business’s role, including any group-health-plan arrangement. Even where HIPAA does not directly apply, benefits, leave, and HR health information can still create breach-response and legal exposure.

For a 15-person business, underwriting risk reflects the number of user accounts, phishing exposure, access controls, and payroll or personnel-data handling rather than headcount alone. Revenue, employee count, industry, data types, MFA status, backup process, endpoint protection, claims history, payment controls, vendor dependency, and desired limits are information gathered before comparing markets.[99]

Remote administration, personal or unmanaged devices, shared accounts, and inadequate training can expand attack paths in a small workforce; underwriting information can include device management, privileged accounts, offboarding, and contractor access.[104]

In a 15-person company, one employee may handle customer service, refunds, vendor payments, payroll, benefits, and website administration, concentrating access and payment-approval responsibilities in a single role.[104][39]

### Controls and coverage choices

Security controls affect the insurer’s view of both the likelihood and severity of a claim. Coalition’s application asks about encryption on laptops, desktops, and portable media; at least weekly offline or network-separated backups; MFA for email, VPN, remote access, and privileged accounts; and secondary verification for wire, ACH, and banking-detail change requests.[64] Its guidance says outdated or vulnerable technology, exposed employee credentials, and missing MFA can make organizations more attractive targets.[94]

Chubb’s application asks whether the business has third-party network protections such as antivirus, encryption, and firewalls; established incident-response plans for breach and business interruption; and no end-of-life hardware or software without required security updates.[60] Its proposal materials seek information on network segmentation; at least annual external penetration testing; web application firewalls for critical external-facing applications; how remote access is secured, including MFA; privileged-account controls; email security; ransomware-safe backups; endpoint detection and response; security monitoring; and patch management.[55][43]

For this e-commerce operation, payment controls deserve the same attention as technical controls. Coalition asks whether a company validates funds-transfer requests over $5,000 and $25,000 through a secondary communication method, and whether it verifies requests to change banking details.[64] Chubb asks retail applicants whether payment-processing networks are segregated from other IT networks, whether POS systems are protected by antimalware and monitoring, what percentage of card terminals meet EMV chip standards, and how POS software is patched.[55]

Prior incidents and known circumstances can affect both eligibility and price. Coalition asks whether, during the previous three years, the business experienced a cyber incident, claim, or loss; privacy or security claims; government action, investigation, or subpoena concerning an alleged privacy-law violation; an extortion demand; or any single claim amount or cyber-incident loss greater than $25,000.[64] Chubb asks about actual or potential cyber-related incidents or claims during the previous three years and known circumstances that could lead to a claim.[60] Coalition states that premiums may become more expensive at renewal following a cyber attack in the prior year.[94]

Known circumstances to disclose before binding include payment-page compromise, suspicious card activity, unresolved vulnerabilities, regulator correspondence, and known vendor incidents; a known-circumstance exclusion may otherwise restrict a later claim.[1][31][4]

Coverage design is also a price lever. Chubb states that premiums scale according to coverage scope and limits, with no minimum premium.[9] Coalition says its underwriting process determines quoted premium rates and minimum self-insured retentions based on selected coverages.[93] A self-insured retention is the amount your business pays before the insurer’s covered payment obligation begins; it is economically similar to a deductible, though the policy wording determines the legal mechanics.

**Controls and loss experience are the practical levers you can influence before quoting: document MFA, encryption, patching, backups, payment verification, incident response, and prior-loss remediation, then use them to seek better terms as well as a lower price.** A control may improve retention, scope, eligibility, or premium; those are different benefits and should not be conflated.[64][60][34]

Budget the insurance premium, the retention, and continuing security and response costs as separate items. Coalition states that a policyholder using Coalition Incident Response (CIR) from its available provider panel has a $0 retention; that is a conditional claim-cost term, not a statement that the policy premium or all response options cost nothing.[3][34] Chubb states that its Crisis Hotline provides initial urgent-event triage but does not meet the policy’s claim-notification requirement, so the business must also complete policy claim notification.[23] Chubb may make third-party loss-mitigation services available at no additional expense, but a business that elects to retain listed third-party vendors for cyber-risk-management inspections, assessments, or audits does so at its own expense.[12] Continue to fund MFA, encryption, offline or separate-network backups, payment-change verification, patching, and an incident-response plan; Coalition and Chubb collect these controls in underwriting applications.[64][60] Cowbell identifies Cowbell Resiliency Services (CRS) as comprehensive subscription-based support to enhance cybersecurity maturity, including Managed Detection and Response (MDR), penetration testing, training, and more.

It identifies Micro Pen Testing as quick, focused assessments identifying vulnerabilities; the Cowbell Rx Cybersecurity Marketplace as curated cybersecurity solutions from trusted partners; and an Incident Response Plan Template as a clear, actionable framework for efficient incident management.[30]

Cowbell’s 2025 underwriting presentation lists service industry, claims history, third-party vendors or supply chain, revenue, record count, year established, public web presence, coverage selection, security controls, and compliance as quote factors. For an online Austin seller, public web presence, ecommerce and payroll/cloud vendors, the count of affected people, and PCI-DSS and HIPAA-related compliance evidence belong in the submission alongside revenue.[106]

Cowbell’s 2025 presentation identifies MFA, employee training, patching cadence, and backup hygiene as key controls. For the health-record portion of the risk, prepare a current security risk analysis and remediation plan; written security, privacy, access-control, and incident-response policies; MFA evidence; asset inventory and vulnerability and patch management; secure backups with restoration testing; security-awareness and phishing-training records; endpoint protection, logging, monitoring, and detection processes; business associate agreements and third-party security oversight; an incident-response plan with assigned roles, escalation procedures, and testing records; and executive or board-level cybersecurity governance.[106][107]

For medical practices, Meriplex’s 2026 underwriting checklist identifies phishing-resistant MFA across systems touching PHI; EDR or XDR on every workstation, laptop, and server with alerts routed to a 24/7 security operations center; encrypted backups isolated from production and restore-tested on a documented schedule; and a written incident-response plan refreshed within 12 months, including a tabletop exercise and after-action report.[92]

For payment-related operations, BestCyber identifies payment controls as underwriting information and says to check social-engineering sublimits, callback requirements, voluntary-parting wording, funds-transfer coverage, and whether a crime policy should coordinate with cyber coverage.[99]

Pro Insurance Group’s 2026 rating-factor list adds vendor and supply-chain dependency, number of records, geography and regulatory exposure, and the size of the IT/security team to the factors already identified here. It says revenue is a proxy for transaction volume and breach exposure; dependence on SaaS providers, MSPs, and payment processors can create contingent business-interruption exposure that some insurers price separately; notification-cost laws differ by state; operating in multiple states or internationally raises regulatory complexity from GDPR, CCPA, and state privacy laws; and insurers consider whether security is handled by a dedicated function or entirely outsourced, with larger internal teams often qualifying for credits.[91]

Pro Insurance Group says each additional $1 million of limit adds 30–60% to premium, and moving from a $5,000 to a $25,000 deductible can reduce premium 15–25%; it lists typical cyber deductibles of $2,500–$25,000. It also says a single prior cyber claim increases premium 25–50% for 3–5 years and that multiple claims may cause some insurers to decline coverage.[91]

Pro Insurance Group gives two different general control-credit ranges: it says MFA on email and remote access, EDR, regular phishing training, off-site or immutable backups, and a written incident-response plan can collectively earn 25–30% in carrier credits; elsewhere it says documented versions of those controls can earn 10–25% credits.[91]

A validated point-to-point encryption (P2PE) solution removes a merchant’s systems from PCI scope for those transactions, reducing compliance burden and risk profile; underwriters may offer premium credits to merchants using validated P2PE.[96]

Coalition states that discovered vulnerabilities do not affect coverage, while resolving them may reduce premium.[15]

Coalition identifies broader market pricing pressures as increasing demands in coverage, growing sophistication of cyber threats and attack methods, and increasing costs associated with cyber-incident remediation.[94]

At renewal, control improvement preserves rate, while a weakened control, skipped tabletop exercise, or a new AI vendor without governance drives rate increases and tighter sublimits.[108]

Underwriters increasingly require evidence of controls before binding, including configuration exports, endpoint-agent counts, and backup-restoration logs. An insurer questionnaire is not itself a security risk analysis; each response should identify the governing internal policy, evidence that the control is implemented, the accountable person, the last review or test date, known exceptions, and the corrective-action plan. Together, these requirements mean questionnaire answers are a starting point: underwriting evidence shows that controls are implemented, tested, and assigned to an accountable owner.[92][107]

Together, Cowbell Factors assess security and compliance characteristics, including network security, cloud, compliance, and other security best practices, and use external data, firmographics, claims data, and dark web intelligence to rate an organization’s cyber risk continuously.[109][16]

Because underwriters price coverage from claims data about controls that were present or missing and revise underwriting requirements each renewal cycle, control improvements can preserve rates, while a weakened control, skipped tabletop, or new AI vendor without governance can drive rate increases and tighter sublimits at renewal.[92][108]

Coalition reported that its 2025 global claims frequency rose 3% while average severity fell 19%, producing a derived frequency-severity loss index decrease of 16.6% from 2024. This is a market-portfolio trend, not a prediction of the premium for this business.[98]

For the e-commerce operation, include the security of public web applications and APIs, the payment gateway, and remote-access users in the control evidence you provide. These can be external entry points beyond the systems that directly handle card data.

Open remote access, missing MFA, weak backups, prior incidents, high record volume, unsupported software, and unusual industry exposure can change eligibility.[99]

### Comparable quotes and remaining uncertainty

Prepare the data counts in the way each application asks for them before requesting quotes. Coalition’s payment-card, PII, and PHI question is limited to data other than its own employees’ information, while Chubb’s protected-information population includes employees, retirees, customers, partners, and responsible third parties, including data held by contracted providers; Chubb counts each person or organization once even if several data types are involved.[64][60] Record whether the business is acting as an employer, group health plan, HIPAA covered entity, or business associate before describing employee health records as HIPAA exposure; HIPAA’s Security Rule addresses covered entities and business associates.[107] Keep the card-processing arrangement, payment processor or other outsourcer, annual transaction volume, PCI-DSS validation status, and merchant-agreement obligations as a separate PCI disclosure. PCI compliance applies to every business that accepts, processes, or transmits card payments.[8][60][55]

Request three matched quotes: the same overall limit, retention or deductible, policy period, retroactive-date treatment where applicable, business-interruption option, and regulatory/privacy liability scope. Then compare the internal sublimits and coverage triggers described in the preceding comparison section, because a lower premium may reflect less available protection rather than better pricing.

Judgment — Prepare a one-page underwriting schedule that starts with two separate populations: Coalition’s nonemployee payment-card, PII, and PHI population and Chubb’s broader unique-person protected-information population, which includes the 15 employees and counts a person once across data types.[64][60] State the business’s actual employer, group-health-plan, HIPAA covered-entity, or business-associate role before listing HIPAA/HITECH compliance.[107] Separately state $2–5 million expected revenue; 15 employees; percentage of revenue from online sales; card-processing architecture; annual transaction volume; whether card data is stored; payment processor, outsourced PCI-DSS duties, PCI-DSS validation status, and merchant-agreement obligations; cloud, payroll, and ecommerce vendors; controls; and all cyber events or claims.[60][55][14][64] Give every insurer the identical schedule, then answer its application questions fully.

Coalition makes its data-oriented approach explicit: it evaluates externally facing exposures and current and emerging threats, using scans to identify exploitable targets that inform underwriting.[110][93] Its application also gathers information about encryption, backups, MFA, funds-transfer verification, payment-card activity, PII/PHI records, employee count, revenue, claims, and known claim circumstances.[14] Chubb likewise says underwriters rely on application statements, which are collected for underwriting purposes.[60]

Chubb’s documentation makes clear that online activity and protected-information population are part of the quote conversation; Chubb separately asks about captured card data, recent PCI self-attestation, captured medical or health information, and HIPAA/HITECH compliance.[60] Cowbell’s stated approach is individualized policies matched to each business’s unique risk profile, supported by continuous risk ratings, risk-remediation advice, and Cowbell Factors that identify and quantify risks through inside-out and outside-in data and anchor underwriting.[39][104] Cowbell also states that actual scope, limits, exclusions, premiums, and other coverage details are governed by the issued insurance agreement or policy.[78]

Austin location by itself is not identified in the carrier materials as a stated rating factor or a published premium adjustment. Texas matters operationally because your broker must place coverage available in Texas and your business must comply with applicable Texas breach-notification and privacy requirements, but the quoted materials do not supply a Texas or Austin rate table. The correct comparison is therefore carrier quote against carrier quote, with identical exposure information and requested coverage.

Judgment — Do not use a specimen policy premium as a benchmark. A premium from another insured reflects that business’s revenue, controls, industry, limit, retention, data profile, claims history, endorsements, and underwriting date. It cannot answer what your dual PCI/HIPAA exposure should cost.

**A comparable-quote process rules out the false economy of choosing a low premium that achieved its price by reducing PCI-related protection, privacy-regulatory protection, response services, business-interruption coverage, or the sublimits that would matter after a breach.** The decision should be based on net retained risk—premium plus retention plus uninsured exposure—not premium alone.[9][93][78]

For the ecommerce website itself, Coalition states that every quote includes Privacy Risk insights in its free Cyber Risk Assessment. The assessment reviews a privacy-risk score by domain, tracking technologies and third-party integrations, missing or outdated privacy-policy disclosures, and consent mechanisms. Include the website domains, tracking tools, customer-data collection practices, privacy notices, consent tools, and external ecommerce integrations in the common underwriting schedule.[111]

Coalition states that U.S. businesses purchasing Coalition Managed Detection & Response (MDR), either standalone or in a package that includes it, may qualify for up to a 12.5% premium credit.[112] Eligibility is determined at quote or renewal from the risk profile and underwriting qualifications, and the carrier also states that customers using another MDR product may be eligible for an MDR premium credit. Ask for the credit and its eligibility decision in writing in each Coalition quote.[112]

Cowbell’s January 22, 2020 Prime 100 announcement stated that premiums started at $1,100 for a $1 million aggregate limit and $1,000 deductible across 200,000 businesses in 15 states; Cowbell Prime 100 was described as serving businesses up to $100 million in revenue, with limits up to $5 million and deductibles as low as $1,000. Chubb states that premiums scale with coverage scope and limits, and Coalition states that the underwriting insurer makes premium decisions under its then-current criteria.[109][113][114][100]

Pro Insurance Group’s 2026 tables list two overlapping annual-premium benchmarks for $1 million/$1 million coverage: $2,000–$4,500 for businesses with $1–5 million revenue and $2,400–$5,000 for mid-size businesses with $1–10 million revenue. Its separate industry tables list $3,000–$20,000+ annually for healthcare and medical practices and $2,500–$10,000 for retail and e-commerce operations.[91]

Pro Insurance Group identifies regulated data as a central pricing exposure: HIPAA and PCI regulated-data exposure affects industry pricing; PII, PHI, and payment-card data each increase exposure; healthcare pricing reflects PHI, HHS Office for Civil Rights penalties, and historically higher claim frequency; and e-commerce pricing reflects PCI-DSS payment-card processing exposure.[91]

Pro Insurance Group says coverage features and sublimits—including social-engineering and funds-transfer-fraud sublimits, ransomware coverage, regulatory-defense limits, and business-interruption waiting periods—affect price. It states that the same business with the same coverage can receive carrier quotes varying by 30–50%.[91]

Some carriers cover all PCI DSS fines and assessments, including restoration costs, for a substantial additional premium; not all insurers offer that coverage, and it may be unaffordable for many small and midsize businesses.[17]

For each matched quote, list the actual PCI-assessment sublimit, regulatory-proceeding and defense terms, treatment of health-related data, business-interruption waiting period, and e-commerce exclusions. A lower price is not a better result if one of those terms removes protection for the loss scenario most likely to matter.

A Texas Attorney General investigation can create substantial legal fees even when no fine is imposed, so confirm that the quote covers regulatory proceedings.[96]

Whether fees for shadow defense counsel or a court-appointed monitor are covered depends on the policy form’s definitions of defense costs and regulatory proceeding.[96]

Revenue, employee count, industry, online-sales share, records at risk, technology, controls, claims history, and selected coverage terms all shape the underwriting picture for this Austin business. Chubb and Coalition plainly collect revenue and employee data, while Coalition identifies revenue, company size, and industry as actuarial inputs.[60][14][93] The more important distinction for dual exposure is operational: PCI-DSS card-data handling is evaluated through transaction, processing, and PCI-compliance questions, while HIPAA-related exposure is evaluated through health-record and compliance questions.[60][14][55]

The carrier-specific pricing levers are not identical. Chubb expressly ties premium scale to scope and limits; Coalition offers an eligible MDR credit of up to 12.5%; and Cowbell connects risk remediation with premium optimization.[9][34][103] The strongest direction is therefore to improve and document controls, disclose both data exposures separately, and compare matched coverage structures rather than seeking a generic cyber-insurance price.

1. Prepare the identical underwriting schedule for Chubb, Coalition, and Cowbell before requesting quotes.
2. State PCI-DSS card volume and HIPAA-related health-record exposure separately, including whether vendors process either data type.
3. Document MFA, encryption, offline backups, patching, endpoint protection, payment-change verification, and an incident-response plan.
4. Request the same limit, retention, and coverage options from each carrier.
5. Ask each insurer to identify the premium effect, if any, of MDR, risk remediation, PCI-related exposure, and HIPAA-related exposure in its actual quote.

**Use the two different data-population counts throughout the applications.** Coalition’s payment-card, PII, and PHI question excludes information about the applicant’s own employees, whereas Chubb’s protected-information count includes employees, retirees, customers, partners, and responsible third parties, including information held by contracted providers. Chubb counts one person or organization once even if that person has multiple records or information types. For this business, keep a Coalition count for customer and other nonemployee card-data/PII/PHI exposure and a separate Chubb unique-person count that adds the 15 employees and other included people.[64][60] Record the actual employer, group-health-plan, HIPAA covered-entity, or business-associate role with the health-record disclosure, and keep payment processing, PCI-DSS outsourcing and compliance level, transaction volume, and merchant-agreement obligations in the separate card-data disclosure.[107][8][55]

Meriplex’s 2026 medical-practice guidance states, “HIPAA compliance and cyber insurance underwriting measure two different things, using two different standards of proof.” It says HIPAA documents a process for managing PHI risk, while cyber underwriting evaluates whether the technical controls are active and effective against severe attacks. Meriplex also says underwriters price from claims data about controls that were present or missing and revise underwriting requirements at each renewal cycle.[92]

Pro Insurance Group identifies regulated-data exposure, including HIPAA and PCI, as the single biggest pricing factor by industry. It states that PII, PHI, and payment-card data each increase exposure.[91]

A report states that dual PCI/HIPAA exposure leads to premium uplifts of 25%–50% compared with plain risks, and that Cowbell and Coalition may discount base rates 5–12% for demonstrable strong controls.[115]

Holding both customer card data and employee health data can broaden a breach claim beyond a business holding only one type. Coalition identifies credit-card data and PHI as sensitive data that criminals may resell or use for ransom, says sensitive financial data and health information are targeted for their value, and identifies both PHI and payment-card data as exposures that affect regulated-data risk. A breach can therefore require a broader combination of response and liability work.[94][95][91]

The issued Chubb, Coalition, and Cowbell quotations must settle the exact PCI-assessment and regulatory-penalty limits, whether those limits are inside the aggregate, defense-cost erosion, retentions, coinsurance, applicable endorsements, and Texas-specific insurability. They must also settle Chubb’s and Cowbell’s contractual response-time commitment, Chubb’s regulatory-defense duty, monitoring duration and limits, and the full e-commerce exclusions. Your merchant-services agreement and the company’s actual HIPAA status must establish which contractual PCI assessments and health-record regulatory exposures exist in the first place.
