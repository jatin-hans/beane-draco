# DiamondRock Financing Costs, Hedging and Cash Flow

## Executive summary

On balance, DiamondRock’s financing transformation effectively improves capital-allocation flexibility while retaining a meaningful, but incomplete, hedge against floating SOFR exposure. Replacing four property-specific fixed-rate mortgages with $1.1 billion of unsecured term loans left the portfolio fully unencumbered, extended contractual maturities through 2028–2030, and preserved a $400 million undrawn revolver, although it also moved more debt into floating-rate corporate funding.[1] The aggregate fair value of the interest-rate swaps fell by $0.394 million, from a $4.321 million asset at December 31, 2023 to a $3.927 million asset at June 30, 2024, but this noncash valuation movement is not itself an interest-cost saving or increase.[2][3] The swaps mitigated only part of the variable-rate exposure, covering $325 million of the $800 million variable-rate term loans at June 30, 2024, or about 40.6%, so quarterly interest-expense changes must be read together with debt balances, SOFR, swap settlements, and refinancing timing rather than attributed to hedging alone.[4] The strategy is therefore a qualified success: operating cash generation and available liquidity supported scheduled mortgage repayments and refinancing, but the company retained residual rate risk and had to fund capital priorities alongside—not instead of—debt repayment.

## Debt structure and interest-rate hedges

DiamondRock’s financing transformation matters because it changes both **where its debt sits** and **how its interest rate behaves**. Mortgage debt is secured by named hotels, whereas unsecured term loans are corporate borrowings supported by the company’s broader balance sheet and covenant package rather than a lien on one property. Interest-rate swaps then modify part of the floating-rate term-loan exposure by exchanging floating SOFR receipts for fixed payments.

The resulting structure offered more freedom to sell, renovate, acquire, or otherwise allocate capital across the hotel portfolio. It also substituted fixed-rate mortgages for mostly floating-rate corporate debt, so the gain in collateral and maturity flexibility had to be accompanied by selective hedging rather than being mistaken for a reduction in interest-rate risk.

### Identify DiamondRock's relevant mortgage debt.

DiamondRock’s relevant mortgage debt in the 2023–2025 transition was **limited-recourse, property-specific debt**. Limited recourse means the lender generally may foreclose on the pledged hotel after a default rather than having unrestricted recourse to the whole company, subject to customary exceptions such as fraud or misuse of funds. Property-specific means each loan is secured by a particular hotel, creating separate collateral, operating-covenant, and cash-management constraints for that asset.[3][5][6]

At December 31, 2023, DiamondRock described its outstanding debt as a combination of unsecured term loans and fixed-rate, property-specific mortgage debt. Four of its 36 hotels were secured by mortgage debt, leaving 32 hotels unencumbered.[3] The company’s preference was to leave a significant part of the portfolio unencumbered because hotels without mortgage liens are more readily available for future financing, asset sales, acquisitions, or other balance-sheet uses.[3]

At December 31, 2023, DiamondRock had **$378.189 million of total mortgage principal outstanding**, separately from its **$800.0 million of unsecured term loans**. The mortgage principal comprised the Courtyard New York Manhattan/Midtown East, Worthington Renaissance Fort Worth Hotel, Hotel Clio, and Westin Boston Seaport District mortgage loans.[7][3]

At June 30, 2024, the four loans aggregated $373.3 million of property-specific, non-recourse mortgage debt. At the same date, DiamondRock had $800.0 million of unsecured term loans, so the company already had a corporate unsecured borrowing base larger than its mortgage balance.[10] The mortgage maturity schedule was, however, concentrated: one loan came due in August 2024, followed by three loans due from May through November 2025.[4]

DiamondRock explicitly planned to repay the August 2024 Courtyard mortgage, which had a $73.4 million principal balance at June 30, 2024, from cash on hand.[4] It planned to refinance the 2025 maturities as they approached, while retaining its senior unsecured revolving credit facility as a fallback if refinancing terms were unattractive.[4] This was a staged plan rather than a single transaction: first use existing liquidity to eliminate the nearest maturity, second seek a corporate refinancing for the remaining maturities, and third retain corporate revolver capacity if market access became less attractive.

The company executed that sequence. It paid the Courtyard loan in August 2024 with cash on hand, reducing the mortgage count from four to three.[7] At December 31, 2024, the remaining three mortgages were Worthington at $71.766 million, Hotel Clio at $54.657 million, and Westin Boston at $169.385 million, for total mortgage debt of $295.808 million.[11] Those balances were all due in 2025, creating a clear 2025 refinancing requirement even though the mortgages’ fixed rates—3.66%, 4.33%, and 4.36%—were below the then-current cost of much floating-rate corporate borrowing.[7][11][8]

At March 31, 2025, the three remaining property-specific mortgages had principal balances of $71.254 million for Worthington, $54.279 million for Hotel Clio, and $168.161 million for Westin Boston. Each was secured by the corresponding hotel.[12] The modest balance reductions from December reflected scheduled amortization and do not change the central financing issue: the loans all carried near-term balloon maturities, meaning that most principal became due at maturity rather than being amortized gradually. **The mortgage portfolio therefore presented a concentrated 2024–2025 maturity wall despite its relatively low fixed coupons.**[7][12]

At March 31, 2025, DiamondRock had approximately $1.1 billion of total debt, a 5.08% weighted-average interest rate and approximately 1.3 years of weighted-average debt maturity; 33 of its 36 hotels were unencumbered.[12]

The company’s actual repayment funding also shows that “mortgage repayment” did not mean one funding source. Worthington was repaid with cash on hand on May 6, 2025; Hotel Clio was repaid at maturity on July 3, 2025 with a $60.0 million revolver draw made July 2 and repaid July 22; and Westin Boston was repaid on September 5, 2025 with Amended Credit Facility proceeds.[1] DiamondRock repaid $295.808 million of mortgage principal during the nine months ended September 30, 2025.[13]  The individual repayments thus combined internal liquidity, temporary revolver liquidity, and long-term unsecured financing.

At June 30, 2025—before the final two mortgage repayments—DiamondRock reported total debt of $1.0 billion, comprising $800.0 million of unsecured term loans and two property-specific mortgages totaling $220.9 million.[14] That two-mortgage figure reflects the prior May repayment of Worthington; Hotel Clio and Westin Boston remained in the capital structure at that reporting date. Hotel Clio had a $53.910 million balance and Westin Boston had a $166.966 million balance in the June 30 debt table.[15]

At June 30, 2025, DiamondRock reported $1.021 billion of principal debt, with a 5.17% weighted-average interest rate including swaps; unsecured term loans represented 78.4% of principal debt.[29]

The apparent difference between the June 2024 liquidity narrative—which identified one $73.4 million mortgage maturing August 6, 2024, to be repaid with cash on hand, plus two additional mortgages and the $300 million term loan maturing within the next 12 months—and the four-loan schedule is not a contradiction.[6] The debt schedule named four mortgages, including the Westin Boston loan due in November 2025; the liquidity narrative focused on the August 2024 mortgage and the two additional mortgages maturing in the next 12 months. The Westin’s later November 2025 contractual maturity fell outside that stated 12-month window.[4][6]

Mortgage debt also affected flexibility through covenants, not merely collateral. DiamondRock’s mortgage agreements included property-specific debt-service-coverage-ratio or debt-yield tests, restrictions on additional borrowing without lender consent, and potential “cash traps.” A cash trap requires excess cash generated by the hotel to be deposited into lender-benefit accounts until performance recovers to the specified threshold.[6] At June 30, 2024, DiamondRock had no cash traps in place, so these provisions were not then restricting cash distributions from its mortgaged hotels.[4]

The absence of active cash traps did not make the restrictions irrelevant. The structure still placed particular hotel cash flows, collateral, and borrowing options under property-level loan documents. Eliminating the mortgages removed those property-specific constraints from the affected hotels and ended the risk that a deterioration in a hotel’s operating performance could divert excess cash to mortgage lenders.[6][1]

By September 5, 2025, after Westin Boston was prepaid, DiamondRock reported that its portfolio was fully unencumbered by secured debt.[1] This was the culmination of a multi-date transition: Courtyard in August 2024, Worthington in May 2025, Hotel Clio in July 2025, and Westin Boston in September 2025. The company subsequently stated that it had no debt maturities until January 2028 and that all debt was fully prepayable without penalty.[16]

**The relevant mortgage debt was fixed-rate, hotel-specific secured borrowing with maturities concentrated from August 2024 to November 2025; removing it replaced a near-term property-level maturity problem with a corporate unsecured financing structure and an unencumbered portfolio.** That outcome matters because it makes individual hotels more deployable assets, although it also means the company no longer receives the same fixed-rate protection from the mortgages themselves.[4][1]

Measurement frame. The observation periods are December 31, 2023 and June 30, 2024 for swap fair values; Q2 2023 and Q2 2024, and Q3 2024 and Q3 2025, for reported interest expense; and the nine months ended September 30, 2025 for cash flow. DiamondRock reported the December 2023 and June 2024 derivative values in its June 30, 2024 Form 10-Q, and reported Q3 2025 interest expense and nine-month 2025 cash-flow inputs in its Form 10-Q filed November 7, 2025.[4][11]

A swap’s **fair value** is its balance-sheet value at the reporting date; it is distinct from the period’s reported interest expense. Reported total interest expense is the income-statement total, including unsecured-term-loan interest, mortgage-debt interest, credit-facility interest and unused fees, amortization of debt issuance costs, and finance-lease expense.[4][11]

For this analysis, **free cash flow** means net cash provided by operating activities less capital expenditures. For the nine months ended September 30, 2025, free cash flow was **$114.789 million**, derived as $175.730 million of operating cash flow less $60.941 million of capital expenditures.[11]

DiamondRock stated when the July 22, 2025 amended facility closed that its $300.0 million upsizing fully funded repayment of the three mortgage loans that matured or would mature in 2025. The Worthington Renaissance Fort Worth Hotel and Hotel Clio loans, with approximately $125.0 million of combined principal, had already been repaid at their respective May and July 2025 maturities before that closing; the facility therefore principally provided the permanent corporate funding capacity for the remaining Westin Boston repayment and the completed mortgage-refinancing programme.[17][13]

DiamondRock’s 2025 full-year results also described the incremental proceeds of the Amended Credit Facility as having been used to repay the $71.1 million Worthington Renaissance mortgage in May 2025, the $53.9 million Hotel Clio mortgage in July 2025, and the $166.2 million Westin Boston mortgage in September 2025.[18]

DiamondRock’s fourth-quarter 2025 release groups the three 2025 repayments as uses of incremental Amended Credit Facility proceeds: $71.1 million for Worthington Renaissance in May, $53.9 million for Hotel Clio in July, and $166.2 million for Westin Boston Seaport District in September.[19] The payment-date sequence above identifies the immediate sources used at each payoff—cash on hand for Worthington, a temporary revolver draw for Hotel Clio, and amended-facility proceeds for Westin—while the later release describes the refinancing’s aggregate use of incremental facility capacity.[1][19]

At December 31, 2024, the remaining fixed-rate property-specific loans were Worthington Renaissance Fort Worth Hotel, $71.766 million, due May 2025; Hotel Clio, $54.657 million, due July 2025; and Westin Boston Seaport District, $169.385 million, due November 2025. They totaled $295.808 million, and total debt carried a 5.21% weighted-average interest rate.[9] This confirms that the fixed-rate mortgages remained a meaningful component of the debt-cost mix immediately before the 2025 unsecured refinancing.[9]

For certain mortgage agreements, DiamondRock generally must obtain lender consent to assign or transfer its rights and obligations, pay a fee equal to a fixed percentage of the outstanding loan balance, and pay the lender’s related costs. These provisions may limit hotel sales and adversely affect the price realized.[7]

DiamondRock’s credit agreement required property, business-interruption, flood, earthquake where applicable, and other customary hotel-related insurance.[1]

### Identify DiamondRock's relevant unsecured term loans.

An **unsecured term loan** is a corporate borrowing with a stated maturity and no lien on a particular hotel. It is still subject to lender covenants and leverage tests, but it does not tie a specified property’s sale proceeds or operating cash to a single mortgage lender. DiamondRock’s term loans were generally priced at adjusted SOFR plus a leverage-based credit spread. SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, is a floating benchmark rate; therefore, an unhedged SOFR-plus loan has a borrowing cost that rises and falls with short-term rates.[6]

At December 31, 2023, DiamondRock had two unsecured term loans totaling $800 million: a $500 million loan maturing January 3, 2028 and a $300 million loan maturing January 3, 2025. Both were priced at SOFR plus 1.35%; the reported rate at year-end was 6.81%, before separating the effect of swaps.[3] The company also had a $400 million senior unsecured revolving credit facility, or revolver, maturing September 2026, with no balance drawn at December 31, 2023.[3]

The revolving facility differs from a term loan. A term loan is generally funded at closing and amortizes or matures on a set schedule. A revolver is a committed borrowing line that can be drawn, repaid, and redrawn, subject to availability and covenants. DiamondRock’s revolver created liquidity flexibility for near-term maturities, illustrated by the brief July 2025 draw used to repay Hotel Clio.[1]

At June 30, 2024, the company still had the same two $800 million aggregate term-loan tranches: $500 million due January 2028 and $300 million due January 2025. Both were variable-rate loans at SOFR plus 1.35%, and both had a stated 6.80% rate at that date before the effect of interest-rate swaps.[4] The $300 million loan could be extended by one year upon payment of fees and satisfaction of customary conditions.[4]

DiamondRock intended to exercise that option and, in September 2024, did so. The extension moved the $300 million loan’s maturity from January 3, 2025 to January 3, 2026.[5] This extension was important: it avoided placing a $300 million corporate maturity immediately alongside the 2025 property-mortgage maturities. It did not repay the term loan or reduce its rate risk; it simply bought a further year before refinancing or repayment became necessary.

The facility also supplied additional contingent capacity. Before July 22, 2025, the Sixth Amended and Restated Credit Agreement provided a $400.0 million senior unsecured revolver scheduled to mature September 27, 2026, with a one-year extension option, and two term loans totaling $800.0 million: $500.0 million due January 3, 2028 and $300.0 million due January 3, 2026. The agreement permitted aggregate facility capacity to increase to $1.4 billion upon satisfaction of customary conditions.[1] At March 31, 2025, DiamondRock reported the full $400 million of revolver borrowing capacity as available.[12] The company’s leverage ratio was 26.0%, below its 60% maximum covenant, and its fixed-charge coverage was above the required threshold.[12]

At June 30, 2024, the Credit Agreement provided a $400 million senior unsecured revolving credit facility and two term loan facilities totaling $800 million, permitted aggregate facilities to increase to $1.4 billion subject to standard conditions, and set the revolver’s maturity at September 27, 2026, subject to a one-year extension option. DiamondRock reported a 29.0% leverage ratio against a 60% maximum and a 2.92x fixed-charge coverage ratio against a 1.50x minimum.[4] These figures do not mean debt capacity was unlimited, but they indicate that DiamondRock had covenant headroom while planning the mortgage repayment programme.

Covenant headroom is the gap between actual covenant ratios and contractual limits; it matters because a borrower’s facility flexibility depends on continuing compliance with those limits.

The July 22, 2025 refinancing reshaped the unsecured facility. DiamondRock entered into the Seventh Amended and Restated Credit Agreement, increasing the facility from $1.2 billion to $1.5 billion. It provided a $400 million revolver and three unsecured term loans totaling $1.1 billion: the $500 million Term 1 Loan due January 3, 2028, the $300 million Term 2 Loan due January 22, 2030, and the $300 million Term 3 Loan due January 22, 2029.[11] The incremental $300 million of term-loan capacity was used to fund the three 2025 mortgage maturities.[1]

The company’s public materials express the July 2025 maturity schedule in two ways. Its later Form 10-Q identifies the contractual maturities as January 2028 for the $500 million Term 1 Loan, January 2029 for the $300 million Term 3 Loan, and January 2030 for the $300 million Term 2 Loan, with the $400 million revolver due January 2030.[11] A July 23, 2025 press-release description instead presents the facility inclusive of extension options: the revolver matures January 2031, the $500 million term loan January 2029, and two $300 million term loans January 2030.[20] The difference arises from whether stated extension options are included in the maturity presentation; the later debt agreement disclosure gives the underlying contractual dates and should be used for the loan-level maturity sequence.[11][20]

The amended facility’s interest cost remained benchmark-linked. It bore interest at a leverage-based pricing grid ranging from SOFR plus 1.35% to SOFR plus 2.25%, with DiamondRock reporting that its current leverage placed it at the lowest 1.35% spread.[21] That low-end spread reflected conservative leverage, but it did not make the loans fixed-rate. Without swaps, the SOFR component remained variable.

At September 30, 2025, DiamondRock reported three unsecured term loans: $500 million due January 2028, $300 million due January 2029, and $300 million due January 2030. Each carried SOFR plus 1.35% contractual pricing.[16] The $500 million loan’s reported rate was 5.02%, including the effect of swaps; the two $300 million loans were each reported at 5.47%.[16] Total debt was $1.100 billion, all in unsecured term loans, with a 5.31% weighted-average interest rate including swaps and an average maturity of 3.1 years.[13][2]

**The financing evolution shifted DiamondRock from an $800 million unsecured-term-loan base plus property-specific mortgages in June 2024 to $1.1 billion of staggered unsecured term loans after the July 2025 refinancing.** In ordinary terms, the company exchanged several individual hotel loans and their short maturity dates for a larger corporate facility with maturities spread from 2028 to 2030.[10][11][2]

This shift improved capital-allocation flexibility in several ways. First, the portfolio became fully unencumbered, permitting corporate management to decide whether to sell, pledge, renovate, or otherwise redeploy hotels without obtaining property-mortgage lender consent.[1] Second, the $400 million undrawn revolver retained a source of contingent liquidity.[21] Third, the staggered 2028–2030 term-loan schedule reduced the concentration of near-term maturities that existed under the 2024–2025 mortgage schedule.[4][11]

The trade-off is that unsecured debt is corporate exposure. Its cost can rise with SOFR, and its credit spread can rise if leverage moves to a less favorable pricing tier. The term-loan margin ranged from 1.35% below 30% leverage to 2.20% at leverage of 55% or more.[1] Unsecured borrowing therefore transfers some risk from property-specific refinancing and collateral restrictions to benchmark-rate exposure and enterprise-wide covenant discipline.

The company also retained flexibility to repay. Following the mortgage repayments, it stated that its debt was fully prepayable without penalty.[16] Prepayment flexibility is valuable where operating cash, disposition proceeds, or asset-allocation decisions create an opportunity to delever before maturity.

It is not, however, equivalent to fixed-rate financing: a borrower can repay a floating loan early, but it still bears SOFR exposure until it does so or hedges it.

The term loans thus supplemented and then replaced mortgage debt rather than merely adding leverage. At June 30, 2024, DiamondRock had $800 million of unsecured term loans alongside $373.3 million of mortgages.[10] At September 30, 2025, it had $1.1 billion of unsecured term loans and no secured mortgage debt.[11] That is the structural foundation for the interest-rate hedge strategy considered next.[11]

Retaining the undrawn revolver has an explicit carrying cost: DiamondRock must pay an unused-facility fee of 0.20% when average revolver usage is at least 50% and 0.25% when average usage is below 50%. This is a financing cost incurred to preserve contingent liquidity and capital-allocation flexibility even when the revolver is not drawn.[17]

The July 22, 2025 Amended Credit Facility also gave DiamondRock a conditional right to increase aggregate facility capacity to $1.8 billion.[23]  The revolver, Term 1 Loan and Term 3 Loan each have two additional six-month extension options, subject to fees and customary conditions.[23]

The $500 million loan’s reported rate was 4.86%, including swaps, and each $300 million loan’s reported rate was 5.01%.[18] DiamondRock reported a 5.0% weighted-average interest rate, 30% fixed-rate debt including swaps, and average maturity of 2.8 years, or 3.6 years including extensions.[18]

The amended facility’s leverage-linked term-loan margin was 1.35% below a 30% leverage ratio and 2.20% at or above 55%. At December 31, 2025, DiamondRock reported a 27.6% maximum-leverage-ratio measure against a 60% limit, 3.38x fixed-charge coverage against a 1.50x minimum, 31.9% unencumbered leverage against a 60.0% limit, and 2.35x unencumbered implied debt-service coverage against a 1.20x minimum.[23] These measures kept the company in the lowest disclosed term-loan pricing tier and supported the unsecured structure’s covenant capacity.[23]

The amended facility also requires secured debt to remain below 45% of total asset value.[17]

The July 22, 2025 refinanced credit facility included a new $300 million unsecured term loan with delayed-draw capacity of up to $215 million.[24]

Delayed-draw borrowing capacity and an undrawn revolver allow DiamondRock to retain committed funding without paying interest on cash borrowed before it is needed.[24]

### Identify DiamondRock's relevant interest rate swaps.

An **interest-rate swap** is a derivative contract that exchanges interest cash flows while leaving the underlying loan principal outstanding. DiamondRock’s swaps involved receiving one-month SOFR from the counterparty and paying a fixed rate. In effect, for the swap notional, the company received an offset to the floating SOFR interest on its term loan and made a fixed payment under the swap.[11] The debt remained legally variable-rate, but the benchmark-rate cash flow on the hedged portion was economically fixed.

DiamondRock used these swaps for variable-rate unsecured term loans, not for its fixed-rate mortgage loans. Its cash-flow hedge swaps received variable-rate amounts and required fixed payments without exchanging notional principal.[4] A cash-flow hedge is an accounting and risk-management designation for a derivative intended to offset variability in future cash flows, here the interest payments on floating-rate debt.

The aggregate fair value declined by $0.394 million between December 31, 2023 and June 30, 2024. The reduction came from the rapid run-off in the value of the two swaps approaching their July 2024 maturity, partly offset by increased values on the two longer-dated swaps.[4] This is a valuation change, not a measure of the cash interest paid or saved during the six-month period.

The distinction is material. A derivative fair value is the balance-sheet estimate of what the contract is worth at a reporting date. Interest expense is the period’s income-statement cost of debt, which includes loan interest and may include debt-issuance-cost amortization, financing-lease expense, and recognized derivative effects. DiamondRock states that derivatives are recorded at fair value, while effective cash-flow-hedge fair-value movements go to other comprehensive income and are reclassified to interest expense as the hedged transaction affects earnings.[1] An increase in a swap asset is therefore not automatically an interest saving in the period, and a decline in a swap asset is not automatically additional cash interest expense.

The term-loan and swap relationship at June 30, 2024 illustrates partial coverage. DiamondRock had $1.2 billion of debt, of which $0.8 billion was variable-rate, and swaps related to $325 million of variable-rate debt.[4]Derived — The swaps therefore covered about 40.6% of the $800 million variable-rate debt at that date: $325 million divided by $800 million. The remainder remained exposed to SOFR, subject to any other debt-mix effects.

The company quantified the remaining sensitivity: a 100-basis-point movement in market rates on unhedged variable-rate debt would increase or decrease annual interest expense by $4.8 million.[6] A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 100 basis points equals 1.00 percentage point. The sensitivity gives a clearer measure of residual rate risk than the fair-value movement because it directly addresses annual interest-expense exposure on unhedged debt.

The hedge portfolio changed after the two 1.63% swaps matured in July 2024. At December 31, 2024, DiamondRock had swaps related to $225 million of variable-rate debt, with another $100 million effective January 2, 2025.[5] The lower $225 million figure reflects that the $175 million July 2024 swaps had expired and that the newer $75 million October 2024 swaps were the active additions at year-end. The January 2025 swaps restored additional coverage.

Basis risk is the risk that the rate or yield of the hedged debt does not correlate perfectly with the swap reference rate. ### DiamondRock's mortgage maturities and collateral

The financing choices cannot be assessed in isolation from the company’s hotel-investment obligations and other calls on cash. DiamondRock is a hotel REIT, so its operating cash supports not only debt service and maturities but also hotel renovations, property-improvement reserves, shareholder distributions, share repurchases, possible acquisitions, ground-lease costs, and corporate expenses.[3] The company’s own liquidity framework identifies debt maturities and debt service alongside capital expenditures, distributions, repurchases, acquisitions, and operating costs.[29]

Mortgage maturities created the immediate financing problem. As of June 30, 2024, the four fixed-rate mortgages were due in August 2024, May 2025, July 2025, and November 2025.[4] The fixed coupons were relatively low, but the maturity sequence would have required either large cash outlays, property-level refinancing, corporate borrowing, or asset sales over roughly fifteen months. The company chose a combination of cash repayment and corporate refinancing rather than rolling those mortgages into new property-specific debt.[1]

The collateral consequence was meaningful. In June 2024, four of 36 hotels were secured by mortgage debt, while 32 were unencumbered.[4] By December 2024, three of 37 hotels were encumbered.[5][7] The change increased the number of hotel assets available to support future financing or disposition decisions without first resolving a property mortgage.

DiamondRock’s unsecured arrangements were designed to make that transition feasible. The 2024 structure contained $800 million of term loans and an undrawn $400 million revolver.[6] The 2025 amendment expanded the facility from $1.2 billion to $1.5 billion, producing $1.1 billion of term loans plus the $400 million revolver.[16] The $300 million facility increase was specifically directed to the three 2025 mortgage loans.[20]

This does not mean the unsecured term loans were a no-cost replacement. The mortgages carried fixed rates between 3.66% and 4.40% as of June 2024.[4] The unsecured term loans were SOFR plus 1.35%, reported at 6.80% before swaps at June 30, 2024.[10] By September 30, 2025, the reported rate on the swap-affected $500 million term loan was 5.02%, while the two unhedged $300 million tranches were each at 5.47%.[16] The comparison shows why the company preserved a partial hedge programme: replacing low-coupon fixed mortgages with SOFR-based debt improved flexibility but increased sensitivity to benchmark rates.

The company’s swaps partly counterbalanced this effect. They did not refinance principal or provide cash proceeds; they fixed the SOFR component on selected term-loan tranches.[4] In June 2024, $325 million of $800 million variable-rate debt was covered.[4] In September 2025, after unsecured term debt had risen to $1.1 billion, swaps still effectively fixed $325 million.[11] The hedge programme therefore preserved a fixed-rate component while allowing the bulk of the new corporate debt to float.

Judgment — This is a deliberate “partial hedge” strategy. It is economically different from funding a property with a fixed-rate mortgage. A fixed-rate mortgage locks the rate on the entire mortgage balance for its contractual term. A floating-rate term loan combined with a swap locks only the notional and tenor of the swap, leaving the unhedged balance, credit spread, and refinancing exposure variable. The benefit is that the borrower can choose how much exposure to retain and can align hedge maturities with expected debt maturities or anticipated repayments.

The company’s reported rates show that swaps were economically material to the new debt profile. At March 31 and June 30, 2025, the $500 million unsecured term loan was reported at 5.12%, including the effect of swaps; the other $300 million term loan was reported at 5.76% and 5.74%, respectively.[30] The lower reported rate on the $500 million tranche is consistent with the continuing swap coverage on that loan, although the disclosure does not isolate a quarter-by-quarter dollar savings attributable to each swap.[29]

The company’s total debt-rate metrics also include swap effects. At June 30, 2024, DiamondRock reported a 5.22% weighted-average interest rate on total debt, including swaps, while the term loans’ stated 6.80% rate excluded swaps.[22] Those numbers cannot be treated as a simple before-and-after comparison of hedge savings because they use different bases: the 5.22% covers both fixed-rate mortgage debt and floating-rate term loans, while the 6.80% is the pre-swap rate for the term loans alone.

The same distinction applies to swap fair value. The distinction is especially important because DiamondRock’s reported interest expense contains more than cash coupon interest. Amortization of debt issuance costs is included in interest expense, and credit-facility issuance costs are amortized over the facility term even when no amount is outstanding.[3] The company also reported interest-rate-swap mark-to-market effects within its 2023 interest-expense analysis.[3] Thus, subsequent sections’ quarterly interest-expense comparisons should be read as total GAAP financing expense, not as a pure comparison of mortgage coupon versus term-loan coupon.

The mortgage repayment programme also intersected with operating cash generation and capital allocation. During the nine months ended September 30, 2025, DiamondRock reported $175.730 million of net cash provided by operating activities, $60.9 million of hotel capital expenditures, $295.808 million of mortgage principal payments, and $300.0 million of senior unsecured term-loan proceeds.[31] The facility proceeds nearly matched the mortgage repayments in size, demonstrating that the final mortgage elimination was principally a refinancing transaction, even though operating cash generation was substantial.[11]

Shareholder returns were another call on liquidity. For the nine months ended September 30, 2025, financing cash uses included $81.868 million of distributions on common stock and units, $7.362 million of preferred-stock distributions, and $35.519 million of common-share repurchases, as well as $11.6 million of financing costs and $5.1 million of tax-withholding share repurchases.[32] The share-repurchase programme was discretionary: the $200 million authorization did not require any particular repurchase amount and could be suspended or discontinued.[11] That discretion is a source of flexibility, but actual repurchases still used cash while the company was refinancing mortgage maturities.

REIT tax rules also constrain retained cash. DiamondRock generally must distribute at least 90% of its REIT taxable income annually to maintain REIT status.[3] Taxable income does not necessarily equal cash flow, but the rule limits the company’s ability to finance all capital investment and debt repayment solely through retained earnings.[3] Preferred dividends are also cumulative and paid before common dividends; at December 31, 2024, the company had approximately $119 million of preferred-stock liquidation preference and approximately $9.8 million of annual preferred dividends.[5][7]

The company itself anticipated multiple sources of cash for the remainder of 2025: hotel operating cash flow, potential dispositions, and debt refinancing proceeds. It also identified mortgage maturities, debt service, capital expenditures, distributions, share repurchases, corporate expenses, and potential acquisitions as cash uses.[29] This is consistent with the observed funding sequence—cash on hand for Worthington, a temporary revolver draw for Hotel Clio, and amended-facility proceeds for Westin Boston.[1]

The company had meaningful liquidity and covenant support for this approach. At June 30, 2025, its maximum leverage-ratio measure was 25.7% against a 60% covenant maximum, and its fixed-charge coverage ratio was 3.14x against a 1.50x minimum.[29] At September 30, 2025, it reported $145.3 million of unrestricted cash and $400 million available under the undrawn revolver.[21] These measures support the conclusion that DiamondRock had financial capacity to execute the unsecured refinancing and retain allocation options.

The resulting capital structure traded collateral rigidity for rate-management complexity. The fully unencumbered portfolio and later-dated maturities improved the company’s ability to allocate capital across hotels, pursue dispositions, fund renovations, or access the revolver without individual mortgage constraints.[16] But the final structure was all variable-rate debt before swaps, with only $325 million of $1.1 billion effectively fixed.[11]

**The mortgage-to-unsecured transition improved balance-sheet and capital-allocation flexibility, but it made the hedge programme more important because the company replaced low-rate fixed mortgages with a larger floating-rate corporate debt base.** The strategy worked as a refinancing and collateral-management programme; its continuing cost effectiveness depends on SOFR, leverage-based spreads, and the adequacy of the partial hedge ratio rather than on the noncash fair value of swaps.[4][11]

### Synthesis

DiamondRock moved from four fixed-rate, hotel-secured mortgages with 2024–2025 maturities to $1.1 billion of unsecured SOFR-based term loans maturing from 2028 through 2030. It repaid the mortgages in sequence using cash, a temporary revolver draw, and amended-facility proceeds, leaving the portfolio fully unencumbered by secured debt.[4][1][11]

The company preserved some interest-rate protection through swaps that effectively fixed $325 million of debt, but that coverage was partial: all $1.1 billion of debt was variable-rate before swaps at September 30, 2025.[11] The December 2023-to-June 2024 swap fair-value decline of $0.394 million was a noncash valuation movement, not an interest saving or refinancing cost.[4] The structural result is clear: DiamondRock gained substantial collateral, maturity, and capital-allocation flexibility, while accepting ongoing exposure to SOFR on most of its corporate debt and retaining significant competing uses for operating cash.[16][11]

The same $325 million hedge book therefore protected a smaller share of the enlarged unsecured floating-rate debt base.[4][11]

Judgment — Interest-rate swaps and debt refinancing answer different problems. ### Assess the effectiveness of their hedging strategy as debt composition

Cash-flow-hedge effectiveness should be assessed primarily as risk reduction: whether the swap offsets changes in the hedged floating-rate cash flows. It should not be inferred from a favorable or unfavorable fair-value movement alone. DiamondRock’s swaps structurally converted SOFR-linked cash flows to fixed payments on selected unsecured term-loan amounts, while leaving the underlying debt principal outstanding.[4][1]

At September 30, 2025, DiamondRock had $1.1 billion of contractually variable-rate debt. Its seven SOFR swaps, designated as cash-flow hedges of variable-rate debt, had $400.0 million aggregate notional, a 3.30625% weighted-average fixed swap rate, and a $0.168 million net fair-value asset. The swaps covered 36.36% of the $1.1 billion unsecured-term-loan principal, leaving $700.0 million unhedged; DiamondRock estimated that a 100-basis-point movement in rates on that unhedged variable-rate debt would change annual interest expense by $7.0 million.[13][35]

The reported reduction of interest expense from derivatives designated as cash-flow hedges weakened as the composition changed. Cash-flow-hedge derivative interest income reduced Q3 interest expense by $0.847 million in 2025, versus a $1.155 million reduction in Q3 2024—a $0.308 million smaller reported reduction. For the first nine months, the reported reduction fell to $2.485 million in 2025 from $5.863 million in 2024.[11]

At the same time, unrealized derivative losses recorded in other comprehensive income were $0.664 million in Q3 2025 and $4.237 million in Q3 2024. These amounts are valuation movements, not additions to quarterly interest expense.[11] The difference matters because DiamondRock’s September 2025 reported interest expense includes the periodic hedge settlements and reclassifications, whereas fair-value movements follow cash-flow-hedge accounting through other comprehensive income before later reclassification.[11][1]

Judgment — The swaps were effective in the narrow economic sense that they fixed SOFR exposure on a defined $400.0 million tranche of otherwise floating-rate unsecured debt at September 30, 2025. They were not comprehensive enough to prevent higher rates from affecting total interest cost in Q2 2024, and their reduced Q3 2025 interest-income contribution meant they did not explain the near-flat Q3 total; mortgage repayments were more important to that quarter’s offset.[4][11][13]

The program also maintained flexibility rather than maximising fixed-rate coverage. In August 2025, DiamondRock executed a $75 million-notional swap, effective January 2, 2026, to replace a maturing swap. This forward commitment preserved hedge coverage while allowing much of the capital structure to remain floating-rate.[11]

Derived — Unhedged floating-rate debt increased by $300.0 million, or 63.2%, from $475 million at June 30, 2024 to $775 million at September 30, 2025. Consistently, disclosed annual interest-expense sensitivity to a 100-basis-point rate movement rose by $3.0 million, or 62.5%, from $4.8 million to $7.8 million.[4][11][10]

During the third quarter of 2024, DiamondRock executed several swaps that became effective in the fourth quarter of 2024 and early 2025 and fixed SOFR at an average 3.2% rate. Management expected those transactions to result in approximately 57% fixed-rate debt entering 2025.[26]

In May 2025, DiamondRock terminated $325.0 million of forward-starting swaps that hedged the 2035 Senior Unsecured Public Notes and received $13.6 million net upon termination. This cash receipt was a separate hedge-termination transaction, not a reduction of Q3 interest expense.[34][13]

At September 30, 2025, DiamondRock reported a 5.3% total weighted-average interest rate including the effect of swaps. Management also described 30% of debt as fixed rate and 70% as floating rate inclusive of swaps, and characterized the remaining floating-rate exposure as advantageous in a declining-interest-rate environment.[35][27]

Neither quarterly change in total interest expense is an isolated hedge-effectiveness measure: each also reflects outstanding principal, loan pricing, timing and other interest-bearing borrowings.[4][11]

### Synthesis

DiamondRock’s swaps reduced, but did not eliminate, SOFR exposure as its debt mix shifted toward unsecured term loans. From December 2023 to June 2024, swap fair value declined $0.394 million, a non-cash valuation movement that does not measure financing cost. In Q2 2024, rising unsecured-term-loan interest more than offset lower mortgage interest, producing a $0.635 million increase in total interest expense. In Q3 2025, by contrast, a $2.113 million mortgage-interest reduction largely offset a $1.558 million rise in term-loan interest, leaving expense only $0.125 million higher. The swaps fixed $325 million of debt but left material floating-rate exposure, including a disclosed $7.8 million annual sensitivity per 100 basis points on unhedged debt. **The evidence supports partial rate-risk protection alongside financing flexibility, not a conclusion that swaps alone lowered overall financing costs.**[4][11]

## Refinancing costs

Judgment — Refinancing economics have two distinct layers. The first is the recurring cost of debt—coupon or floating-rate interest, swap settlements and financing-fee amortization. The second is the valuation of the interest-rate swaps themselves. Keeping those layers separate is essential: a lower swap asset value can be economically unfavorable at the reporting date without being a current-period cash interest cost, while a swap settlement can reduce interest expense without changing the underlying loan principal.

### Distinguish changes in swap fair value from interest expense when

DiamondRock’s interest-rate swaps are cash-flow hedges on portions of its floating-rate unsecured term loans. In a cash-flow hedge, DiamondRock receives one-month SOFR from the swap counterparty and pays a fixed rate, with no exchange of the swap’s notional principal.[4] Thus, the swap is an overlay on the debt: it changes the rate exposure of covered borrowing but does not repay a mortgage, fund a term loan, or itself provide refinancing proceeds.

At June 30, 2024, four swaps hedged $325 million of senior unsecured term loans. They comprised two $87.5 million swaps at 1.63%, maturing July 25, 2024; a $75 million swap at 3.36%, maturing January 1, 2028; and a $75 million swap at 3.50%, maturing January 1, 2027.[4] The aggregate fair value of those swap assets was $4.321 million at December 31, 2023 and $3.927 million at June 30, 2024.[4]

Derived — **swap-asset fair value decreased $0.394 million, or 9.1%, between December 31, 2023 and June 30, 2024: $3.927 million minus $4.321 million.** The direction means the portfolio had a less favorable balance-sheet valuation at June 2024 than at December 2023. It does not mean DiamondRock paid $0.394 million more interest, saved $0.394 million in cash interest, or incurred a refinancing cost of that amount.

### Synthesis

The financing transition produced two different results that should be read together. First, replacing mortgage debt with unsecured term loans lowered Q3 2025 mortgage interest by more than it increased term-loan interest, creating a derived $0.555 million reduction in the two debt-interest components. Second, total interest expense nonetheless rose $0.125 million because facility costs, issuance-cost amortization and finance-lease expense rose by $0.680 million.[11] Swaps reduced the interest expense of part of the floating-rate term-loan structure, but covered only $325 million of $1.1 billion of variable-rate debt at September 30, 2025; their Q3 interest-expense reduction was also $0.308 million lower year over year.[11] The $0.394 million December 2023-to-June 2024 decline in swap fair value is therefore not a financing-cost saving or expense.[4][5]

1. Treat the $0.125 million Q3 increase as the all-in GAAP financing-cost outcome, not as the cost of the mortgage-to-term-loan replacement.
2. Evaluate hedging through covered debt, swap settlements and interest-expense reclassifications, not through OCI fair-value movements.
3. Preserve the unsecured facility’s flexibility while monitoring the large unhedged SOFR exposure that remains after the mortgage repayments.

The debt transformation also improved repayment and maturity flexibility: after the September 5, 2025 Westin repayment, all debt was fully prepayable without fees or penalties and, including extension options, the earliest maturity was in 2029.[23][27]

A matched-principal annualized comparison measures contractual run-rate financing cost, whereas the 2024–2025 income-statement amounts reflect the actual dates on which mortgages were outstanding, unsecured borrowings were drawn, and derivative settlements and other financing charges were recognized.[31]

### Analyze how the mortgage debt repayment strategy supports this financing

DiamondRock’s financing transformation was a deliberate shift from property-specific mortgage debt—debt secured by individual hotels—to corporate unsecured term loans and an unsecured revolving credit facility. A mortgage is secured by specified hotel collateral; an unsecured term loan is backed by the borrower’s general credit rather than a lien on a particular property. Replacing the former with the latter can free hotels from mortgage liens and property-level restrictions, but it can also increase exposure to floating interest rates.

At December 31, 2023, four of DiamondRock’s 36 hotels were subject to mortgage debt. The company described this debt as limited-recourse and property-specific: in a default, lenders could generally foreclose on pledged assets, subject to customary recourse exceptions. The mortgage agreements also included property-specific covenants, restrictions on additional borrowing, and debt-service-coverage or debt-yield tests that could trap hotel cash in lender-controlled accounts.[3][5] All cash traps had been released at year-end 2023, so the restrictions were not then withholding cash, but their presence made encumbered hotels less flexible sources of corporate liquidity.[3][5]

The mortgage-repayment sequence began before the 2025 facility amendment. DiamondRock repaid the $73.3 million Courtyard New York Manhattan/Midtown East mortgage on August 6, 2024 using cash on hand.[7] This was not a refinancing funded by new term-loan proceeds; it was a direct use of liquidity to remove a near-term property-level maturity.

By December 31, 2024, mortgage debt had declined from $378.189 million at year-end 2023 to $295.808 million, while unsecured term loans remained $800.0 million.[7]

The remaining mortgage loans at December 31, 2024 were secured by the Worthington Renaissance Fort Worth Hotel, Hotel Clio and Westin Boston Seaport District. Their principal balances were $71.766 million, $54.657 million and $169.385 million, respectively, with stated fixed rates of 3.66%, 4.33% and 4.36% and maturities in May, July and November 2025.[11] These lower fixed mortgage coupons mattered economically because replacing them with SOFR-based corporate term loans could raise financing cost even while improving maturity and collateral flexibility; the separate refinancing-cost analysis addresses that trade-off.

The agreement provides a $400.0 million senior unsecured revolving facility and $1.1 billion of term loans: a $500.0 million Term 1 Loan maturing January 3, 2028, a $300.0 million Term 2 Loan maturing January 22, 2030, and a $300.0 million Term 3 Loan maturing January 22, 2029.[29][16][21] The amendment increased the company’s term-loan capacity by $300 million, and management said the incremental $300 million fully funded repayment of the three mortgages that matured or would mature in 2025.[29]

For the nine months ended September 30, 2025, the $300.000 million of unsecured term-loan proceeds exceeded $295.808 million of mortgage-principal payments by $4.192 million before financing costs, but fell $7.382 million short after $11.574 million of financing-cost payments.[11]

The financing structure also reduced refinancing concentration. After the September 5, 2025 Westin Boston repayment, DiamondRock reported that its portfolio was fully unencumbered by secured debt, that debt was fully prepayable without penalty, and that it had no debt maturities until January 2028.[16] At September 30, the company had $1.1 billion of debt outstanding, consisting of three unsecured term loans, with a 5.3% weighted-average interest rate including swaps and a weighted-average maturity of 3.1 years.[2]

There is a maturity-convention distinction rather than necessarily a conflict in the company’s disclosures. Following the July 2025 Hotel Clio repayment of $53.9 million and the September 2025 Westin Boston Seaport District repayment of $166.2 million using incremental Amended Credit Facility proceeds, DiamondRock stated that it had no maturities until January 2028, a fully unencumbered portfolio and debt fully prepayable without penalty.[16] At the Q3 2026 earnings call, DiamondRock stated that its earliest debt maturity was in 2029, all debt was fully prepayable, and its leverage profile remained among the most conservative in its peer group.[19][39] The practical conclusion is that the 2025 repayment program removed the immediate 2025 mortgage wall and materially extended the refinancing runway.

Judgment — The mortgage-repayment strategy improved capital-allocation flexibility more than it reduced nominal debt. It exchanged four property-specific maturity events for a corporate maturity ladder, released every hotel from secured-debt collateral, and preserved the ability to sell, renovate, refinance or otherwise redeploy individual properties without obtaining mortgage-lender releases.

The strategy also made the remaining debt more flexible at the corporate level. At September 30, 2025, the $400 million revolver was undrawn and DiamondRock held approximately $145.3 million of unrestricted cash.[21][11] This means the company finished the mortgage-repayment program with both an unencumbered hotel portfolio and residual liquidity, rather than exhausting all cash to eliminate mortgages.

The repayment strategy therefore supports the financing transformation by replacing near-term secured maturities with unsecured corporate funding and by freeing hotel assets from property-level collateral constraints. Its cost is that a greater share of the debt stack became SOFR-linked corporate debt, making the swap program and capital-allocation discipline more important.

DiamondRock stated when it entered the July 22, 2025 facility that the $300.0 million upsizing fully funded repayment of the three mortgages that matured or would mature in 2025. The Worthington Renaissance Fort Worth Hotel and Hotel Clio mortgages had approximately $125.0 million of combined principal and were repaid at their May and July 2025 maturities before the amended facility closed; DiamondRock then intended to prepay the $166.6 million Westin Boston Seaport District mortgage in September 2025.[17]

The amended facility also included conditional expansion and extension capacity: DiamondRock may increase aggregate capacity to $1.8 billion subject to standard conditions; the revolver matures January 21, 2030 and may be extended twice for six months; and the Term 1 and Term 3 loans each have two additional six-month extension options, subject to fees and standard conditions.[17]

The amended facility’s revolving credit facility is described as maturing January 22, 2030, with two six-month extension options.[29][17]

The debt-mix result is measurable. From June 30, 2024 to September 30, 2025, mortgage principal declined from $373.285 million to zero, unsecured term loans increased from $800.000 million to $1.100 billion, and total debt principal declined by $73.285 million. The weighted-average debt rate including swaps increased by 9 basis points, from 5.22% to 5.31%.[13]

At December 31, 2024, DiamondRock reported $1.1 billion of debt, comprising $800.0 million of unsecured term loans and $295.8 million of three property-specific mortgages, at a 5.21% weighted-average interest rate. It also reported $584.3 million of liquidity, consisting of $81.4 million of unrestricted corporate cash, $102.9 million of unrestricted hotel cash and full revolver availability. Management was actively pursuing financing for the three 2025 mortgage maturities and identified cash on hand and the revolver as the fallback if that financing was not obtained.[9]

The amended facility also permits DiamondRock to request additional lender commitments that would increase aggregate capacity to $1.8 billion, subject to standard conditions.[23]

At September 30, 2025, DiamondRock reported $1.1 billion of debt outstanding and stated that its portfolio was fully unencumbered by secured debt.[11] The reported mortgage weighted-average rate was 3.68%.[34] The mortgages were generally non-recourse, and none had full or partial recourse to DiamondRock at September 30, 2025.[34]

Management’s third-quarter 2024 actions foreshadowed the transition: it repaid the $73.3 million Courtyard Manhattan Midtown East mortgage with cash on hand, stated that the next maturity was in May 2025, and was assessing both secured and unsecured alternatives.[26] Management also stated that it was working to maximize financial flexibility while keeping overall financing costs low.[26]

DiamondRock’s later facility disclosure describes the incremental Amended Credit Facility proceeds as funding all three 2025 mortgage repayments: $71.1 million for Worthington Renaissance in May, $53.9 million for Hotel Clio in July, and $166.2 million for Westin Boston Seaport District in September.[19] This differs from the cash-on-hand funding description for Worthington stated above.[11][19]

Before the final repayment, DiamondRock stated that it intended to prepay the $166.6 million Westin Boston Seaport District mortgage in September 2025; following that repayment, it expected no debt maturities until January 2028 and a portfolio fully unencumbered by secured debt.[17]

### Analyze how capital allocation priorities support this financing transformation.

Capital allocation is the decision process for distributing cash and financing capacity among debt reduction, hotel investment, acquisitions, dividends, repurchases and liquidity reserves. For a hotel REIT, this choice is unusually consequential because hotel properties require recurring capital expenditures while REIT tax rules limit the amount of taxable income that can be retained.

DiamondRock identifies acquisitions, debt service and maturities, share repurchases, capital expenditures, operating costs, ground-lease payments, corporate expenses and distributions to common stockholders, common operating-partnership unit holders and preferred stockholders as principal uses of cash.[3] Mortgage repayment is therefore one competing claim among several, not a use of a separate ring-fenced cash pool.

The company’s capital priorities during 2025 demonstrate that it did not direct all cash toward debt repayment. During the six months ended June 30, 2025, DiamondRock paid $65.276 million in common-stock and common-unit distributions, $4.908 million of preferred distributions, $23.704 million for common-stock repurchases, $4.888 million for tax-withholding share redemptions, $3.844 million in scheduled mortgage principal payments and $71.089 million in mortgage repayment.[29] These outflows preceded the July facility amendment and illustrate the simultaneous demands on cash during the refinancing period.

For the nine months ended September 30, 2025, financing activity included $295.8 million of mortgage debt repayments, $89.2 million of common and preferred distributions, $35.5 million of share repurchases, $11.6 million of financing costs and $5.1 million of tax-withholding-related share repurchases. The $300.0 million term-loan proceeds partly offset these uses.[11] Thus, the company continued returns of capital to shareholders while completing the debt transition.

Net cash used in financing activities for the nine months ended September 30, 2025 was $137.184 million.[11]

Share repurchases were the clearest discretionary cash use. DiamondRock’s board authorized a $200.0 million repurchase program through May 1, 2026, but the program did not require any minimum repurchase amount and can be suspended or discontinued at management’s discretion.[11] DiamondRock repurchased 4,597,942 shares for $35.5 million during the first nine months of 2025.[11] This discretion gives management a practical liquidity lever: it can preserve cash for debt maturities, capex or acquisitions by reducing buybacks without breaching a contractual funding commitment.

DiamondRock reported an average purchase price of $7.71 per share for the 4,597,942 common shares it repurchased during the nine months ended September 30, 2025.[11]

Common and preferred distributions are less discretionary in economic terms. DiamondRock intends to distribute dividends at least equal to REIT taxable income, and the company generally must distribute at least 90% of REIT taxable income annually to maintain REIT qualification.[11][3] This requirement applies to taxable income rather than GAAP net income, FFO, Adjusted FFO or operating cash flow. It can therefore compel distributions even when cash available after capex and debt service is constrained.[3]

The annual requirement to distribute at least 90% of REIT taxable income excludes net capital gains and is calculated without regard to the dividends-paid deduction.[12]

At the same time, DiamondRock’s board retains discretion over the timing, form and amount of distributions, subject to REIT requirements and other factors. The company specifically says the board considers financial performance, debt service, debt covenants, capital-expenditure requirements and REIT qualification requirements.[11][7][1] The ability to calibrate common dividends above the minimum required level provides some flexibility, although preferred dividends are cumulative and must be paid before common dividends can be declared or set aside.[7]

Hotel reinvestment is another durable claim on cash. The company spent approximately $60.9 million on capital improvements in the first nine months of 2025 and expected total 2025 spending of $85.0 million to $90.0 million.[11][16] Significant 2025 projects included the repositioning of Orchards Inn as the Cliffs at L’Auberge, guestroom renovations at Hilton Garden Inn New York/Times Square Central and Kimpton Hotel Palomar Phoenix, and a planned renovation at Courtyard New York Manhattan/Midtown East.[7] These investments can sustain property competitiveness and future hotel cash flow; deferring them indiscriminately to retire debt could weaken future earnings capacity.

DiamondRock’s capital expenditures averaged approximately 9% of revenue during 2018–2024.[46]

Management identified a $25 million renovation of The Cliffs at L’Auberge for which it expected a 10% stabilized yield.[46]

DiamondRock had also committed to at least $7.0 million of capital improvements by the end of 2026 to preserve a second renewal option on the Courtyard New York Manhattan/Fifth Avenue ground lease.[11] This is an example of capex that is not merely opportunistic: some spending protects contractual rights and the value of a specific hotel asset.

Asset recycling supplemented operating cash and debt financing. DiamondRock sold the Westin Washington D.C. City Center for $92.0 million on February 19, 2025 and received approximately $89.0 million of net proceeds after transaction costs and working-capital adjustments.[1] The company described the sale as harvesting capital from an asset with low free-cash-flow growth and redeploying it to higher-return opportunities.[8] The cash-flow statement confirms that disposition proceeds offset hotel capital expenditures, resulting in $28.1 million of net cash provided by investing activities for the nine months ended September 30, 2025.[11]

In November 2024, DiamondRock acquired the fee-simple interest in the 245-room AC Hotel Minneapolis Downtown for $30 million in cash.[47][48]

The company also retained alternative funding channels. Its ATM equity program allowed up to $200.0 million of common-stock issuance, although no shares were sold through the program during the three or nine months ended September 30, 2025 or the comparable 2024 periods.[11] It also identifies cash from operations, borrowings, equity and debt securities, and property-disposition proceeds as sources to meet long-term liquidity requirements.[11][12] The availability of alternatives matters because it avoids making every hotel renovation, maturity or acquisition dependent on current-period operating cash flow.

DiamondRock also sought to make hotels more marketable through ground-lease extensions, pre-negotiated property-improvement plans and financing alternatives.[48][49]

Judgment — DiamondRock’s allocation framework supported the refinancing by treating debt reduction as an important but not exclusive use of capital. The company retained the ability to curtail discretionary buybacks, adjust selected capex timing, sell non-core assets, draw the revolver, issue equity through the ATM program, or access debt markets while maintaining required REIT distributions and hotel investment.

This flexibility has limits. Hotel acquisitions, renovations, operating costs, ground-lease payments, debt service, distributions and potential preferred-stock redemption are all identified liquidity requirements.[11] In addition, a REIT generally cannot retain most taxable income, so operating cash cannot be presumed to accumulate indefinitely for debt repayment.[1] The capital-allocation framework preserves options; it does not eliminate the need for recurring external financing and asset recycling.

The capital-allocation priorities therefore support the financing transformation because they give DiamondRock adjustable sources and uses of cash while protecting essential hotel investment and REIT compliance. They also rule out the simplistic conclusion that positive operating cash flow was wholly available to pay mortgages.

For the nine months ended September 30, 2025, DiamondRock paid $81.868 million in distributions on common stock and units, $7.362 million in preferred-stock distributions, and $35.519 million to repurchase common stock. These reported outflows confirm that capital returns continued concurrently with the mortgage refinancing.[32]

DiamondRock’s stated capital-allocation strategy is disciplined capital allocation into high-quality North American lodging properties in markets with superior growth prospects and high barriers to entry, active asset management, conservative leverage, and regular assessment of hotels for investment or sale of non-core assets to improve portfolio quality.[40] The February 2025 Westin Washington D.C. City Center sale applied that approach: management characterized it as harvesting capital from a low-free-cash-flow-growth asset for redeployment into higher-return investment opportunities.[9]

Management’s stated allocation priorities also included acquisitions and dispositions, share repurchases and high-return internal investments. By the third-quarter 2024 call, it had repurchased 3.1 million shares at a weighted-average $8.33 per share, for approximately $26 million.[26] Management also described dispositions as capital that could be reinvested in share repurchases or another hotel with a higher free-cash-flow yield.[26]

Capital-expenditure discipline is a source of flexibility rather than simply a reduction in spending. Management reduced 2024 capex guidance to $85 million from $90 million–$100 million through reassessment of project scope, project savings, and improved planning and execution, while stating that capital expenditures are its single largest cost and targeting 7%–9% of revenue over time.[26] Management’s stated approach is to make capex more efficient and impactful without foregoing critical items.[26]

DiamondRock had 4,760,000 outstanding 8.250% Series A cumulative redeemable preferred shares at September 30, 2025, and the shares became redeemable at the company’s option on August 31, 2025.[11]

At the third-quarter 2025 call, DiamondRock said only 5% of its hotels were brand managed, giving it more control over renovation scope and timing. It said a two-year extension of a typical seven-year renovation cycle would reduce average annual renovation expenditure by 28%; this is a stated means of balancing operating performance, value creation and capital expenditure.[27][41] The company reported spending 7% of revenue on capex over the preceding three years, versus 10.5% for peers, while targeting 7%–9% annually.[27][41]

DiamondRock regularly assesses capital availability and affordability to maximize stockholder value and minimize enterprise risk.[11]

DiamondRock’s 2025 Series A preferred dividend was $0.515625 per share on each of the March, June and September payment dates.[11]

DiamondRock describes its strategy as applying aggressive asset management, prudent financial strategy and disciplined capital allocation to high-quality lodging properties to generate long-term stockholder returns through dividends and capital appreciation.[11]

### Assess their ability to fund debt refinancing through operational cash

Operational cash generation clearly contributed to DiamondRock’s refinancing capacity. Net cash provided by operating activities was $175.730 million for the nine months ended September 30, 2025, and the derived operating-cash-flow FCF measure was $114.789 million after capital expenditures.[11] This cash generation supported corporate liquidity, reduced reliance on immediate asset sales for ordinary hotel capital needs and gave management capacity to use cash on hand for selected mortgage repayments.

The company funded the gap through a combination of financing, cash balances and asset-recycling proceeds. Financing costs deserve separate treatment. DiamondRock paid $11.574 million of financing costs during the nine months ended September 30, 2025.[11] These are transaction costs associated with arranging or amending debt facilities; they are not the same as interest expense and are not captured merely by comparing mortgage coupons with term-loan rates. The company’s 2025 cash deployment confirms that operating cash was not committed exclusively to mortgages. In addition to the $295.8 million of mortgage principal payments, the company paid $89.2 million of common and preferred distributions and $35.5 million of common-share repurchases during the nine-month period.[11] The existence of those concurrent uses does not imply poor liquidity management; it shows that DiamondRock had sufficient access to a broader capital base to refinance mortgages while continuing shareholder-return policies.

Judgment — DiamondRock demonstrated meaningful capacity to support refinancing from operations, because its hotel portfolio produced $175.730 million of operating cash and $114.789 million after reported capex over nine months. But it did not fund the 2025 mortgage payoff program solely from operations: term-loan proceeds were the central matched funding source, with disposition proceeds, cash on hand and temporary revolver capacity supplying additional flexibility.

This distinction matters for future refinancing risk. A hotel REIT with positive operating cash flow can service interest, fund some capex and accumulate liquidity, but its debt is typically characterized by limited amortization and substantial balloon repayments at maturity.[7] Large maturities therefore ordinarily require refinancing, asset sales, cash reserves or some combination, even if annual operating cash flow remains positive.

DiamondRock itself identifies hotel operating cash flow, potential dispositions and debt refinancing as significant sources of cash, while identifying loan maturities, debt service, capex, distributions, share repurchases, corporate expenses and possible acquisitions as cash uses.[29][12] This is a diversified-liquidity model, not an operating-cash-only model.

The strength of the model is that it reduces dependence on any one source; the vulnerability is that debt-market access and asset-sale values can be less favorable during a lodging downturn or a period of high rates.

The company’s relatively low leverage and unencumbered portfolio improve access to alternatives. At June 30, 2025, its maximum leverage ratio was 25.7% against a 60% covenant limit and its fixed-charge coverage ratio was 3.14x against a 1.50x minimum.[29] These covenant cushions supported the July refinancing and the low-end leverage-based pricing margin, but they do not immunize the company from higher future SOFR, weaker hotel cash flow or tighter credit markets.[21][1]

The ability to fund refinancing through operations is therefore best characterized as partial but meaningful. Operating cash financed a portion of the overall liquidity requirement, supported cash-on-hand repayments and preserved financial capacity, while unsecured term-loan proceeds carried the principal burden of replacing the 2025 mortgage maturities.

### Analyze whether DiamondRock's evolving financing strategy effectively balances interest rate

The financing transformation achieved a clear improvement in capital-allocation flexibility. DiamondRock moved from a mix of fixed-rate, property-specific mortgages and unsecured term loans to a fully unencumbered portfolio funded by three unsecured term loans and an undrawn revolver.[11][21] The July 2025 amended facility increased capacity to $1.5 billion, extended maturities into 2028–2030 and retained a $400 million revolver.[16] Those features provide flexibility to fund hotel capex, acquisitions, distributions, repurchases or future maturities without pledging a particular hotel as collateral.

The offset is increased floating-rate exposure. At September 30, 2025, DiamondRock had $1.100 billion of unsecured term loans and seven SOFR interest-rate swaps designated as cash-flow hedges of variable-rate debt. The swaps had $400.000 million aggregate notional and a 3.30625% weighted-average fixed swap rate. An interest-rate swap in this structure causes DiamondRock to receive one-month SOFR and pay a fixed rate, thereby converting the benchmark-rate exposure on the covered debt from floating to fixed.[13][11] Including interest-rate swaps, management described 30% of debt as fixed rate and 70% as floating rate, and characterized the floating-rate exposure as an advantage in a declining-interest-rate environment.[41]

The uncovered portion remained economically sensitive to SOFR. At June 30, 2025, when DiamondRock had $325 million of swaps, the company estimated that a 100-basis-point movement in rates on unhedged variable-rate debt would change annual interest expense by $4.8 million.[29] A basis point is one-hundredth of one percentage point; thus, 100 basis points equals one percentage point. This sensitivity confirms that the swaps mitigate rather than eliminate interest-rate risk.

At September 30, 2025, the $500 million term loan had a stated 5.02% rate including the effect of swaps, while the two $300 million term loans were each stated at 5.47%; the three unsecured loans had a total weighted-average rate of 5.3%, also including swaps.[16] The amended facility was priced on a leverage-based grid ranging from SOFR plus 1.35% to SOFR plus 2.25%, and DiamondRock said its leverage placed it at the low end of the grid.[16][21] The resulting structure preserves some benefit if SOFR falls while protecting a selected portion of debt if rates rise.

The preceding section, “Refinancing costs,” addresses the requested quarterly interest-expense comparisons and the calculation of financing-cost changes as mortgage debt shifted to unsecured term loans. Those comparisons show how reported interest expense evolved with debt composition. For this section’s purpose, their key implication is narrower: the debt transition did not make quarterly reported interest expense move one-for-one with the increase in unsecured term loans because mortgage interest fell at the same time and swaps altered the effective rate on part of the term-loan portfolio.[11][16]

Swap fair value must remain distinct from both cash interest and financing costs. DiamondRock recognizes derivatives at fair value on the balance sheet. For an effective cash-flow hedge, fair-value changes are recorded in other comprehensive income and reclassified to interest expense when the hedged transaction affects earnings; undesignated-derivative changes are recognized in current earnings.[5] The company also excludes unrealized fair-value adjustments to interest-rate swaps from Adjusted FFO because it views them as non-cash amounts that do not reflect underlying performance.[29][30]

Judgment — A swap fair-value gain or loss is a mark-to-market valuation of the derivative at a reporting date, not an immediate interest saving or cash interest payment. The relevant economic test of hedging is whether the fixed-rate payments and floating-rate receipts reduce the variability of the company’s interest cash flows on the debt being hedged over time.

DiamondRock’s hedge design is consistent with partial-rate-risk management rather than complete rate locking. At December 31, 2024, swaps covered $225 million of variable-rate debt, with an additional $100 million becoming effective January 2, 2025; by March 31, June 30 and September 30, 2025, the reported effective fixed-rate amount was $325 million.[5][12][29][11] The company executed a $75 million replacement swap in August 2025 that becomes effective January 2, 2026, explaining why that agreement was listed at September 30 but was not yet effective.[11] This rollover activity shows an intention to maintain hedging coverage as older instruments mature.

The strategy also balances flexibility through prepayment rights. After the mortgage repayments, DiamondRock stated that its debt was fully prepayable without penalty.[16] This matters because a fully prepayable unsecured term-loan structure allows the company to reduce debt using future asset-sale proceeds, excess operating cash, equity issuance or other financing without needing to pay mortgage prepayment penalties or negotiate releases of property collateral.

Capital-allocation flexibility is not unlimited, because the floating-rate facility exposes the company to higher SOFR and because multiple cash uses compete for liquidity. DiamondRock must fund hotel capex, debt service, distributions, ground-lease obligations, corporate costs, possible acquisitions and discretionary repurchases.[11][29] The REIT distribution requirement structurally limits retained taxable income, while hotel properties need ongoing investment to remain competitive.[3][1] However, buybacks remain discretionary, the revolver was undrawn at September 30, 2025, unrestricted cash was $145.3 million, and the portfolio was fully unencumbered.[11]

The strategy therefore balances two objectives with different time horizons. First, seven cash-flow-hedge swaps reduce near-term cash-flow volatility on $400.000 million, or 36.36%, of SOFR-based term-loan principal. Second, unsecured financing and mortgage repayments create longer-term corporate flexibility by removing property liens, delaying maturities and preserving corporate borrowing capacity. The cost of this balance is that roughly $700 million remained floating-rate exposure, so rate movements still affect earnings and cash flow.[13][18]

On a broader swap-inclusive debt-cost basis, applying the disclosed weighted-average rates to reported debt principal produces an indicative annual financing-cost run rate of $61.245 million at June 30, 2024 ($1.173285 billion × 5.22%) and $58.410 million at September 30, 2025 ($1.100 billion × 5.31%).Derived — the indicative annual run rate decreased by $2.835 million, despite the 9-basis-point increase in the weighted-average rate, because total debt principal declined by $73.285 million.[13]

The unsecured structure also contains a $350.0 million 2029 Unsecured Term Loan that closed in July 2023 as a 5.5-year loan. Its SOFR spread is 80 to 160 basis points plus a 10-basis-point SOFR adjustment, based on credit ratings, and it has an accordion permitting requests for total lender commitments up to $500.0 million.[34] This loan is the debt specifically associated with the $350.0 million swap hedge through January 2029.[34]

DiamondRock’s September 30, 2025 debt disclosures separately report $400.0 million of 5.600% senior unsecured public notes due 2035, issued in May 2025 at 99.297% of principal for $397.2 million of proceeds before debt-issuance costs. The notes had a 5.35% all-in rate at September 30; that all-in rate reflects straight-line amortization of terminated swaps and original-issue discount, rather than only the 5.60% cash coupon.[34] This is a fixed-coupon unsecured funding source distinct from the SOFR-based term loans and their swap overlays.

Quarterly interest-expense comparisons cannot by themselves isolate hedge effectiveness because average debt outstanding, benchmark rates, debt-issuance-cost amortization, prepayment costs and the timing of refinancings also affect reported interest expense.

By the third-quarter 2025 earnings call, management described the realized post-refinancing mix as 30% fixed and 70% floating, inclusive of swaps.[27][41] This is consistent with the $325 million effective fixed-rate amount against $1.1 billion of debt stated above and confirms that the strategy retained substantial floating-rate exposure rather than fully locking rates.[11][27]

The rate protection has defined costs and limits. DiamondRock stated that hedging can be expensive during volatile-rate periods; available hedges may not correspond directly to the risk being hedged; hedge duration may not match liability duration; hedges may not adequately mitigate rate increases; and counterparties may not perform. It also stated that, if rates decline, hedges may limit the benefit of lower market rates and result in higher interest expense than otherwise.[1]

Early termination can create a cash breakage payment on a swap, whereas an interest-rate cap, after its upfront premium is paid, does not become a purchaser liability or create that future breakage-payment risk.[42] For a secured swap, prepayment of the underlying loan typically requires termination; an unsecured swap may not automatically terminate on a sale or refinancing.[42] A blend-and-extend embeds an existing swap liability into a new, longer-dated swap and defers rather than forgives that liability through a higher new swap rate.[42]

A swap-termination payment or receipt, often called swap breakage, compares the original contracted swap rate with the market replacement rate for the remaining term. The borrower pays the swap provider when the contracted rate exceeds the replacement rate and receives payment when the contractual rate is below the replacement rate.[42]

### Synthesis

DiamondRock’s 2024–2025 financing transformation was effective in strengthening capital-allocation flexibility: it used cash on hand for the Courtyard and Worthington mortgage repayments, a temporary revolver bridge for Hotel Clio, and amended-facility proceeds for the Westin Boston payoff. The $300.000 million of senior unsecured term-loan proceeds broadly matched the $295.808 million of mortgage principal payments, leaving every hotel unencumbered and eliminating stated maturities until January 2028.[11][7][16] Operations contributed meaningful capacity, producing $175.730 million of operating cash flow and $114.789 million after reported capex, but did not independently fund the mortgage program because repayments exceeded both amounts and coincided with distributions, buybacks and financing costs.[11] The interest-rate trade-off is deliberate: unsecured financing increased floating-rate exposure, while seven cash-flow-hedge swaps covered $400.000 million, or 36.36%, of $1.100 billion of variable-rate term-loan principal.[13] **Overall, the strategy favors flexibility over full rate certainty, while using partial swaps to keep the resulting SOFR risk manageable rather than eliminate it.**
