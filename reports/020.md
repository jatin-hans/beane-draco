Select Caterpillar only if the purchase contract supplies a model-specific −40°C package and mine-site spares commitment. Caterpillar has the strongest documented Mongolia support footprint, including Wagner Asia’s Ulaanbaatar facilities and supported mine fleets, while its published standard Cat 395 cold-start option reaches −32°C and critical 777F differential/final-drive guidance does not establish routine −40°C operation.[1] Komatsu and Volvo CE remain viable only after they demonstrate equivalent −40°C whole-machine configuration, Ulaanbaatar response, local critical-parts stock and Mongolian-language training for the selected excavator and haul-truck models.[2][3][4] Fuel selection should be based on a controlled winter trial in the mine’s own payload, route and idle conditions, not published figures with different duty cycles.

## Caterpillar, Komatsu, and Volvo CE excavators and haul trucks

**Assessment date: 19 November 2026.** This comparison uses five procurement fields: documented whole-machine −40°C capability means a published model-specific ambient operating or cold-start rating, not a component-fluid range or an option list; an equipped Ulaanbaatar service centre means technicians, diagnostics, tooling and component-repair capability; rapid-repair inventory means part-number-confirmed Mongolian stock; comparable winter fuel use means the same duty, ambient temperature, fuel, payload and warm-up/idle boundary; and locally usable operator training means Mongolian-language or workforce-language delivery with assessed winter practice. Cat’s published 395 cold-start figures, Volvo’s R100E Arctic Kit wording, Wagner Asia’s 23 January 2012 facilities presentation, and Transwest’s current Mongolia dealership and UB repair statements must be read against those definitions.[1] A published heater, an Eco mode, or a dealer name alone does not make an excavator or truck dependable after a cold soak.[5][3][6][7]

Relevant mining-scale candidates in the supplied manufacturer material are Cat 395 and 6015–6090 hydraulic shovels with Cat 777/793-class off-highway trucks; Komatsu PC1250-11 and PC2000-11 hydraulic excavators with HD785 and 930E-class haul trucks; and Volvo EC550E, EC750E and EC950F excavators with A25G–A60H articulated haulers and the R100E rigid hauler. The equipment classes are not interchangeable: the Cat 793 and Komatsu 930E-5 are ultra-class mining trucks, while Volvo’s A-series are articulated haulers; fleet selection must start with required payload, haul-road geometry, grade, loading-pass target and production rate.[8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15]

The practical conclusion is that Caterpillar has the strongest documented Mongolia support footprint and the most detailed published −40°C component-preparation guidance, but no named Cat excavator or complete Cat haul truck in the supplied material has a published whole-machine reliability rating at −40°C. Komatsu and Volvo CE both offer relevant mining equipment and cold-weather features, but the available material provides less direct evidence of Mongolian repair support and no equivalent complete-machine −40°C qualification.[16][17][1][18][5]

Before tender award, require each bidder to supply a serial-number-specific cold-climate configuration, a written ambient operating and cold-start limit, fluids schedule, heater electrical-load schedule, fuel specification, spare-parts stocking list by location, field-response commitment, and Mongolian-language operator and maintainer training plan.

### Caterpillar excavators

Caterpillar’s relevant excavator range includes small, medium and large excavators, as well as hydraulic shovels from the 6015 through the 6090. The Cat 395 is a 95-tonne large hydraulic excavator; Caterpillar lists a 5.2 m³ bucket in one product description, while another listed mass-excavation configuration uses a 6.5 m³ bucket. Its stated operating weight is 91,800 kg for one GP configuration and 94,100 kg for another listed configuration, so bucket and weight must be fixed in the purchase specification rather than assumed from the model number.[17][1][19][20]

The Cat 6015 is a mining hydraulic shovel. Caterpillar describes cold-weather packages as available options for that model, alongside access packages, stick options and track-pad options. The 6015 has a heated seat and cab heat/defroster arrangement, and Caterpillar describes its undercarriage and structures as designed for mine terrain and tough digging. Those features support mine use and operator comfort, but they are not a declared −40°C excavator operating limit.[21]

Cat publishes extensive cold-weather operating guidance down to −40°C for Caterpillar machines. It treats −30°C to −40°C as Category 4 cold conditions and calls for SAE 0W-40 or lower-viscosity multigrade oil. For cold-soaked starts or operating temperatures below −30°C, Caterpillar calls for synthetic-basestock SAE 0W or 5W multigrade oil with pour point below −40°C; it gives SAE 0W-30 as an example for a −40°C cold-soaked engine start.[16][17]

Caterpillar’s general fluid tables support a −40°C preparation regime for several excavator components. Cat DEO Cold Weather SAE 0W-40 is listed for all Cat diesel engines from −40°C to 40°C. Cat TDTO Cold Weather SAE 0W-20 is listed from −40°C to 0°C for excavator final drives and swing drives, and For excavator track idlers and track rollers, Cat DEO Cold Weather SAE 0W-40 is listed from −40°C to 40°C, while Cat DEO-ULS SYN SAE 5W-40 is listed from −35°C to 40°C. Arctic grease can be pumped through standard machine-mounted automatic lubrication systems down to −50°C when using the stated NLGI 0.5 product.[17]

Those component-fluid limits must not be confused with complete-excavator approval. Caterpillar’s hydraulic-shovel table for models 6015–6090 lists the hydraulic-system oil at a minimum ambient temperature of −15°C, while the pump-distribution, travel and swing gearboxes are listed to −35°C. The same table lists Arctic grease from −50°C to 130°C for central lubrication and external lubrication points. Therefore, a hydraulic shovel’s engine oil, grease and some gearbox lubricants may be suitable at −40°C while its cited hydraulic-system fluid entry is not.[17]

The Cat 395 makes the same distinction visible in a current product specification. Caterpillar lists standard cold-start capability at −18°C and an optional cold-start capability at −32°C, with cold-start block heaters and automatic hydraulic-oil warm-up. The stated −32°C option is still 8°C warmer than the requested −40°C condition.[1][19]

The Cat 349F/352F operating manual illustrates why a special cold-climate configuration must be separately specified. It says the standard machine configuration is intended for −18°C to 43°C; special configurations for other temperatures may be available. The manual says the standard engine can start to −18°C and that a cold-weather starting kit is available for colder locations, but does not publish that kit’s minimum start temperature.[22]

The Cat 336 GC manual specifies insulated breather hoses and a heated canister for C4.4 and C7.1 engines below −25°C; a frozen breather can increase crankcase pressure and cause oil leakage.[134]

Cold-weather operation also changes the excavator start and warm-up procedure. The 349F/352F manual calls for at least 10 minutes at low idle before other operations in cold conditions. Below 0°C, it advises approximately 30 minutes of engine warm-up, with additional time below −18°C or if hydraulic functions are sluggish; it says hydraulic oil should exceed 25°C before work. Caterpillar’s generic procedure similarly calls for progressive, low-throttle hydraulic cycling and light-load operation until systems reach normal temperature.[22][17][16]

**For Cat excavators, the decision is configuration-specific: Cat documents a credible −40°C engine, drive, undercarriage and lubricant-preparation regime, but the published Cat 395 cold-start limit and the 6015–6090 hydraulic-oil table rule out treating standard excavators or shovels as blanket −40°C machines.**[17][1][22]

Caterpillar offers several excavator fuel-management features. The Cat 395 has Power, Smart and Eco modes; Cat says Smart mode automatically adjusts power for highest fuel efficiency. ### Caterpillar haul trucks

Caterpillar’s relevant haul-truck range includes off-highway models from the 768C through 772, larger 785D, 793D, 793F, 795F and 797 variants, and Unit Rig MT3300–MT6300 mining trucks. The Cat 777F is an earthmoving off-highway truck classified as a dumper under ISO 6165:2001: a self-propelled wheeled machine with a rigid frame and wheel steering, with an open body intended to transport and dump loose material.[2] The Cat 793 is a 240-tonne-payload mining truck with a gross machine weight of 404,000 kg and Cat C175-16 engine gross power of 1,976 kW.[17][2][14]

For the Cat 777F, Caterpillar publishes several −40°C component-fluid entries. The engine-crankcase table lists Cat DEO-ULS Cold Weather SAE 0W-40 from −40°C to 40°C. The powershift-transmission table lists Cat TDTO Cold Weather SAE 0W-20 from −40°C to 10°C. The hoist, brake and torque-converter system has Cat TDTO Cold Weather SAE 0W-20 listed from −40°C to 40°C (−40°F to 104°F), and the steering system has SAE 0W-20 listed over the same ambient range.[17][2]

The critical limitation is the differential, front-wheel and final-drive system. Caterpillar’s fluids publication lists FDAO SYN Multi-Grade from −25°C to 50°C for those components. The 777F manual requires differential warm-up below −10°C and says that below −25°C, or below −35°C when using FDAO SYN, the owner must consult a Cat dealer; it warns that failure to warm the oil before operation will damage the machine.[17][2]

**For Cat rigid haul trucks, the axle/differential/final-drive arrangement is the selection-critical −40°C issue: several engine, transmission, steering, hoist and brake-fluid entries reach −40°C, while the cited differential/final-drive oil range does not.**[17][2]

The 777F gives further cold-start requirements. Below −18°C, Caterpillar recommends additional cold-weather aids, which may include a coolant heater, fuel heater or extra battery capacity. Below −23°C, the manual directs the operator to consult the dealer or Caterpillar’s cold-weather publication. The engine ECM enters cold-start strategy when coolant is below 20°C; it increases low idle, limits engine power and adjusts injection timing and duration for white-smoke cleanup.[2]

The truck must also be warmed in operation. Caterpillar directs 777F operators to use fourth gear or lower until differential oil reaches 38°C (100°F).[2] The manual says to run the engine at low rpm until normal operating temperature is reached after cold-mode operation.

It cautions that ice, snow and wet conditions make roads slick, requiring reduced speed, and it warns that a frozen load dumped on a slope can tip the truck.[2]

The Cat 793 has several cold-weather options, but its published cab and coolant specifications still need careful reading. The 793’s standard extended-life coolant is stated to protect to −35°C. Optional antifreeze/coolant protection to −50°C and an engine coolant-and-oil heater for cold-weather starts are available. The published heater/defroster ambient capability is down to −30°C, with an 11 kW heater/defroster rating.[14]

The 793 therefore has coolant protection and heating options that can be specified for a −40°C site, but the listed heater/defroster ambient capability is 10°C warmer than the target condition. The purchase specification should require cabin heat and defrost performance at the actual design temperature, not merely the availability of a heated seat or engine heater.[14]

The Cat 793 product page states mechanical availability consistently greater than 90%, alongside longer-life components, extended service intervals and easier maintenance. It also describes a modular HVAC system, modular radiator, remote flash and remote troubleshooting intended to reduce software-related downtime by up to 50%. These are manufacturer claims without a Mongolia or −40°C measurement period, but they are relevant serviceability provisions when assessing a supported fleet.[14]

The Cat 777F has basic cold-weather safety features. Its cab has adjustable heating controls, and heated exterior mirrors may be fitted. The retarder guide is based on 32°C ambient temperature and says actual retarding capability varies with outside temperature, rolling resistance, load, cooling-system condition, tyre size and altitude. The manual also says aggressive retarder application on slippery roads can cause wheel lock and power-train damage.[2]

Caterpillar has demonstrated mine-scale truck support in Mongolia. The Ukhaa Khudag example includes Cat 793D, 785C and 777D fleet support and a nearby Gobi Distribution Centre. At Oyu Tolgoi, Caterpillar material identifies parts, maintenance, on-site support, trained technicians and a 5,000 m² parts warehouse two hours from the mine. These examples are strongest for remote mine support rather than for a defined Ulaanbaatar service-centre promise.[4][26]

Cat haul-truck fuel features are substantial, but the published values have unlike bases. The 777F has Normal and Economy fuel-consumption modes. The Cat 793 is marketed as over 10% more fuel efficient and as having zero fuel burn during retarding. Caterpillar does not state the comparison truck, duty cycle, payload, ambient temperature or test period for the 793’s 10% claim, so it is not a −40°C fuel forecast.[2][14]

The Cat Performance Handbook provides fuel-use benchmarks under defined idle assumptions. It lists the Cat 770G at 5.22, 6.7 and 8.18 gal/h; the 772G at 5.88, 7.6 and 9.33 gal/h; the 773G at 7.66, 9.92 and 12.17 gal/h; the 775G at 8.26, 10.73 and 13.21 gal/h; and the 785-08 at 17.65, 23.3 and 28.95 gal/h for low, medium and high consumption cases. Caterpillar says these estimates use VisionLink data by region and assume 50%, 30% and 10% idle time respectively.[24]

The values above should not be compared against another manufacturer’s litres per tonne, litres per hour, litres per cycle or fuel-per-pass claim. Fuel use for mining trucks changes with gross vehicle weight, speed, rolling and grade resistance, rimpull, payload, tyre condition, engine condition, idling, weather, traffic and operator behaviour. Uphill consumption is higher because the engine must overcome gravity; downhill consumption can be minimal where gravity assists movement.[57][58]

The support decision for a Cat haul fleet is consequently clear. A Cat dealer relationship and Mongolia mine examples are positive selection factors, but the cold-climate truck package must include a written axle/differential warm-up procedure, fluid selection by compartment, heater arrangement, cold-start electrical capacity, cab-performance requirement and a stocked mine-site kit. The support scope must cover fuel filters, water separators, starter and charging parts, preheat components, hoses, seals, brake and steering parts, tyre-service contingencies, and diagnostics.[2]

The documented Ukhaa Khudag Cat support example covered 78 haul trucks in total: 16 Cat 793Ds, 25 Cat 785Cs and 37 Cat 777Ds. This is evidence of support experience across a substantial mixed Cat mining-truck population in Mongolia.[26]

Caterpillar’s Category 4 guidance adds a cold-soak requirement relevant to haul trucks: after a shutdown of at least 16 hours, the engine cools to outside temperature. Idling keeps the engine and transmission compartments warm but does not keep hydraulic systems warm.[32]

Caterpillar directs weekly inspection of hoses, tyres and fan-drive belts, plus electrical wiring and connections, in cold conditions. It also directs hydraulic-hose conditioning before temperatures fall below −40°C by operating for at least one hour with hydraulic oil at 66°C and oil having a −54°C pour point.[32]

The Cat 793 offers a 100% airless electric-start option that eliminates air-system maintenance and improves uptime.[14]

### Extreme cold

At −40°C, diesel fuel economy is an operating-system result rather than an engine-brochure result. Cold conditions increase the fuel consumed before productive work begins through preheat, warm-up, extended idling, battery and starter stress, fuel treatment and cold-related downtime. Low temperatures also affect fuel, coolant, oil and starting systems, making diesel combustion harder.[5][85][86]

Cold diesel fuel can wax, gel or form ice-related filter restrictions. Caterpillar explains that cloud point is the temperature at which wax components begin to solidify, potentially filling and plugging filters. It says fuel cloud point and low-temperature flow/CFPP must not exceed the lowest expected ambient temperature. For a −40°C operation, that means fuel must be selected and batch-tested for a temperature no warmer than −40°C.[17][87]

Caterpillar also specifies a margin for pour point: it should be at least 6°C below the lowest ambient needed for engine start and operation. Applied to a −40°C requirement, the derived minimum pour-point target is −46°C or lower. Caterpillar says No. 1 fuel or No. 1-D may be necessary for extremely cold operation because of lower pour points.[17]

Fuel water is a separate winter risk. Caterpillar gives an example in which fuel holding 100 ppm dissolved water at 18°C may hold only 30 ppm at 4°C. When fuel temperature falls rapidly to freezing, the water can form fine ice particles that fill and plug filters, stopping fuel flow. Daily draining of water separators and fuel filters is therefore a reliability task, not just a housekeeping task.[17][2]

Cold fuel must still lubricate the injection system. Caterpillar says improper viscosity can impair atomization and spray pattern, causing poor combustion and loss of performance; fuel below 1.4 cSt at the injection pump or unit injector can cause excessive scuffing and seizure. For distillate fuel engines, it recommends 1.4–4.5 cSt at rotary injection pumps and 1.4–20 cSt at other injection pumps.[17]

Cetane number affects starting. Caterpillar says the starting temperature can improve by approximately 7–8°C for every 10-point increase in cetane number, and that higher cetane may be required for cold weather or high altitude. At Mongolian mine elevation and −40°C, fuel purchasing should therefore specify both low-temperature flow properties and cetane, not only sulfur content or nominal diesel grade.[17]

Winter fuel blending has a trade-off. Caterpillar says suppliers typically blend No. 1 and No. 2 diesel in winter to meet regional cold-flow needs. It also says lighter winter fuel has lower viscosity, cloud point and pour point, but lighter fuels may not produce rated power and can reduce fuel-system component life through reduced lubricity.[17]

Biodiesel adds another constraint. Caterpillar says B100 has about 5–8% less energy per gallon than No. 2 diesel, while B20 or B30 has 1–2% less energy content. It also says biodiesel has higher cloud point, pour point and CFPP than diesel and may gel or plug filters at low temperatures, with risk generally increasing at higher blend levels.[17]

Caterpillar identifies alternative cold-weather fuels intended for operating temperatures down to −54°C, including JP-5, JP-8, Jet A and Jet A-1, subject to the required distillate-fuel specification. These alternatives are lighter than No. 2 diesel and require minimum cetane of 40; Cat says not to use fuel below 1.2 cSt at 40°C and may require fuel cooling to maintain at least 1.4 cSt at the injection pump. Jet A has a freezing-point requirement of −40°C (−40°F), while Jet A-1 has a freezing-point requirement of −47°C (−52.6°F); the fuel purchaser and supplier may agree on other freezing points.[17]

**The fuel rule for a Mongolian −40°C mine is simple: specify cold-flow performance, cetane, viscosity, cleanliness and water control together, because a fuel that flows at −40°C but lacks lubricity or has poor ignition quality can still reduce power, damage injection equipment or stop the fleet.**[17]

Fuel cleanliness is a direct reliability control. Caterpillar recommends fuel at ISO 18/16/13 cleanliness or cleaner when dispensed into a machine tank, with 4 µm absolute filtration at the dispensing point. It also calls for water removal to 500 ppm or less and recommends bulk fuel filter/coalescer units that remove particles and water in one pass, with fueling rates from 50 to 300 gpm.[17]

Cold operation also affects engine condition. Caterpillar says fuel and oil are not completely burned when the engine operates below normal temperature, which can create soft carbon deposits on valve stems. It says the engine should be run until coolant reaches at least 82°C (180°F) to limit deposits and protect engine components.[16] It also warns that excessive idling can cause water in crankcase oil, corrosion, sludge, injector fouling, deposits and increased oil consumption.[16][17]

A mine should therefore separate productive fuel from winter-support fuel. Productive fuel is fuel consumed while loading, hauling, digging or dumping. Winter-support fuel is preheat energy, warm-up fuel, idle fuel required to protect equipment or maintain availability, and fuel lost to delays. Combining them into a single litres-per-hour number hides whether the problem is the machine, the road, the fuel batch, dispatch, maintenance or operator practice.

Parking-heater fuel and fuel consumed during delayed-start rework are additional winter-support consumption categories to record separately from productive digging or hauling.[138][134]

Judgment — Track winter fuel performance in four measures: litres per productive tonne moved; litres per operating hour; litres per idle/preheat hour; and delay hours attributable to cold-related causes. Record ambient temperature, machine cold-soak duration, fuel batch and CFPP result, payload, road grade, rolling resistance, cycle time, operator ID and engine idle time against each shift.

The need for those fields is supported by mining-truck fuel research. Gross vehicle weight, truck velocity, total resistance—rolling resistance plus grade resistance—and rimpull are identified as critical factors affecting mining-truck fuel consumption. Another study identifies tyre use, vehicle age and maintenance, idling, engine parameters, shift patterns, temperature, humidity, wind, traffic and driver behaviour as material factors.[57][58]

Road grade is especially important in Mongolia. A study of Erdenet routes measured gradient sections from 146.5 m to 989.7 m long with gradients of 4.6–5.7%. The same source says increasing road gradient reduces truck speed and raises fuel consumption and tyre wear while worsening safety.[88]

Fuel consumption changes by direction as well as road design. A mining-truck study states that uphill fuel consumption is higher because the engine overcomes gravity, while downhill consumption is minimal when gravity assists travel. At Erdenet, empty-direction speeds were 40% higher than loaded-direction speeds on the processing-plant route when July and December averages were compared.[58][88]

Truck size alone does not answer the fuel question. A production-scenario study found that fuel consumption per tonne doubled in larger trucks as pit size increased. Its results show why a 95-tonne rigid truck, a 100-tonne articulated hauler and a 240–290-tonne mining truck should be compared against mine production requirements and haul geometry, not against a simple brochure fuel claim.[58][15][10][14]

Dispatch and queue control also matter. A mining-truck energy study identifies empty idle time in loading queues as a significant fuel inefficiency and names improved dispatching, muck-pile shape and size, and better shovel or loader operating skill as ways to reduce bottlenecks. A cold-weather fleet should not use extended idle as the default substitute for dispatch discipline or proper preheat.[57][85]

Reliable −40°C availability requires heated parking or workshop capacity and shore power or generator capacity for overnight preheat. Truck winter preparation must also prevent moisture freezing in air systems.

For haul-truck comparisons across routes of different length or elevation, record litres per tonne-kilometre alongside litres per productive tonne, operating hour, and idle/preheat hour.

### Operator preparation

Operator training for a Mongolian cold-weather fleet must be equipment-specific and workforce-specific. Cat publishes Level I and Level II training paths, and Cat machines provide coaching and onboard manuals. Komatsu provides fuel-monitoring and operator-identification tools. Volvo’s supplied material does not establish Mongolia-specific operator training. None of the supplied materials demonstrates a complete operator-training programme adapted to Mongolian language, local workforce needs and −40°C mine procedures for all three manufacturers.[27][9][19]

The training requirement should be attached to the purchase contract. First, operators need machine-family induction and competency assessment. Second, they need winter operating modules: cold inspection, battery and heater checks, fuel-water draining, controlled starts, hydraulic and drivetrain warm-up, ice and traction management, visibility, retarding on slippery roads, frozen-material dumping risk, shutdown and parking. Third, maintenance personnel need model-specific diagnostics, heater systems, winter fluid management, DEF handling where applicable, and recovery practices.

Caterpillar’s 777F manual shows the relevant operational content. Operators must drain water and sediment from the tank and water from the fuel-system separator. The manual requires reduced speed on ice or snow, gradual retarder application on slippery roads and avoidance of dumping a frozen load on a slope. Those topics should be built into both classroom and supervised field assessment.[2]

DEF introduces another training and logistics requirement on emissions-equipped equipment. Caterpillar says DEF is 67.5% water and 32.5% urea; 32.5% DEF has a freeze point of −11.5°C. Machine-mounted tanks are designed to freeze and thaw and have heaters, while bulk DEF should be stored above freezing and dispensing tanks should be heated in cold climates.[85][17][86]

The mine should distinguish a local service centre from a local dealer office, warehouse or sales presence. A service centre for this procurement means a facility with qualified technicians, diagnostic hardware and software, lifting and tooling capacity, component-repair capability, stocked parts, field-service vehicles and a stated response model. A parts warehouse means inventory that can be identified by part number, quantity, ownership and replenishment time. Neither should be inferred from a dealer locator, global parts network or a general statement of support.

Caterpillar has the strongest documented Mongolia mine-support examples, including the Gobi Distribution Centre, parts support near Oyu Tolgoi and historical Ulaanbaatar facilities. Transwest has documented Ulaanbaatar warehouse stock for undercarriage components and multi-brand repair capability. Volvo’s supplied parts evidence identifies Europe stock and worldwide carriers rather than a Mongolia warehouse.[4][26][3][33][61][39]

The spare-parts strategy should therefore have three layers. First, keep consumables and cold-failure parts at the mine: fuel filters, water-separator elements, air filters, lubricants, coolants, hoses, fittings, seals, belts, batteries, starter components, alternators, heater elements, sensors and electrical connectors. Second, keep high-value, predictable failure components within the Mongolian support network: pumps, injectors, turbocharger assemblies, hydraulic valves, electronic controllers, brake components, wheel-end parts and suspension items. Third, define emergency international supply routes and customs responsibility for long-lead components.

Caterpillar explicitly advises keeping spare filters available for fuel gelling or freezing and replacing a gelled filter rather than heating it. Its 777F schedule requires water-separator and fuel-tank draining every 10 service hours or daily. This supports stocking filters and separator elements at the mine for all diesel equipment regardless of brand.[85][86][2]

Caterpillar offers identifiable Level I and Level II operator courses and jobsite, learning-centre and virtual delivery; Wagner Asia and Energy Resources also conducted Caterpillar-oriented technical maintenance training in Mongolia. The documented Mongolia programme is maintenance training, not excavator or haul-truck operator training. Komatsu’s cited evidence identifies PC1250 fuel-monitoring and Operator Identification System functions and Transwest technical support, not a Mongolia-adapted operator course. The cited Volvo evidence identifies no delivered Mongolia operator course.[27][28][9][61][39]

Contract acceptance must require practical operator and maintainer training in Mongolian or the workforce’s operating language. The modules must cover cold inspection; battery, coolant, oil and heater checks; fuel-water draining; controlled starts; hydraulic and drivetrain warm-up; ice, traction and visibility management; retarding; frozen-load dumping; parking and shutdown; and early reporting of hose, seal, battery, heater and fuel-filter faults.[17][2][85]

#### Excavators

The relevant Russian excavator alternatives fall into two different classes. IZ-KARTEX/UZTM-KARTEX EKG machines are large electric rope shovels for mine production. UMG machines are wheeled and crawler hydraulic excavators in much smaller diesel classes. They should not be compared as substitutes solely because both are called excavators.

“EKG” denotes a quarry or mining crawler excavator; these single-bucket electromechanical face shovels use trailing mine power rather than an onboard diesel engine.[157]

IZ-KARTEX’s published EKG range includes the EKG-10, EKG-12K, EKG-15M, EKG-18R, EKG-20KM and EKG-32R. The table lists bucket capacities from 10 to 35 m³ and operating masses from 410 to 1,050 t. It identifies the EKG-10 as a rope shovel with a 10 m³ bucket and 410 t operating mass; the EKG-12K as a 12 m³, 420 t rope shovel; the EKG-15M as 15 m³ and 700 t; the EKG-18R as 20 m³ and 720 t; the EKG-20KM as 25 m³ and 760 t; and the EKG-32R as 35 m³ and 1,050 t.[79]

UZGM lists the EKG-5A electric rope shovel with a 5.2 m³ bucket, 200 t mass, 250 kW mains motor and 6,000 V/50 Hz supply; its configuration-dependent operating range is −45°C to +45°C.[158]

The EKG-10, EKG-12K and EKG-15M families are listed for a 6 kV/50 Hz electrical supply.[159]

IZ-KARTEX has supplied an EKG-15M in a northern ХЛ climatic configuration; the cited GOST 15150-69 climatic-execution reference specifies −40°C to +40°C for У1 and −60°C to +40°C for ХЛ. Those climatic-execution ranges are not a documented full-system −40°C operating result for a particular offered shovel.[160][159]

The EKG-32R is intended for benches up to 20 m and for transport equipment with 190–360 t payload capacity. Its principal bucket is 35 m³ and its working mass is 1,050 t.[79] This is a production shovel for a large open-pit system, not a general-purpose hydraulic excavator.

Its truck fleet must be selected around shovel pass-match, bench geometry, fragmentation, electrical supply, cable handling, shovel relocation requirements, and the maintenance capacity to support a large electric drive system.

IZ-KARTEX has a substantial historical supply record. The manufacturer reports 3,804 mining excavators delivered to mining enterprises in Russia, the CIS and other countries over 60 years.[79] It also identifies Mongolia as a country where its excavators can find application in mining iron ore, coal, copper, gold, diamonds, bauxite, apatite and construction materials.[79] The second statement identifies Mongolia as a target mining market; Erdenet provides the more useful evidence of Mongolian operation.

Erdenet commissioned two Russian-made EKG-12K rope shovels in December 2020. Each was described as weighing 430 t with a 24 t payload.[42] Erdenet had procured four EKG-12K machines since 2018: one supplied in 2018, one in 2019, and two commissioned in December 2020.[42] Erdenet’s open-pit mine manager stated that the company chose IZ-KARTEX because the machines were optimally adapted to Mongolian mine conditions, and its chief engineer described IZ-KARTEX machines as reliable and user-friendly.[42]

One EKG-12K at Erdenet was reported after six months of operation to have confirmed the manufacturer’s declared technical parameters, with no complaints reported at a scheduled inspection.[96] Erdenet was also reported to use 11 different IZ-KARTEX earth-moving machinery models.[42] These are meaningful operator-side observations: they show that IZ-KARTEX is not merely a theoretical Mongolia option and that EKG-12K units have been repeatedly acquired by a major Mongolian mine. They are site-specific operating testimony rather than a published −40°C availability result.

Erdenet’s general director said the EKG-12K met the mine’s evaluation criteria and that direct contracting shortened delivery.[156]

Other operating examples support the EKG line’s mining role. Two EKG-20K excavators had worked at Muruntau from 2014, excavating ore and hard-rock overburden, with a reported maximum achieved monthly production of 560 thousand m³ per month.[79] An EKG-20KM entered service at Lebedinsky GOK in 2014 and excavated iron ore and hard-rock overburden.

The source reports in-situ rock density of 3.4 t/m³, loosened density up to 2.5 t/m³, and daily production reaching 15,000 m³ with appropriate mine organization and blast preparation.[79] These figures help screen production class and geology fit, but they do not substitute for an Erdenet-like duty-cycle trial at the intended Mongolian mine.

The EKG-10 is also a mine-scale crawler excavator. A documented EKG-10 at PAO Uralasbest was commissioned on 28 January 2004. Its listed specifications include a 10.0 m³ bucket, 395,000 kg working mass, 800 kW mains motor and 160 kW transformer.[43] The mains motor and transformer matter for fleet evaluation: the EKG-10 is electrically powered, so diesel litres per hour, diesel cold-start performance, and diesel-specific fuel-consumption figures are not applicable to the shovel itself.

Published hoist-drive ratings are 270 kW × 2 for the EKG-10, 350 kW × 2 for the EKG-12K and 500 kW × 2 for the EKG-15M; these are drive ratings, not measured electricity consumption.[159]

The EKG-5A specification lists a 40 or 63 kVA transformer.[158]

The Uralasbest record provides practical maintenance data for EKG-10 No. 333. From 01.01.2020 to 01.04.2022, undercarriage maintenance and repairs totalled 966.34 hours, including 657.34 hours associated with hoist-travel generator work and related tasks, 182 hours for main-drive and travel-motor inspections, 84 hours removing and installing a support-wheel cover, and 43 hours for other inspections and work.[43] From 01.01.2022 to 31.08.2023, EKG-10 No.

333 had five unscheduled repairs and 57 maintenance operations; undercarriage work comprised four unscheduled repairs to the right travel motor, one unscheduled repair replacing left travel-reducer brake discs, and 22 maintenance operations. The record separately reports 4.5 hours for mechanical undercarriage defects and 39 hours for electrical defects in that later period.[43]

Technical availability is the percentage of a defined period during which equipment is ready for use; mean restoration time is the expected time to restore operability after failure.[43] For procurement, these are the correct measures to demand from an EKG supplier, alongside operating hours, ambient-temperature distribution, failure mode, repair location, critical-spare consumption, and restoration time.

The published Uralasbest intervention counts and repair hours should be used to identify maintainable systems and parts requirements, not converted into an availability percentage without the machine’s operating hours and downtime basis.

#### Haul trucks

The Russian-built quarry-truck field is concentrated in the 35–100 t range, with one KAMAZ model carrying an explicit −40°C ambient specification. This is useful for auxiliary haulage, smaller pits, satellite pits, construction haulage, waste movement, and selected ore routes. It is not a direct substitute for the 190–360 t truck match stated for the EKG-32R.[79]

KAMAZ’s Atlant family includes 6×4, 6×6 and 8×4 configurations with stated payloads from 39 to 100 t.[45][109] The KAMAZ-4554, Atlant 49, is intended for quarry work and hauling coal, overburden and ores.

It has a 49 t payload, 72 t gross weight, and 35 m³ body.[45][109] A 25 April 2024 account describes Atlant 49 as a four-axle truck with a 35 m³ coal body, whereas the published KAMAZ material describes it as 6×4; the tender must reconcile this configuration discrepancy before selecting driveline spares, tyres, axle assemblies or road design.[110][45] It includes an interactive hydraulic-equipment monitoring system, overload and roll control, data-transmitting monitoring and fault warnings.[45][109]

The KAMAZ-4555-0000030-BC, Atlant 50, is the most important Russian truck for the −40°C screen. KAMAZ describes it as a 6×4 quarry truck designed for quarry work and ambient temperatures from −40°C to +40°C in climate version U1.[45][109] At publication on 25 April 2024, Atlant 50 had not been shown publicly before and KAMAZ planned to launch serial production in the following year.[110] The truck has a 50 t rear balancing-spring-suspension carrying capacity and a 30 m³ universal body for overburden and various rock types, with platform heating; KAMAZ says a coal-body design can also be developed.[45][109] It has FOPS and ROPS cab protection and an arrangement allowing engine and system servicing without raising the body.[45][109]

The Atlant 50 uses a 13 L KAMAZ R6 diesel engine described by KAMAZ as economical and without exhaust-gas aftertreatment.[45] That statement does not provide a fuel-consumption rate. It is therefore unsuitable for comparing diesel cost per operating hour, litre per tonne, or litre per tonne-kilometre against another truck fleet.

TONAR is a Russian manufacturer of quarry dump trucks for open-pit mining.[111][112] Relevant models include the 45 t TONAR-4525/45251 family, the 60 t TONAR-7501, the 55 t TONAR-7504, the 35 t articulated TONAR-7507, and the 45 t articulated TONAR-7508.[93][47] Procurement status matters: TONAR-7504 is listed as in development and TONAR-7508 as under testing, while TONAR-7507 is listed at 35 t and priced at RUB44,000,000 including VAT.[93]

The TONAR-7501 is a 6×4 quarry truck with a stated 60 t payload and gross mass approaching 88 t. It uses a 15 L Cummins QSX15 turbo-diesel rated at 540 hp and 2,344 N·m.[47][112] Its standard equipment includes an independent heater, while its body uses exhaust-gas heating to prevent material freezing to the body.[47][112] The truck is also described as operating in mining conditions involving climbs, descents, extreme weather, and near-continuous short-haul work.[112]

The TONAR-7501’s rear suspension uses elastic rubber elements. The manufacturer-side description says it has greater cold resistance than hydropneumatic suspension and effectively needs no maintenance.[113] This is a suspension comparison, rather than a published whole-truck −40°C certification.

It does not resolve cold-start performance, fuel waxing, battery capacity, tyre behaviour, brake-fluid and hydraulic-fluid operation, cab defrosting, or truck availability at the required ambient temperature.

TONAR’s fuel figures use different bases. For the TONAR-7501, specific fuel consumption is stated as no more than 210 g/kWh at maximum power.[112] In a reported 4 km haul comparison against HOWO, a TONAR-45251 carried 39 t per trip, made 30 trips per shift, consumed 120 L per shift, and moved 1,170 t per shift; HOWO carried 19.5 t per trip, consumed 96 L per shift, and moved 585 t per shift.[47] In a separate 1.2 km dolomite-haul trial, TONAR-4525 carried 39 t, made 28 trips per shift, used 140 L per shift and recorded 0.053 L/t-km; the BelAZ-7522 comparator carried 26 t, used 125 L per shift and recorded 0.072 L/t-km.[47]

These trials show that productive output and fuel use must be read together. The TONAR examples have more tonnes per trip and more total shift fuel than their comparators. Their published bases differ by truck, haul distance, material, payload, comparator, and reported measure. Neither trial is a −40°C result. A winter evaluation must therefore collect litres per productive hour, litres per tonne, litres per tonne-kilometre, idle litres, warm-up fuel, payload distribution, distance, grade, rolling resistance, tyre condition, ambient temperature, fuel properties, and delay time on the same route.

BELAZ is an important regional alternative but is Belarusian rather than Russian-made. Erdenet used 40 t BelAZ-548A trucks from 1978 to 1988, introduced 110 t BelAZ-7519 trucks in 1988, used 120 t BelAZ-75122 trucks from 1994 onward, and has employed 130 t BelAZ-75131 trucks with KTA 50 engines since 2010.[88] United Belaz Machinery LLC described itself in 2020 as BelAZ’s official and exclusive distributor in Mongolia and said it could supply 30–450 t trucks across Mongolia.[49] These facts make BelAZ a regionally proven Mongolian haulage benchmark, but it must be classified separately from Russian-made alternatives in a country-of-origin or trade-preference evaluation.

BELAZ’s Russian-engine programme illustrates the difference between harsh-condition intent and a −40°C operational result. BELAZ said its 220 t-payload BELAZ-75304 began a one-year pilot operation at the Krasnoborodsky open-pit coal mine in Kuzbass under varying production and climatic conditions.[48] The manufacturer said the truck had a 2,400 hp Kolomna Plant diesel engine adapted to extreme conditions, while the customer described testing to adapt the machine to winter periods with sharp temperature changes.[48] Neither statement supplies a −40°C result for Mongolia.

Service support is the central Russian-truck constraint. No Mongolian-worker-adapted operator-training programme is identified for KAMAZ, TONAR or the Russian truck alternatives. Truck configuration does not replace training: KAMAZ’s Atlant 49 monitoring systems and TONAR’s retarder and interaxle-differential-lock controls require operating procedures, fault-response training, winter stopping-distance rules, payload-control discipline, and slope-specific retarding instruction.[45][109][113] The truck supplier should provide Mongolian-language operator and maintainer manuals, instructor-led practical training, winter-operation drills, competence testing, and a trainer seat or equivalent practical-training arrangement.

### Selection verdict and outstanding checks

The Russian alternative should be split by application. For a large electric shovel requirement, EKG-12K deserves a formal bid because Erdenet has operated multiple units and repeatedly procured the model.[42] For a smaller diesel-excavator role, UMG may be evaluated where 15–42 t machines match the mine plan, but these machines are not equivalents to EKG shovels in production scale.[80] For 35–60 t quarry hauling, TONAR and KAMAZ are credible candidates; for a 50 t truck that must meet the stated −40°C ambient target, Atlant 50 is the only Russian truck in the supplied product material with an explicit −40°C to +40°C specification.[45][93][47]

The Russian fleet should remain ring-fenced or secondary rather than become the primary standard fleet. The deciding criterion is winter availability coupled to repair recovery. EKG-12K has actual Mongolian mine deployment, EKG-20K and EKG-20KM list an automatic-lubrication-system operating range of −45°C to +50°C, KAMAZ Atlant 50 has an explicit −40°C to +40°C ambient-temperature range, and Russian components demonstrably move into Mongolia.[42][121][45][97] The available supplier support descriptions place TONAR service and delivery in Russia, BELAZ technical support in Kuzbass, and KAMAZ parts supply in global or non-Mongolian channels.[93][48][114][115]

**The fleet should not standardise on Russian excavators or haul trucks for downtime-critical −40°C service until the supplier makes Mongolia-based winter support contractual: a named Ulaanbaatar service location or mine-site field team, resident technicians, a critical-spares list held in Mongolia, emergency replenishment terms, and a model-specific −40°C commissioning and acceptance test.**[45][42][44][111][114]

Judgment — The acceptance test should run in the coldest practical operating period and reproduce the mine’s actual duty rather than a static cold-start demonstration. For diesel trucks and hydraulic excavators, it should include cold soak, start, oil and coolant warm-up, brake and steering function, hydraulic response, body hoist cycles, loaded and empty traction, cab defrosting, telematics connectivity, fuel-water separation, and recovery from a planned shutdown. For electric rope shovels, it should include cable handling, electrical cabinet heating, insulation checks, traction, swing, hoist and crowd performance, lubrication circulation, and restoration following a realistic electrical fault.

For fuel comparison, the mine should require a common Winter Duty Cycle. The cycle should record the same payload target, material density, haul distance, grade, rolling resistance, loading passes, queue time, idle policy, ambient temperature, fuel specification, and operator shift length for every candidate. The output should be reported as litres per operating hour, litres per tonne, litres per tonne-kilometre, warm-up litres per shift, and productive tonnes per hour. This prevents a maximum-power g/kWh figure, a no-load road norm in L/100 km, and a loaded mine-shift result from being treated as comparable measures. KAMAZ publishes summer and winter no-load norms in L/100 km for KAMAZ-45141A, 33.5 and 36.9; 45143, 26 and 28.6; 43255, 22 and 24.2; 55102, 26.5 and 29.2; 55111, 27 and 29.7; 65111, 29.8 and 32.8; 65115, 27.4 and 30.1; 6520, 29.2 and 32.1; 65201, 37.1 and 40.8; 6522, 35.6 and 39.2; and 6540, 34 and 37.4, respectively.[112][47][118][116]

A mine-planning study reinforces the need for this route-specific approach. The study found fuel consumption per tonne doubled as truck size and pit size increased in its scenarios.[58] Its large-truck case reduced unit count but increased stripping ratio by 7% and reduced NPV by 20% relative to the medium scenario; the small-truck case increased stripping ratio by 3% and increased NPV by 11%.[58] It also found that small trucks can increase fleet count by 150%, while larger trucks can reduce unit count by 30% but require more specialised labour and face stricter regulation.[58] The correct truck class therefore depends on the planned pit geometry, road width, ramp grade, excavator pass-match, maintenance labour, and winter availability—not on capacity alone.

Russian equipment could change from a ring-fenced option to a primary candidate if a supplier provides all of the following in a binding proposal:

1. A named model and full −40°C configuration, including cold-start, coolant, engine-oil, hydraulic-oil, fuel heating or conditioning, batteries, cab heating and defrosting, body heating, electrical and brake-system provisions.
2. A published or witnessed −40°C acceptance test at the mine’s altitude and production duty cycle.
3. A Mongolia-based service arrangement naming the Ulaanbaatar workshop, mine-site mobile-service coverage, technician count, diagnostic tooling, escalation path, and response-time commitment.
4. A Mongolia-held critical-spares package covering winter failure items, filters, electrical components, hydraulic and cooling-system parts, tyres or tyre-service arrangements, and major-component exchange terms.
5. Mongolian-language operator and maintainer training, practical commissioning training, winter procedures, competency assessment, and refresher requirements.
6. A common Winter Duty Cycle fuel and productivity trial against the selected Caterpillar, Komatsu, or Volvo CE alternative.

The Russian truck screen should be run against equipment of the same haul class and route duty. Volvo CE’s A60 articulated hauler has a 55,000 kg payload capacity, 33.6 m³ SAE 2:1-heaped body, Volvo D16F engine rated at 495 kW at 1,800 rpm, and gross weight of 98,400 kg. Volvo CE states that the A60 operates in all seasons, terrains and applications and provides automatic traction control, Terrain Memory and 100% differential locks; it gives no temperature range.[40] Volvo CE also states that rigid haulers can be the better match for high-speed runs longer than 1 km because their tyres dissipate heat faster than articulated-hauler tyres.[82]

Russia has a usable trade and transport connection to Mongolia, proven IZ-KARTEX deliveries to Erdenet, and documented shipments of Russian excavator components into Mongolia.[96][42][97] EKG-12K is the strongest Russian excavator alternative for a large electric-shovel fleet, while KAMAZ Atlant 50 is the clearest Russian truck candidate for a stated −40°C ambient requirement.[42][45] The Russian option remains weaker on locally defined repair recovery: domestic Russian support claims, global export claims, and cross-border shipments do not identify Ulaanbaatar service capacity or Mongolia-held critical stock.[93][48][111][114] Regional trade terms can reduce import friction for eligible goods, but they do not create technicians, tools, or parts depots.[92][100]

The lightest listed IZ-KARTEX EKG rope shovel is 9.7 times the mass of the heaviest listed UMG crawler excavator: 410 t versus 42.1 t. The two Russian product groups therefore belong to fundamentally different loading and maintenance classes.[79][80]

The EKG-20K and EKG-20KM are a larger Russian rope-shovel option than EKG-12K. IZ-KARTEX states that their automatic lubrication system using modern lubricants operates from −45°C to +50°C. The EKG-20K is designed for 130–220 t dump trucks and has a stated 40 t bucket payload; the EKG-20KM is designed for the same truck class and has a stated 50 t bucket payload.[121]

IZ-KARTEX states that 16 EKG-20K, EKG-20KM and modified shovels were supplied during 2010–2019. Its listed deliveries include EKG-20K units to Navoi Mining and Metallurgy Combinat in 2014, 2018 and 2019, and EKG-20KM units to Lebedinsky GOK and Karelskiy Okatysh Mine during 2014–2017.[121] The presentation states an average vehicle-availability rate of 0.80–0.90 and productivity reaching 5.5 million m³ per year for the series.

IZ-KARTEX lists a 9–10 month manufacturing period and 50–70 days for turnkey assembly, commissioning and acceptance testing for the listed shovel models. It lists a higher-insulation, air-purified cab, a nominal 20-year service life, one complete overhaul, annual energy costs of KUSD 84–93 for EKG-20K and KUSD 70–81 for EKG-20KM, and average annual maintenance cost of KUSD 64 for each model.[121] Bogatyr Komir, a coal-mining company in Kazakhstan, commissioned an IZ-KARTEX EKG-15M in December 2022.[131]

UZTM-KARTEX demonstrated that EKG working equipment can be specified around the loading fleet: an EKG-15M supplied to Bogatyr Komir in 2022 received an 18 m³ bucket instead of the standard 15 m³ bucket at the customer’s request, and Bogatyr Komir stated that the machine suited its 130 t trucks. The EKG-10 has an established production and mining history: production began in 1984, IZ-KARTEX delivered its 500th unit on 25 January 2012, and the model represented 80% of the manufacturer’s excavator production during 2000–2011.[119]

A UMG dealer states that it sells Russian-made wheeled and crawler excavators and spare parts, provides pre-delivery preparation, warranty support and servicing, and can dispatch mobile service crews to the equipment location.[133] The dealer also states that it delivers equipment anywhere in the country.[133]

The selected Cat, Komatsu and Volvo CE excavator and haul-truck models still need written whole-machine cold-soak start and operating limits at −40°C, including cab heat, defrost, hydraulics, driveline and electrical performance. The current Ulaanbaatar workshop capability, field-response time, stocked part numbers and quantities, replenishment lead times, and Mongolian-language operator and maintainer training commitments need confirmation for each bidder. Comparable winter fuel consumption needs measurement on the proposed mine route at matched payload, material, idle policy and fuel specification. Russian-made excavator and haul-truck availability, Mongolia parts stock, trained support capacity, cross-border supply continuity and applicable trade or sanctions constraints also need confirmation.
