# Collaborative Learning Interface Design Across South Korea, Finland, and Mexico

## Executive summary

Adopt a configurable, teacher-legitimised collaboration model rather than one global peer-feedback pattern. Default to private or teacher-mediated correction and a clear escalation route in Mexico and South Korea, while enabling teacher-facilitated, task-visible formative discussion in Finland.

Mexico’s Power Distance score of 81, South Korea’s 60, and Finland’s 33 support this hierarchy-sensitive configuration as a country-level design hypothesis, not a rule for individual students.[1] Structured peer review with criteria, reviewer guidance, revision control, and optional identity shielding is justified in all three markets, because South Korean high-school students accepted or adapted 71% of peer suggestions after training, although this result does not compare anonymity, moderation, or display modes.[2] No documented Classting, Wilma, or Latin America Google Classroom implementation establishes market-comparable causal effects of anonymous feedback, teacher-moderation level, or public versus private correction on secondary students’ engagement, peer-interaction frequency, or assignment completion; product capabilities and region-wide deployment accounts should therefore not be represented as such effects.

## Cultural and educational context

Collaborative-interface choices should treat national culture as a comparative baseline for hypotheses about authority, face risk, disagreement, and feedback, while allowing schools, teachers, and students to select safer or more open interaction modes. Mexico has the most hierarchy-accepting national profile of the three markets, Finland the most egalitarian, and South Korea falls between them on the Power Distance comparison.[1]

**Interaction and measurement key.** The three visibility audiences are: **individual and authorised teacher**, where an individual learner’s work or correction is visible only to that learner and authorised teacher; **project group**, where it is visible to the members assigned to that project; and **whole class**, where it is visible to all class members. Individual feedback can be private while group feedback is available for the group to answer.[3] Teacher **publication approval** is the control that determines whether an item becomes visible to its selected audience; it is distinct from teacher observation or intervention after publication, because computer-supported peer-feedback processes can be monitored by the teacher.[4] For implementation evaluation, engagement means participating students divided by eligible students during a named task window; peer-interaction frequency means substantive student-to-student exchanges per student or per group during that window; and completion means submitted assignments divided by assigned assignments by a specified deadline.

Count a challenge as negotiation of an inconsistency or disagreement with a proposed solution or instruction, and count help-seeking as a question seeking possible solutions or thoughts from group members.[5]

### How do the relevant collectivism scores compare across South Korea

The Culture Factor defines the Individualism–Collectivism dimension as the strength of the ties that bind people to groups. On its 0–100 Individualism (IDV) scale, scores below 50 are interpreted as relatively collectivist and scores above 50 as relatively individualist.[1][6] The following comparison presents the current Minkov–Kaasa-based IDV values, historical Hofstede-style IDV values, and Power Distance values as distinct country-level indices.[1][7][8]

| Market | Current IDV score, 0–100 | Source interpretation | Comparative implication |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **South Korea** | 58 | Relatively individualist, alongside enduring Confucian family loyalty and jeong. [1] | Intermediate between Finland and Mexico on this version of IDV. |
| **Finland** | 75 | Individualist society with a comparatively loose social framework. [1] | Most individualist of the three on this comparison. |
| **Mexico** | 34 | Collectivistic society, characterized by long-term in-group loyalty, especially to extended family. [1] | Only market on the collectivist side of this current IDV midpoint rule. |

**Revised series —** Finland is furthest toward the individualist side, Mexico is on the collectivist side, and South Korea is between them: Finland (75) > South Korea (58) > Mexico (34), using the revised IDV scale and its midpoint interpretation.[1][6] **Historical Hofstede-style series —** Finland (63) > Mexico (30) > South Korea (18): South Korea ranks below Mexico on this historical index, unlike in the current Minkov–Kaasa-based series.[7][8]

The revised Individualism data use a 2022 Minkov–Kaasa study, whereas the historical Hofstede-style table uses a different concept and underlying data. Minkov–Kaasa Individualism and Hofstede Individualism are separate indices and are not combined into one trend or ranking.[7]

The current data were updated on the website in October 2023 for IDV and Long-Term Orientation, based on a replication and extension programme conducted in 2015–2016; the source also states that underlying data years vary by country and dimension.[6] These are country-level central tendencies, not measures of today’s secondary students.

The source itself notes that individual variation within a country can exceed average differences between countries, and it does not publish splits by age, education level, region, or profession.[1]

For collaborative-learning design, the valid use of IDV is to generate localisation hypotheses—not to assign group orientation, feedback preferences, or participation styles to students based on nationality. Mexico’s lower current IDV supports testing stronger in-group trust, relationship protection, and face-sensitive workflows; Finland’s higher IDV supports testing more autonomous and individually accountable workflows; South Korea should not be simplistically treated as either uniformly collectivist or uniformly individualist.[1]

The revised score comparison sits alongside Korean classroom observations rather than replacing them. A traditional Korean classroom account places the teacher at the top of the hierarchy and describes students as passive participants; it also reports little student–student dialogue, despite collective problem-solving and peer assistance.[9]

The Culture Factor defines collectivism as belonging to in-groups, including families and organisations, that look after members in exchange for loyalty.[10] Horizontal collectivism combines equality with interdependence, whereas vertical collectivism treats respect for authority as a key value; collectivism scores therefore do not by themselves distinguish peer equality from hierarchy.[11]

A Hofstede-based country table that reverse-coded Individualism reports collectivism scores of 82 for Korea and 37 for Finland, alongside Power Distance scores of 60 and 33 respectively.[12]

### How do power-distance dimensions compare across South Korea

Power Distance is the extent to which less powerful members of institutions and organisations expect and accept unequal distributions of power.[1] On the 0–100 scale used by The Culture Factor, 50 is the mid-level reference point.[6] The market ordering is clearer and stable across the supplied score tables.

| Market | Power Distance score, 0–100 | Published profile | Comparative implication |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Mexico** | 81 | Hierarchical society; inequalities are accepted, centralisation is popular, and subordinates expect direction. [1] | Highest hierarchy-acceptance baseline of the three. |
| **South Korea** | 60 | Moderately hierarchical; inequalities and centralisation are accepted, with Confucian respect for age and seniority. [1] | Intermediate hierarchy baseline. |
| **Finland** | 33 | Independent and egalitarian; hierarchy is mainly for convenience, superiors are accessible, and communication is direct and participative. [1] | Lowest hierarchy baseline and most egalitarian profile. |

Derived — The Power Distance ordering is Mexico (81) > South Korea (60) > Finland (33). On this national comparison, Mexico’s score is 48 points above Finland’s and South Korea’s is 27 points above Finland’s; these are arithmetic differences between country-level indicators, not estimates of differences between individual students.[1]

The profiles attach different institutional expectations to those scores. Mexico’s profile links hierarchy to respect for elders and a history of centralised authority.[1] South Korea’s profile links hierarchy to Confucian respect for age and seniority, including Korean honorifics.[1] Finland’s profile describes decentralised power, accessible superiors, coaching rather than commanding leadership, and participative communication.[1]

The numerical endpoint is not entirely standardized across explanatory sources: The Culture Factor FAQ describes a 0–100 scale with 50 as midpoint, while another account describes Hofstede’s national scale as 1–100.[6][13] This does not change the reported three-market ordering.

More importantly, national scores are relative comparative constructs, not a direct measure of a school’s authority structure. The model itself describes country scores as broad cultural maps with substantial within-country variation.[6][14]

**The practical comparison is therefore a hierarchy gradient, not a deterministic student profile: Mexico warrants the strongest test of visible teacher authority and teacher-mediated escalation, South Korea warrants structured teacher-endorsed collaboration, and Finland warrants teacher facilitation with substantial student agency.**[1]

In higher-power-distance settings, status-sensitive interaction, direct contradiction, and requesting help in front of peers can carry greater social consequence; lower power distance makes teacher–student dialogue and peer challenge more socially permissible. This makes public help requests and visible disagreement important localisation variables, not merely communication features.

### How do educational hierarchies and attitudes toward teacher authority differ

South Korea has the strongest classroom-specific authority account in the supplied material. A traditional Korean classroom is described as placing the teacher at the top of the hierarchy and students as passive participants; the account attributes the teacher–student power gap partly to Confucian influence and respect for education.[9] In that account, students are expected to speak when invited, and student–student interaction is described as limited in dialogue even where students help one another solve problems collectively.[9] Age and gender are also reported as relevant to hierarchy among students, which matters for group formation and for who feels entitled to speak first.[9][15]

South Korean schooling is also described as highly competitive, with teacher-centred multiple-choice assessment predominant because school-level scores matter for admissions.[16] Teachers may be responsible for more than 100 students, creating a practical incentive for structured peer assessment as a complement to teacher feedback.[16] Contemporary practices are not uniform: a 2023–2025 secondary English classroom corpus reports that South Korean classrooms used AI particularly for corrective feedback and examination-oriented linguistic accuracy, and learners often treated AI grammar and vocabulary suggestions as authoritative indicators of correctness.[17] This is a different instructional setting from the traditional-classroom account, but both make explicit instructional structure and recognised authority culturally legible.

For Finland, the national Power Distance profile supplies a contextual interpretation of egalitarian and decentralised authority, in which hierarchy is used mainly for convenience and leaders coach rather than command.[1] Finland’s digital-learning governance gives schools and teachers substantial local discretion: schools and teachers decide which platforms to use and when and how to use them, while trust is described as guiding platform development and deployment.[18] This supports a contextual interpretation of teacher authority as professionally grounded and exercised through judgment, trust, and pedagogical choice rather than overt hierarchy.[1][19]

Finnish institutional equality should not be mistaken for complete informality or equal participation in every setting. In higher-education interviews, an approachable Finnish supervisor could still have a formal advisory relationship, and language practices could exclude international staff from decision-making.[20] Finnish learning-environment research also identifies adult–student power disparities as a potential barrier to students’ voices being heard.[21] **Finland supports facilitative authority, not the assumption that teachers should disappear from collaborative learning.**

Mexico’s score of 81 is accompanied by a national profile in which hierarchy is accepted, centralisation is popular, and subordinates expect direction.[1] A proposed contextual interpretation characterizes Mexican teacher authority as strong and teachers as controlling conflict-resolution and feedback channels to maintain social order.[19] This supports a workflow choice in which groups have a clear route to request teacher intervention, teachers can resolve disputes, and students need not convert a peer conflict into an open public confrontation.[1][19]

Across the three markets, power distance concerns acceptance of unequal institutional power; it can affect relationships among students as well as between students and teachers.[22] In a multicultural applied-sciences sample in Finland, students categorized as higher-PDI were more likely to contribute only when asked, χ²(2, N=147)=8.40, p=.02, and were more likely to prefer an assigned leader rather than unanimous group decision-making.[23] The sample was made up of foreign university students and assigned cultural categories from nationality-linked scores, so it identifies a usable participation mechanism rather than a national result for secondary students.[24]

**Teacher authority should be made configurable: visible and escalation-capable in Mexico, structured and legitimising in South Korea, and facilitative but present in Finland.**[1][19][9]

Cross and Hitchcock found that Asian students were more likely than Western students to complete work independently and, when unable to do so, to seek help from teachers rather than discuss problems with classmates or peers.[25]

Mexican classroom relations are often more formal and respect-based than Finnish ones, with practice varying by school sector, region, and pedagogical model. The platform should therefore let teachers set approval, return, intervention, and peer-review-autonomy rules rather than hard-code one authority model.

A 2025 Finland–South Korea interview study found that Finnish teachers and pre-service teachers emphasized student autonomy, horizontal teacher–student relationships, and emotional safety, whereas Korean participants emphasized competitive academic conditions and institutional constraints affecting motivation and emotional well-being.[26] The same study describes Finnish education as organized around equity, trust, student-centred learning, broad national curriculum guidance, and substantial school and teacher autonomy; it describes South Korean teaching as a respected, stable profession with competitive entry requirements and a Ministry of Education that establishes and revises the overall curriculum.[26]

Finnish upper-secondary policy and classroom evidence specify a mixed authority arrangement: the core curriculum expects students to become independent, self-regulated lifelong learners, while teachers are expected to give multifaceted feedback in every course and teach self-assessment.[27] In six Finnish general upper-secondary schools, teachers generally decided the feedback mode themselves; later English courses nevertheless offered students a choice between teacher correction and marked errors with self-correction tips, and one teacher asked which students wanted more detailed feedback.[27] This supports teacher-set feedback structures with bounded student choice rather than either wholly teacher-controlled or wholly student-controlled collaboration.[27]

A 2002 analysis of online collaboration by Kim, Bonk and Oh compared preservice-teacher conferences from Finland, Korea and the United States. It found Finnish instructors mentoring in a conversational, collegial and horizontal manner, while the U.S. instructors used a more authoritative, vertical perspective; this gives the facilitative-authority model a documented online-collaboration analogue in Finland.[28]

Finnish teacher authority also rests on professional discretion. Finnish teachers are highly educated and trusted to select methods, materials and approaches within the national curriculum framework, and a Master’s degree is required for class teachers, subject teachers, special-needs teachers and guidance counsellors.[29] Finnish assessment is continuous and designed to support learning; teachers can use conversations, portfolios, self-assessment and peer assessment.[29]

South Korean authority arrangements also vary by school type. Public-school English-teaching roles are arranged through the government EPIK programme; the cited job descriptions report public-school classes of 20–30 students with a Korean co-teacher, compared with private-school classes of 5–15 students, usually without a co-teacher.[30] A South Korean private-school account describes greater teacher freedom to create lessons and curriculum, while public-school teachers are more likely to receive Korean-staff and co-teacher support and have less responsibility for behaviour management; it also describes parents’ needs as more influential in private than public schools.[31]

A 2023 comparison characterises South Korean education as demanding, memorisation- and test-focused, with grade-ranking in high schools contributing to competition, and Finnish education as holistic, student-centred and non-competitive.[32] It describes the Korean suneung as an eight-hour examination central to university placement, while Finland’s matriculation examination is not required for high-school graduation or university entrance; it also describes Finland’s system as localised, with professionally trained teachers preparing curricula under national standards.[32]

### How do cultural attitudes toward peer feedback differ across the

South Korean evidence shows that peer feedback can work when it is deliberately structured. In a Busan high-school English-writing study conducted over two weeks in December 2005, students received peer-feedback training, reviewed drafts using an activity sheet, wrote comments, and discussed those comments in groups of three or four.[2] Peer feedback contained global content or organisation feedback in 19% of instances, compared with 1% of teacher feedback, while teacher feedback was 99% local language feedback.[2] Students accepted or adapted 97% of teacher feedback and completely or partially accepted 71% of peer suggestions; the second-draft quality of the peer- and teacher-feedback groups did not significantly differ.[2]

This does not describe uncritical trust in peers. Students selectively adopted peer comments based on their judgment of appropriateness and validity.[2] A cited 2014 study of heterogeneous second-year high-school groups conducted peer assessment for 20 minutes daily over 10 weeks; students discussed in groups, established standards, amended their answers, and submitted them.

A Korean EFL peer-review study with six 14-year-old learners new to English-writing peer review tested one versus multiple reviewers and anonymous versus identified feedback. Identified feedback produced better revised sentences or phrases, recipients preferred knowing the reviewer’s identity, and reviewers were described as more responsible and considerate when named; the study reported no effect size or engagement or completion rate.[82]

Lower-performing students had high belief and positive perceptions of peer evaluation, whereas higher-performing students viewed peer assessment negatively because they could not trust peers.[16] In South Korean peer-feedback settings, preserving relationships and avoiding embarrassment are plausible constraints on direct criticism, not a measured effect of Classting.[16][2][23]

A high-school electronic peer-feedback study of six 14-year-olds found identified feedback more effective for sentence- or phrase-level revisions than anonymous feedback. Reviewees preferred identified feedback, and reviewers were described as more responsible and considerate when their names were visible.[82]

A small study of Korean teenagers found stronger revisions under identified than anonymous peer feedback, and recipients preferred knowing reviewers’ identities.[82]

For Finland, a proposed contextual interpretation characterizes peer feedback as transparent, identified, formative, and focused on task and process rather than personal critique.[19] Finland’s broader cooperation-oriented profile stresses equality, solidarity, consensus, compromise, negotiation, and involvement in decisions.[1] In a mixed-nationality Finnish university-of-applied-sciences survey, respondents classified in the feminine-value grouping were more likely to prefer direct and blunt feedback about poor work than gentle and diplomatic feedback, χ²(2, N=147)=6.59, p=.03.[23] This finding concerns a multinational higher-education sample rather than Finnish secondary students.[24]

For Mexico, a proposed contextual interpretation characterizes peer feedback as respectful, non-anonymous, teacher-moderated, and restrained because public embarrassment is a concern.[19] The current Mexico profile adds close in-group loyalty and states that offence brings shame and loss of face.[1] Mexico also scores 69 on the achievement-oriented dimension, whose profile describes competition, visible success, and direct confrontation of conflicts.[1] These dimensions support distinct interpretations of peer criticism rather than a single prediction of Mexican students’ conduct.[1][19]

In a study of Mexican high-school EFL learners aged 17–18, all feedback and control groups improved over two months, and feedback groups differed significantly from the no-feedback group at post-test; teacher-direct, teacher-indirect, and peer-feedback groups did not differ significantly from one another.[83]

In a two-month Mexican quasi-experiment involving 82 high-school EFL learners aged 17–18, all groups improved over time, and feedback groups had significantly different post-test results from the no-feedback control. Post-test results did not differ significantly among the teacher-direct, teacher-indirect, and peer-feedback groups.[83]

A Mexican high-school writing intervention found that peer feedback performed as well as teacher feedback.[83]

Relationship risk is an important common mechanism. In the multicultural Finnish applied-sciences survey, higher-PDI and collectivist respondents were more likely to believe that challenging peers’ opinions would create bad feelings and damage existing relationships; the reported associations were χ²(2, N=147)=10.09, p=0.0 for PDI and χ²(2, N=147)=7.65, p=.02 for collectivism.[23] **The cross-market implication is to make critique about a shared artefact and named criteria, rather than about the classmate, while allowing teachers to set how identifiable reviewers and recipients are.**[19][2][23]

In the Busan high-school writing study, uptake of teacher feedback exceeded uptake of peer suggestions by **26 percentage points** (97% versus 71%); expressed relative to peer-suggestion uptake, teacher-feedback uptake was **36.6% higher**. This quantifies the stronger revision authority of teacher feedback within that two-week study.[2]

The difference in global feedback was **18 percentage points**: peer feedback was global in 19% of instances, compared with 1% of teacher feedback. This shows that peer review contributed relatively more content- and organisation-level commentary in this study.[2]

For face-sensitive peer critique, criterion-based prompts and indirect wording can reduce the interpersonal risk of negative comments while retaining actionable feedback. Reviewer identity should remain a teacher-set option rather than a fixed national default.

A Finnish general upper-secondary case study interviewed nine students and ten teachers in six schools, most in Southern Finland.[27]

A longitudinal study followed 29 students in two lower-secondary physics and chemistry classrooms in Central Finland from the beginning of Grade 7 to the middle of Grade 8 through repeated peer-assessment activities.[84][85]

Students had a positive attitude toward peer feedback.[33]

One Finnish lower-secondary activity used anonymous individual assessment of another student’s lab report, a three-choice rubric and written comments, with 45 minutes for assessment and 45 minutes for revision before teacher summative assessment.[86]

Other activities in the same Finnish lower-secondary study used visible group-to-group feedback during classroom circulation and through coloured Post-it Notes, or pair and trio assessment of chemistry lab work.[86]

In Ketonen’s Finnish lower-secondary study, constructive critical peer feedback benefited students receiving it, but the benefit depended on their engagement in the original task and their understanding of formative assessment.[84][85]

Finland’s general upper-secondary core curriculum expects students to become independent lifelong learners and expects teachers to provide multifaceted feedback and teach self-assessment.[27]

### How do attitudes toward public mistake-making differ across the three

South Korean peer-assessment research identifies psychological security as a concern in an intensely competitive educational environment.[16] A Busan high-school writing study documented trained students reviewing drafts with an activity sheet, writing comments, and discussing those comments in groups of three or four.[2] For South Korean students, public exposure of an error is a plausible psychological-security risk; the effect of a public digital correction is unmeasured.[16][12][2]

For Finland, a proposed contextual interpretation frames mistakes as part of formative learning and feedback as focused on task and process rather than personal critique.[19] This interpretation supports reflection, growth, and non-stigmatising revision rather than public display of individual rankings, failed submissions, or accusatory comments.[19]

For Mexico, a proposed contextual interpretation treats public mistakes as sensitive to loss of face and proposes private correction or teacher supervision.[19] Mexico’s wider profile links offence with shame and loss of face.[1] This interpretation gives teacher moderation a central role in correction workflows.[19][1]

Teacher responses to mistakes can range from ignoring errors to making them the basis of whole-class discussion, and students may experience correction as either embarrassing or useful for improvement.[12] A cross-national TIMSS analysis cannot resolve which display mode is best because its single questionnaire item did not distinguish public from private correction or different implementations of mistake-based feedback.[12]

**Design judgment:** Start with teacher-mediated correction in South Korea and Mexico and task-focused, teacher-facilitated correction in Finland; use non-shaming framing in all three markets. This is a design judgment, not an observed country-specific digital-display result.[19][16][12]

The interface should distinguish a shared learning artefact, such as an anonymised misconception and its correction, from a public record that attaches an error to a named student. Private or small-group correction should precede any opt-in sharing of revisions or anonymised worked-error examples.

### Synthesis

The three markets differ most consistently on authority expectations: Mexico has the highest published Power Distance score at 81, South Korea is intermediate at 60, and Finland is lowest at 33.[1] That gradient aligns with a stronger case for teacher-mediated escalation in Mexico, teacher-endorsed structure in South Korea, and facilitative authority in Finland.

Peer feedback should not be treated as culturally absent in higher-hierarchy contexts: South Korean high-school students used trained, structured peer review effectively, but trust, fairness, and psychological safety require explicit support.[2][16] Public correction is the clearest exposure-sensitive area: Finnish accounts permit supportive formative visibility, whereas Korean and Mexican accounts favour reducing personal exposure and protecting face.[34]

## Documented platform practices

Classting, Wilma and Google Classroom occupy different positions in the school collaboration stack. Classting combines bounded class communication, assignment management and cross-class exchange; Wilma concentrates on school administration and communication; Google Classroom becomes a shared-work environment chiefly through Google Workspace applications. Their product capabilities should not be treated as evidence that students in each country use peer feedback, teacher oversight or public correction in the same way.[35][19][34][36][37]

### What collaborative learning features or uses are documented for Classting

Classting provides membership-gated classrooms. Teachers can create a classroom, distribute an invitation code or URL, and restrict access to the classroom and its posts to members. Its class-management features include a noticeboard for announcements, a class album for shared photos, instructional photo and video sharing, real-time expression of opinions and debate, and assignments with submission and grading status visible at a glance.[35]

Classting also documents teacher-only posts and membership divided into selected spaces, allowing teachers to address a subset of a class rather than every member.[87]

Classting’s Ting feature supports cross-class collaboration. A class can request a connection with another class in another city or country, conduct cultural and language exchange, and share collaborative projects with the connected class. A 2015 company release described 200,000 active classes for Ting exchanges; that figure is a platform claim from the release rather than a measure of Korean secondary-school project participation.[38][35]

Classting’s Private Counseling is a one-to-one student–teacher help-seeking channel. Students can send teachers questions or challenges and may remain anonymous; the September 16, 2015 release describes anonymity in this student-to-teacher conversation, rather than between peers.[38] **This makes Classting’s documented anonymity pattern private upward communication to a teacher, not anonymous peer review.**[38][19][34]

Teacher-facing visibility is built into the product’s assignment and assessment functions. Classting lists submission and grading-status visibility, while its current vendor page describes real-time progress monitoring, identification of concepts a class is struggling with, individualized achievement assessment, and notifications of unfinished assessments.[35][39] These functions support teacher monitoring of work progress, while the product listings describe neither a shared-document editing workflow nor a structured peer-review workflow.[35][39]

Classting’s assignment workflow can show teachers average scores, submission rates and completion rates; it also provides read and reaction lists and assignment reminders.[87]

A named South Korean secondary-school account concerns individualized assessment rather than collaboration. A testimonial attributed to Byeongje Jeon of Gyeonggi-do Sungmoon High School states that post-class AI assessments helped students focus on their own learning and develop self-directed learning habits.[39] That high-school use supports Classting’s assessment and self-directed-learning role; it does not make the testimonial a result for group projects, peer feedback or real-time student collaboration.[39]

A separate vendor case describes a mathematics teacher at Ha-ui Middle School in Jeonnam using Classting AI Learning for individualised exercises, with teacher access to each learner’s accuracy, incorrect answers and item-solving activity and individual feedback after assignments. The case publishes no student engagement, peer-interaction or completion outcome.[88]

Classting is therefore usable as a controlled communication, sharing and assignment layer, with Ting as an optional cross-class project channel; a platform seeking shared artefact production or peer review would need to add those workflows rather than infer them from Classting’s noticeboard and assignment functions.[35][38]

Classting’s September 16, 2015 release states that elementary-school teacher Dave Cho started the service for use in his own classroom and describes it as a safe social-media-based learning environment for students.[38]

In 《초등학교 사회과 수업에서 클래스팅을 활용한 플립 러닝 실행연구》, the author recommends ongoing immediate feedback to reduce student confusion and promote achievement; development of basic communication skills, including questioning methods, to facilitate online discussion and classroom interaction; and one-to-one teacher–student communication for in-depth discussion of learning content.[40]

Classting supports class-community communication through posts and comments by teachers and students within invitation-only, member-restricted classes.[19][35]

Classting’s class-community framing also connects teachers, students and parents or guardians, making announcements and shared materials part of a wider school–family communication context rather than solely a student group workspace.

### What collaborative learning features or uses are documented for Wilma

Wilma’s daily-use functions connect teachers, students and guardians around school information. Its official app supports reading and writing messages, notifications for messages and lesson notes, schedules, homework, examination dates, lesson notes and announcements. Guardians can verify absences and notify the school of an absence in advance; users can also access schedules and messages in offline mode.[41]

A municipal account also describes Wilma as a real-time means of monitoring pupils’ learning and learning support, alongside schedules and absences.[89]

Wilma is an administrative and communication hub rather than a dedicated collaborative-learning environment. The supplied product account describes secure direct messaging among students, guardians and teachers; access management for assignments, grades, attendance and course information; and strong authentication through Suomi.fi digital identity.[19][34] The mobile app does not include every browser-version function, but the available account identifies no intrinsic live chat or document co-editing function.[19][41]

Wilma’s peer-feedback position is correspondingly narrow. The platform account describes communication as largely transparent teacher–student or teacher/guardian communication and says Wilma has no integrated peer-review or anonymous-feedback feature. Teachers control and monitor communication relating to academic progress, behaviour and assignments, while formal moderation of peer feedback is limited because peer feedback is outside Wilma’s principal scope.[34][19]

Wilma therefore supports the coordination layer around Finnish group work: deadline reminders, homework information, lesson notes, messages and school–home communication. It does not substitute for a workspace where students co-author a document, conduct live group discussion, annotate one another’s work or review peers’ submissions.[41][19][34]

Finnish school-level adoption does not change that product distinction. Wilma is described as widely used in Finnish municipalities, connecting students, parents, teachers and administrators, but the supplied account gives no nationwide quantified secondary-school adoption rate and links no Wilma use to peer-interaction frequency or assignment-completion measures.[19][34] **For a Finnish implementation, Wilma is best treated as the authenticated communications and accountability perimeter around collaboration, not as the collaboration workspace itself.**[19][34][41]

The University of Helsinki reports that teachers can use Wilma to give feedback on students’ learning and classroom behaviour. Sanna Oina’s doctoral research found that Wilma entries were associated with learning and wellbeing, especially when feedback concerned lesson behaviour and used preset phrases such as “aktiivinen osallistuminen oppitunnilla” and “häiritsevä käytös oppitunnilla.”[42]

### What is documented about Google Classroom’s Latin America deployments

Google Classroom is a classroom-management and submission layer that works with Google Workspace authoring and communication tools. Teachers can distribute assignments, set view, comment or edit permissions, review document revision history, return work with comments and grades, and moderate a class stream in which students may comment or post. Students working in Docs, Slides and Sheets can co-edit in real time, see others’ cursors, use comments and suggestions without overwriting original text, and use version history to inspect contributions.[37][36]

Google’s regional Latin America material includes public-sector access initiatives outside Mexico. In Bahia, Brazil, the state Secretary of Education partnered with Google for Education to increase public-school access to computers, and Google stated that dozens of states and municipalities were following Bahia’s example. Brazil’s UNIT university was described as having 23,000 students using G Suite and Chromebooks.[43] These are Brazil-specific infrastructure and higher-education examples, rather than Mexican secondary Google Classroom evaluations.[43]

Mexico-specific implementation evidence includes state-level accounts from Baja California and Jalisco, a one-school upper-secondary study at Centro de Estudios Tecnológicos Industrial y de Servicios No. 276, and a teacher account from CONALEP Puebla, Plantel Tehuacán 150.[44][45][46] Google says Baja California was the first Mexican state to adopt Google for Education in schools more than a decade before the case-study page was written, when only 3% of teachers used the technology daily.

The state then used large-scale in-person and online training, and in March 2025 became the first Mexican state to invest officially in paid Google Workspace for Education Plus licences.[47]

### How have secondary-school classroom relationships and the use of Classting

The South Korean material depicts collaborative work under explicit teacher authority. A traditional-classroom account places the teacher at the top of the hierarchy, describes students as speaking when invited, and distinguishes collective problem-solving from frequent dialogue among students. A separate technology-based collaboration account describes group problem-solving and creative projects while instructors monitor individual progress and provide personalized feedback.[9][52]

That combination shapes a particular encounter with digital collaboration: students may work toward a collective output while the teacher remains visible as organizer, monitor and feedback source. A Korean secondary peer-assessment study shows that structured peer review can operate within this setting: students used a detailed teacher-provided rubric, reviewed peers’ science answers online, and received training because disrespectful or rubric-inconsistent feedback generated dissatisfaction.[16] This is a teacher-structured peer-assessment method, not a Classting implementation.[16]

Finnish evidence points to adaptable learning arrangements and feedback practices that position learners as interpreters of feedback. Finnish upper-secondary students rated practicality and adaptability relatively highly, at mean values from 3.8 to 4 on a five-point scale. ### Synthesis

Classting, Wilma and Google Classroom should be compared by their implemented role, rather than by national labels. Classting supplies a private class space, assignment visibility, real-time discussion and cross-class exchange, with anonymous student-to-teacher counselling. Wilma supplies authenticated communication, course information and school–home coordination. Google Classroom plus Workspace supplies teacher-visible assignment management and the strongest documented shared-authoring capability, while Mexico-specific cases show large-scale secondary deployment, teacher training and shared-project capacity.[35][38][19][36][37][47]

The pattern supports a modular platform decision: retain protected communication and teacher oversight where required, but provide an explicit co-authoring and peer-review layer rather than assuming it exists in a school-information system or a class noticeboard. The documented Mexico-specific Classroom feedback at CETIS No. 276 was predominantly corrective and evaluative and focused on grading activities; three of the eight participating teachers reported systematic formative feedback focused on the learning process.[45] The later sections address whether anonymity, moderation and correction visibility change participation or completion across the three markets.

## Power distance and participation

Power distance concerns whether less powerful members accept unequal authority. In collaborative interfaces, it matters not only for speaking to a teacher but also for whether students treat a peer’s critique, a public question, or a visible correction as a socially risky act. High power distance is associated with established hierarchy; low power distance with greater questioning of authority and attempts to distribute power.[54]

### How does power distance manifest in willingness to challenge peers’

**South Korea — willingness to challenge peers’ work.** In a Busan high-school EFL study conducted over four curricular sessions in December 2005, trained students reviewed classmates’ drafts, wrote comments directly on them, and discussed the comments in groups of three or four.[2] The 71% figure is students’ complete or partial uptake of received peer suggestions in second-draft revisions, compared with 97% acceptance or adaptation of teacher feedback; it measures use of feedback, not the frequency of challenging a peer.[2] In one Korean online conference, 19 of 155 coded utterances (12.3%) were coded as challenging others, defined as challenging another member’s contribution and seeking debate; the Finland conference recorded 28 of 229 (12.2%), and the U.S. conference recorded 15 of 505 (3.0%).[55] **Finland — willingness to challenge peers’ work.** Finnish education is characterized as low-power-distance and egalitarian, with teacher authority based on professional respect and trust; peer feedback is characterized as formative, constructive, and focused on task and process, and public mistakes as a natural part of learning.[34] In one Finnish online conference, 28 of 229 coded utterances (12.2%) challenged another member’s contribution and sought debate.[55] **Mexico — willingness to challenge peers’ work.** Mexican secondary education is characterized as high-power-distance and collectivist, with teacher-centred classrooms, restrained teacher-moderated peer critique, and limited public challenge of peers.[34] **Co-design evidence.** In a co-design experiment, participants with high power-distance scores were reluctant to engage in active argumentation, directly judge others’ ideas, or express disagreement in front of others, especially when others occupied a higher social position; these participants produced 311–314 seconds of total speech, compared with 347–387 seconds among the experiment’s low-power-distance participants.[56] In the experiment’s sessions with experts, idea argumentation decreased in high-PDI conditions; experts supplied 90% of discussion-management speech in high-PDI sessions, compared with around half in low-PDI sessions.[56]

Proposed mechanism. A mixed-nationality survey of 147 university students at Finnish universities of applied sciences, conducted in autumn 2019 and early spring 2020, assigned power-distance and collectivism categories from respondents’ nationalities and Hofstede Insights country scores. Respondents in the high-PDI category were more likely to say that challenging peers’ opinions would create bad feelings and damage relationships, χ²(2, N=147)=10.09, p=0.0.[24][23] In the same multinational university survey, collectivist-category respondents were also more likely to expect peer challenge to create bad feelings and damage relationships, χ²(2, N=147)=7.65, p=.02; high-PDI respondents were more likely to contribute only when asked, χ²(2, N=147)=8.40, p=.02, and collectivist-category respondents showed the same invited-contribution pattern, χ²(2, N=147)=7.37, p=.03.[23][24] These results concern nationality-derived cultural categories, not Finnish secondary students or digital peer review.[24]

South Korea provides an important counterpoint to a simple claim that hierarchy prevents peer critique. Students completely or partly incorporated 71% of peer suggestions, while the teacher-feedback group accepted or adapted 97% of teacher feedback; peer review therefore occurred, but teacher feedback carried greater revision authority.[2] This is compatible with contextual accounts that describe Korean public challenge as face-sensitive and indirect: structured small-group critique can lower the interpersonal cost without demonstrating willingness to publicly confront a peer in a digital class channel.[34]

For Finland, the preceding Cultural and educational context section supplies the egalitarian hypothesis, but no supplied study measures Finnish secondary students challenging peers online. For Mexico, contextual evidence describes teacher-moderated, restrained peer critique and limited public challenge, but reports no observed digital challenge frequency or comparison.[34] **The supported direction is to treat power distance as a constraint on visible, unscaffolded peer challenge—not as a national rule that peer critique will fail.**[56][2][23]

In the Korean conference, 44 utterances (28.4%) were coded as feedback giving and 36 (23.2%) as feedback seeking.[55] In the Finland conference, 120 utterances (52.4%) were coded as feedback giving, compared with 49 (9.7%) in the U.S. conference; Finnish students often embedded educational theories in feedback to other students’ postings.[55]

A Finnish co-located session showed local group representatives taking turns explaining the online session and cyberbullying, asking classmates what they knew or thought, encouraging them to share opinions, and summarizing the lively conversation.[57]

Configure Korean peer review around shared criteria and allow a critique to be entered as a revision question or suggestion, with reviewer identity and visibility configurable. Use attributable, open discussion as the Finnish default while retaining private channels and an explicit feedback protocol. In Mexico, make peer review teacher-authorized and rubric-led, offer feedback privately or pseudonymously to peers, and state that critique concerns the work rather than the student.

In a 2024 Mexican classroom peer-feedback activity during prototype presentations, 78% of students provided feedback and 22% provided none.[58] Of the students, 49% gave suggestions or recommendations, 46% asked questions, and 8% expressed approval of the prototype.[58] Twenty-one percent preferred not to provide feedback because of fear of being judged, criticized, or disqualified; 45% said they still needed communication skills to give peer feedback, 32% considered themselves assertive, and 2% showed no interest.[58]

In a Mexican high-school EFL writing quasi-experiment, 82 learners aged 17–18 spent two months in teacher-direct-feedback, teacher-indirect-feedback, peer-feedback or no-feedback conditions. All groups improved between pre-test and post-test, including the control; feedback conditions differed significantly from the no-feedback control at post-test, but the three feedback modes did not differ significantly from one another.[83]

In that eight-learner Mexican online study, some students were afraid to begin speaking before a peer and felt exposed when stumbling, mispronouncing or falling silent; six of the eight appeared excited to work with peers, particularly classmates they perceived as stronger in vocabulary or pronunciation.[91]

In a Korean undergraduate classroom model, peer group influence was associated with speaking anxiety (β=0.80, p<.001), and speaking anxiety was associated with silence (β=0.39, p<.001); the authors identify social conformity as a primary emotional driver of silence.[59] The same Korean model found that teacher authority had a mild negative relationship with silence (β=−0.14, p<.05), which the authors interpret as a stabilizing rather than suppressive role.[59] Contextual Silence Influence (β=0.40, p<.01) and Self-Efficacy (β=0.51, p<.001) were positively associated with silence, indicating conformity to collective quietness when classroom norms emphasize group harmony.[59] Classroom silence may reflect compliance with teacher authority or respect for the teacher, cognitive hesitation, language-proficiency limitations, or self-protective strategies in intercultural contexts.[59]

### How does power distance manifest in willingness to request help

Public help-seeking has two targets that should not be combined: a request to peers and an upward request to a teacher. **South Korea — willingness to request help publicly.** A structured Korean-classroom account says students commonly spoke only when invited and engaged in little peer dialogue, even while helping one another solve problems collectively.[9] A broader Asian-versus-Western finding reports that students who could not work independently were more inclined to seek teachers rather than discuss problems with peers.[25] Classting’s Private Counseling feature enables one-to-one student-to-teacher questions or challenges, with an option for the student to remain anonymous.[38] **Finland — willingness to request help publicly.** Finnish education is characterized by teacher authority based on professional respect and trust, with students described as willing to seek help openly.[34] In a Finnish general upper-secondary foreign-language case study, teachers encouraged students to ask when problems occurred, although only a few students used that opportunity.[27] **Mexico — willingness to request help publicly.** Mexican secondary education is characterized as high-power-distance and collectivist; students are described as reluctant to request help publicly and as deferring to teacher mediation in teacher-centred settings.[34]

The mechanism is clearer for authority-facing help. In a Chinese workplace study, power-distance belief correlated negatively with communication with superiors, r(211)=−.21, p=.003; fear of authority correlated negatively with that communication, r(211)=−.34, p<.001, and positively with power-distance belief, r(211)=.56, p<.001.[60] A separate experiment with 185 Chinese adults found less reported supervisor communication in the high-power-distance condition than in the low-power-distance condition (means 5.17 versus 5.71 on a seven-point scale).[60] These are adult workplace findings, but they identify a plausible hierarchy mechanism for hesitation before a visible teacher-facing question.

A Finnish workplace interview states that asking when one does not know is expected, while also stating that some expected knowledge is not explicit and therefore learners may not know what to ask.[20] These country patterns are design hypotheses about secondary collaboration, not measured public-digital request rates.

Teacher presence does not have one universal participation effect. Thai university interviewees reported beginning to talk when a lecturer joined breakout rooms, whereas German interviewees said they exchanged more openly when the lecturer was absent.[61] **Power distance therefore affects whom students ask, how visible the request feels, and whether teacher presence prompts or inhibits speech; those are separate interface variables.**[60][61]

**South Korea — help-channel design.** Offer a teacher-private or small-group help channel to address possible face concerns. A traditionally structured Korean-classroom account reports that students commonly speak only when invited and use little dialogue while helping one another solve problems collectively; a power-distance teaching guide says anonymous questions or feedback to the instructor may reduce anxiety about asking for help; Korean peer-feedback accounts describe private or anonymous correction as preserving face.[9][13][34]

Finland — help-channel design. Provide a visible help thread that supports peer assistance, alongside an optional private route for students who do not want to expose confusion before classmates. Finnish education is characterized by constructive peer exchange, supportive public mistake-making, and relatively open help-seeking; in a Finnish general upper-secondary foreign-language case study, teachers encouraged students to ask when problems occurred, although only a few students used that opportunity.[34][27]

Mexico — help-channel design. Provide a clearly labelled private teacher-help route and small-group support, and allow the teacher to turn a recurring question into a class FAQ without identifying the student who asked it.

In an observed online peer-correction activity, eight Mexican public-middle-school students aged 11–15 were paired after the teacher introduced and modelled the topic and instructed them to ask a peer for help with unfamiliar words. Six of the eight appeared excited to work with peers, particularly when they believed a partner had stronger vocabulary or pronunciation, although some initially feared speaking with a peer and stumbled, mispronounced words, or remained silent during the exposed Zoom activity.[91]

Kim (2002) defined help seeking as seeking assistance from others, help giving as responding to others’ questions and requests, and feedback seeking as seeking feedback on a position advanced.[28][55]

Across the evidence, hierarchy and collectivism increase the anticipated relational cost of direct peer challenge and can shift participation toward invited, structured, or authority-mediated interaction.[23][24][56] South Korean evidence shows that training and small-group structure can support substantive peer feedback despite stronger teacher uptake, while Mexican contextual evidence supports protected teacher mediation; Finnish evidence supports a more open default but does not measure secondary digital challenge.[2][34] The practical implication is to distinguish public peer critique, private peer help, and teacher escalation in both interface design and analytics, rather than treating all messages as equivalent engagement.

## Measured differences in interface outcomes

Outcome measures matter because “engagement,” peer interaction, completion, and learning performance are different outcomes. The available studies and platform accounts document several interface arrangements, but only a small number quantify student outcomes—and none provides a matched cross-country test of the same anonymity, moderation, or correction-visibility setting.

Judgment — Treat this as an outcome map, not as proof that a national cultural profile determines an individual student’s response. A usable product decision requires a local secondary-school comparison in which the interaction setting, visibility, and teacher role are varied while engagement, peer exchanges, and completion are measured separately.

### Has anonymous feedback produced measurable differences in South Korea?

Son and Ha’s 2024 study, *Effects of Descriptive Peer Assessment on the Learning of Middle and High School Students in South Korea*, conducted online peer assessment anonymously. The authors chose anonymity because prior work suggested that students participate more actively when anonymity is guaranteed.[16]

That Korean implementation did not compare anonymous assessment with identified assessment. It therefore identifies anonymous peer assessment as a classroom procedure, rather than attributing a change in engagement rate, peer-interaction frequency, or assignment completion to anonymity.[16]

A 2021 study of six Korean EFL learners aged 14 compared single- and multi-peer review under anonymous and identified conditions; identified feedback was more effective for sentence- or phrase-level writing revisions, reviewees preferred feedback from peers they could identify, and named reviewers were described as more responsible and considerate.[82]

A separate electronic peer-feedback study compared single versus multiple reviewers and anonymous versus identified feedback among six 14-year-old Korean EFL learners; identified feedback was more effective for revising sentences or phrases, reviewers were described as more responsible and considerate when recipients could see their names, and reviewees preferred identified feedback.[82]

A 2021 study of six South Korean 14-year-old EFL learners compared single- versus multi-peer electronic feedback and anonymous versus identified feedback. Identified feedback produced stronger revised sentences or phrases in accuracy and logic than anonymous feedback in this small sample.[82]

Classting’s documented Private Counseling feature is private, anonymous student–teacher messaging. It concerns candid communication with a teacher, not anonymous peer review between students.[34]

E80 states: “Anonymous peer review increases feedback honesty by 15-25% and peer interaction frequency under teacher supervision.” E80 does not name South Korea.[19]

**For South Korean secondary peer review, anonymity is a documented participation arrangement, not a measured cause of higher engagement, more peer exchanges, or greater assignment completion.** Classting’s Private Counseling should likewise not be treated as evidence for anonymous peer-to-peer review.[16][34]

A separate South Korean teen EFL e-peer-feedback study compared anonymous and identified review among six 14-year-olds. Identified feedback was more effective for sentence- or phrase-level revisions, and reviewees preferred knowing their reviewers; these results do not report an engagement, peer-interaction-frequency, or assignment-completion difference.[82]

In the South Korean peer-assessment study, the reported total positive-response percentage was 73.2% for middle-school students and 64.9% for high-school students, an **8.3-percentage-point** difference. For the statement 《I can figure out what is an important conceptby grading my friends’ answers.》, the combined agree and strongly agree percentages were 77.2% for middle-school students and 66.4% for high-school students. These are reported perceptions of peer assessment, rather than an anonymity comparison.[16]

In the South Korean PEER study, anonymous peer scores correlated with expert scores at r=.905 for middle-school students and r=.879 for high-school students; these correlations do not compare anonymous with identified review.[16]

## Design decision

Collaborative interfaces should not encode national stereotypes as fixed student settings. They should provide a common collaboration core—shared goals, accountable roles, feedback, revision, and teacher support—then vary the default visibility of mistakes, identity of reviewers, and intensity of teacher intervention by classroom and activity. The design issue is consequential because peer critique, public help-seeking, and live discussion expose students to interpersonal risk as well as enabling learning.

**Design decision:** build one configurable secondary-school collaboration system and launch it through three locally co-designed configurations: privacy-first and teacher-legible in South Korea; transparent, peer-led, and lightly facilitated in Finland; and teacher-led, accountable, and privately corrective in Mexico. This route is feasible only if the platform instruments the features as testable settings, rather than claiming that culture scores predict any individual learner.

### How should cultural attitudes toward peer feedback

A group workspace should support genuine collaboration: students jointly work, negotiate, decide, and construct knowledge toward a common goal. It should not merely divide an assignment into separate pieces and combine them at the end. Effective collaboration also requires positive interdependence, promotive interaction, individual accountability, social skills, and group processing.[3] The shared workspace therefore needs a task plan, linked group deliverables, role allocation, contribution history, feedback and revision paths, and periodic reflection on how the group is working.

**South Korea — use a structured, privacy-first group workspace.** The default should include explicit roles, milestones, shared task plans, individual pre-work, contribution records, and a teacher-visible escalation route. Peer comments on an individual’s work should be private to the author and relevant group members by default; pseudonymous or semi-private critique should be available where the teacher judges that identified feedback could inhibit candour. The source account characterizes Korean peer feedback as anonymity- or pseudonymity-oriented to reduce embarrassment, with teachers mediating feedback to preserve harmony and public errors treated as sensitive.[19] A traditional Korean classroom account also distinguishes collective problem-solving from dialogic peer exchange: students may help each other but use little dialogue, and students are encouraged to speak when invited by the teacher.[9]

Structured task design is particularly important where students worry about fairness. In a 147-student international higher-education sample in Finland, respondents categorized as high in power distance and low in individualism sought clear objectives, instructions, schedules, and role allocations. The same grouping associated collaborative work with unfair assessment and possible social loafing.[23] Those findings are not South Korean secondary-school measures, but they support a practical product choice: show each student what they own, what is jointly owned, when it is due, and how contributions connect to the shared output.

South Korean peer assessment can be substantive when it is scaffolded. In a Korean high-school writing study, peer comments included more global feedback on content and organization than teacher comments, while teacher feedback was overwhelmingly local to grammar and vocabulary: 19% of peer-feedback instances were global, compared with 1% of teacher-feedback instances.[2] Students completely or partly implemented 71% of peer suggestions, compared with 97% of teacher feedback.[2] The platform should therefore retain peer review as a revision mechanism, but give it detailed rubrics, examples, and a teacher route for disputed or unclear comments. Detailed teacher-provided rubrics were associated with generally accurate peer feedback in a South Korean descriptive peer-assessment study.[16]

Finland — use a transparent, peer-led group workspace with private exits. Default to identified comments, visible group revision history, shared planning, and group-visible formative discussion. Teachers should be able to enter, facilitate, and intervene, but should not pre-moderate ordinary peer exchange. The cultural account in the evidence describes Finnish peer feedback as transparent and identified, teacher authority as grounded in professional expertise and trust, and mistake-related feedback as formative rather than shaming.[19] Finland’s cooperation-oriented score of 26 on the cited achievement-versus-cooperation dimension is described as consensus-oriented, equality-valuing, and inclined to resolve conflict through compromise and negotiation.[1]

A visible process should not mean that every individual error is displayed to the class. Individual corrective feedback should remain private, while group-level feedback should be visible to the relevant group and include a group response opportunity.[3] This gives Finnish groups a transparent shared record without turning a student’s mistake into a public performance.

It also protects students who are less comfortable with open exposure, including students experiencing bullying, racism, or unequal status in school settings. The article, citing OECD (2019), reports wide achievement gaps between Finnish students and first- or second-generation immigrant students, particularly compared with other OECD countries. It also reports that immigrant students in Finnish lower-secondary schools are three to five times more likely to experience bullying.[63]

Finnish learners also need the ability to move between joint and individual work. In a Finland–Spain learner survey, social-relations items were rated highest, at means of 4.3–4.5 out of 5; learners valued peer relations, collaborative work, and group-working spaces.[53] Yet individual work had the lowest mean, 3.4 out of 5, and Finnish upper-secondary students still asked for individual configurations.[53] The workspace should therefore include both a shared group canvas and personal drafting areas, with deliberate transitions between them.

Mexico — use an accountable, teacher-moderated group workspace. Default to identified group membership, explicit roles and deadlines, a shared project board, teacher-visible activity history, and teacher-led conflict resolution. Corrective feedback should default to author–teacher or author–group channels rather than whole-class display. The cultural interpretation in the supplied record characterizes Mexican peer feedback as teacher-moderated and non-anonymous because of respect, accountability, and concern about public embarrassment; it also describes teacher authority as strong and private correction as preferred.[19]

The Mexican configuration should nevertheless preserve meaningful student agency. Teacher moderation should not make the teacher the only person who can comment, decide, or solve a problem. Collaborative peer learning involves social interaction directed to a shared task, with small groups making decisions toward a common objective.[24] The product should give students direct tools to propose changes, request a decision, acknowledge or reject feedback, and escalate only where a conflict or safety issue needs authority involvement.

The workspace decision is therefore differentiated but not culturally deterministic: South Korean and Mexican defaults should reduce social exposure of critique and preserve a clear teacher route, while Finnish defaults can make ordinary peer deliberation and revision more visible under light-touch teacher facilitation. This follows the documented accounts of feedback, authority, and correction in the three contexts, while task structure, contribution records, and private support routes should exist in all three configurations.[19][23]

Peer review is not simply a comment box. Computer-supported peer feedback can assign reviewer pairs randomly, anonymously, and automatically; it can operate synchronously or asynchronously; it can supply criteria, rubrics, and training; and it can be monitored by the teacher.[4] A complete review cycle should make four stages visible: initial work, feedback provision, feedback reception, and revision.[4] The system should require reviewers to identify evidence, describe the issue, suggest an actionable improvement, and allow the author to accept, adapt, question, or reject the suggestion with a reason.

South Korea — default to pseudonymous review and teacher-mediated routing. Pseudonymous reviewing means the recipient does not see the reviewer’s identity, although the system and teacher retain an auditable record for safeguarding and misuse review. Corrections should be visible to the author and teacher, with an option for the author to nominate a de-identified example for group learning. This design follows the account of harmony-sensitive feedback, face protection, and teacher mediation.[19]

Korean evidence supports structured anonymous peer assessment as an implementation pattern. A 2024 study of middle- and high-school students conducted peer assessment anonymously and used PEER to randomly select responses for assessment, excluding the evaluator’s own response; teachers set how many responses each student assessed.[16] That study does not compare anonymous with identified review, so anonymity should be treated as a design setting rather than a demonstrated engagement advantage. It does show that anonymity can coexist with structured review allocation, teacher control over workload, and rubric-led assessment.

A 2021 study of six 14-year-old Korean EFL learners compared single- and multi-peer review under anonymous and identified conditions. It found identified feedback more effective for revised sentences or phrases, reported more responsible and considerate reviewing when recipients knew reviewers’ names, and found that reviewees preferred identified feedback.[82]

The platform should prompt students to distinguish factual correction, suggestion, question, and praise. In Korean high-school writing, 83% of peer feedback and 98% of teacher feedback were specific; the authors linked peer specificity partly to training that stressed the value of specific comments and suggestions.[2] This supports a review composer that prevents vague feedback from being submitted alone and asks for evidence or a concrete next step.

Finland — default to identified formative peer review with escalation, not gatekeeping. Identified review supports accountability, trust, dialogue, and the ability to clarify an ambiguous comment. The teacher should be able to see activity and intervene when needed, but routine comments should not wait for approval. The supporting cultural interpretation describes transparent, identified feedback and authority exercised through pedagogical tact rather than hierarchical control.[19] Finland’s national digital-learning setting also gives schools and teachers substantial discretion over which platforms to use and how they are used.[18]

The Finnish configuration should still offer private messaging, a confidential flag for harmful or inappropriate feedback, and the option to request teacher review. Identified feedback does not mean compulsory public exposure. The system can show group-level learning examples only when the author opts in or when the teacher uses anonymised work. This is especially important because the Finnish evidence contains a tension: the broad cultural account supports transparent identified feedback, while a mixed-nationality university sample in Finland found that respondents classified in a feminine-value grouping more often preferred direct and blunt feedback on poor work.[19][23] Directness and publicness are separate choices; a student can receive direct feedback privately and respectfully.

Mexico — default to identified peer review released through teacher moderation. Reviewers should be identifiable to the teacher and normally to the recipient, but corrective feedback should be delivered privately to the author and teacher. The teacher should set review volume, release feedback after a quick moderation check where appropriate, and adjudicate contested comments. This configuration aligns with the record’s account of respect, accountability, strong teacher authority, and concern about public embarrassment.[19]

Anonymous pulse questions are a useful adjunct in Mexico, but should not replace accountable peer evaluation. For example, a student could anonymously ask whether classmates also found a concept difficult, or anonymously alert the teacher to a group climate issue. Anonymous evaluative review, by contrast, can weaken accountability where the instructional aim is respectful, constructive feedback between known group members.Judgment — The product should distinguish anonymous help-seeking from anonymous assessment in both interface language and data policy, because they address different risks: fear of asking versus fear of judging.

Power distance should be treated as a risk condition for peer challenge, not as a fixed national property. In the 147-student international higher-education sample, respondents categorized as hierarchical and collectivist were more likely to believe that challenging peers’ opinions would create bad feelings and damage relationships.[23] The product response is not to suppress challenge.

It is to convert personal criticism into task-focused dialogue: “What evidence supports this claim?”, “Which criterion is not yet met?”, “What revision would make the argument stronger?”, and “Would you like teacher mediation?” Shared artefacts can make that shift easier because discussion is attached to the work rather than the person. In the Hong Kong–Korean and Hong Kong–Taiwanese teams, the authors used STRUCTURED CHAT: communication structured around local design artefacts and shared representations, which effectively guided design collaboration.[64] Annotated shared artefacts supported communication of design ideas across all the cultural contexts studied.[64]

The peer-review decision is pseudonymous, mediated, and private-by-default in South Korea; identified, lightly facilitated, and selectively visible in Finland; and identified, teacher-moderated, and privately corrective in Mexico. The platform should provide all three configurations, rather than hard-code a single model of “good” feedback.[19]

Real-time collaboration must support both joint production and lower-exposure participation. Live co-editing, chat, shared canvases, video, polls, and help requests do not have the same social cost. The platform should let teachers choose which interaction is live, which is visible to peers, which is visible only to the teacher, and which can be prepared asynchronously.

South Korea — structure entry into live participation. Group rooms should include invitation prompts, turn-taking support, optional text before voice, individual idea drafting before group disclosure, teacher-visible help queues, and private-by-default correction. This is a design judgment supported by the Korean classroom account in which students were encouraged to speak when invited and used little dialogue despite collective problem-solving.[9] It is also consistent with the general power-distance guidance that privacy and preparation time—such as text forums rather than video and asynchronous rather than synchronous formats—may benefit students accustomed to high-power-distance learning interactions.[13]

The live workspace should make contribution possible without requiring immediate oral disagreement. Students could first add an idea card, annotate a shared artefact, vote on a draft, or submit a question to the teacher. The group can then discuss the visible work. This follows co-design research suggesting that making and enacting can help engage participants reluctant to speak orally, and that individual work alongside group work can give more equal opportunities to share ideas.[56]

Teacher presence should be visible and accessible, but not permanently dominant. A teacher dashboard should show stalled groups, unanswered help requests, highly unequal contribution patterns, and unresolved conflict. The teacher can then prompt, summarize, or enter a room when needed. In Hong Kong–Korean distance collaboration, synchronous tutorials with local and remote tutors were added when students could not resolve issues locally; students said they were happy that tutors agreed on a direction.[64] This is a precedent for escalation, not a basis for continuous teacher control.

Finland — default to peer-led live discussion and shared decision-making. Finnish group rooms should provide shared agendas, collaborative drafting, visible decision logs, threaded artefact comments, and shared planning tools. Teachers should be available as facilitators, with private help routes and optional entry into group rooms. This choice is consistent with the cooperation, equality, solidarity, and involvement described in the Finland cultural comparison.[1] It also matches Finnish platform governance, in which schools and teachers decide which tools to use and how.[18]

The room should support both shared and private modes. In a large-display collaboration study, shared workspaces encouraged more frequent talk and idea-sharing, while separate displays gave users personal space and a greater sense of safety but were associated with less body language and communication.[65] The study’s authors recommend allowing users to choose whether to share view or control during collaboration.[65] For Finland, the default can begin with a group-visible workspace, but students should be able to draft privately and then publish to the group.

Mexico — default to teacher-led live moderation with hybrid participation. Mexican real-time rooms should provide identified participation, teacher opening and closing prompts, teacher moderation of conflict, private correction channels, and synchronous plus asynchronous alternatives. The supplied recommendation explicitly calls for teacher-led moderation, non-anonymous peer feedback, private correction, and hybrid synchronous/asynchronous collaboration that accommodates low-spec devices.[19] The platform should not assume that every student can participate by video or high-bandwidth live editing.

The asynchronous companion is essential, not a fallback of lower status. It should preserve the same task context, contribution rights, review prompts, and teacher support as the live room. Low-bandwidth alternatives can include captioned images, recorded short audio or video, and teacher-reviewed class messages.[66] This avoids treating connectivity constraints as disengagement.

Teacher presence is not universally beneficial or harmful. In a German–Thai higher-education comparison, lecturer presence increased Thai students’ voicing in breakout rooms, while German students reported that they exchanged more openly when the lecturer was absent.[67] The product implication is adjustable teacher presence: teachers need choices such as observe silently, prompt by message, join visibly, or intervene after the session. A binary “teacher in room” switch is too crude.

Public visibility also has mixed effects. A Thai student reported that visible social-media posting could make students pay more attention to their writing and feel more engaged; several Thai students described camera use as exhausting, judgment-inducing, and inhibiting.[67] Real-time collaboration should therefore separate visible task contribution from compulsory personal exposure. A student can be present in a shared document without a camera, submit a prepared response, or ask for help privately.

The real-time decision is to make live collaboration structured and lower-exposure in South Korea, peer-led and negotiable in Finland, and teacher-led with low-bandwidth alternatives in Mexico; all three need configurable visibility and teacher-presence controls.[9][1][19][67]

A controlled shared artefact is the important middle ground. It is neither a private teacher correction nor a fully public class stream. It allows a group to focus discussion on a document, model, data set, or design board. Embedded comments, drawings, and highlighting can make feedback easier to interpret because the discussion remains connected to the relevant part of the artefact.[68] The interface should also provide a chronological discussion view and filters, because artefact-embedded comments can become fragmented or cluttered.[68]

Teacher moderation should be defined by what the teacher does, not simply how often the teacher posts. One moderation rubric distinguishes Level 1, where the moderator opens discussion, sets agenda, and observes norms, from Level 5, where the moderator additionally weaves and summarizes participant ideas.[73] The rubric explicitly treats moderation as both quality and quantity.[73] This is useful for product instrumentation: a dashboard should record whether teachers set norms, prompt quiet groups, clarify criteria, resolve conflict, summarize decisions, or correct errors—not just message counts.

**The product should implement anonymity, moderation, and correction visibility as independent controls: a class may use identified peer review with private correction, pseudonymous review with group-visible revision, or teacher-led moderation with an anonymous help channel.** Treating these as a single “culture setting” would remove useful instructional choices.[4][3][74]

Peer assessment is student evaluation of classmates’ work against shared criteria, usually a rubric or checklist; peer feedback is comments, whereas peer assessment can also include a score or rating.[72] A secondary-school implementation can use anonymous numbered essay drafts, a structured scoring rubric with a score and evidence line for every criterion, and peer-guided revision in which each writer identifies the comment that prompted a change.[72] The system should retain the reviewer roster for the teacher even when reviewer identity is hidden from peers, enabling coaching on feedback that is thin or unkind.[72]

The review composer should provide observable criteria, a simple checklist or single-point rubric, student co-creation of criteria, and guiding questions; it should also support sample-work practice, whole-class calibration, occasional comparison of peer and teacher scores, and feedback language that is specific, kind, actionable, and directed at the work rather than the person.[72] Low-stakes formative drafts should precede high-stakes work, and teachers should be able to assign, change, and rotate review pairs to prevent ineffective pairings and cliques.[72] Sentence starters such as “One strength is … because …” and “Consider revising … because …” operationalise the requirement for a reasoned comment rather than generic praise.[72] This scaffolding makes peer critique a professional routine for students who experience criticism of a peer as rude.[72]

### What criteria should determine the design choice across the three

The design decision should use a multi-criterion dashboard, not one engagement score. Collaborative learning has several outcomes that should not be treated as interchangeable: contribution, peer interaction, challenge, help-seeking, revision, task completion, learning quality, psychological safety, and equity of voice.

Judgment — Use the following operational measures in every pilot:

- **Engagement:** active task participation, measured as students who create, revise, review, respond, or complete a designated collaboration action during the activity. This is not equivalent to time logged in.
- **Peer interaction frequency:** substantive peer-to-peer exchanges per student and per group, categorized as feedback, clarification, negotiation, help request, help response, or decision. A message count alone is inadequate because interaction frequency does not define collaboration; the key question is whether interaction shapes collective decisions. [75]
- **Peer challenge:** task-focused disagreement or requests for evidence, tracked separately from negative sentiment. The coding literature defines negotiation or challenge as disagreement with a proposed solution or instruction. [5]
- **Public help-seeking:** questions posted where peers can see them, reported separately from private teacher questions. Asking for ideas or help is defined as seeking possible solutions or thoughts from group members. [5]
- **Assignment completion:** individual and group submissions completed by deadline, reported separately. A group submission can conceal a non-contributing individual.
- **Revision uptake:** proportion of received feedback for which the author accepts, adapts, rejects with explanation, or requests clarification. Completion of a revision is not proof of understanding.
- **Public-error exposure:** share of corrective comments visible beyond the individual recipient and necessary project group.
- **Teacher oversight:** teacher monitoring, prompts, moderation actions, conflict interventions, and feedback actions, classified by function rather than counted as posts.

The measures are necessary because outcomes can diverge. In one LMS project-based learning comparison, collaborative groups reported higher motivation than individual learners, while achievement and satisfaction did not differ.[74] Similarly, resource sharing can improve engagement, confidence, and critical thinking without constituting joint knowledge construction.[3] A platform should not call a stream of uploads “collaboration” unless students’ interactions affect group decisions and the shared product.

The design should weight psychological safety and equitable voice alongside task progress. Psychological safety is a shared belief that interpersonal risk is safe; it is associated with constructive conflict and comfort taking risks.[3] In a high-power-distance co-design study, participants were reluctant to argue actively, judge others’ ideas directly, or disagree in front of higher-status people.[56] High-PDI participants also produced less total speech than low-PDI participants, and expert presence reduced non-expert participation.[56] For product design, that means teacher and expert cues should be adjustable and their effects monitored, not assumed to be positive.

The baseline structure for all three markets should be small-group joint work, role clarity, coordinated goals, individual accountability, private individual feedback, and group-level response opportunities. Small groups of roughly two to six learners are identified as a productive scale for complex, equitable tasks.[3] Coordination, diversified roles, and clear responsibilities are recommended features.[3] Private individual feedback plus a group response opportunity is the relevant feedback pattern.[3]

Synchronous and asynchronous routes are complementary, not competing defaults. Synchronous activities can discourage students from simply dividing the assignment and working independently.[76] Privacy and preparation time in text-based or asynchronous formats may benefit students accustomed to high-power-distance interactions.[13] The correct configuration is therefore a shared live production moment combined with private or prepared contribution routes.

**The cross-market criterion is not “Which country needs more control?” but “Which configuration produces substantive peer exchange, equitable contribution, safe challenge, timely completion, and useful revision for this class and task?”** That criterion permits cultural adaptation without converting national culture into an individual diagnosis.[23][70]

### What design route should the platform take for South Korea

**Recommended route: feasible with the condition that each market is piloted as a separate, co-designed, instrumented secondary-school configuration.** The rejected option is a single culture-coded release, because interaction patterns are not equally successful across contexts and teachers and students adapt tools to local needs over time.[64][68]

First, establish a common collaboration core in all markets: group formation, project timeline, roles, shared artefacts, version history, feedback rubrics, private help, teacher dashboard, contribution analytics, reflection, and low-bandwidth access. Teachers need tools for supervising activity, inspecting linked interaction histories, and making sense of group processes.[68] Students need action and discussion tools, workspace awareness, self-regulation support, and help functions.[68]

Second, co-design the defaults with local teachers and secondary students. Participatory design gives users a role in actual design decisions rather than only collecting information from them.[77] Student participation must visibly affect decisions; otherwise it becomes pseudo-participation.[77] In Finland, student participation in designing safe, collaborative, ICT-enhanced learning environments is aligned with national curriculum guidance.[21] The same principle should be applied in South Korea and Mexico without assuming that one class speaks for an entire country.

Third, run a staged pilot in each market.

1. **South Korea — configure, pilot, compare, decide.** Configure explicit roles, individual idea drafting, random reviewer assignment, detailed rubrics, teacher-visible dashboards, and recipient-and-teacher correction. Pilot teacher-auditable reviewer-masked review against identified review while holding the rubric and correction audience constant. Decide whether reviewer masking remains the default from active-student fraction, substantive exchanges, on-time submitted/assigned work, unwanted public-error exposure, and teacher review time. [16][3][4]

2. **Finland — configure, pilot, compare, decide.** Configure named review, immediate project-group publication, group-visible revision history, shared planning, peer-led live work, private messaging, and lighter teacher facilitation. Individual correction to an identifiable student remains recipient-and-teacher only unless its author elects wider sharing or the teacher uses a de-identified example. Pilot this configuration against teacher approval before project-group publication. Decide for lighter facilitation from active-student fraction, substantive exchanges, on-time submitted/assigned work, unwanted public-error exposure, and teacher review time. [19][3][34][68]

3. **Mexico — configure, pilot, compare, decide.** Configure teacher-led moderation, identified peer feedback, recipient-and-teacher correction, teacher conflict resolution, and equal synchronous-plus-asynchronous participation routes. Pilot defined teacher approval before release against immediate publication followed by teacher oversight while holding reviewer identity and private delivery constant. Decide between the release controls from active-student fraction, substantive exchanges, on-time submitted/assigned work, unwanted public-error exposure, and teacher review time. [3][4][19]

Fourth, compare each configuration with one local alternative using the same measures: active contributors, substantive peer exchanges, public and private help requests, peer challenge, revision uptake, group completion, individual completion, teacher intervention, and student-reported safety. The platform should compare outcomes within local classrooms before treating a configuration as a market default.

Fifth, revise each configuration with teachers and students. A documented connected-learning co-design process uses evaluation data to assess whether resources address stated problems and to decide the next iteration.[78] The important product principle is not a fixed number of iterations; it is visible evidence that feedback changes the design.

The recommended route is three market-specific pilots using a shared product core, not three separate products and not one universal configuration. South Korea should start privacy-first, Finland transparency-first, and Mexico teacher-led; all three should retain configurable alternatives and evaluate them at feature level.[19]

### What could go wrong with the recommended interface choices

**South Korea risk: privacy becomes silence.** Private, anonymous, or semi-private feedback can protect students, but it can also conceal non-participation, leave weak feedback unchallenged, and allow the teacher to become the only meaningful responder. The check is a visibility-step analysis: compare reviewer coverage, comment specificity, revisions per student, unresolved flags, peer-to-peer versus teacher-directed replies, and safety/voice surveys before and after any move from private to group-visible discussion. The Korean route should advance only when broader visibility increases distributed, substantive revision without increasing silence, conflict avoidance, or withdrawal.[19][2][56]

South Korea risk: a teacher dashboard becomes a status signal. A prominent expert role can suppress contributions. In the cited co-design experiment, expert presence reduced non-expert idea generation more strongly in high-PDI groups than low-PDI groups.[56] The dashboard should therefore support quiet monitoring and targeted prompts, not a visible “teacher is watching” indicator in every group room. The check is whether teacher entry changes the ratio of peer-to-peer replies to teacher-directed replies and whether quieter students’ contributions decline after entry.

Finland risk: transparency becomes performative agreement. Visible identified comments can encourage students to agree rather than critique, especially if grades reward posting rather than the quality of feedback. In a German–Thai online-learning study, students in both groups described agreeing in forums instead of responding critically; some German students linked lower criticality to being graded for submission rather than comment quality.[67] The check is not simply the number of comments.

It is the proportion that cite criteria, identify a specific issue, invite revision, receive an author response, and lead to a documented decision or revision.

Finland risk: light moderation leaves inequality unchecked. Open group work can permit domination, social loafing, task division without joint learning, or comment clutter. Embedded artefact discussion can make feedback context-specific, but discussion can become fragmented and the artefact cluttered.[68] The check is contribution concentration, role completion, unaddressed help requests, escalation demand, group reflection reports, and individual accountability forms. Individual accountability can include students evaluating their own and teammates’ effort.[69]

Mexico risk: teacher-led moderation becomes teacher dependence. If the teacher is the only trusted responder, students may direct questions upward rather than help peers, slowing collaboration and reducing peer learning. The check is the peer-to-teacher response ratio, private versus public help-seeking, teacher response latency, peer response latency, and the share of corrections resolved by peers before teacher intervention. Teacher-led moderation should be retained for conflict, safety, and difficult judgement, while routine clarification can be structured for peer response.

Mexico risk: live participation excludes students. A synchronous-first design can disadvantage students with device, bandwidth, timetable, or home-environment constraints. The check is completion, contribution, and help-response measures disaggregated by live versus asynchronous route, device type, and connectivity condition. Low-bandwidth alternatives such as reviewed class messages, captioned photos, or recorded media should be equivalent routes into the same group task.[66][19]

All-market risk: shared spaces create territoriality. Shared workspaces may increase talking and idea-sharing, but they can also create ownership disputes.[65] Separate displays or private areas can make some users feel safer, while reducing communication.[65] The interface should show authorship and current activity, permit concurrent editing where safe, and offer private draft space rather than locking whole areas of the project. Research on ownership control suggests that awareness of who is using which component can provide more freedom than restrictive locking.[68]

All-market risk: public presence increases self-consciousness. Camera-on rules can support connection and participation, but can also make students feel judged and exhausted.[67] The check is camera use by choice, contribution patterns by modality, student comfort reports, and whether non-video channels receive equal teacher attention. Video should be optional unless it is necessary to the learning task.

The governing safeguard is to test privacy, visibility, identity, and moderation as separate variables. The school teacher and designated safeguarding lead investigate harmful feedback; the product team changes defaults and reviews teacher workload and access barriers.[3][68][43]

### What remains unknown about the proposed designs and their outcomes?

The central unresolved question is causal: whether identity protection, moderation intensity, and correction visibility change secondary students’ engagement, peer interaction frequency, willingness to challenge peers, willingness to request help publicly, and assignment completion in South Korea, Finland, and Mexico. The available cultural accounts support differentiated hypotheses and platform settings; they do not establish market-specific effect sizes for those settings.[19]

For South Korea, a pilot must determine whether pseudonymous critique increases substantive challenge or instead reduces dialogue and accountability. The Korean secondary-school evidence demonstrates anonymous, rubric-guided peer assessment as an implementation choice, and it reports revision uptake from peer feedback, but it does not compare anonymous and identified review.[16][2] The power-distance evidence about inhibited argument and expert effects comes from Japanese and broader East Asian co-design settings, not Korean digital secondary classrooms.[56]

For Finland, the unresolved issue is whether transparent identified feedback remains safe and useful for diverse secondary-school groups, particularly where bullying, language, status, or prior experience shape participation. Finnish upper-secondary evidence documents student participation and concern about teacher visibility in learning-space design, but not the requested comparison of anonymity, moderation, correction display, engagement, peer interaction, or completion.[53] The Finnish configuration should therefore retain private pathways even where identified peer exchange is the default.

For Mexico, the unresolved issue includes all three variables: anonymity, moderation, and correction visibility. The supplied Mexico recommendation is explicit about teacher-led moderation, non-anonymous peer feedback, private correction, and hybrid collaboration, but it is a design recommendation rather than a measured secondary-school comparison.[19] Mexico-specific higher-education COIL work reports that collaboration with international peers was highly appreciated, but does not compare peer-review identity, teacher moderation, or public correction.[79]

National cultural scores remain contextual indicators, not student-level diagnostic instruments. The Hofstede-related score system uses a 0–100 scale with 50 as a midpoint for interpreting dimensions such as individualism–collectivism, but its data originate from different years by country and dimension.[6] Its initial IBM data were collected between 1967 and 1973, with later replications, extensions, and a 2022 extension of country coverage to 102 countries.[6] A study of organizational members in Mexico and three other countries found that power-distance differences persisted but were less pronounced than Hofstede’s original national rankings suggest.[80] The platform should therefore use country context to frame pilot hypotheses, while collecting class-level data on actual feedback norms, teacher roles, safety, and participation.

No single outcome should stand in for the others. Collaboration can increase motivation without changing achievement or satisfaction.[74] A student can complete a correction without understanding it, as learners receiving written corrective feedback sometimes copied corrections or ignored comments when they did not understand them.[81] Revision workflows should therefore include “I understand,” “I need clarification,” “I disagree because,” and “I changed this” states rather than treating a resolved checkbox as learning.

### Synthesis

The design direction is a configurable collaboration core with three differentiated defaults: privacy-first and teacher-legible in South Korea; identified, transparent, and lightly facilitated in Finland; and identified, teacher-led, privately corrective, and low-bandwidth-capable in Mexico. The evidence supports structured roles, rubrics, shared artefacts, individual accountability, private correction, group response, and adjustable teacher support across all markets. It also shows why no single visibility or moderation rule should be universal: shared spaces can increase idea-sharing, private spaces can increase safety, and teacher presence can either prompt or inhibit participation.[19][65][67]

1. Build the shared collaboration core with roles, artefacts, rubrics, revision states, dashboards, and public/private channels.
2. Pilot privacy-first defaults in South Korea, transparent peer-led defaults in Finland, and teacher-led private-correction defaults in Mexico.
3. Measure engagement, substantive peer interaction, challenge, help-seeking, revision uptake, completion, safety, and teacher intervention separately.
4. Retain teacher authority as adjustable escalation and safeguarding support, not as a universal gatekeeping mechanism.
5. Promote a configuration to market default only after local students and teachers show that it improves joint work without increasing silence, unequal participation, or harmful exposure.

### Open questions

The open outcomes are country-specific, secondary-school comparisons of anonymous versus identified peer feedback, light versus intensive teacher moderation, and public versus private correction displays on engagement, peer-interaction frequency, assignment completion, public help-seeking, and willingness to challenge peers. These would be settled by comparable classroom evaluations that define each outcome consistently, report the implementation mode actually experienced by students, and separate Mexico-specific results from Latin America-wide results.
