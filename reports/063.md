# Monitoring and Authorization for Suspected Intermittent High-Grade AV Block

## Executive summary

The feasible and preferred route is urgent clinician-ordered 14-day continuous patch monitoring with automatic event detection, or 14–30-day mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry when rapid alerts and inability to activate a device make that safer, while metoprolol is reviewed by its prescriber the same day.[1][2][3] The order and authorization appeal should document four presyncope visits in six weeks, PR prolongation from 200 to 240 ms, reported dropped beats not captured during 4–6-hour emergency observation, resting heart rate 52 beats/min, and the need to detect intermittent second-degree, high-grade, or complete AV block rather than a routine short Holter.[4][5] He should not drive, should avoid being alone when symptomatic if feasible, and should seek emergency assessment rather than await authorization for syncope, persistent or recurrent near-syncope, chest pain, dyspnea, confusion, or a low-rate episode.

If expedited external monitoring or cardiology review cannot be arranged promptly, recurrent symptoms with suspected conduction disease warrant emergency-department reassessment with monitored observation or hospital admission. Documented Mobitz II block, high-grade AV block, complete AV block, or symptomatic AV block not resolving after correction of a reversible cause warrants immediate electrophysiology or pacemaker consultation, whereas first-degree AV delay alone and unclassified dropped beats require rhythm capture and medication review first.[6]

## Clinical problem and decision

This 68-year-old man has had four emergency-department visits in six weeks for lightheadedness and fatigue. At the most recent visit, two days ago, his heart rate was 52 beats/min, blood pressure was 118/74 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation was 98%; the ECG showed sinus rhythm, PR interval 240 ms, and first-degree AV delay, while occasional reported dropped beats were not captured on a telemetry strip. Three weeks earlier the PR interval was 200 ms. He started metoprolol 50 mg twice daily eight weeks ago, has a three-month cardiology wait and an insurance barrier to monitoring, lives alone, and drives daily.

The stated insurance rule denies Holter monitoring without a documented arrhythmia.[36]

The decision criteria are timely capture of an intermittent event, automatic recording without relying only on his ability to activate a device, prompt human review of concerning recordings, practical access with minimal clinic attendance, and safety while the diagnosis is pending. Intermittent AV block may be absent during brief observation, and the clinical effect depends on the ventricular rate and duration of ventricular asystole; failure to recognize a genuine ambulatory ECG arrhythmia can have serious consequences.[4][3]

The immediate clinical question is not whether a PR interval of 240 ms alone requires a pacemaker. It is whether recurrent lightheadedness and fatigue, together with the separate report of dropped beats, represent intermittent clinically important atrioventricular (AV) block that brief emergency-department observation has not captured. AV block is impaired electrical conduction from the atria, the upper chambers, to the ventricles, the pumping chambers. The distinction determines whether this is an outpatient correlation problem or a potentially urgent pacing problem.

**Disposition decision — Conditionally feasible outpatient plan:** This man should be treated as having suspected intermittent bradyarrhythmia with a potentially reversible medication contribution, not as established high-grade AV block. Prompt automatic ambulatory monitoring is feasible only if he is clinically stable and the monitor, qualified clinician review of concerning recordings, and a defined emergency-escalation route can begin promptly. The 2017 ISHNE-HRS expert consensus states that monitor selection must account for automaticity, local availability, the patient’s overall clinical condition, and the probability of life-threatening arrhythmia; failure to recognize a genuine arrhythmia on a recording can have serious implications.[3] If these conditions cannot be met, urgent monitored assessment is favored rather than waiting for outpatient monitoring.

Under the Blue Shield of California policy, mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry as an alternative to an external event monitor for symptoms occurring less often than every 48 hours requires that arrhythmia be the most likely diagnosis, that Holter monitoring and/or an external ambulatory event monitor be nondiagnostic or contraindicated, and that real-time monitoring be documented as essential for safety and expected to produce immediate medication changes or emergent procedures.[7] This distinction supports urgent diagnostic capture and medication review, while reserving definitive pacing decisions for a diagnostic rhythm recording or a clinically unstable event.

### How should first-degree AV block

First-degree AV block means a PR interval longer than 200 milliseconds with 1:1 atrioventricular conduction: every atrial P wave conducts to a ventricular QRS complex. It is slowed conduction rather than a true blocked impulse.[8][9] The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline calls this more accurately first-degree AV delay because no atrial impulse is actually blocked.

His current PR interval of 240 ms therefore documents first-degree AV delay on conducted sinus beats; the prior PR interval of 200 ms was at the upper boundary of normal rather than first-degree delay.[8][6]

The change from 200 to 240 ms over three weeks is clinically relevant as evolving conduction slowing, especially in a patient who has symptoms and recently began an AV-node-slowing medicine. It does not, however, turn first-degree delay into second-degree or high-grade AV block. The current PR interval is also below the approximately 300-ms range in which marked first-degree delay can impair atrioventricular synchrony—timing between atrial and ventricular contraction—and produce fatigue or exercise intolerance resembling pacemaker syndrome.[1][5][4]

The PR increase does not establish progression of disease in the His–Purkinje system, the specialized conduction pathway below the AV node.[4][101]

A prolonged PR interval alone does not establish whether the conduction delay lies in the AV node or farther downstream in the His–Purkinje system.[8]

A “dropped beat” must be classified from an ECG rhythm strip that displays both atrial P waves and ventricular QRS complexes. Second-degree AV block means that atrial impulses occur at a regular rate but ventricular conduction is not 1:1. Mobitz type I, also called Wenckach block, requires P waves at a constant rate below 100 beats/min, a periodic single nonconducted P wave, and inconstant PR intervals in the P waves before and after it; Mobitz type II requires P waves at a constant rate below 100 beats/min, a periodic single nonconducted P wave, and constant PR intervals before and after it.[8][6][10][9]

A 2:1 conduction pattern means every other P wave conducts. It cannot be called Mobitz I or Mobitz II from the ratio alone because there are not enough successive conducted beats to judge PR-interval behavior; determining whether the block is in the AV node or below it in the His–Purkinje system becomes important.[8][1][11] A blocked premature atrial contraction can also imitate AV block, so the actual tracing must be reviewed rather than treating every reported missing QRS complex as true conduction-system disease.[4]

High-grade, high-degree, or advanced AV block has a specific threshold: at least two consecutive P waves at a constant physiologic rate fail to conduct, while some AV conduction remains.[2][6][9] Third-degree, or complete, AV block means that no atrial P wave conducts to the ventricles, producing complete AV dissociation.

Third-degree heart block can be lethal.[2][6][9][12] The 2021 ESC guideline describes high-grade AV block as a P:QRS ratio of 3:1 or higher, a related way of expressing substantial failure of atrial impulses to reach the ventricles.[5][11]

The level of block matters because AV-nodal block has slower progression and a faster, more reliable junctional escape rhythm, whereas infranodal block may progress rapidly or unexpectedly and has a slow, unpredictable ventricular escape rhythm.[1][4][13] An electrophysiology study (EPS), an invasive electrical-conduction test, can localize the block when ECG and telemetry do not do so.

In patients with symptoms suggestive of intermittent bradycardia, including lightheadedness or syncope, conduction-system disease on ECG, and no demonstrated AV block, EPS is reasonable.[1][4][13][6]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that a narrow QRS complex with Mobitz I block, or severe first-degree AV block with a narrow QRS complex and PR interval greater than 0.30 seconds, makes the AV node more likely to be the site of block.[6] Conversely, second-degree block with bundle-branch block raises concern for infranodal disease, although the guideline estimates the likelihood at 70% rather than certainty; even 2:1 block with bundle-branch block remains nodal in 15% to 20% of cases.[6][4] The reported material does not provide QRS width or bundle-branch-block status, so those ECG features should be retrieved from all emergency-department tracings.

Symptoms must also be interpreted correctly. Symptomatic bradycardia means a documented bradyarrhythmia directly responsible for syncope, presyncope, lightheadedness, heart-failure symptoms, or confusion from reduced brain perfusion. Fatigue and modest sinus slowing are nonspecific, and direct attribution to bradycardia can be difficult.[1][4][2] His heart rate of 52 beats/min is not itself a pacemaker threshold; the guideline’s statement that no minimum heart rate or pause duration mandates pacing applies specifically to sinus node dysfunction.[1][4]

**The decision from this distinction is clear: his 240-ms PR interval is worsening first-degree AV delay, but the reported dropped beats remain a separate, unclassified concern that requires an interpretable event recording before they are treated as Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block.**

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline likewise defines first-degree AV delay as PR >200 ms with every P wave conducted, defines high-grade or advanced AV block as at least two consecutive nonconducted P waves with some AV conduction preserved, and defines third-degree AV block as absence of AV conduction.[13]

A sufficiently long surface ECG strip usually permits characterization and localization of AV block. If a recording shows 2:1 conduction, a long strip may reveal Wenckebach cycles; exercise, atropine, carotid sinus pressure testing, or EPS may also help determine the level and type of block.[14][13]

For marked first-degree AV block or Mobitz I AV block, permanent pacing is reasonable only when symptoms are clearly attributable to the AV block.[4][6] Infranodal Mobitz I block is different: it has a high risk of progression to complete heart block, syncope, and sudden death and warrants pacing even without symptoms.[11]

A recording of transient high-grade AV block should be reviewed for the sinus rate at the time conduction fails. Sudden interruption of AV conduction without slowing of sinus discharge identifies transient or paroxysmal high-degree AV block; accompanying sinus slowing instead suggests a vagal or neurocardiogenic mechanism and supports avoiding pacemaker implantation.[15] Paroxysmal AV block has the same pacing indications as permanent AV block because of the risks of syncope, sudden cardiac death, and progression to permanent AV block.[11][5]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline gives a Class I recommendation for a resting 12-lead ECG in suspected bradycardia. All available emergency-department 12-lead ECGs should therefore be assembled for comparison of PR interval, QRS width, bundle-branch-block pattern, and any captured nonconducted P waves.[13]

Exercise testing is recommended when symptoms are suspicious for bradycardia during or immediately after exertion.[5] Exercise testing may be considered to expose infranodal block in patients with intraventricular conduction disease.[5]

### How should recently started metoprolol affect the escalation decision?

Metoprolol is a beta-adrenergic blocker. It slows sinus rate and decreases AV-nodal conduction, and its labeling identifies worsening AV block as a potential adverse reaction.[16][17] The timing is therefore important: metoprolol 50 mg twice daily began eight weeks ago, before the four emergency presentations over six weeks and before the documented PR increase from 200 to 240 ms.

Metoprolol is a beta1-selective adrenergic receptor blocker.[21]

This makes metoprolol a plausible contributor to three observed features: the heart rate of 52 beats/min, longer PR interval, and symptoms of fatigue or lightheadedness. For hypertension or angina, the metoprolol label reports tiredness and dizziness in about 10 of 100 patients, bradycardia in approximately 3 of 100 patients, and hypotension in about 1 of 100 patients.[17][16] Metoprolol can also contribute to orthostatic hypotension, a blood-pressure fall after standing, which should be checked with supine and standing heart rate and blood pressure rather than inferred from his resting blood pressure of 118/74 mm Hg.[16][17][18][19][20]

Medication review should be prompt and specific. It should verify the dose actually taken, start date, indication, adherence, renal and hepatic context, over-the-counter drugs, and other rate-slowing agents. Digitalis glycosides, clonidine, diltiazem, and verapamil slow AV conduction and heart rate, and their combination with beta blockers can increase bradycardia risk. CYP2D6 inhibitors such as quinidine, fluoxetine, paroxetine, and propafenone can increase metoprolol concentrations.[16][21]

The laboratory data already supplied reduce concern for some common reversible metabolic contributors: potassium is 4.3, magnesium 2.0, thyroid-stimulating hormone 2.8, and creatinine 1.1. Targeted evaluation remains appropriate when clinical features suggest ischemia, infection including Lyme disease where relevant, acidosis, calcium abnormality, sleep-related vagal block, structural heart disease, or another systemic cause of AV block. AV block can arise from degenerative conduction disease, ischemia or infarction, infiltrative or inflammatory disease, endocrine or metabolic abnormalities, autonomic effects, and medication effects.[1][6][4][11]

Hypoxia is another potentially reversible cause of AV block to consider when clinically indicated.[6]

Obstructive sleep apnea is also listed among potentially reversible contributors to AV block.[102][8]

Hypoxia is another potentially reversible contributor to AV block that should be assessed when clinically indicated.[6][103]

Judgment — The prescribing clinician should review metoprolol urgently—ideally the same day—not wait for a routine cardiology appointment. If the drug is being used only for hypertension and contributes to clinically important bradycardia or hypotension, supervised dose reduction or substitution is often preferable to retaining an avoidable AV-node-slowing drug. This is a medication decision that must occur alongside rhythm surveillance, not instead of it.

He should not abruptly stop metoprolol on his own. The prescribing information warns that abrupt discontinuation of chronic metoprolol can worsen angina and, in some cases, precipitate myocardial infarction; it advises physician-directed dose reduction over one to two weeks when discontinuation is needed.[16][17] The syncope guideline likewise advises that reduction or withdrawal of negative chronotropes be done only when safe and in conjunction with the prescribing clinician, because medication changes can worsen cardiac arrhythmias.[18]

The drug explanation has an important limit. Beta blockers are listed among medication-related causes of AV block, but therapeutic beta-blocker doses are not commonly the sole explanation for new AV block. The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline notes that many such patients ultimately require permanent pacing, and that block may recur even if it improves after the AV-nodal-blocking medication is withdrawn.[1][2][4] Thus, a suspected drug effect is a reason to correct a possible reversible contributor, not a reason to dismiss intermittent intrinsic conduction-system disease.

The bradycardia guideline notes that AV block caused by beta-blocker overdose or toxicity can resolve after withdrawal and supportive care.[102]

The guidelines reconcile these two principles. If symptomatic AV block has a known reversible cause and fails to resolve after that cause is treated, permanent pacing is recommended. If acute AV block from a known, reversible, nonrecurrent cause completely resolves after treatment, permanent pacing should not be performed.[6][4] In selected patients with documented symptomatic second- or third-degree AV block who require medically necessary chronic beta-blocker therapy, proceeding to permanent pacing without waiting for drug washout is reasonable.[1][6]

Metoprolol labeling differs by indication. For hypertension or angina, heart block greater than first degree is a contraindication. In the myocardial-infarction section, significant first-degree block is defined as PR interval at least 0.24 seconds; that myocardial-infarction-specific threshold should not be misapplied as a general hypertension-treatment rule.[17] His PR of 240 ms therefore strengthens the case for immediate prescriber review, but it does not by itself prove that metoprolol must be stopped or that a pacemaker is indicated.

**The medication decision is therefore to treat metoprolol as a potentially modifiable amplifier of slowing while continuing to pursue rhythm capture and escalating immediately if true second-degree, high-grade, or complete AV block is documented.**

The 2018 ESC syncope guideline states that elimination of drugs that exacerbate or unmask susceptibility to bradycardia is important in preventing recurrent syncope and that cardiac pacing is not indicated when bradycardia has reversible causes.[22]

### The subject is a 68-year-old man with four emergency-department visits

He has had four emergency-department visits in six weeks for lightheadedness and fatigue, with only 4–6 hours of observation each time. Intermittent AV block may be absent between episodes; the clinical consequences depend on whether the block is fixed or intermittent, the ventricular escape rate, and the duration of ventricular asystole.[1][2][4] Short, unrevealing observation therefore does not exclude paroxysmal AV block.

Longer-term monitoring has identified intermittent AV block after negative initial evaluation. The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline cites one study in which 8% of syncope patients with normal ECG and echocardiogram had paroxysmal idiopathic AV block on long-term monitoring.[1] The Schreiber et al.

2014 study summarized in an ambulatory-monitor policy reported a median of 5.8 days to detect significant heart block, including Mobitz II or third-degree AV block, among emergency-department patients monitored with a patch.[23] The next section, **Rhythm-monitoring strategy**, addresses the appropriate monitoring design and urgency pathway.[23]

In the same emergency-department study of 174 symptomatic patients using an extended patch, the median time to detection of a sinus pause was 4.2 days.[104]

His age and sex add weight to the concern without determining its cause. Male sex, age over 60 years, and abnormal ECG are listed as short-term risk factors in syncope evaluation, and men over age 60 are more likely to have cardiac syncope.[18][24][25][19][26] He has presyncope unless he has actually had transient complete loss of consciousness with loss of postural tone and spontaneous recovery, which is the definition of syncope.[18][19]

The repeated negative serial troponins are reassuring against acute myocardial injury during the emergency visits, and his recent blood pressure and oxygen saturation do not show persistent hemodynamic instability. They do not rule out an intermittent bradyarrhythmia, because ECG and vital-sign abnormalities may disappear between paroxysmal events.[19][2] The patient’s reported dropped beats and evolving PR delay make a rhythm cause plausible enough to pursue promptly, while orthostatic, medication-related, reflex, and other nonarrhythmic explanations remain active alternatives.[1][27][20]

A 12-lead ECG during symptoms or a symptom-time rhythm strip provides the definitive diagnosis. A standard ECG obtained while asymptomatic may document baseline delay but cannot establish a symptom–rhythm relationship.[11][4][5] The immediate practical clinical objective is therefore to obtain a high-quality recording that makes P waves visible and permits review of their relation to QRS complexes, not merely to document a slow pulse.

**This pattern supports expedited rhythm assessment rather than reassurance after another brief negative observation period, because the clinically decisive event is intermittent and has not yet been recorded.**

His described episodes are presyncope, not syncope, unless they included abrupt transient complete loss of consciousness, inability to maintain postural tone, and rapid spontaneous recovery. The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline defines presyncope as symptoms such as extreme lightheadedness, visual changes, or altered consciousness without complete loss of consciousness.[28][29]

Presyncope should receive a similar evaluation to syncope because adverse-outcome rates are similar. Arrhythmic syncope is confirmed only when syncope is correlated with bradyarrhythmia or tachyarrhythmia; in the absence of a documented arrhythmia, presyncope cannot substitute for syncope, whereas a significant arrhythmia recorded during presyncope is diagnostically informative.[30][31]

The 2018 ESC syncope guideline identifies persistent awake sinus bradycardia below 40 beats/min, awake sinus pauses longer than 3 seconds, Mobitz II second-degree AV block, third-degree AV block, alternating left and right bundle-branch block, ventricular tachycardia or rapid paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, nonsustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with long or short QT interval, and pacemaker or defibrillator malfunction with cardiac pauses as ECG findings making arrhythmic syncope highly probable.[22] Documented Mobitz II requires admission to a monitored bed with transcutaneous and transvenous pacing capability, with transcutaneous pacing pads applied even when the patient is asymptomatic.[32] A symptomatic Mobitz I block requires admission to telemetry with transcutaneous pacing capability.[32] If a 2:1 block cannot be classified as Mobitz I or II, admission and cardiology consultation are required, and it should be treated as Mobitz II for safety.[32][22]

His PR change is also an ECG change from baseline. In patients with syncope, any ECG abnormality or change from baseline increases the risk of arrhythmia or death within one year; the 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline lists male sex, age over 60 years, abnormal ECG, and persistent abnormal vital signs among short-term risk factors.[30][28]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline reports that ambulatory-monitoring yield for significant bradyarrhythmias is typically less than 15%. In 95 people with syncope of uncertain origin, continuous monitoring for up to 72 hours detected significant bradyarrhythmia in 11%; among 518 consecutive 24-hour Holters obtained for a broad range of cardiac symptoms, significant bradyarrhythmia was detected in 4% and no recording showed advanced AV block.[4]

A symptom recorded during continuous ambulatory monitoring without an associated arrhythmia is diagnostically useful: the guideline reports this result in one-third of people with nonspecific potentially arrhythmic symptoms, and it often excludes arrhythmia or conduction disorder as the cause of the recorded symptom.[4][12]

Because presyncope or syncope may prevent him from pressing a button, a multiday continuous patch monitor, mobile cardiac telemetry, or another continuously recording external monitor with automatic bradycardia, pause, and possible AV-block capture is more appropriate than a patient-activated event recorder alone. ### Synthesis —

The established finding is first-degree AV delay: PR 240 ms with conducted sinus beats. The unresolved danger signal is the reported dropped beats, because a captured sequence could show benign AV-nodal Wenckebach, uncertain 2:1 block, infranodal Mobitz II, advanced AV block, or intermittent complete block. The latter three categories carry a pacing recommendation once reversible or physiologic causes are excluded; first-degree delay and presumed AV-nodal Mobitz I generally require symptom–rhythm correlation.[6]

Metoprolol can slow sinus and AV-nodal conduction and deserves urgent clinician-led review, but therapeutic beta-blocker exposure does not safely explain away newly developing AV block or remove the need for surveillance. Four symptomatic presentations over six weeks and repeatedly brief unrevealing observations fit an intermittent process that needs longer rhythm capture.[16][1][4][23]

Immediate pacemaker-capable specialist consultation is warranted for documented Mobitz II, high-grade or complete AV block, or symptomatic pauses or bradycardia with evidence of poor perfusion. First-degree AV delay and unclassified dropped beats justify expedited diagnostic capture and safety precautions, but are not themselves documented pacing indications.

Newly identified Mobitz II second-degree AV block, high-grade AV block, or third-degree AV block requires transthoracic echocardiography whether or not structural heart disease or coronary artery disease is apparent.[1]

## Rhythm-monitoring strategy

Intermittent atrioventricular (AV) block can be missed when the rhythm is normal during a short emergency-department observation. The monitoring objective is therefore to obtain an electrocardiogram (ECG) during lightheadedness or to capture an automatically detected clinically important pause or AV block. A monitor that records continuously, detects events automatically, and is worn long enough to match the recurrence pattern is more likely to answer that question than another brief observation period.

### What relevant ambulatory and urgent rhythm-monitoring approaches could capture intermittent

Ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring means recording the heart rhythm during ordinary life rather than only during a brief resting ECG. Its key purpose here is symptom–rhythm correlation: determining whether lightheadedness or fatigue occurs at the same time as bradycardia, a pause, or impaired AV conduction. The ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline identifies ambulatory ECG monitoring as useful in symptomatic patients with conduction-system disease when AV block is suspected.[4] The ESC pacing guideline likewise recommends ambulatory ECG monitoring to correlate suspected bradycardia with symptoms.[5]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline gives a Class I recommendation for cardiac rhythm monitoring in patients with documented or suspected bradycardia or conduction disorders, with device choice guided by symptom frequency, symptom characteristics, and patient preferences.[6][1]

A resting 12-lead ECG remains important because it defines baseline rhythm, rate, PR interval, QRS duration, and other clues to structural or systemic disease. It can give a definitive diagnosis if obtained during symptoms, but a normal or only mildly abnormal ECG between episodes cannot provide symptom–rhythm correlation.[1][4] The reported PR prolongation and uncaptured dropped beats therefore justify extended rhythm recording, rather than allowing the normal parts of the emergency-department tracings to close the evaluation.

The relevant approaches differ principally by recording duration, whether the device records continuously, whether it requires the wearer to press a button, whether it automatically detects pauses or bradycardia, and whether it transmits an alert while the device is still being worn. In the prospective Sivakumaran trial of patients referred for syncope or presyncope monitoring, one-month loop recorders had a 56% diagnostic yield versus 22% with 48-hour Holters (P < 0.001).[3][44] In a separate study summarized by the consensus, loop-recorder monitoring produced a median time to symptom–rhythm correlation of 16 days, and 87% of participants had correlation by one month.[3] These are study-population results, not a predicted probability for this man.

A 14-day continuous patch can capture clinically important arrhythmias beyond the first day. In a prospective comparison of 146 people, an adhesive patch detected 96 arrhythmia events versus 61 on a 24-hour Holter, including 14 clinically significant events not detected by Holter; the arrhythmia categories included Mobitz II and third-degree AV block.[45] In a 14-day patch registry of 122,454 patients, 52.5% of potentially high-risk arrhythmias were detected by 24 hours, 65.5% by 48 hours, and 92.9% by seven days.

The potentially high-risk-arrhythmia category included sustained ventricular tachycardia, sinus pauses of at least 3 seconds, atrial-fibrillation pauses of at least 3 seconds, symptomatic pauses, and high-grade heart block.[23] The registry Results reported high-grade heart block in 1,468 patients, or 0.4% of the registry population, while the article abstract reported 1,486 high-grade-heart-block recordings, or 1.2% of 122,815 ZIO recordings.[23][46]

An auto-triggered ELR is more appropriate than a patient-trigger-only recorder if lightheadedness becomes severe, because the ELR can store rhythm before the event and can trigger for asymptomatic bradycardia or pauses.[1][3] This distinction has special importance for a person living alone: failed activation is reported as a common problem with event monitors, particularly among people who live alone or are unfamiliar with the technology.[44] A non-looping handheld or smartphone ECG is therefore not an adequate primary strategy for suspected intermittent high-grade AV block; smartphone-based systems function as patient-activated intermittent nonlooping ambulatory electrocardiograms (AECGs) and record only after the patient recognizes symptoms and initiates recording.[3][15]

An external auto-triggered loop monitor described in a monitoring policy retains the preceding 30–90 seconds of ECG and roughly 60 seconds after activation, including when its algorithm rather than the patient detects the rhythm change.[107]

MCOT adds automatic event detection, transmission, and attended surveillance; its recording, retrievable ECG-storage, raw-strip availability, and human-review capabilities must be verified for the selected service.[1][2][23][38] The ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline describes MCOT as a device that records and transmits data for up to 30 days, automatically sends significant arrhythmias to a central station attended by trained technicians 24 hours per day, and is intended for symptoms too brief, subtle, or infrequent for patient-activated monitors and for high-risk patients requiring real-time rhythm monitoring.[2][4] For mobile telemetry, CMS billing article A57476 states that CPT code 93228 is the professional component and includes review and interpretation of each 24-hour cardiac surveillance period plus 24-hour availability and response to monitoring events during a treatment course of up to 30 consecutive days.[23][47]

The described mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry service includes prescribed daily and emergent reports in addition to transmission to an attended surveillance center. [45] [108] [45]

MCOT has detected more arrhythmias than a patient-activated looping monitor in some comparative studies. In the 266-person randomized study summarized in the ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline, an arrhythmia diagnosis occurred in 88% of MCOT participants and 75% of external-loop-recorder participants; the corresponding syncope or presyncope subgroup results were 89% and 69%.[18] In the CMS LCD’s summary of the same 30-day strategy after nondiagnostic 24-hour Holter monitoring, clinically significant arrhythmias occurred in 41% of the MCOT group and 15% of the external-loop-monitor group; among people with prior syncope or presyncope, the reported proportions were 52% and 16%.[33] These results support prolonged automatic monitoring but do not measure detection specifically for intermittent high-grade AV block.

MCOT is not infallible. In one simultaneous-monitoring comparison, continuous long-term ECG (LT-ECG) identified significant arrhythmias in 23 of 46 patients (50%), while the studied mobile cardiac telemetry (MCT) system identified them in 11 of 46 (24%) (P = .018); LT-ECG detected 61 significant-arrhythmia episodes and MCT detected 19 (P < .001). LT-ECG recorded second-degree AV block in three patients that MCT did not capture; the LT-ECG table recorded nine second-degree heart-block episodes and MCT recorded none.[38] The authors describe MCT as algorithmic detection followed by human review of algorithm-identified events, whereas continuous LT-ECG uses human review of the full disclosure supplemented by algorithmic processes.[38] The result applies to the tested systems, but it makes the practical requirement clear: the ordering clinician should verify that the selected service records analyzable ECG, retains raw strips, and has a reliable pathway for human review of possible pauses and AV block.[38]

An ICM or ILR is the long-duration escalation option. It can automatically detect events without active patient participation and monitor for years.[1][4] The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline identifies an ICM as reasonable when suspected bradycardia symptoms occur more than 30 days apart and initial noninvasive evaluation is nondiagnostic.[1][4] ESC guidance recommends ILR monitoring for unexplained symptoms suspected to be caused by bradycardia when episodes are less frequent than monthly and comprehensive evaluation has not found the cause.[5] It is not the first choice for a person with symptoms recurring over a six-week interval if a prompt 30-day external study can be deployed, but it becomes the appropriate next diagnostic device when external monitoring is technically adequate yet nondiagnostic or when events prove too infrequent for external wear.[2][45]

Urgent monitored evaluation serves a different purpose from ambulatory diagnosis. Hospital telemetry is warranted for high-risk features suggesting arrhythmic syncope because the value is avoidance of immediate risk, even though reported diagnostic yield varies from 1.9% to 17.6%.[42] Continuous ECG monitoring is useful for hospitalized patients admitted for syncope evaluation when a cardiac cause is suspected.[18] Symptomatic bradyarrhythmia is an indication for continuous monitoring until clinically treated, and suspected cardiac syncope may be monitored for up to 72 hours or until a diagnostic plan is in place.[41]

**The practical consequence is that a short Holter or patient-trigger-only monitor is not the preferred primary test for intermittent, potentially incapacitating conduction symptoms; the viable external choices are a continuous patch, auto-triggered ELR, or MCOT, with hospital telemetry reserved for immediate-risk presentations and ILR reserved for a longer diagnostic horizon.**[1][42]

One-month loop recording produced 34 additional symptom–rhythm correlations per 100 monitored patients compared with 48-hour Holter monitoring (56% versus 22%) in the cited study.[3]

In the 266-participant trial, MCOT produced an arrhythmia diagnosis 13 percentage points more often than external loop recording (88% versus 75%), a 17.3% relative increase.[18]

Consumer pulse-monitoring technologies are not substitutes for prescribed ECG monitoring in this setting. Photoplethysmography (PPG), the principal non-ECG modality in mobile-health devices, requires ECG confirmation of an arrhythmia; on-demand smartphone PPG is a brief, user-activated snapshot that generally requires the user to remain still for 30 seconds.[48]

Exercise electrocardiographic testing is recommended when symptoms suspicious for bradycardia occur during or immediately after exertion, and it may be considered in AV block of unknown level to expose infranodal block, meaning block below the AV node.[5]

### What monitoring strategy would capture intermittent high-grade block missed during

The proposed strategy is **prompt home-deployed 30-day MCOT with automatic bradycardia, pause, and possible AV-block detection, plus patient symptom marking and a defined critical-alert response plan**. This is feasible if he is clinically stable at the time of ordering and if the monitoring vendor can confirm 24-hour attended surveillance, timely transmission, and direct communication of critical strips to an accountable clinician. MCOT is the strongest external option for this presentation because it extends observation beyond the prior 4–6-hour emergency-department windows, automatically transmits qualifying events, and does not rely solely on the patient being able to press a button while lightheaded.[1][2][3][18]

First, the ordering clinician should document exact symptom timing: whether lightheadedness has been daily, every few days, weekly, or less often; whether it occurs at rest, upright, with exertion, after medications, or during sleep; and whether it includes actual syncope. Monitor choice should follow event frequency and the nature of possible incapacitation.[18][5] Four emergency-department visits over six weeks show recurrence, but they do not establish that symptoms occur every day; that pattern is more consistent with extended rather than 24–48-hour monitoring.[1][3]

Second, order the MCOT as an **auto-detecting real-time ambulatory ECG service for up to 30 days**, not as a simple patient-activated event monitor. The order should require capture and transmission of preprogrammed bradycardia, pauses, and suspected AV-conduction events, while retaining patient-trigger capability for lightheadedness or fatigue. MCOT can transmit data from preprogrammed arrhythmias or patient activation, and central monitoring staff can distribute alarms to caregivers without requiring the patient to transmit the event himself.[1][3][49]

Third, the monitoring service should provide the patient with remote or home delivery and initial application support where available. External monitors may be mailed to the home and self-applied using a patch or three electrodes and wires, an approach that can reduce dependence on repeated clinic visits.[50] If the service uses wire electrodes, the patient needs simple written instructions for daily electrode changes, skin care, charging, keeping the phone or hub nearby, and what to do if the device displays poor contact or loss of connection. Daily electrode changes reduce long-term acceptance of MCOT, and signal loss, electrode detachment, dermatitis, and adherence problems can impair usable recording time.[3][51]

Fourth, the ordering service and receiving clinician must test the alert chain on the day of enrollment. This means confirming that the device is transmitting, that the central station has the correct contact information, that the responsible clinician or covering service receives urgent calls, and that the patient understands that MCOT is not an emergency-response system. This operational check is necessary because a Food and Drug Administration correction reported that some Philips/Braemar monitoring-service events from July 2022 through July 2024, including algorithm-identified pauses and second- or third-degree AV block, were not properly routed for technician review or clinician notification.[39] A 24-hour monitoring station should have staff able to receive ECG data, immediate physician access for data outside parameters, and knowledge of how to contact facilities for cardiac emergencies.[33]

Fifth, require raw ECG-strip review of any apparent pause, dropped beat, second-degree block, high-grade block, or complete block, and verify before enrollment whether the selected MCOT service makes those strips retrievable and whether human review is limited to algorithm-identified events or includes the recording needed to assess suspected AV block.[23][38] AV-block classification depends on the relationship between P waves, which represent atrial activation, and QRS complexes, which represent ventricular activation.[52] Motion artifact, poor electrode contact, or recorder malfunction can produce pseudo-pauses that mimic sinus arrest or high-grade AV block; multichannel ECG makes many artifacts easier to recognize.[3] If the device is single-lead, the reviewer must ensure that P waves are visible enough to distinguish true nonconducted atrial beats from artifact or sinus slowing.[3][35]

Sixth, record a symptom diary despite automatic monitoring. The patient should note clock time, activity, posture, medication timing, food or fluid intake, and whether he had lightheadedness, weakness, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, near-fainting, or loss of consciousness. Symptom annotations help determine whether a detected conduction event caused the symptom; conversely, symptoms recorded during adequate ECG without bradyarrhythmia weigh against an arrhythmic mechanism for those particular episodes.[1][3]

A 14-day continuous patch is the external fallback only when the ordering clinician decides that retrospective review is safe and specifies when the returned tracing will be interpreted. A patch can be self-applied and continuously records for up to 14 days; in one emergency-department patch study, the median time to detection of significant heart block was 5.8 days.[44][23] It is not an interchangeable real-time alert service: it is typically single-lead, lacks real-time attended escalation, and may not be read until it is returned and analyzed after the wear period.[2][3][53]

Extended patches are usually water resistant and generally require little interaction beyond marking symptoms, which may make them practical for someone who lives alone.[12]

Some extended patches can be worn for 14–28 days.[105] [12]

Some continuous adhesive patches are worn for 14–28 days, although they may provide only post-wear review rather than real-time surveillance.[12]

An auto-triggered ELR is the next fallback if MCOT is denied but a 30-day event-monitor authorization is possible. The ELR continuously maintains loop memory over weeks to months and can automatically save pre-event and post-event rhythm segments for pauses and bradycardia.[1][2][3] It provides substantially better protection against failure to activate than a patient-trigger-only event recorder, but it may not provide the same continuous real-time surveillance or full-disclosure review as the selected MCOT service.[3]

A 24–48-hour Holter is the option not chosen as the initial strategy. It is appropriate when events are expected within that short interval, especially with daily symptoms.[1][3] The reported pattern instead consists of recurrent episodes over six weeks that escaped four separate 4–6-hour emergency-department observations, so a short Holter would leave a high risk of another nondiagnostic study.[1][2] The appropriate exception is a reliable history that symptoms are actually daily or near-daily, in which case a continuous multilead Holter could be a rapid first recording, but it still lacks the real-time safety features of MCOT.[33][53]

If a technically successful 30-day external study is nondiagnostic and the symptoms become less frequent than monthly, proceed to ILR/ICM evaluation. The implanted device can automatically capture clinically important bradyarrhythmias over years without requiring the patient to be conscious or able to operate it.[1][5][2] ICM studies have identified bradycardia-mediated high-grade AV block among conditions treated with permanent pacing.[2] This is a diagnostic escalation, not a reason to defer urgent care if severe symptoms or documented advanced block emerge.

An implantable cardiac monitor is also an escalation option if external monitoring cannot be used reliably.[101][102]

**The selected route is therefore 30-day auto-detecting MCOT now, a continuous patch or auto-triggered ELR only if MCOT cannot be obtained without delay, and ILR/ICM if adequate external monitoring remains nondiagnostic or the recurrence interval becomes too long for external wear.**[2][45][4]

In the 14-day patch-monitor registry, 34.5% of potentially high-risk arrhythmias—including high-grade heart block—were not detected in the first 48 hours; this quantifies why a 24–48-hour study can be mismatched to an intermittent-block question.[23]

Within that registry, detection rose by 27.4 percentage points between 48 hours and day 7, showing that much of the incremental yield from extended continuous recording accrued during the first week beyond a short Holter window.[23]

The reported median 5.8-day time to detect significant heart block represents 41.4% of a 14-day patch-monitoring period, supporting deployment now rather than a further short observation interval.[23][44]

In the cited post-nondiagnostic-Holter trial, 30-day MCOT detected clinically significant arrhythmias 26 percentage points more often than external loop monitoring, a 173.3% relative increase (41% versus 15%).[33]

Among the trial participants with prior syncope or presyncope, the corresponding MCOT advantage was 36 percentage points, a 225.0% relative increase (52% versus 16%).[33]

In the cited up-to-30-day trial, MCOT reached a diagnosis a median 2 days earlier than the looping monitor (7 versus 9 days), a 22.2% shorter median time to diagnosis; this supports selecting an automatic, transmitting monitor when a prompt actionable recording is needed.[23]

In one MCOT comparison, 7% of participants did not comply with the protocol requiring at least 25 monitoring days, and reported barriers included device-use difficulties, interference with work or travel, and electrode-related skin irritation.[44]

### When is outpatient ambulatory monitoring appropriate

Outpatient ambulatory monitoring is appropriate when the person is clinically stable, no high-grade AV block has been documented, and there is a functioning route to review recordings and respond to critical alerts. First-degree AV block with unexplained lightheadedness or dizziness is a guideline-supported reason to use ambulatory ECG monitoring to establish symptom–rhythm correlation.[1] The PR interval of 240 ms identifies first-degree AV block but does not classify the reported dropped beats.

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline also considers ambulatory ECG monitoring reasonable for unexplained lightheadedness or dizziness in a person with Mobitz I second-degree AV block, to correlate symptoms with rhythm abnormalities.[6][36]

Review the original 12-lead ECGs and any available telemetry, and obtain a diagnostic rhythm strip when a dropped beat recurs; the captured P-wave–QRS relationship distinguishes AV block from an apparent missed beat and identifies the level of block when needed.[1][3]

Outpatient monitoring is especially appropriate when the immediate clinical question is whether symptoms correlate with transient bradycardia or AV block. The ambulatory ECG strategy has two valid outcomes: capture a serious arrhythmia that itself requires intervention, or document symptoms without an associated arrhythmia, which argues against an arrhythmic basis for those recorded symptoms.[3] A symptom-free normal recording does not answer the same question, because it does not test the rhythm during the symptom.[1][42]

The fact that he lives alone and drives daily does not itself convert first-degree AV block into documented high-grade block. It does, however, shifts device choice toward automatic recording rather than a device that depends on manual activation. Patient-triggered recorders require the wearer to remain coherent enough to start recording, whereas auto-trigger recording begins when the rhythm abnormality occurs.[36] That is why MCOT or an auto-trigger ELR is favored over a handheld, smartphone, or symptom-only event recorder in this scenario.[3][35]

Judgment — Outpatient MCOT is appropriate only if it can be started promptly, the man can keep the device attached and transmitting, and an identified clinician or cardiology service accepts responsibility for same-day review of critical alerts. A remote monitoring center can detect and report an event; it cannot provide bedside assessment, temporary pacing, or immediate transport.

Exercise electrocardiographic testing is recommended when symptoms suspicious for bradycardia occur during or immediately after exertion, and it may be considered to expose infranodal block when intraventricular conduction disease or AV block of unclear level is present.[11]

### When is urgent monitored evaluation needed instead?

Urgent monitored evaluation is needed when the clinical situation has crossed from diagnostic uncertainty to possible immediate instability or a pacing decision. Hospital ECG monitoring is intended to detect acute rhythm or rate changes that require urgent response.[23][54] The key dividing line is therefore not PR prolongation alone but a combination of symptoms, documented rhythm severity, hemodynamic condition, and whether a detected event could require immediate pacing or emergency intervention.

Immediate emergency-department assessment with continuous ECG monitoring is appropriate for actual syncope, recurrent near-syncope at rest, injury from a suspected blackout, chest pain, acute dyspnea, confusion, new neurologic deficit, persistent marked weakness, or signs of poor perfusion. Acute bradycardia algorithms call for ECG monitoring, measurement of blood pressure and oxygen saturation, and recording a 12-lead ECG to determine whether bradycardia is causing cardiopulmonary compromise.[55][56] This section addresses monitoring rather than the detailed safety instructions and escalation thresholds, which are set out in **Safety until definitive evaluation** and **Thresholds for urgent evaluation and pacemaker consultation**.

Documented Mobitz II second-degree AV block requires monitored evaluation rather than routine outpatient workup. Clinical guidance directs that all patients with Mobitz II be admitted to monitored beds with transcutaneous and transvenous pacing available, including asymptomatic patients, because of risk of progression to complete block.[32] New Mobitz II warrants cardiology consultation regardless of symptoms.[32] A documented transient third-degree AV block or higher-grade AV block is likewise a serious arrhythmic finding that can explain syncope or warrant intervention.[15]

The reported dropped beats are not yet equivalent to documented Mobitz II or high-grade AV block. They could represent blocked atrial ectopy, vagally mediated nodal block, Mobitz I block, 2:1 block, artifact, or more serious infranodal conduction disease.Judgment — The clinically safe response is to treat the report as a reason for expedited recording and clear emergency return precautions, not to label it as high-grade AV block without an interpretable tracing.

Hospital telemetry is also appropriate when an acute medication effect is plausible and the clinician believes medication adjustment could unmask or worsen a dangerous rhythm. Ambulatory electrocardiographic (AECG) monitoring is used to evaluate a patient’s response to initiation, revision, or discontinuation of arrhythmic drug therapy.[3][57] Published case reports describe telemetry-confirmed intermittent AV block associated with metoprolol, followed by metoprolol discontinuation and, in one case, temporary pacing.[58] Those reports illustrate a possible reversible contributor; they do not prove that metoprolol caused this man’s PR prolongation or dropped beats.

Judgment — Recently introduced metoprolol should prompt prompt prescribing-clinician review because beta-blockade can slow sinus rate and AV nodal conduction. It should not be used to dismiss a captured Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block as medication-only until the rhythm, medication response, and need for ongoing rate-slowing treatment have been assessed in a monitored setting.

Electrophysiology study (EPS), an invasive study that measures conduction through the AV node and His–Purkinje system, is not first-line for most patients. It becomes a specialist-directed option when symptoms suggest intermittent bradycardia, ECG shows conduction-system disease, and noninvasive monitoring has not demonstrated the suspected block.[1][4] If ECG and telemetry capture second-degree block but cannot identify its level, EPS can determine whether the block is in the AV node, His bundle, or below the His bundle.[1][4]

**Outpatient MCOT is appropriate for stable, undocumented intermittent symptoms with a rapid response pathway; urgent telemetry replaces it when symptoms suggest immediate instability or when Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block is recorded or strongly suspected during an acute presentation.**[1][2][18][32]

Replacement point — If the current clinical assessment finds outpatient care unsafe, or if prompt recording and actionable review of critical alerts cannot be arranged, obtain urgent monitored clinical reassessment instead of issuing an unmonitored ambulatory-monitor order.[1][18][42]

When symptoms are exertional, exercise testing is useful for exertional syncope, ischemia, and arrhythmia evaluation and can precipitate Mobitz type II second-degree or third-degree block during exercise.[30]

### Synthesis

The selected route is prompt 30-day auto-detecting MCOT with an accountable clinician for critical-alert review. Use an auto-triggered ELR if MCOT is unavailable; use a 14-day patch only when retrospective review is safe and the timing of interpretation is specified; and evaluate an ILR/ICM after technically adequate nondiagnostic external monitoring or when events become too infrequent for external wear. Replace outpatient ordering with urgent monitored reassessment when current safety is inadequate or prompt recording and actionable alert review cannot be arranged.[1][2][53][18][42]

## Authorization and timely access

### What clinical documentation supports expedited monitoring authorization or an appeal

This request should be framed as an unresolved, recurrent symptomatic conduction-disorder evaluation, not as a request to repeat a short emergency-department observation or to obtain a Holter monitor merely because of a low resting pulse. The clinical purpose is to capture a symptom–rhythm correlation: an ECG recording during lightheadedness or fatigue that either documents intermittent atrioventricular (AV) block or shows that symptoms occur without an arrhythmia. Ambulatory rhythm monitoring is useful for suspected bradycardia or conduction disorders, with monitor choice based on symptom frequency, symptom nature, and patient preference.[6][1]

**The expedited request should seek an auto-triggered extended external monitor now, with mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry (MCOT) as the preferred request if the ordering clinician judges real-time review necessary for safety.** A patient- and auto-activated external event monitor can retain ECG around an event and transmit data remotely for up to 30 days; CPT 93268 describes that service. MCOT adds computerized real-time analysis, retrievable ECG storage exceeding 24 hours, and transmissions of ECG-triggered and patient-selected events to an attended surveillance center for up to 30 days; CPT 93228 covers professional review and interpretation, and CPT 93229 covers technical support, surveillance, analysis, and report transmission.[23][36]

CPT codes 93268–93272 describe external patient- and auto-activated event recording with remote download for up to 30 days; CPT codes 93228/93229 describe mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry for up to 30 days.[105][106][45]

The order should state that a 24–48-hour monitor is most appropriate for daily or near-daily symptoms and, for other symptom frequencies, that the clinician selected the ambulatory electrocardiographic (AECG) monitoring type based on the clinical evaluation, symptoms, and the device’s labeled indications.[45] The ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline says that less frequent symptoms are best evaluated by prolonged ambulatory ECG monitoring and that external loop recorders, transtelephonic event recorders, adhesive patch recorders, and mobile telemetry have higher diagnostic yield than 24- or 48-hour Holter monitoring because they record longer.[2] In one trial cited by the ISHNE-HRS consensus statement, symptom–rhythm correlation occurred in 22% with a 48-hour Holter and 56% with one month of ECG loop recording.[3] A Holter is most appropriate when symptoms occur daily or near-daily; the symptom frequency should be recorded explicitly rather than assumed from the number of emergency visits.[45][19]

Carelon states that Holter monitoring is unsuitable when symptoms occur less often than every 48 hours.[36]

The submission should attach a dated clinical chronology:

- **Recurrent clinically significant symptoms:** Four emergency-department presentations over six weeks for lightheadedness and fatigue, with the duration, severity, activity at onset, position, triggers, recovery, and whether there was presyncope or actual syncope stated for every episode. Bradycardia guidance calls for documentation of symptom frequency, timing, duration, severity, circumstances, triggers, alleviating factors, and their relationship to medications and positional change. [1][4]

- **Objective conduction findings:** The recent ECG showed sinus rhythm at 52 beats/minute with first-degree AV block, defined here by the documented PR interval of 240 milliseconds, compared with 200 milliseconds three weeks earlier. The record should include the actual ECGs or official readings and describe the reported dropped beats precisely as unclassified and not captured on a rhythm strip. A 12-lead ECG is recommended to document rate, rhythm, and conduction in suspected bradycardia or conduction disease. [6]

- **Results of prior acute assessment:** Each prior 4–6-hour monitored emergency evaluation, serial negative troponins, discharge disposition, and absence or presence of symptoms while being monitored should be included. An abnormal ECG is a short-term risk factor in syncope assessment; negative troponins and currently acceptable vital signs do not answer whether intermittent AV block occurred outside the observation window. [19][18]

- **Reversible-cause assessment:** Include creatinine 1.1, potassium 4.3, magnesium 2.0, thyroid-stimulating hormone 2.8, and orthostatic heart rate and blood-pressure measurements if obtained. The clinical history and physical examination for syncope should address medications and include orthostatic measurements through three minutes upright. [18]

- **Medication timeline:** Record metoprolol tartrate 50 mg twice daily, the exact start date eight weeks ago, the indication of hypertension, adherence, other prescribed and nonprescription agents, and the temporal relation between drug initiation, symptoms, bradycardia, and PR prolongation. Beta-adrenergic receptor blockers can induce or worsen bradycardia or conduction disorders, and guideline-directed evaluation requires a thorough medication review. [1][8][4]

The request must preserve the diagnostic distinction that protects both the patient and the claim. First-degree AV block is documented and can be coded as I44.0. Second-degree AV block, complete AV block, and other AV block are separate coding categories, but the reported dropped beats are not a documented second-degree or complete block because no tracing classified them.[47][45] The appeal should therefore use the documented first-degree AV block, bradycardia if clinically diagnosed, and the symptom code for dizziness/giddiness or presyncope as appropriate; it should describe suspected intermittent high-grade block as the clinical question, not as an established diagnosis. Medicare memory-loop billing guidance lists first-degree block, second-degree block, complete block, bradycardia, dizziness, and syncope as distinct diagnoses supporting specified claims.[47]

The medical-necessity letter should make the management consequence explicit. The requested recording will determine whether symptoms correlate with clinically important bradyarrhythmia or AV-conduction interruption, whether metoprolol requires clinician-directed reassessment, and whether urgent electrophysiology or pacemaker evaluation is needed. The 2004 Medicare national coverage determination, as summarized in the cited policies, covers ambulatory cardiac monitors when medical necessity is documented, including for detection of symptomatic transient arrhythmias and for determining whether arrhythmic drug therapy should be initiated, revised, or discontinued.[36][51][7][54]

For an MCOT request, add a specific “real-time safety rationale.” MCOT records and transmits automatically detected or patient-activated events for up to 30 days; trained technicians at a central monitoring station review significant transmissions 24 hours daily. It is identified for high-risk patients whose rhythm requires real-time monitoring and for symptoms too brief, subtle, or infrequent to be reliably captured by patient activation alone.[19][4] The letter should specify the action that will follow an alert: same-day clinician contact, emergency evaluation when directed by the monitoring protocol, urgent electrophysiology assessment, and medication reassessment. This turns a general monitoring request into a defined diagnostic-and-treatment pathway.

Payer rules differ materially, so the office must retrieve the member-specific benefit document, utilization-management policy, and denial rationale before submitting. One current insurer policy considers an external event monitor or continuous monitor storing more than 48 hours medically necessary for arrhythmia-suggestive dizziness, presyncope, or syncope occurring less often than every 48 hours.[7] That same policy considers MCOT an alternative to event monitoring only when symptoms are most likely arrhythmic, Holter or external event monitoring was nondiagnostic or contraindicated, and real-time monitoring is essential for safety and expected to prompt immediate management changes.[7][23] Thus, a Holter denial can support the request only if the denial reason demonstrates that a short Holter is unavailable, inadequate, or contraindicated under the plan’s criteria; it does not automatically satisfy every MCOT criterion.

Aetna considers external event monitoring and long-term continuous ECG monitoring medically necessary for lightheadedness or suspected arrhythmia when symptoms are too infrequent for Holter capture. [109] [109]

Some payer policies permit MCOT as the first test when real-time monitoring is essential for safety and likely to change management; others require a nondiagnostic Holter, 14-day event monitor, or longer external monitoring first.[23][36][110]

The applicable MCOT rule can depend on the insurance product, provider network, jurisdiction, delegated utilization-management organization, and current policy version.[23][36][110]

Judgment — **The preferred submission is a two-tier request: authorize a 14–30-day continuously recording, auto-triggered external monitor immediately; authorize MCOT instead when the clinician can document that unattended or patient-triggered recording is unsafe because a potentially incapacitating episode may prevent activation and a critical result has an immediate action pathway.** This avoids falsely claiming documented high-grade block while directly addressing the failure mode of short observation: no ECG was being recorded when the intermittent event occurred.

This documentation makes an expedited request clinically coherent and gives the reviewer a precise alternative to the denied short Holter.

Automatic continuous recording is medically important, not merely a convenience. In a 14-day patch-monitor study, 82 of 94 participants with detected arrhythmias (87.2%) were unaware of the event and did not activate the recorder. The order should therefore specify continuous recording with auto-detection of bradyarrhythmia or AV-conduction events, plus a patient symptom marker when he is able to use it.[59]

A 14-day continuous patch is a defensible minimum external-recording alternative when a 30-day auto-triggered monitor is unavailable. In the 158-participant study, the 14-day patch detected study-defined arrhythmias in 59.5% of participants versus 19.0% with a 24-hour Holter (P < 0.001), and detected critical arrhythmias in 16.5% over two weeks versus 10.1% over three days and 12.7% over one week. More than 90% of the critical arrhythmias recorded during the full monitoring period were detected after more than 10.2 days of wear.[59]

The appeal should identify the denied test and the requested alternative by service category and duration: continuous external ECG recording and storage for more than 48 hours up to 7 days, more than 7 days up to 15 days, or an external patient- and auto-activated memory-loop recorder with remote download up to 30 days. The policy’s ICD-10 list identifies I44.0–I45.9 for atrioventricular and other conduction disorders, R00.1 for bradycardia, R42 for dizziness and giddiness, and R55 for syncope and collapse, including presyncope, as codes considered medically necessary when the monitor criteria are met. The request should use the codes actually supported by this record: I44.0 for documented first-degree AV block, R42 for lightheadedness/dizziness, R00.1 if bradycardia is clinically diagnosed, and R55 if syncope or presyncope is documented.[60][61]

CPT codes 93224–93227 describe Holter recording limited to up to 48 hours.[105]

The clinical packet should document that other plausible contributors have been assessed and treated as appropriate, because one utilization-management policy excludes rhythm monitoring when suggestive symptoms are absent or other potential causes have not been ruled out and/or treated appropriately. Medication reconciliation is particularly material: medications contribute to 5% to 15% of syncope cases through mechanisms including symptomatic bradycardia and orthostatic hypotension, and medication initiation or dosage change is a relevant feature.[61][30]

The authorization packet should include an office or consultation note documenting a face-to-face evaluation that states the indication and the symptoms or rhythm question. A request requiring clinical review is a coverage determination, which uses plan documents and, when applicable, clinical information to determine whether coverage criteria are met; it is not merely notification, which is a data-entry process without benefits-coverage judgment or interpretation.[62][63]

### Synthesis

The authorization case is strongest when it rests on recurrent, clinically significant symptoms; documented evolving conduction delay; repeated short non-diagnostic observations; a monitor duration matched to episode frequency; and a stated action plan for a dangerous recording. Prolonged external monitoring is the first practical diagnostic route because it is more likely than a 24–48-hour Holter to capture intermittent events, while MCOT is justified when automatic detection and real-time review are clinically necessary.[2][3][19]

The plan-specific barrier matters: some payers authorize extended external monitoring directly for symptoms occurring less often than every 48 hours, whereas MCOT or ILR policies may require a nondiagnostic extended external study first.[7][36][51] If this route cannot produce prompt monitor placement and review, urgent specialist, EPS, or monitored acute-care evaluation replaces the waiting list; medication review proceeds in parallel, without assuming metoprolol explains away intermittent AV block.[1][10][16]

## Safety until definitive evaluation

Recurrent lightheadedness while a possible intermittent conduction disorder is unresolved creates two immediate risks: sudden incapacity during driving or a fall, and delayed recognition of a clinically important rhythm event. The safety plan must therefore reduce exposure to injury, provide a way to summon help, and make remote rhythm monitoring operationally reliable; it must not treat a monitor as emergency protection.

### What interim safety plan addresses recurrent lightheadedness and daily driving?

**He should stop driving now, rather than wait for a rhythm result.** He has recurrent, unpredictable lightheadedness, lives alone, and has not yet had an episode recorded during symptoms. Metoprolol labeling advises patients not to operate automobiles, machinery, or other equipment requiring alertness until their individual response is known; tiredness, dizziness, bradycardia, hypotension, syncope, and heart block are recognized metoprolol-associated effects. This does not prove metoprolol is causing his symptoms, but it makes continued driving during active symptoms unsafe.[16][21]

This is a temporary safety restriction, not a determination that he has syncope. Syncope means transient loss of consciousness from inadequate brain blood flow; the record reports lightheadedness and fatigue, not documented loss of consciousness. Nevertheless, the syncope guideline lists untreated syncope due to nonreflex bradycardia as not fit to drive and recommends a one-month symptom-free interval for syncope of undetermined cause. U.S. private-driver regulation is state-specific, so the treating clinician should identify and discuss the applicable state rule rather than applying the table automatically to presyncope.[75][18]

Under the United Kingdom DVLA’s Group 1 driving standard, an arrhythmia that has caused or is likely to cause incapacity requires at least four weeks off driving; afterward, its cause must be identified and treated and recurrence must be unlikely.[112] The DVLA defines incapacity as a symptom, condition, or treatment likely to prevent safe vehicle control or stopping, and includes significant AV-conduction defects in arrhythmia driving assessments.[112]

Under the UK Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA) standard, a person should not drive when an arrhythmia has caused or is likely to cause incapacity, defined as being unable to safely control or stop a vehicle; local rules govern this patient’s formal restriction.[112]

Until the cause is assessed, he should avoid other settings in which a brief loss of balance or consciousness could cause serious injury. This includes ladders, roofs or heights, stairs when alone, swimming or bathing unattended, power tools, heavy machinery, and walking near traffic. He should arrange rides for work, groceries, testing, and urgent care; he should not drive himself to an emergency department. Advice to avoid hazards around stairs, water, traffic, heights, and machinery, and to discuss driving safety, is consistent with syncope injury-prevention guidance.[76][77]

When lightheadedness begins, he should immediately stop the activity, sit or lie down, preferably lie supine with his legs raised if feasible, and remain down until symptoms have fully passed. He should stand only slowly afterward. He should not try to continue walking, drive home, or test whether the symptom will pass while upright.[78][76][27]

He should call 911 rather than wait for a monitor transmission if he faints, is injured, has chest pain, shortness of breath, persistent or worsening lightheadedness, cannot safely get to the floor or recover, or considers the episode a medical emergency. A mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry (MCOT) device is a diagnostic test, not an emergency-response system; even systems that transmit data in near real time cannot ensure emergency medical services arrive within minutes. Monitoring-service guidance specifically directs patients to call 911 for a perceived medical emergency, and ambulatory-telemetry notification examples place every reported syncopal episode in the urgent notification tier.[73][79][80][3]

The immediate result is a practical harm-reduction plan: no driving or hazardous solo activity, an episode response that prevents falls, emergency calling rather than self-transport, and prompt medication review while diagnostic monitoring is arranged.

### How should the plan account for his living alone

Living alone is both a safety issue and a monitor-design issue. A patient-triggered event recorder requires the person to recognize the symptom, remain coherent, and press the button in time. Failed activation is particularly common in people who live alone, are unfamiliar with the technology, or have low motivation; a sudden presyncopal or syncopal episode may prevent activation altogether.[36][44][34]

**The preferred outpatient arrangement is a continuously worn, auto-triggered mobile cardiac telemetry service, with home delivery and remote data transmission where locally available, rather than a patient-trigger-only recorder.** MCOT can record and transmit preprogrammed arrhythmia events or patient-marked symptoms for up to 30 days; significant detected rhythms are sent automatically to a central monitoring station staffed by trained technicians 24 hours a day. This directly addresses the danger that he may be unable to activate a device during a symptomatic pause or dropped beat.[4][2][49]

A home-based setup can reduce his travel burden. External monitors may be sent to the patient’s home for self-application, and one MCOT program describes home delivery within days of a prescription, 24/7 patient support, and home pickup after monitoring ends. These arrangements are practical only if the supplying service actually offers them under his insurer and local network.[50][86][87]

Before he leaves with the monitor, the ordering clinician or monitor team should perform a **closed-loop setup check**:

- **Emergency contacts:** Record his working mobile number, address, a consented backup contact such as a neighbor or friend, and how emergency services can reach him. Social isolation and inadequate support can increase harm from a fall, particularly in older adults. [20][73]

- **Alert ownership:** Identify the ordering clinician, after-hours covering clinician, monitoring vendor, and the exact route for urgent alerts. Ambulatory monitoring requires infrastructure for timely notification of both the patient and clinician when a potentially dangerous abnormality is found; a 24-hour monitoring station should have staff who can reach a physician and know how to contact emergency facilities. [2][45][33]

- **Device competence:** Have him demonstrate patch or electrode application, symptom marking, charging, and response to connectivity warnings. Give him written instructions plus the technical-support number. Daily electrode changes, electrode detachment, dermatitis, poor signal quality, and incomplete symptom logs are established practical limits of external monitoring. [51][73]

- **Connectivity and response:** Confirm cellular coverage at home, that he can keep the monitor and sensor close together, and that he can answer calls. Cellular gaps can delay transmission; some devices store data until connectivity returns, but transmission delay means the monitor cannot be relied upon to summon help. [73]

He should also keep a concise symptom diary: date and time, activity, posture, symptoms, whether he nearly fainted or lost consciousness, pulse if available without delaying safety actions, and when he took metoprolol. Patient time stamps and a diary help correlate symptoms with the rhythm recording and medication timing.[73][35]

The practical consequence is that living alone favors automated capture and remote logistics, but it also requires an explicit human support plan: a daily check-in by a neighbor or friend, a charged phone kept on his person, and a clear 911 plan if he cannot obtain help.

The planned monitoring period must be long enough to overlap a usual episode. External loop recorders can continuously store rhythm data over weeks to months and can be patient- or auto-triggered, retaining 3–14 minutes before and 1–4 minutes after a trigger; they are limited when events are infrequent.[25] MCOT provides up to 30 days of recording and automatic transmission of significant detected arrhythmias to a trained 24-hour central station, making it the preferred initial external option when abrupt incapacity makes patient activation unreliable.[25][28] The 2021 ESC pacing guideline recommends progressively longer ambulatory ECG recording as symptoms become less frequent and recommends an implantable loop recorder (ILR) after nondiagnostic non-invasive testing for infrequent bradycardia episodes occurring less than once per month.[88] An implantable cardiac monitor (ICM) provides prolonged monitoring without external electrodes and is the investigative tool of choice for recurrent unexplained syncope after negative initial investigations when infrequent recurrent syncope is thought to be arrhythmic.[89]

The insurer-policy descriptions of auto-triggered loop monitors specify approximately 30–90 seconds of ECG before activation and about 60 seconds afterward.[107][23]

The ordering clinician shares responsibility for obtaining the ordered-study result and must name a qualified designee who receives results, notifies the patient, and initiates follow-up if the ordering clinician is unavailable. The Washington Medical Commission’s 2025 time-critical-medical-information guidance requires identification of the responsible person, confirmation that both the care team and patient received time-critical information, and documentation of who communicated it, how, and when. Findings suggesting immediate or urgent intervention require non-routine communication; merely posting the result in the electronic medical record may not be sufficient when time is critical to a safe outcome. Documentation of communication of time-critical medical information should be placed contemporaneously in the medical record.[90]

MCOT provides real-time, comprehensive data and immediate transmission without patient participation in transmission, but its real-time data may require clinician review daily at any time of day or night; wire-electrode MCOT also requires daily electrode changes and has lower acceptance for long-term use.[3] MCOT requires significant manpower and averages three times the cost of other standard external monitors.[12] In a retrospective comparison, MCOT monitoring averaged 20 days versus 27 days for auto-trigger looping event recorder monitoring and had diagnostic yields 128% higher for atrial fibrillation, 54% higher for bradycardia, 17% higher for ventricular pause, 80% higher for supraventricular tachycardia, and 222% higher for ventricular tachycardia; mean time to diagnosis of each asymptomatic arrhythmia was shorter with MCOT, and management changes and health outcomes were not discussed.[54] No randomized studies have addressed whether MCOT changes management of, or outcomes after, potentially emergent events.[54][23][7]

Consumer devices are not a substitute for prescribed ambulatory ECG monitoring in this situation. Recording completeness must be checked as analyzable time, meaning recorded time minus ECG signal rendered unusable by artifact, rather than by wear time alone.[46] In one 14-day patch-monitoring study, mean wear time was 12.3 ± 3.2 days but mean analyzable data were 11.4 ± 3.6 days; 70.9% completed 14 days, and 29.1% did not.[59] The monitor report and follow-up review must therefore document wear duration, analyzable duration, signal gaps, whether his usual symptoms occurred, and the raw tracing for each suspected block event.[46][59]

LT-ECG used human review of the full recording, whereas MCT used human review of algorithm-identified portions.[38]

The cited MCT-versus-LT-ECG findings apply to the one MCT system and one continuous LT-ECG monitor tested.[38]

### Synthesis

The immediate safety priority is to prevent injury while diagnostic uncertainty remains: he should not drive, should avoid high-risk solo activities, should lie or sit down promptly with symptoms, and should call 911 for syncope, injury, serious associated symptoms, or inability to recover safely. Metoprolol may contribute to bradycardia or conduction worsening and needs urgent prescriber review, but abrupt self-discontinuation is unsafe.[21][17][78][73]

Living alone makes a patient-triggered recorder and a monitor-only safety plan poor choices. A home-delivered, continuously worn, auto-triggered service with 24-hour alert handling is the more workable outpatient design, provided the team confirms connectivity, signal quality, who receives alerts, and how he will be reached. Even then, remote monitoring is diagnostic surveillance—not rescue—and a monitor gap, unclear single-lead tracing, or delayed alert must trigger direct clinical reassessment rather than reassurance.[2][3][79]

## Thresholds for urgent evaluation and pacemaker consultation

An intermittent conduction disorder can be dangerous when the heart suddenly fails to transmit atrial impulses to the ventricles, even if the resting ECG is relatively reassuring between episodes. The immediate task is therefore to separate symptoms that require emergency monitored care from findings that justify rapid ambulatory diagnosis, and to distinguish a pacemaker indication from a concerning but still unclassified ECG observation.

### Which findings or symptoms require immediate emergency evaluation rather than

**Actual syncope requires emergency assessment.** Syncope means transient loss of consciousness from reduced blood flow to the brain; it is not the same as lightheadedness without loss of consciousness. The Emergency Physicians page 《Fainting — Know When to Go to the ER》 directs people to “call 911 or your local emergency number” if someone faints or appears to be fainting, and to call 911 if a person who has fainted “doesn’t regain consciousness within a minute.”[20][78][27]

Cardiopulmonary compromise is an emergency regardless of the exact rhythm. The American Heart Association adult bradycardia algorithm identifies hypotension, acute altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest discomfort, and acute heart failure as signs of cardiopulmonary compromise. A slow pulse with one of these findings requires emergency evaluation, continuous ECG monitoring, and treatment capability rather than outpatient workup.[55]

The acute bradycardia pathway includes establishing intravenous access alongside continuous ECG, blood-pressure and oxygen-saturation monitoring, a 12-lead ECG, and assessment for reversible causes.[56]

The following symptoms accompanying fainting or near-fainting should also redirect care to an emergency department: chest pain, trouble breathing, a rapid or irregular heartbeat, one-sided weakness or numbness, speech difficulty, severe headache, serious injury, prolonged unresponsiveness, difficulty awakening, or persistent confusion. Syncope during exertion or while supine is also an emergency warning pattern.[76][20]

Worsening presyncope requires urgent reassessment when a rhythm disorder is plausible. Presyncope is the sensation of impending fainting without loss of consciousness. Recurrent or intensifying lightheadedness, especially with a slow or irregular pulse, should not be managed by simply waiting for a routine cardiology appointment in a 68-year-old man with an abnormal ECG and possible dropped beats. Symptoms attributable to bradycardia include presyncope, transient dizziness or lightheadedness, heart-failure symptoms, and confusion from low cerebral blood flow.[49][1][4]

A systolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or lower, heart rate of 40 beats/minute or lower, and new high-risk ECG abnormalities are high-risk initial-assessment findings in syncope evaluation. Persistent awake sinus bradycardia below 40 beats/minute or an awake sinus pause longer than 3 seconds make arrhythmic syncope highly probable in the ESC syncope guideline.[20][42]

One acute bradyarrhythmia guideline identifies systolic blood pressure below 90 mm Hg or heart rate below 40 beats/min as adverse signs; these values must be interpreted alongside symptoms, perfusion, and rhythm.[113]

An awake escape rhythm below 40 beats/minute, an asystolic period of at least 3 seconds, or an infra-Hisian escape rhythm is a reported marker supporting permanent-pacemaker assessment when AV block is irreversible.[103]

The reported resting blood pressure of 118/74 mmHg and heart rate of 52 beats/minute do not meet those cited instability cutoffs. They do not remove the need for expedited evaluation because the symptoms are recurrent and the suspected conduction abnormality is intermittent. AV-block manifestations depend on whether block is fixed or intermittent and on the ventricular rate or duration of ventricular asystole when the block occurs.[4][2]

Bradyarrhythmia is conventionally defined as a heart rate below 60 beats/minute, but rates below that threshold can be benign in some settings.[113]

Recorded high-risk rhythms require monitored emergency assessment. Recent asystole, Mobitz II second-degree AV block, complete heart block with a broad QRS complex, and a ventricular pause longer than 3 seconds are risk-of-asystole features in the Resuscitation Council UK adult bradycardia algorithm. Mobitz II or third-degree AV block on ECG also makes arrhythmic syncope highly probable in the ESC syncope guideline.[56][42]

High-grade or advanced AV block means that at least two consecutive P waves fail to conduct to the ventricles while some atrioventricular conduction remains. Third-degree AV block, also called complete heart block, means no atrial impulses conduct to the ventricles. These are materially different findings from a single reported dropped beat.[8][6][4]

A newly recorded Mobitz II pattern warrants cardiology consultation even without symptoms. Clinical management guidance recommends admission to a monitored bed with transcutaneous and transvenous pacing capability for Mobitz II, including in an asymptomatic patient, because it can progress to complete heart block.[32]

Second- or third-degree AV block with symptoms or hemodynamic compromise that persists despite treatment of reversible contributors warrants acute pacing management. Temporary transvenous pacing is reasonable in that situation, while temporary transcutaneous pacing can serve as a short bridge when hemodynamic compromise persists until transvenous or permanent pacing is placed or the bradyarrhythmia resolves.[6][4]

Transcutaneous pacing is not a durable substitute for transvenous or permanent pacing because prolonged use can be unreliable and poorly tolerated.[102]

For symptomatic sinus bradycardia or AV block, atropine 0.5–1 mg intravenously may be repeated every 3–5 minutes to a maximum total dose of 3 mg.[8]

If atropine is ineffective and cardiopulmonary compromise persists, Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support guidance calls for transcutaneous pacing and/or a dopamine or epinephrine infusion.[114]

A monitor alert is not a disposition decision. An ambulatory-telemetry example protocol uses 24-hour physician notification for Mobitz II or third-degree AV block and for a patient-reported symptom-associated pause of at least 4 seconds; that workflow does not replace monitored admission and pacing capability for new Mobitz II block. A ventricular pause longer than 3 seconds is a risk-of-asystole feature, not a universal permanent-pacemaker threshold.[3][56][32]

The same distinction applies to pauses. An ambulatory-telemetry example uses a symptom-associated pause of at least 4 seconds for 24-hour notification, while the bradycardia algorithm lists a ventricular pause longer than 3 seconds as a risk-of-asystole feature. Neither number is a universal stand-alone permanent-pacemaker threshold; the rhythm mechanism, symptoms, perfusion, and clinical context govern disposition.[3][56][2]

Immediate emergency evaluation is required for syncope, cardiopulmonary compromise, or a documented dangerous conduction rhythm; recurrent presyncope with suspected block should trigger urgent reassessment rather than passive outpatient waiting. This distinguishes an emergency state from the current diagnostic question of whether his intermittent symptoms are caused by clinically significant AV block.[55][18][20]

The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline recommends hospital evaluation and treatment when initial assessment identifies a serious condition relevant to syncope, including symptomatic conduction-system disease, Mobitz II or third-degree AV block, or symptomatic bradycardia or sinus pauses not due to neurally mediated syncope. Its serious-condition examples also include sustained or symptomatic ventricular tachycardia, symptomatic supraventricular tachycardia, pacemaker or defibrillator malfunction, cardiac ischemia, severe aortic stenosis, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, acute heart failure, moderate-to-severe left-ventricular dysfunction, severe anemia or gastrointestinal bleeding, major traumatic injury from syncope, and persistent vital-sign abnormalities.[25][28]

When high-risk features suggest arrhythmic syncope, the 2018 ESC guideline states that in-hospital ECG monitoring in bed or by telemetry is warranted, particularly when applied immediately after syncope; its reported diagnostic yield is 1.9–17.6%. The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guidance gives continuous ECG monitoring for hospitalized patients admitted for syncope evaluation with suspected cardiac etiology a Class I-B-NR recommendation. High-risk patients require early intensive evaluation in an emergency-department observation unit, syncope unit, or hospital.[22][31]

An event or loop recorder is not the initial diagnostic tool when the clinical and ECG findings indicate a high risk of life-threatening arrhythmia or an independent pacemaker or defibrillator indication. In that setting, hospitalization for treatment or extensive evaluation, including prolonged in-hospital telemetry or electrophysiologic study, is the recommended route.[37]

The American Academy of Family Physicians article 《Syncope: Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis》 reports that presyncope and syncope have similar adverse-outcome rates and states that both should receive a similar evaluation. Thus, recurrent presyncope in this patient warrants the same urgent clinical evaluation pathway as syncope when arrhythmia is suspected.[30]

The ESC summary distinguishes patients with neither high- nor low-risk features from high-risk patients: they require expert syncope assessment that can probably be managed safely as an outpatient, and management in an emergency-department observation unit and/or fast-track to an outpatient syncope unit is beneficial. This is the appropriate route when immediate high-risk criteria are absent but routine three-month follow-up is unsafe.[94]

Presyncope while seated, recurrent at rest, or severe enough that he cannot stand or walk safely requires emergency assessment, particularly because it can cause a fall or motor-vehicle crash. During an acute episode, he should call emergency medical services and not drive himself.

Patients judged high risk should be admitted to a unit with 24-hour ECG telemetry and the capability to manage clinical deterioration.[20]

In a study of 174 emergency-department patients with symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia, the median time to detection of significant heart block was 5.8 days; the significant-arrhythmia endpoint included Mobitz II block, third-degree AV block, a pause of at least 3 seconds, and symptomatic bradycardia.[95]

In that study of 174 emergency-department patients, 83 patients (47.7%) had a significant arrhythmia under the study’s broad definition; the median time to detect a sinus pause was 4.2 days.[104]

### Which clinical thresholds should prompt immediate pacemaker consultation rather than

The central pacemaker threshold is a documented acquired Mobitz II second-degree AV block, high-grade AV block, or third-degree AV block that is not due to a reversible or physiologic cause. The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline recommends permanent pacing for these findings regardless of whether the patient reports symptoms.[1][96][6][4]

The ACC/AHA/HRS recommendation for permanent pacing in acquired Mobitz II, high-grade, or third-degree AV block without a reversible or physiologic cause is Class I; the cited evidence level for third-degree AV block is B-NR.[6][102]

High-grade AV block is not merely a long PR interval or one omitted QRS complex. It requires at least two consecutive nonconducted P waves at a normal physiologic rate with intermittent remaining AV conduction. Complete AV block requires no evidence of AV conduction.[1][6]

**Immediate cardiology or electrophysiology consultation should follow any recording of Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block.** The consultation should occur in a monitored, pacing-capable setting when the finding is new, when symptoms are present, or when the escape rhythm is slow, broad-complex, or unreliable. Infranodal block—block in or below the His bundle—can progress rapidly and unexpectedly, has a slower and less predictable escape rhythm, and may not respond to atropine.[32][4][10]

Atropine may be unreliable in suspected infranodal block and can worsen conduction by accelerating the atrial rate; atropine failure with hypotension and suspected distal block therefore calls for pacing readiness.[115]

Paroxysmal block does not become benign because normal conduction returns between episodes. ESC pacing guidance states that permanent or paroxysmal third-degree, Mobitz II, infranodal 2:1, and high-degree AV block have pacing indications irrespective of symptoms once reversible causes are excluded.[5][11]

The ACC/AHA/HRS chronic AV-block algorithm places evidence of infranodal block on its Class I pacing pathway alongside complete, advanced, and Mobitz II AV block.[8]

Syncope plus symptomatic conduction-system disease is a hospital-level threshold. The ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline recommends hospital evaluation and treatment when syncope is associated with a serious condition identified in the initial assessment. Its examples include symptomatic conduction-system disease, Mobitz II or third-degree heart block, and symptomatic bradycardia or sinus pauses not caused by neurally mediated syncope.[18][19]

The 2024 article 《Syncope in the Emergency Department: A Practical Approach》 lists the following diagnostic ECG features in cardiac arrhythmic syncope: a pause longer than 3 seconds; sustained or nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, whether symptomatic or asymptomatic; rapid paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia; high-grade AV block, including Mobitz II second-degree and third-degree AV block; bradycardia below 30 beats/minute, whether symptomatic or asymptomatic; bradycardia below 50 beats/minute in a symptomatic patient; tachycardia above 120 beats/minute in a symptomatic patient; implantable pacemaker or defibrillator dysfunction; and alternating left and right bundle branch block. These findings prompt urgent clinical review and are not interchangeable universal criteria for permanent-pacemaker implantation.[20][56]

A heart rate below 50 beats/minute defines bradycardia in the cited guideline context, but that rate alone does not establish a permanent-pacing indication.[116][117]

For adults older than 40 years with severe, recurrent, unpredictable syncope, ESC pacing guidance identifies spontaneous pauses due to sinus arrest or AV block longer than 3 seconds when symptomatic, or longer than 6 seconds when asymptomatic, as criteria for dual-chamber pacing to reduce recurrent syncope. Those thresholds apply to that defined recurrent-syncope population.[5][97][98]

The ACC/AHA/HRS guidance addresses pause duration differently: no single pause duration alone warrants permanent pacing in an asymptomatic patient. A pause that causes symptoms or reflects infranodal block carries different significance from an incidental asymptomatic pause.[2][8]

First-degree AV delay does not meet the advanced-block threshold. First-degree AV block is more accurately called first-degree AV delay: every P wave conducts, but the PR interval exceeds 200 ms. The patient’s PR interval of 240 ms therefore indicates conduction delay, not high-grade block.[1][4]

A PR interval of 240 ms is below the greater-than-0.30-second threshold used to describe severe first-degree AV block; a PR interval greater than 0.30 seconds with a narrow QRS usually indicates AV-nodal delay. Severe first-degree delay can produce pacemaker-syndrome-like fatigue, exertional intolerance, or reduced cardiac output, but pacing is considered when symptoms are clearly attributable to the conduction delay rather than from PR prolongation alone.[1][5][6]

Mobitz I second-degree AV block, also called Wenckebach block, and AV-nodal 2:1 block usually require symptom–rhythm correlation before pacing. The guideline advises against permanent pacing when these patterns are asymptomatic or when the symptoms do not occur at the time of the block.[1][6][4]

A 2:1 pattern deserves more caution than an isolated dropped beat because it may be difficult to classify as nodal Mobitz I or infranodal disease. When 2:1 block cannot be classified, clinical guidance recommends admission and cardiology consultation and advises treating it as Mobitz II for safety until its level is clarified.[32][11]

Metoprolol changes the reversible-cause assessment, not the emergency threshold for confirmed advanced block. Beta blockers can slow sinus rate and AV conduction and are listed among medication-related causes of AV block. The prescriber should promptly review the recent metoprolol initiation, the exact dose and adherence, other rate-slowing medicines, blood pressure response, and whether the drug remains necessary for hypertension control.[5][2]

A therapeutic beta-blocker dose should not be assumed to be the sole explanation for new advanced AV block. The ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that therapeutic beta-blocker doses are not commonly responsible for presentation with new AV block, and that block may persist or recur after the medication is stopped.[2][4]

If a known reversible cause produces symptomatic AV block and the block does not resolve after the cause is treated, permanent pacing is recommended. Conversely, a completely resolved block due to a known reversible, nonrecurrent cause should not lead automatically to permanent pacing.[6][4]

If symptomatic second- or third-degree AV block is documented while a patient is taking a chronic, stable, medically necessary beta blocker, proceeding to permanent pacing without waiting for drug washout is reasonable in selected patients. That pathway presupposes documented advanced block and a medication that must continue; it does not convert this man’s PR interval of 240 ms or unrecorded dropped beats into a pacemaker indication.[1][6][4]

The decisive threshold is documented nonreversible advanced conduction disease: Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block requires urgent pacing-capable consultation, whereas first-degree AV delay and unclassified dropped beats require expedited rhythm definition unless instability or dangerous symptoms intervene.[1][32]

The 2024 emergency-medicine syncope consensus classifies Mobitz II second-degree and third-degree AV block as major high-risk ECG features. It classifies Mobitz I second-degree block, first-degree AV block with a markedly prolonged PR interval, and asymptomatic bradycardia of 40–50 beats/minute as minor high-risk ECG features only when the history is consistent with arrhythmogenic syncope. This supports immediate escalation for documented Mobitz II or complete block, not for this man’s PR interval of 240 ms or heart rate of 52 beats/minute alone.[94]

Intermittent or paroxysmal intrinsic second- or third-degree AV block is an ESC pacing indication even when symptom–ECG correlation has not been recorded. Cardiac pacing is also indicated when syncope has an established relationship to symptomatic bradycardia or symptomatic AV block.[22]

New second- or third-degree AV block during exercise, particularly when not due to ischemia, strongly suggests infranodal conduction disease, carries a high risk of progression, and is a guideline-described indication for permanent pacing.[52][22]

A reversible cause must be addressed before permanent pacing for bradycardia. The ESC guideline identifies elimination of drugs that exacerbate or unmask susceptibility to bradycardia as an important measure to prevent recurrent syncope, which supports clinician-led review of the recently initiated metoprolol.[22]

The 2024 syncope consensus identifies additional major high-risk ECG features: signs of acute ischemia, ischemic heart disease, or cardiomyopathy; bundle branch block; intraventricular conduction disturbances; ventricular hypertrophy; sustained or nonsustained ventricular tachycardia; type 1 Brugada pattern with ST elevation in V1–V3; repeated QTc longer than 460 milliseconds suggesting long-QT syndrome; slow atrial fibrillation below 40 beats/minute; persistent sinus bradycardia below 40 beats/minute; repetitive sinoatrial block or awake sinus pauses longer than 3 seconds; and pacemaker or implantable-cardioverter-defibrillator dysfunction.[94]

Mobitz II must be identified by its actual ECG pattern. Mobitz II second-degree AV block is a single nonconducted P wave with constant PR intervals before and after the blocked impulse and stable P-P and R-R intervals; the pause spanning the blocked P wave equals two P-P cycles. Once this pattern is recorded, it is not an unclassified dropped beat and should trigger pacing-capable consultation.[14][13]

Mobitz II second-degree AV block has consistent PR intervals in conducted beats followed by a nonconducted P wave and dropped QRS complex, without the progressive PR prolongation of Mobitz I block.[99]

A 2:1 AV-block recording cannot be labeled Mobitz I or Mobitz II from the ratio alone because only one PR interval precedes the blocked P wave. The block can be at the AV node or in the His-Purkinje system; intraventricular conduction block points to a distal site, whereas a narrow QRS complex is usually associated with AV-nodal block.[14][9]

An asymptomatic narrow-QRS 2:1 block that disappears with exercise and has accompanying Wenckebach behavior may arise above the His bundle and may not require immediate pacing.[118]

If a 2:1 AV-block pattern worsens with exertion, that increases concern that the block is infranodal, below the AV node; electrophysiology-study evidence can also support that location.[115]

Intermittent complete AV block warrants the same urgency as persistent complete block. It can begin suddenly with no ventricular escape rhythm, produce asystole, then revert to an escape rhythm or conducted beats; a normal interval between events does not exclude this dangerous pattern.[14][13]

In complete AV block, the atria and ventricles are completely dissociated, with no observable relationship between P waves and QRS complexes because no atrial impulses conduct to the ventricles.[99][100]

### What remains unknown until a diagnostic rhythm recording and definitive

The dropped beats must be classified on a rhythm strip or 12-lead ECG during an episode. A recording during symptoms provides the definitive diagnosis because it can show whether the event is sinus slowing, Mobitz I, Mobitz II, 2:1 AV block, high-grade AV block, complete AV block, a sinus pause, or another arrhythmia.[5][11][4][8]

The recording must also show the event’s severity: whether block is intermittent or fixed, the number of nonconducted P waves, the ventricular escape rate, QRS width, and the duration of any pause or ventricular asystole. These variables determine the clinical effect and help distinguish AV-nodal disease from disease in or below the His bundle.[1][2][4]

A normal or narrow QRS complex between episodes does not exclude clinically significant block within or below the His bundle.[119]

**Symptom–rhythm correlation remains the key diagnostic answer.** Symptomatic bradycardia requires a documented bradyarrhythmia directly responsible for lightheadedness, presyncope, syncope, heart-failure symptoms, or confusion. Fatigue is nonspecific and often has multiple causes, so fatigue alone cannot identify the rhythm mechanism.[1][4][2]

If typical lightheadedness occurs during monitoring without a rhythm abnormality, that recorded episode is unlikely to be caused by an arrhythmia. If lightheadedness or syncope occurs with a documented pause or AV block, the recording establishes the symptom–rhythm relationship needed for targeted treatment.[1][36][42][40]

The mechanism of any captured AV block also remains to be determined. Transient high-grade AV block without slowing of sinus discharge supports intrinsic conduction-system disease, whereas simultaneous sinus slowing and P-P interval prolongation suggests a vagal or neurocardiogenic mechanism. The distinction matters because asymptomatic vagally mediated AV block is not a pacing indication.[3][15][4][6]

Metoprolol may contribute to sinus bradycardia, AV block, or orthostatic hypotension. Orthostatic hypotension requires supine and standing measurements and is defined by a systolic fall of at least 20 mmHg, a diastolic fall of at least 10 mmHg, or standing systolic pressure below 90 mmHg; a resting pressure of 118/74 mmHg does not answer that question.[20][42]

Potential non-drug contributors also require clinical assessment. Intrinsic and extrinsic causes of bradycardia and AV block include degenerative conduction disease, ischemia or infarction, cardiomyopathy, infiltrative or infectious disease, vagal reflexes, electrolyte imbalance, metabolic disorders, and hypothyroidism.[11][4]

The supplied potassium, magnesium, creatinine, and thyroid-stimulating hormone values reduce concern for several common metabolic contributors but do not classify the rhythm mechanism. Targeted testing for suspected reversible causes may include thyroid function, Lyme testing, potassium, calcium, pH, and drug levels where clinically relevant.[1][5][6]

Structural evaluation remains part of definitive risk assessment. Cardiac imaging is recommended for suspected or documented symptomatic bradycardia to identify structural heart disease, measure left-ventricular systolic function, and investigate causes of conduction disturbance. If permanent pacing becomes indicated, ventricular-arrhythmia risk and the possible need for defibrillator capability require separate assessment.[5][1][2]

An electrophysiology study, or EPS, is an invasive catheter test that can locate AV block at the AV node, within the His bundle, or below it when ECG and telemetry do not settle the question. EPS is generally not first-line testing, but may clarify block level and pacing benefit after nondiagnostic noninvasive evaluation.[4][52]

For syncope with bundle branch block, an HV interval—the measured conduction time from the His bundle to the ventricles—of at least 70 milliseconds or definite infranodal block at EPS is a permanent-pacing indication. Alternating right and left bundle branch block is itself a pacing indication because it signals substantial infranodal disease and risk of sudden complete block.[4]

Alternating bundle-branch block can be recognized when serial ECGs show right bundle-branch block at one time and left bundle-branch block at another, indicating disease in both major ventricular conduction pathways.[120]

Bifascicular block usually consists of right bundle-branch block plus left anterior or left posterior fascicular block. Unexplained syncope or recurrent true presyncope in this setting warrants urgent monitored evaluation and electrophysiology assessment because intermittent complete AV block may be responsible.[121]

The unresolved clinical question is not whether the PR interval is abnormal; it is whether intermittent symptoms represent intrinsic, clinically significant AV block, a reversible medication or physiologic effect, orthostatic hypotension, or a non-rhythm cause. A diagnostic recording during a typical episode, together with medication review and structural evaluation, resolves that question and determines whether monitoring, medication adjustment, temporary pacing, or permanent pacing is appropriate.[5][1][20][2]

A rhythm recording establishes arrhythmic syncope when syncope is correlated with bradyarrhythmia or tachyarrhythmia. Conversely, absence of arrhythmia during an actually recorded syncopal episode excludes arrhythmic syncope for that episode.[31][94]

ECG monitoring cannot record blood pressure concurrently. Thus, bradycardia or asystole captured during an event does not exclude a hypotensive reflex as the principal mechanism, with the rhythm abnormality occurring later. Supine and standing blood pressure and heart-rate measurements during 3 minutes of active standing are part of the initial assessment for orthostatic hypotension.[22][94]

An uncaptured dropped beat may also be a blocked premature atrial beat, an early atrial impulse that fails to conduct, rather than true AV block. The recording should establish the frequency, duration, and timing of events, including whether they occur awake, asleep, at rest, or with activity.

### Synthesis —

The patient’s PR prolongation to 240 ms and reported dropped beats justify expedited investigation, but they are not equivalent to documented high-grade or complete AV block. The disposition changes immediately if he faints, develops hypotension, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest discomfort, acute heart failure, or has a recording of Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block. Confirmed nonreversible Mobitz II, high-grade, or third-degree block carries a permanent-pacing recommendation irrespective of symptoms; first-degree AV delay and likely nodal block instead require symptom–rhythm correlation. Metoprolol warrants prompt clinician-led review, but it does not safely explain away confirmed advanced block.[55][1][2][4]

1. **Seek emergency care now** for syncope, severe or worsening near-syncope, chest pain, breathlessness, new confusion, shock symptoms, or acute heart-failure symptoms.
2. **Treat any recorded Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block as an urgent cardiology/electrophysiology consultation**, with monitored pacing-capable evaluation rather than outpatient follow-up.
3. **Do not treat PR 240 ms or an unrecorded dropped beat as a pacemaker indication by itself;** obtain the diagnostic rhythm recording that classifies the event and links it to symptoms.
4. **Have the prescribing clinician review metoprolol promptly; do not independently stop or alter it.** For chronic LOPRESSOR therapy, the FDA label directs clinician-supervised gradual dose reduction over 1–2 weeks with monitoring when discontinuation is chosen. This outpatient discontinuation advice does not delay emergency clinician-directed treatment of documented second- or third-degree AV block with symptoms or hemodynamic compromise: when the block is refractory to medical therapy, temporary transvenous pacing is reasonable, and temporary transcutaneous pacing may be used as a bridge until transvenous or permanent pacing is placed or the bradyarrhythmia resolves. [16][6][4]

### Open questions

The open clinical question is the rhythm during the reported dropped beats and symptoms. A diagnostic tracing showing the P-wave-to-QRS relationship, along with QRS width, bundle-branch-block status, orthostatic vital signs, and response to clinician-directed metoprolol adjustment, would determine whether this is medication-amplified AV-nodal delay, intermittent high-grade infranodal block, or a nonarrhythmic cause of lightheadedness.
