# Comparison of Maternal Health in Cox’s Bazar and Mae La

## Executive summary

The defensible verdict is that Cox’s Bazar and Mae La cannot be ranked on maternal or newborn outcomes for 2017–2023 because the requested camp-specific indicators are not available on a common, comparable basis.

Cox’s Bazar had a large humanitarian-and-government-linked referral network, with NGO field hospitals as important nodes, while Mae La’s documented routine maternity care was NGO-led camp care with referral to outside hospitals rather than a Thai Ministry of Public Health-operated camp model.[1][2][3][4] Cox’s Bazar nevertheless documented extensive emergency-referral capacity and persistent gaps in round-the-clock surgery, blood, laboratory and staffing capacity, whereas Mae La documented clinic ANC, delivery and newborn care, Special Care Baby Units and hospital transfer for caesarean delivery.[5][6][7][3][8] These service differences plausibly shape access and delay, but they do not establish that one setting’s delivery model, staffing, transport or traditional-birth-attendant mediation caused a difference in maternal mortality, four-visit ANC completion, skilled attendance, postpartum haemorrhage or 28-day neonatal mortality.

A reported 2021 UNFPA funding reduction cannot be treated as a demonstrated cause of maternal-service discontinuity in either location, and it should not be attributed to Mae La without location-specific documentation.

This comparison covers events and data recorded from 2017-01-01 through 2023-12-31; publication after that end date does not make a later event part of the comparison. The verdict turns on whether comparable camp-specific maternal and newborn outcome measures exist; it would change if both settings supplied aligned indicators with the same population coverage, period, numerator, denominator and case definitions.

## Care settings, delivery models and indicator coverage

### What in the circumstances of Rohingya refugees in Cox's Bazar

Judgment — Maternal care in a refugee settlement is a care pathway, not simply a building. It begins with recognising pregnancy and danger signs in the community, continues through antenatal care (ANC, care during pregnancy), childbirth and postnatal care (PNC, care after birth), and depends on rapid escalation when haemorrhage, eclampsia, obstructed labour, sepsis or newborn illness requires capabilities beyond a primary facility. Cox’s Bazar operated such a tiered pathway, but under a large, multi-provider humanitarian response rather than a single NGO field-hospital system.

From the 2017 influx onward, Cox’s Bazar’s Rohingya response combined community health workers, health posts, primary health-care centres (PHCs), labour rooms and sexual and reproductive health facilities, secondary-care facilities, field hospitals, government Upazila Health Complexes, and tertiary hospitals. The 2023–2024 Health Sector Strategic Plan placed health posts and PHCs at the primary level; field hospitals and the Teknaf and Ukhiya Upazila Health Complexes at the secondary level; and Cox’s Bazar Sadar District Hospital and Chittagong Medical College Hospital at the tertiary referral level. The World Bank-supported model similarly described camp community health volunteers, outpatient posts, primary facilities providing basic emergency obstetric care, and strengthened adjacent government facilities.[1][9]

The mass displacement that began on 25 August 2017 had brought 370,000 new Rohingya arrivals by 13 September 2017, changing the population denominator during the early emergency response.[99][100]

Basic emergency obstetric and newborn care (BEmONC) is the initial emergency package for women and newborns with complications. It includes immediate clinical actions such as giving uterotonics to stop uterine bleeding, antibiotics for infection, anticonvulsants for eclampsia, manual removal of the placenta, newborn resuscitation and referral where surgery or blood transfusion is required. Cox’s Bazar’s February 2020 minimum package assigned ANC and PNC to both health posts and PHCs; health posts were expected to stabilise and refer obstetric and newborn emergencies, while PHCs were expected to provide normal deliveries and BEmONC.[10]

The 2017–2023 system was strongly humanitarian and NGO-supported, but it retained formal Bangladeshi governmental leadership and referral links. By October 2019, more than 100 health partners coordinated over 200 facilities offering primary, secondary and specialised care. The Ministry of Health’s Civil Surgeon’s Office, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare Coordination Centre, and WHO led health-sector coordination. Healthcare provision in the camps was described as a collaborative effort among NGOs, United Nations agencies and the Bangladesh government.[11][12]

By late 2018, the Cox’s Bazar response involved Bangladesh authorities, more than 100 NGOs, UN agencies and donors, with more than 200 doctors and nurses and more than 1,200 community health workers.[101][102]

The delivery network changed as the emergency became protracted. In 2019, following an assessment of facility quality and distribution, more than one-third of facilities were relocated or closed, with continued workforce development and community advocacy. By the end of 2021, the camps had 42 PHCs, 89 health posts and three field hospitals operating under the Government of Bangladesh and Health Sector’s Minimum Essential Service Package; PHCs and field hospitals were described as operating 24 hours a day despite COVID-19 restrictions.[13][14]

In 2023, the Health Sector reported 77 partners, 67 health posts, 47 PHCs, eight secondary-care facilities and one facility listed as providing comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care (CEmONC). CEmONC is the higher-level package that adds surgery, including caesarean section, and blood transfusion to emergency obstetric and newborn functions. The same bulletin named pregnancy, birthing and newborn care among the continuing priorities of the facility-based and community-health response.[15][16]

The reported CEmONC footprint differs across 2023 publications. The Sexual and Reproductive Health Working Group (SRH WG) identified four secondary referral facilities with CEmONC in the first quarter: IOM-supported Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes, the IOM-supported Friendship Maternity Centre, and the UNFPA-supported Hope Field Hospital. The December Health Sector summary listed one facility with CEmONC services and eight secondary-care facilities. The SRH WG’s first-quarter 2023 bulletin reported four secondary referral facilities providing CEmONC, while the December 2023 Health Sector summary listed one facility with CEmONC services.[5][15][16]

NGO field hospitals were therefore important secondary-care nodes, not the entirety of maternity provision. The Strategic Plan grouped field hospitals with government Upazila Health Complexes as secondary care, while the wider response relied on community and primary-care facilities to identify, manage and refer pregnant women. In 2021, Health Sector partners operated 41 PHCs, 90 health posts, 23 special facilities and three field hospitals; in May 2021 the corresponding network was reported as 38 PHCs, 97 health posts, 23 special facilities and three field hospitals.[1][17][18]

MSF provides the clearest operational account of an NGO’s role in this network. In May 2019, Médecins Sans Frontières reported operating three field hospitals with inpatient capacity, four 24-hour PHCs, a specialised reproductive and mental-health clinic, a disease-outbreak isolation centre and two fixed health posts. MSF described itself as one of the major providers of inpatient paediatric, neonatal and adult care.[6]

From August 2017 through March 2019, MSF recorded 47,556 antenatal consultations and 2,750 deliveries in Cox’s Bazar. These are service contacts and births managed by MSF, not the number or proportion of all Rohingya women receiving ANC or completing four visits.[6]

MSF’s 2023 operational report describes Kutupalong Hospital as a 131-bed referral hub with 24-hour inpatient and emergency services, antenatal care, PNC, family planning, maternity, neonatal and paediatric care, and sexual and reproductive healthcare. It referred 3,437 critical and priority patients to Cox’s Bazar and Chittagong hospitals in 2023. That referral volume shows that NGO hospital care was linked to, rather than a substitute for, higher-level external services.[2]

MSF’s 2023 operational report recorded 57,213 antenatal-care patients at Kutupalong Field Hospital; this is a facility service volume, not a Rohingya-wide coverage percentage.[2]

MSF’s Goyalmara Mother and Child Hospital supplied emergency, paediatric, neonatal, gynaecological and obstetric care to Rohingya refugees and host communities. Its outpatient and outreach catchment was approximately 100,000 people, and its 24-hour inpatient services included an 18-bed maternity unit, a 10-bed level-1 neonatal intensive care unit, nine neonatology beds, a 12-bed paediatric intensive-care unit and a 19-bed paediatric/inpatient therapeutic-feeding service.[2]

In Camps 14 and 15, MSF’s Hakimpara and Jamtoli PHCs served approximately 93,000 refugees as well as nearby host-community members. Both provided emergency and sexual and reproductive healthcare; deliveries and emergency services operated 24 hours a day. MSF reported assisting 5,224 births across Cox’s Bazar in 2023.[2]

MSF reported that its Jamtoli service moved to a new clinic in June 2023.[2]

In Camp 22, which housed 23,000 refugees, MSF was the sole facility maternity provider.[91]

MSF’s service network did not operate without pressure. In 2023, MSF reported that demand exceeded capacity in several areas. Hospital on the Hill restricted daily outpatient visits from September and redirected some patients, while Kutupalong Hospital redirected lower-priority outpatient cases to other facilities. This was a service-prioritisation response to demand; it does not describe a withdrawal of MSF maternity or neonatal services.[2]

UNFPA, IOM, UNHCR, UNICEF, government services and NGO implementing partners also had specific maternal-health roles. UNFPA led the SRH WG, which involved more than 40 government, NGO, international NGO and UN partners. In the first quarter of 2023, the Health Sector included 77 active partners, 45 PHCs, 77 health posts, three facilities reported as providing CEmONC and eight secondary-care facilities.[5]

UNFPA reported supporting 22 facilities serving Rohingya camps and host communities in January–March 2022. Those facilities recorded 23,616 ANC visits, 2,442 facility deliveries attended by skilled birth attendants (SBAs), 6,474 PNC visits and 32,482 family-planning visits. A skilled birth attendant is a doctor, nurse or midwife trained to manage normal birth and recognise and respond to obstetric emergencies; a traditional birth attendant is excluded from the UNHCR HIS definition.[19][20]

UNHCR supported 28 health facilities in the camps in 2022, together with 10 mental-health and psychosocial-support centres, 19 nutrition centres and two stabilisation centres. Its supported facilities aimed to provide primary and secondary healthcare and make tertiary referrals when needed. UNHCR also supported the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare with construction, repair, equipment, medical items, human resources and capacity building; the Ukhiya Specialised Hospital was operational by the end of 2022.[21]

MSF supported the Bangladesh government’s 250-bed Sadar Hospital from 2018 to 2023 and trained 700 staff before handing over that support.[2]

UNICEF supported a network of 17 public health centres providing basic healthcare, family planning, maternal and child health services, and treatment for infectious and non-communicable diseases. Its stated maternal-health approach included encouraging completion of ANC checks, medically supervised birth and newborn immunisation. UNICEF also provided technical assistance to the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare on protocols for low-birth-weight and sick newborn care and standard operating procedures for Special Care Neonatal Units.[22]

The principal conclusion is that Cox’s Bazar was a **multi-provider humanitarian-and-government-linked maternal-care system in which NGO-operated field hospitals supplied important inpatient, maternity, neonatal and referral capacity but did not define the whole delivery model.** Community workers, primary facilities, UN-supported referral services, government hospitals and tertiary centres were integral parts of the same pathway.[1][5][2][21]

#### Referral, emergency care and field-hospital limits

A functioning maternity pathway depends on referral because many life-threatening complications cannot be managed at a health post or routine delivery room. In Cox’s Bazar, community health workers identified pregnant women and danger signs, primary facilities provided BEmONC, secondary facilities were intended to provide CEmONC, and tertiary hospitals received more complex referrals.[5]

MSF reported that Kutupalong operated under Bangladesh Ministry of Health authorization, provided free basic emergency obstetric and newborn care, and referred patients to Ministry-staffed hospitals.[103]

UNFPA and its implementing partners developed a harmonised 24-hour obstetric referral service in 2021 for community-to-facility, facility-to-community and facility-to-facility movement. The response also created emergency referral transport for obstetric patients. This formalised referral pathway directly addressed the second delay in the three-delay model: delay in reaching an appropriate facility.[23]

The Cox’s Bazar referral system used standard operating procedures, decision support and referral committees to assign responsibilities across primary centres, field hospitals, Upazila Health Complexes and Sadar hospitals.[104]

The three-delay model distinguishes: first, delay in deciding to seek care; second, delay in reaching a facility; and third, delay in receiving appropriate care after arrival. It is a framework used to classify the sequence through which complications become fatal or severe. In 2023, the Rohingya Maternal and Perinatal Mortality Surveillance and Response system recorded at least one delay for 55 of 57 maternal deaths with delay status reported: 29 first delays, three second delays and 23 third delays.[24]

By the first quarter of 2023, a network of 1,400 community health workers identified and referred obstetric cases. They used stretcher carriers, wheelchairs and assigned Tom Tom agents, a local motorised transport arrangement, to move women with pregnancy danger signs toward care. The community system thus covered more than health education: it was part of emergency transportation.[5]

IOM’s Dispatch and Referral Unit provided 24-hour ambulance dispatch and transfer support, including for obstetric emergencies, and supported 14 ambulances dedicated to emergency inter-facility transfers. IOM also strengthened referral-level clinical capacity by deploying six obstetricians/gynaecologists, six anaesthetists and two medical officers; it provided operational and referral-cost support to Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes and supported tertiary referral at Sadar Hospital and Chittagong Medical College Hospital.[5]

The Cox’s Bazar referral system recorded 10,400 referral episodes in 2022 and 22,374 in January–October 2023, a reported 115% increase; episodes are not counts of unique women or confirmation that emergency treatment was completed.[104]

UNFPA-supported Emergency Referral Transport Services operated 24 hours a day for community referrals of emergency obstetric and neonatal cases. In the first quarter of 2023, eight ambulances were deployed with active hotline numbers; each ambulance carried a midwife and referral facilitators who could provide emergency obstetric care during transport. UNFPA also supported IRC with 12 ambulances linked to 12 referral hubs.[5]

The referral hubs were introduced in hard-to-reach parts of the camps where health facilities were sparse and home birth was common. Eight hubs began in 2019, and four additional hubs were added in July 2020. Between January and August 2020, these hubs recorded 3,330 referrals, of which 2,040, or 61%, were obstetric.[25][26]

Referral-hub monthly obstetric referrals rose from 203 in January 2020 to 342 after the July 2020 expansion.[105]

The hubs combined household identification, telephone access, assessment, ambulance-like transport, facility referral and return transport. Community health volunteers conducted door-to-door pregnancy identification, shared hotline numbers and arranged pickups. Among 98 surveyed mothers familiar with the hubs, 91% identified emergency transport as a service and 70% identified emergency care; among users, timely transport, 24-hour availability and staff behaviour were commonly reported reasons for valuing the service.[27]

Referral transport faced operational constraints. Providers reported poor mobile-phone coverage, inadequate roads and ambulance shortages; one ambulance usually supported two hubs. Camp terrain was hilly, roads were often unpaved and transfer could be slowed by security checks. These operational facts explain why a referral system can exist while urgency and actual transit remain variable.[27][10]

Qualitative accounts also described centres that were not continuously open, limited laboratories, missing inputs for manual removal of the placenta and assisted delivery, and shortages of referral management and 24-hour transport.[101]

Cox’s Bazar’s emergency pathway had clinical capacity gaps as well as transport barriers. A first-quarter 2021 facility-monitoring exercise found 21 facilities providing BEmONC with six signal functions but without assisted vaginal delivery; 41 facilities provided only three signal functions; 22 performed manual removal of the placenta; and 60 provided post-abortion care. The strategic plan treated universal BEmONC coverage through PHCs as a priority rather than an achieved condition.[1]

The 2020 MPMSR review reported limited 24-hour facilities around the camps and stated that Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes were not yet functional for 24-hour CEmONC. It also reported no blood bank in or near the camps, intermittent blood and laboratory services at most CEmONC facilities, and insufficient staff for concurrent critical caseloads.[7]

These were not merely theoretical deficiencies. MSF reported in 2019 that Rohingya refugees had limited access to round-the-clock lifesaving surgery within and outside camps. Surgical patients, including women requiring emergency obstetric procedures, were often referred to Cox’s Bazar town or Chittagong; the Chittagong journey took several hours by road, and delays could turn stable cases into life-threatening ones.[6]

The field-hospital model contained similar constraints. The health-sector coordination review found that, although the approximate ratio of hospitals to refugees exceeded the Sphere benchmark of one CEmONC facility per 500,000 people, many field hospitals lacked sufficient surgeons and anaesthetists to sustain 24-hour, seven-day services. A hospital rotation plan kept at least one facility available each day for emergency referrals, but blood availability, service reliability, workforce capacity and staff turnover still led to many emergency obstetric referrals to government hospitals.[11]

The issue is therefore not whether CEmONC sites and referral ambulances existed; they did. The decisive operational distinction is that the response had **a documented emergency pathway with variable 24-hour surgical, blood, laboratory and staffing capacity**, so nominal placement of a facility at referral level did not itself guarantee immediate comprehensive care.[5][6][7][11]

**Observation window —** This section covers maternal and newborn health events, services and indicator data from 1 January 2017 through 31 December 2023. The MSF *Activity Report 2023* was published in 2024 and reports 2023 Cox’s Bazar operations; UNHCR’s *Annual Results Report 2023* was also published in 2024 and reports 2023 Thai–Myanmar border activity.[2][4]

### Karen refugees in Mae La camp

Mae La Temporary Shelter is a predominantly Karen refugee setting. UNHCR’s 30 June 2019 profile recorded 35,373 persons of concern and reported that 93% of residents were Karen, 4% Burman, 1% Mon and 2% other ethnicities. Mae La is located in Tha Song Yang District, Tak Province, and was established in 1984.[28][29]

Mae La had 36,637 verified residents in May 2017; it lay 57 km from Mae Sot and approximately 8 km from Myanmar.[106][28]

Population counts vary because agencies used different administrative bases. UNHCR’s 2018 site report recorded 39,061 refugees; a December 2020 population table listed 29,505 people in the TBC verified caseload, 28,640 in the TBC assisted population and 34,320 in the Ministry of Interior/UNHCR verified population; TBC reported 29,746 residents in 2021 and approximately 29,615 in 2023. These figures describe different registration or assistance populations rather than a single maternal-care denominator.[30][31][32][33]

Mae La had camp-based care and Thai-hospital referrals.[3] UNHCR’s 2023 Thailand report states that NGOs covered health services along the Thai–Myanmar border while UNHCR’s role focused primarily on protection and solutions. It identifies IRC and Malteser International as providers of inpatient and outpatient care, including ANC for pregnant women, in border camps.[4]

The provider history reinforces this NGO-led pattern. In Mae La, MSF handed services to Aide Médicale Internationale in 2005, the service later passed to the American Refugee Council, and IRC took over in 2015. This sequence preceded the 2017–2023 period and establishes that the camp’s routine health provision was not an MSF-operated model during the requested years.[3]

Mae La’s preventive and curative services transferred to a single NGO in early 2018; the earlier SMRU service-reach figures do not measure care after that transfer.[107][34]

SMRU, the Shoklo Malaria Research Unit, documented a historical maternal and newborn-care model on the Thai–Myanmar border that included ANC, delivery care, newborn care, malaria screening and referral for caesarean delivery. A 2023 review reports that maternal and newborn care had been provided to refugees since 1984 and later expanded to migrant populations.[34]

A historical account reports that preventive and curative services transferred to a single NGO in early 2018 and identifies IRC as the camp health provider in 2019; pre-transfer SMRU clinical records therefore have a different provider basis from later service reporting.[34][28]

The same review describes substantial historical service reach in Mae La: SMRU provided ANC to approximately 90% of women in the camp, and 75% of those women delivered at the SMRU clinic. Those values are not assigned to a specific year in 2017–2023, but they describe the service configuration that shaped the camp’s maternity pathway.[34][8]

A separate account reports that 60% of women delivered in SMRU delivery suites and that nearly 20% of preterm births occurred at home. The 60% figure is a delivery-location measure, while the 20% relates specifically to preterm births; neither is a skilled-birth-attendance percentage.[34]

In 2008, SMRU established Special Care Baby Units (SCBUs) within its facilities for preterm, small or sick newborns. The units were described as the first such units for refugees and migrants outside the Thai hospital system, documenting specialised neonatal care in the historical SMRU facility model.[8]

For women requiring caesarean delivery, SMRU’s longstanding model transferred them to the nearest hospital in an emergency standby car. The cohort profile states that 5–10% of women required this transfer over the years covered by the programme description. It describes a clinical referral route but does not assign a travel time or name a Mae La receiving hospital for the 2017–2023 period.[3]

Referral-hospital involvement is not equivalent to operating all camp services: NGOs covered health services along the Thai–Myanmar border in 2023, while the documented caesarean pathway transferred women to the nearest hospital.[3][4] The UNHCR 2023 account identifies NGOs as the health-service providers in the border camps.

The candidate account identifies Thai regulation, surveillance and vaccination as public-sector links to Mae La’s NGO-delivered camp care, distinct from Thai public-hospital referrals and direct Ministry operation of routine camp maternity services.[98][34][2][108]

The available description of SMRU’s model identifies clinic-based maternity care, special newborn care and external-hospital referral, not Ministry-operated routine camp maternity care.[4][8][3]

Thai public-health involvement also included national protocols and surveillance definitions, reports to local authorities and the Ministry of Public Health’s Bureau of Epidemiology, and Ministry-supplied vaccines; the Royal Thai Government supported birth certification and selected newborn medicines.[108]

**The supported comparison is therefore not NGO field hospitals in Cox’s Bazar versus Thai Ministry of Public Health maternal-service integration in Mae La. Mae La’s documented 2017–2023 model is NGO-led camp care, with serious cases referred beyond the camp.**[3][4]

Thai public-sector links are visible in the wider border-care environment. A 2021–2024 study of migrant services in Tak Province reported that Thai public-health collaboration was essential for outreach-site placement, infectious-disease surveillance and prevention, and caesarean sections for 11% of women in that migrant cohort. That study concerns Karen and Myanmar migrants in Tak Province, not Mae La camp residents.[35]

The Ministry of Public Health also operates One Stop Crisis Centre pathways for survivors of gender-based violence in Thailand, but this is a violence-response pathway and should not be treated as the organisation of routine Mae La maternity services. The documented camp maternal-care arrangement remains NGO provision plus hospital referral.[4][3]

An earlier account identifies the Planned Parenthood Association of Thailand as having a UNFPA-supported sexual and reproductive health role within Mae La’s division of reproductive-health responsibilities; it does not establish UNFPA financing of the camp’s maternal services in 2021.[109][110]

In 2023, BHF/SMRU reported broader border-area activity: maternal and child health outreach in Mae Ramat and Phop Phra districts across 16 sites, nearly 33 visits a month, ANC counselling registrations, skilled-attendant births and clinic deliveries managed by trained maternal and neonatal-care professionals. These figures describe the Thai–Myanmar border programme rather than Mae La-specific refugee care.[36]

The documented Mae La pathway comprises NGO-led care and outside-hospital referral, with historical SMRU descriptions of ANC, delivery, newborn care and SCBU capacity. The available service map supports comparison with Cox’s Bazar at the level of organisational architecture, but not the proposed contrast of humanitarian NGOs against a fully Ministry-integrated camp maternity system.[34][3][8][4]

SMRU provided routine and specialised antenatal, intrapartum, delivery, postpartum and newborn care; its facilities had ANC structures and labour-and-delivery wards equipped for basic emergency obstetric and newborn life support.[8]

SMRU’s historical model delivered women with problem cases at SMRU facilities or referred them to a Thai public hospital.[37]

### Camp-specific maternal and newborn indicator series for Rohingya refugees

The relevant surveillance architecture has three layers. First, the UNHCR Health Information System (HIS) defines standard refugee-health indicators. Second, Cox’s Bazar’s Health Sector and SRH WG reported service outputs and selected coverage measures through partner reporting, 4W data and HeRAMS. Third, the Maternal and Perinatal Mortality Surveillance and Response system (MPMSR) identified, reviewed and audited maternal and perinatal deaths.[15][24]

UNHCR’s Health Information System uses a camp and a month as its reporting unit.[20]

The UNHCR HIS defines complete ANC coverage as the percentage of pregnant women who had made at least four ANC visits by delivery, using live births as the denominator. It defines skilled birth attendance as deliveries attended by doctors or personnel with midwifery skills who can manage normal births and obstetric emergencies, divided by all deliveries, including live births and stillbirths; trained and untrained traditional birth attendants are excluded.[20]

The HIS defines the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) as pregnancy-related deaths during pregnancy or within 42 days after pregnancy ends, divided by live births and multiplied by 100,000. It defines neonatal mortality rate (NNMR) as newborn deaths during the first 28 days of life divided by live births and multiplied by 1,000. These definitions matter because counts of maternal deaths, stillbirths, facility births or early neonatal deaths are not substitutes for MMR or 28-day NNMR.[20]

The HIS framework also names indicators for deliveries at emergency obstetric care facilities, low birth weight, stillbirth, caesarean section, complete PNC, reported maternal deaths and investigation of reported maternal deaths. It thereby defines a common indicator vocabulary, even where a particular site report or year did not publish every value.[20]

The 2018 UNHCR HIS entry for Mae La Oon describes a different camp: it recorded 10,206 residents, 94% antenatal-care coverage, 78% skilled birth attendance, zero maternal deaths and neonatal mortality of zero. Thailand-operation aggregates covered 102,223 refugees and reported 91%–98% antenatal-care coverage; neither series is a Mae La-specific measure.[111][73]

A pooled UNHCR HIS neonatal analysis for 2006–2017 covered 150 camps, 663,892 live births and 3,382 neonatal deaths, with country-level rates ranging from 12 to 56 per 1,000 live births; those pooled figures are not estimates for either Cox’s Bazar or Mae La.[20][112]

Kutupalong New reported 94% ANC coverage, 83% skilled attendance, two maternal deaths and an NNMR of 22 per 1,000 live births. Nayapara reported 79% ANC coverage, 100% skilled attendance, two maternal deaths and an NNMR of 20 per 1,000 live births.[38][40]

The site reports cannot be combined into a Cox’s Bazar-wide annual series without matching denominators and site coverage. Kutupalong New had 25,071 refugees in the 2018 report; Nayapara had 19,895. These are discrete site snapshots from a refugee population distributed across many camps, not response-wide maternal-health estimates.[38][40]

The strongest early response-wide neonatal estimate comes from Amsalu, Costello, Hasna and Handzel’s 2022 *BMJ Global Health* study, 《Estimating stillbirth and neonatal mortality rate among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, September 2017 to December 2018: a prospective surveillance》. It covered 29 of 34 camps, approximately 811,543 refugees and 19,477 estimated live births. It defined neonatal death as death between day 0 and day 28 and estimated NNMR at 27.0 per 1,000 live births, with a 95% confidence interval of 22.3 to 31.8.[47][12]

That study used estimated rather than directly counted births. The investigators applied Myanmar’s 2015–2016 crude birth rate of 18 per 1,000 people per year to the refugee population and study period, and stated that this choice might overestimate NNMR and stillbirth rates. Using the UNHCR population estimate of 796,304 produced NNMR of 27.7 per 1,000 live births, 95% CI 22.8–32.5.[12]

The neonatal study also produced camp-specific estimates: the highest reported NNMR was 59.8 per 1,000 live births in Camp 6 and the lowest was 10.2 in Camp 12. It found no statistically significant correlation between camp NNMR and the number of health facilities, with Spearman’s rho –0.305 and p=0.11. It did report lower NNMR in camps closer to secondary facilities than in camps closer to labour room/SRH units.[12][48]

The study’s place-of-death data show why facility-only statistics cannot represent the full neonatal burden. Of 531 neonatal deaths, 405, or 76.3%, occurred at home or in the community; 202 of 280 stillbirths, or 72.1%, also occurred outside facilities.[47]

Maternal death surveillance evolved later. MPMSR began in the Rohingya response in 2019, and its explicit response activities began in 2021. It links community-based death reporting, verbal autopsy for women of reproductive age, facility-based maternal-death review and inter-agency audit.[24][23]

A separate account dates the start of Cox’s Bazar’s MPMSR work to September 2018 and identifies UNFPA, CDC, CIPRB and Health Sector working groups as participants. It describes facility maternal-death review, community reporting through a customized Early Warning, Alert and Response System, and verbal autopsy.[113][7]

In 2020, community-based surveillance reported 144 deaths among women of reproductive age, and all had verbal autopsies; 45 deaths, or 31%, were classified as maternal. The annual report gives timing of these deaths—15 during pregnancy, nine during delivery or within two hours after delivery, 19 within 42 days after birth and two following abortion—but not a live-birth denominator.[7]

The 2020 maternal-death surveillance account located 13 of the 45 identified maternal deaths in the community and 32 in facilities, whereas facility notifications alone recorded 24 deaths.[7]

In 2023, MPMSR reported 57 maternal deaths in the Rohingya response. Of these, 43 were facility-based and 14 community-based. The SRH WG’s annual bulletin separately reported 84 maternal deaths in the broader response in 2023, following 83 deaths in 2022, and supplied annual rates of 295 and 321 per 100,000 live births respectively. The two 2023 death counts arise from differently described reporting systems and scopes, so they should remain separate rather than be merged.[24][43][49]

The annual SRH WG bulletin reported that 67% of maternal deaths occurred in health facilities and 33% in the community in 2023. MPMSR reported 75% of its maternal deaths occurring in facilities or during facility-to-facility referral, while 25% occurred at home or before primary-facility admission. These are mortality case-location distributions, not skilled-birth-attendance measures.[49][24]

Health Sector reporting became more detailed in 2021–2023. During January–March 2023, partners recorded 43,152 first ANC visits, 77% among Rohingya patients and 23% among host-community patients. ANC4 coverage reached 85% at the quarter’s end. The same quarterly bulletin recorded 7,453 facility births, of which 65.61% were Rohingya, and stated that the births were attended by SBAs.[5]

The 85% quarterly ANC4 figure and the 75% 2023 annual Rohingya figure have different stated bases. The quarterly bulletin reports mixed Rohingya and host-community ANC1 activity alongside an end-of-quarter ANC4 coverage percentage. The annual table defines the 75% measure as ANC4 coverage among Rohingya women with live births. The annual figure is therefore the clearest response-wide Rohingya ANC4 result for 2023.[5][15][43]

The annual 2023 SRH WG bulletin additionally reported 25,946 mothers with four or more ANC visits, of whom 69.1% were Rohingya, and 156,397 first ANC visits, of which 75% were Rohingya. It stated that the Rohingya ANC4 attendance rate was 75% for births occurring in health facilities. This facility-birth basis is narrower than all pregnancies in the camps.[49]

A separate April 2023 survey in Kutupalong camps describes a different dimension of prenatal access: among 708 Rohingya women asked about their most recent pregnancy, 334, or 47.2%, reported no antenatal healthcare uptake and 374, or 52.8%, reported at least one antenatal contact. This is self-reported utilisation among a sample, while the Health Sector ANC4 indicator uses service reporting and a birth denominator; both describe access, but they are not interchangeable.[50]

Facility delivery increased over the course of the response. Community-health-worker reports put home delivery at 88% in 2018, 59% in 2019, 42% in 2020 and 30% in July–September 2021. Facility-based delivery reached 71% in September 2021 and 75% in December 2021. These are place-of-birth measures, not necessarily population skilled-birth-attendance percentages.[51][52]

One reporting stream placed facility births at 22% of births by February 2018 and 35% in June 2019; a separate UNHCR series reported facility delivery at 12% in 2018 and 70% in 2021.[83][11][102]

By 2023, 33,854 facility deliveries were reported, with 70.9% among Rohingya mothers. Facility births represented 85% of all Rohingya-response deliveries in December 2023, compared with 81% in December 2022. The 2023 performance framework separately reported 82% of births assisted by a skilled attendant; it equated this indicator with facility-based delivery.[49][43]

Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) appears in Cox’s Bazar reporting as a complication category and cause of maternal death rather than as an incidence rate among all births. In 2020, eight of 45 reported maternal deaths were classified as PPH deaths. In 2023, obstetric haemorrhage accounted for 18 of 43 facility-based maternal deaths, and 67% of those haemorrhage deaths were attributed to PPH.[7][24]

The candidate’s 2020 surveillance figures classify obstetric haemorrhage as the cause of 12 of 45 maternal deaths, including eight postpartum- and four antepartum-haemorrhage deaths; these are death counts, not PPH incidence among deliveries.[7]

Service reports also describe PPH management. In the second quarter of 2022, partners reported 1,699 obstetric complications managed, up from 1,551 in the first quarter; PPH, severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, obstructed or prolonged labour and puerperal sepsis were the leading categories. In the third quarter of 2021, 1,633 obstetric complications were managed, and PPH was identified as one of the important complication groups.[53][54]

Perinatal surveillance was incomplete in the earlier years. The 2020 MPMSR report stated that perinatal mortality surveillance had not yet been established because of technical limitations. In 2021, community-based surveillance recorded 476 perinatal deaths, including 251 stillbirths and 220 neonatal deaths, but timing was unavailable for five deaths and causes were described as unknown.[55][7][23]

WHO reported perinatal mortality of 16.55 deaths per 1,000 live births in Cox’s Bazar in 2022 and 10.69 per 1,000 live births in 2023. These perinatal figures do not measure deaths throughout the first 28 days of life.[98]

Active perinatal death surveillance began in August 2023 across all 33 camps. The MPMSR report identified 342 perinatal deaths through formal and informal sources, conducted verbal autopsies for 304, and classified 91 as fresh stillbirths, 98 as macerated stillbirths and 115 as early neonatal deaths. Early neonatal deaths cover the first seven days of life; they are not the requested full 28-day neonatal outcome.[24][56]

UNHCR also reported health-service activity in Cox’s Bazar in 2022: 3,144 live deliveries at UNHCR health facilities, 1,644 patients referred to secondary facilities for emergency care, and ongoing access to ANC, PNC and family-planning clinics. Its factsheet states that camp facilities offered BEmONC around the clock and referred obstetric emergencies.[21]

UNHCR published a Cox’s Bazar facilities dashboard on 22 March 2023; it maps facilities and providers rather than measuring individual maternal or neonatal outcomes.[113][114]

The indicator conclusion is clear. **Cox’s Bazar has a substantial but non-uniform 2017–2023 record: early UNHCR site indicators, a 2017–2018 community neonatal-mortality estimate, MPMSR maternal-death and later perinatal surveillance, and 2021–2023 Health Sector service and coverage indicators.** The series are not a single annual, camp-complete register using one denominator and one definition throughout.[38][12][7][15][43][24]

The retrospective Reproductive Age Mortality Study (RAMOS) provides the earliest response-wide maternal-mortality estimate in the requested period. For September 2017–August 2018, it used exhaustive census-based general-mortality collection in settlements hosting new arrivals, followed by verbal autopsies for every identified death among women of reproductive age; pregnancy-related and maternal deaths were classified using ICD-10, and causes and contributing conditions were determined by an expert panel using ICD-MM. It identified 405 deaths among women aged 12–50 among 3,094 recorded deaths, confirmed 52 maternal deaths and estimated an MMR of 179 per 100,000 live births (95% CI 130–228). It separately identified 82 pregnancy-related deaths and estimated a pregnancy-related death ratio of 282 per 100,000 live births (95% CI 221–343).[41][42]

RAMOS also recorded 531 neonatal deaths aged 0–28 days and reported an NMR of 18 per 1,000 live births, with a 95% confidence interval printed as 17–51; 55% of the neonatal deaths occurred at ages 1–7 days. Its neonatal estimate is distinct from the 27.0-per-1,000 estimate from prospective community surveillance in 29 camps during September 2017–December 2018.[41][42][57]

Among the 82 RAMOS pregnancy-related deaths, obstetric haemorrhage was the leading cause at 28%; 61% of haemorrhage deaths were classified as postpartum haemorrhage and 39% as antepartum haemorrhage. Half of pregnancy-related deaths occurred at home, 44% in a facility and 6% on the road. RAMOS identified delays in referral as a significant contributing factor, and 26% of verbal-autopsy interviews reported that no treatment was sought before the woman’s death.[41][42]

### Camp-specific maternal and newborn indicator series for Karen refugees

Mae La’s direct camp-specific maternal/newborn indicator record is much narrower than Cox’s Bazar’s. The UNHCR HIS Mae La site report covers 2018 and lists 39,061 refugees. It reported 91% antenatal-care coverage, 95% of births attended by skilled personnel, zero maternal deaths, neonatal mortality of 6 per 1,000 live births, 13% births by caesarean section, 11% low birth weight among newborns weighed within 72 hours, and a stillbirth rate of two per 1,000 total births per month.[30]

These three measures have different denominators. The HIS defines caesarean-section proportion as live births delivered by caesarean section divided by all live births. It defines low birth weight as live births under 2,500 grams divided by all live births. It defines stillbirth rate as stillbirths divided by live births plus stillbirths, multiplied by 1,000.[20]

The 2018 Mae La site report therefore supplies a delivery-intervention measure, a newborn-size measure and a stillbirth measure. It does not provide a maternal mortality ratio, maternal-death count, ANC4 completion rate, skilled birth-attendance percentage, PPH incidence or neonatal mortality rate for Mae La in the supplied indicators.[30]

SMRU’s preterm-newborn review reaches the beginning of the requested period. It analysed medical charts of preterm infants born to women attending SMRU ANC clinics from January 2008 to December 2017. Among live births to women attending SMRU clinics, preterm birth fell from 10.5% in 2008 to 7.4% in 2017.[34]

The same review examined neonatal mortality timing across 0–7 days, 8–28 days and after the neonatal period among preterm newborns. This is clinically useful because it distinguishes early from later neonatal deaths, but it is a preterm-neonate clinical cohort, not a camp-wide NNMR for all Mae La births.[34]

A separate SMRU study reported early neonatal mortality of 6.6 deaths per 1,000 live births, 95% CI 5.40–8.06, but the period was 2008–2015 and the outcome was death in the first seven days. It does not represent the requested 2017–2023 28-day neonatal mortality measure.[56]

BHF/SMRU reported 5,131 women registering for clinic-based ANC in its Thailand–Myanmar border programme in 2023 and stated that the service ensured at least four contacts during pregnancy. It also reported 2,498 clinic deliveries managed by trained maternal and neonatal professionals. These are programme-wide service results rather than Mae La camp indicators.[36]

The nearest available 2023 skilled-attendance figure is also broader than Mae La. BHF/SMRU reported 2,241 births assisted by skilled attendants through its Health Equity Initiative, with community healthcare volunteers supporting delivery. The report does not give a Mae La denominator or identify what share of births this represented.[36]

### Circumstances bearing on the comparison

The two settings differ first in scale and institutional context. Cox’s Bazar held approximately 963,303 Rohingya refugees in camps in August 2023 and 969,719 in December 2023. Mae La held approximately 29,615 residents in TBC’s 2023 account and was predominantly Karen. A health system serving nearly one million displaced people across 33 camps necessarily has a more dispersed provider and referral architecture than one serving a single camp of roughly 30,000 to 40,000 residents.[58][15][33][28]

Cox’s Bazar had a large SBA workforce. An April 2022 facility assessment counted 1,055 SBAs: 392 medical officers, 369 nurses and 294 midwives. The Strategic Plan expressed this as 23 SBAs per 19,699 people, against a Sphere comparator of 23 per 10,000 people, and calculated staffing at 51% of the 2,078 expected SBAs.[1]

In 2023, Health Sector reporting recorded a monthly average of 1,277 SBAs, including 37% medical doctors, 27% nurses and 36% midwives. That composition implies approximately 460 midwives if calculated from the average and the reported 36% share, but the source does not publish a formal midwife-to-population ratio. UNFPA reported support for 283 midwives, described as more than 60% of the Health Sector’s reported midwives.[15][58][49]

Derived — Applying the September 2023 reported count of 450 midwives to the August 2023 Rohingya population of 963,303 produces approximately one reported midwife per 2,141 refugees. This uses 450 midwives and 963,303 refugees; it is a crude workforce ratio, not full-time-equivalent maternity staffing and not a measure of women’s access to a midwife.[59][16][58]

Mae La has no corresponding published 2017–2023 midwife count or midwife-to-population ratio in the supplied camp sources. The maternal-health service model identifies midwives and trained staff within NGO provision, but the quantitative workforce basis needed for a direct ratio is not present in the reported Mae La camp figures.[34][3][4]

Cox’s Bazar also had substantial community mobilisation. In 2021, more than 1,440 CHWs and over 120 supervisors or managers delivered community-based surveillance, risk communication and messages about ANC and facility delivery. In 2023, the response described 1,400 CHWs involved in obstetric-case identification and referral.[5][52]

Cultural mediation is especially important in the Rohingya setting because home delivery with traditional birth attendants was widely described as a socio-cultural preference or expectation. The health-sector coordination review reported that misconceptions and fears about facilities accompanied this preference, while late referral of complications increased maternal-death risk.[11]

The 2023 SRH WG bulletin documents an operational approach to traditional birth attendants, locally known as Doronis. IOM’s Maboinor Rosom study identified them as trusted community resources for improving obstetric access and referral; IOM-supported implementing partners worked with 95 TBAs as community referral agents. The SRH WG and Community Health Working Group were reviewing how to use TBAs in referral pathways while addressing risks associated with home birth.[5]

Other cultural intermediaries were also engaged. CHWs made household visits to promote skilled delivery in facilities, and Imams and Mahjis were sensitised regarding facility birth for adolescents and young mothers. In the referral-hub study, Majhis helped persuade mothers and families to use emergency services and helped carry patients to transport at night.[5][27]

These interventions did not erase gendered barriers. Providers described veiling practices, family pressure, discomfort with mixed-gender transport, fear of male clinical staff, concerns about undressing, need for privacy and attachment to familiar birth practices as factors shaping home birth and delayed care-seeking. MSF’s Camp 22 qualitative work also reported concern over family separation, onward referral, medical procedures, birthing ropes, perceived inexperience of midwives and limited cultural adaptation in facilities.[27][60]

The relevant conclusion is that cultural mediation in Cox’s Bazar was operational, not merely aspirational: TBAs, CHWs, Imams and Mahjis were used to encourage referral and facility delivery. It was designed to improve timely service use; the documented figures describe the mediation and referral roles rather than a measured causal change in maternal or newborn outcomes.[5][27]

Comparable Mae La-specific traditional-birth-attendant mediation is not described in the supplied 2017–2023 camp sources. Wider Thai border programmes trained health personnel, volunteers and traditional birth attendants, and distributed home-delivery kits across several districts and mixed Thai/non-Thai populations, but that project reporting is not Mae La-specific.[61]

Cox’s Bazar’s data continuity was constrained by the complexity of a multi-provider response. The Health Sector Strategic Plan stated that DHIS2 reporting was poor and substantially incomplete and that data quality and completeness needed improvement across reporting platforms. The MPMSR process responded to a different problem: identifying deaths occurring outside facilities and reviewing facility deaths through structured surveillance and audit.[1][24]

This explains why several apparently similar figures differ. For example, the December 2023 Health Sector bulletin reported 10 maternal deaths in its four-week summary, while MPMSR reported 57 maternal deaths in the 2023 Rohingya response, and the annual SRH WG bulletin reported 84 maternal deaths and an annual rate of 295 per 100,000 live births. These figures have different stated periods and surveillance scopes.[15][24][43]

The systems also captured different birth and newborn events. The 2023 annual reporting platform named mothers with ANC4 or more visits, facility caesareans, post-abortion care, SBAs, facility births, live births, health-facility births and maternal deaths. MPMSR added maternal, perinatal, fresh-stillbirth, macerated-stillbirth and early-neonatal death review.[15][24]

For Mae La, the 2018 HIS site report demonstrates formal indicator collection but does not provide a comparable multi-year published sequence. The camp’s smaller evidence footprint limits direct annual comparison, not because Mae La lacked maternity care, but because the published camp-level data presented here consist primarily of selected 2018 delivery/newborn indicators and historical programme descriptions.[30][8]

### Synthesis —

**Cox’s Bazar and Mae La both used layered maternity pathways, but their documented institutional forms differ: Cox’s Bazar was a large, multi-provider humanitarian system with NGO field hospitals embedded in government-linked referral networks, whereas Mae La was NGO-led camp care with outside-hospital referral.** Cox’s Bazar has substantially richer 2017–2023 operational and surveillance reporting, including site HIS snapshots, MPMSR mortality review, Health Sector ANC4 and skilled-attendance indicators, CEmONC/referral mapping and field-hospital reports. Mae La has directly camp-specific 2018 caesarean, low-birth-weight and stillbirth figures plus historical ANC and clinic-delivery descriptions, but not a corresponding seven-year outcome series. The appropriate comparative conclusion is therefore architectural rather than numerical at this stage: Cox’s Bazar’s field hospitals were important but non-exclusive components of a broad humanitarian continuum; Mae La’s recorded model was NGO provision and referral rather than an explicitly Ministry-operated maternity system.[1][5][2][4][30]

### What were maternal mortality rates among Rohingya refugee populations in

The September 2023 Health Sector Bulletin estimated an MMR of nearly 300 per 100,000 live births from mortality-surveillance data; it was not a mortality-survey result.[59]

The earlier 2017–2018 RAMOS estimate and the later reported ratios should not be read as a single annual trend. RAMOS was a retrospective mortality study, while the September 2023 Health Sector Bulletin described its nearly-300 estimate as based on mortality surveillance and called it a new baseline following stronger surveillance and audit systems.[24][59] The calendar-year 2023 Health Sector total also differs from the MPMSR surveillance total in the table, so the two 2023 series use reporting coverage or reconciliation procedures that are not shown to be identical.[43][24]

Maternal and Perinatal Mortality Surveillance and Response (MPMSR) began for Rohingya refugees in 2019, with response activities beginning in 2021. It reviews deaths of women of reproductive age in the community through verbal autopsy—interviews used to establish a probable cause and circumstances of death—and audits facility deaths during pregnancy, labour, postpartum care or referral.[23][53] In 2020, community surveillance recorded 144 deaths of women of reproductive age, of which 45 were classified as maternal; these are death counts and classifications, rather than an MMR.[7][62]

In 2021, reported facility maternal deaths increased from 16 in 2020 to 23, while the third-quarter MPMSR review identified 17 facility-based maternal deaths in Cox’s Bazar and recorded that seven had not been reported through the parallel EWARS Events Based Surveillance system.[14][54] Annual death counts cannot substitute for an MMR because the calculation requires same-period live births.[62] Cox’s Bazar therefore has mortality surveillance and a September 2023 surveillance-based rate estimate, but no annual camp-wide MMR series for 2017–2023 is established here.[59][62]

In September 2023, the Cox’s Bazar Health Sector Bulletin estimated the maternal mortality rate at nearly 300 per 100,000 live births. The bulletin stated that this estimate was based on mortality-surveillance data rather than a mortality survey.[59]

A maternal mortality ratio is maternal deaths divided by live births in the same period, multiplied by 100,000. A maternal mortality rate instead uses the population of women of reproductive age as its denominator.

RAMOS used exhaustive census-based general-mortality collection in settlements hosting new arrivals, followed by verbal autopsies for each identified death among women of reproductive age. Pregnancy-related and maternal deaths were identified using the ICD-10 definition, and an expert panel assigned causes and contributing conditions using ICD-MM, the International Classification of Diseases for Maternal Mortality.[41] The study recorded 405 deaths among women aged 12–50 among 3,094 total deaths during September 2017–August 2018.[41]

The RAMOS pregnancy-related-death findings describe urgent access circumstances: 50% of pregnancy-related deaths occurred at home, 44% in a facility and 6% on the road; 30% of cases did not seek treatment, and 40% of those who did not seek treatment said they did not think treatment was needed.[41] These findings concern women who died and do not measure ANC coverage among all pregnant Rohingya women.[41]

### What were maternal mortality rates among Karen refugee communities in

Mae La’s in-period camp-specific maternal mortality observation is zero reported maternal deaths in 2018.[30] **That finding describes no reported maternal deaths in Mae La during 2018, not a zero MMR, because the corresponding number of live births is not reported.**[30][62]

Thailand-wide and provincial MMRs answer a different geographical question. Thailand’s national MMR was reported as 23.10 per 100,000 live births in 2020, and a UNFPA project reported Tak Province’s MMR as 45.85 in 2020 and 15.85 in 2021; Mae La is a camp population and cannot be represented by either figure.[61][64] The historical SMRU refugee-camp series similarly cannot provide a 2017–2023 Mae La estimate: it reported 79 per 100,000 live births for refugee camps in 2006–2010.[37][65]

Thai maternal-death surveillance required notification to hospital and provincial authorities within 24 hours and confidential enquiry within 30 days. ### What was prenatal care access among Rohingya refugee populations in

### Synthesis —

Cox’s Bazar has a September 2023 surveillance-based maternal-mortality estimate of nearly 300 per 100,000 live births. Mae La’s reported 2018 zero maternal-death count cannot be converted into an MMR, so no mortality ranking between the populations is justified.[59][30][62]

## Staffing, emergency obstetric access and cultural mediation

### What emergency obstetric care was available within a two-hour transport

Cox’s Bazar operated a tiered pathway. Camp facilities provided BEmONC on a 24-hour basis and referred obstetric emergencies upward; Q1 2021 monitoring found 21 facilities delivering six BEmONC signal functions without assisted vaginal delivery, 41 providing three signal functions, 22 conducting manual placental removal and 60 providing post-abortion care.[21][1] In January–March 2022, facility monitoring listed 22 facilities in Ukhiya and 10 in Teknaf with six BEmONC signal functions excluding assisted vaginal delivery.[72]

For roughly 900,000 people, an assessment counted 11 basic emergency obstetric and newborn care facilities in Cox’s Bazar and cited a Sphere threshold of five facilities per 500,000 people; the numerical threshold does not measure practical access.[83]

CEmONC capacity changed over time. The 2020 maternal and perinatal mortality surveillance review reported that Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes were not yet functional 24/7 CEmONC facilities, with very limited round-the-clock services around the camps and weak blood and laboratory availability.[7] By Q1 2023, four secondary referral facilities were described as providing CEmONC through 24/7 referral pathways: IOM-supported Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes, the Friendship Maternity Centre and UNFPA-supported Hope Field Hospital.[5]

In a 2019 survey of 104 Cox’s Bazar emergency-obstetric-care personnel, 12 reported blood-bank support, 55 reported laboratory support and 75 reported a dedicated operating theatre, despite reports of free, around-the-clock care and dedicated delivery rooms.[117]

Referral transport was substantial. IOM’s Dispatch and Referral Unit operated 24/7 and supported 14 ambulances for emergency inter-facility transfers; UNFPA’s service used eight midwife-staffed ambulances, IRC supported 12 ambulances attached to camp referral hubs, and 1,400 community health workers identified and referred obstetric cases.[5] In 2023, the Matri Sheba Emergency Referral Transport Service covered all 33 camps, referred 10,492 patients—97% emergency obstetric cases—and provided shuttle services to 22,852 mothers.[49]

The response used a two-hour secondary-care planning criterion, naming Ukhiya and Teknaf Health Complexes, Ukhiya Specialized Hospital and several field hospitals, but no camp-by-camp measurement states the proportion of Rohingya residents who reached definitive EmONC within two hours.[15] Roads, hilly terrain, seasonal mud, mobile-network failures, ambulance shortages and unavailable clinicians at receiving facilities delayed transfers; Chittagong referrals took several hours by road.[27][25][6] **Cox’s Bazar had an extensive BEmONC-to-CEmONC referral pathway, but the period shows variable round-the-clock definitive-care capacity rather than demonstrated two-hour access.**

In that 2019 provider survey, reported time to treatment *after arrival* was immediate for 58.7% of cases, under 30 minutes for 26.9% and 30–60 minutes for 14.4%; these are not home-to-comprehensive-care travel times.[117]

SMRU provided antenatal, intrapartum, delivery, postpartum and newborn services in its facilities. Its labour and delivery wards were equipped for BEmONC life support and staff undertook newborn-resuscitation training and exercises.[34]

Women requiring caesarean delivery were transferred to the nearest hospital in an emergency standby SMRU car; SMRU studies also describe high-risk deliveries as referred to Thailand’s public-hospital system.[3][56] A low-cost health-access fund launched in September 2017 for migrants along the Thailand–Myanmar border made tertiary hospital care easier to access for pregnant women attending SMRU antenatal clinics who developed obstetric complications, including preterm labour.[8]

In the historical SMRU model, a 24-hour car and an on-call doctor supported transfer to Thai hospitals described as about one hour away; SMRU did not routinely provide on-site caesarean sections or operate a blood bank.[107]

This is a camp-facility-plus-hospital-referral model: basic emergency capacity was available at SMRU, while caesarean and other high-risk care relied on external hospitals. The named materials give no Mae La-specific route, destination travel time or two-hour catchment measure. **Mae La’s pathway linked facility-based BEmONC and emergency vehicle referral to Thai public hospitals, not documented in-camp comprehensive surgery within a two-hour radius.**[34][3][56]

Tha Song Yang and Mae Ramat hospitals were described as approximately 20 km from Mae La, while a broader border-service account—not a Mae La-specific travel-time study—reported referrals taking 15 minutes to one hour.[110][116]

A border-service account reported one journey taking 1.5 hours by motorbike or two to three hours on foot, illustrating why nominal hospital distance does not establish a two-hour journey for every Mae La resident.[34]

A May 2018 mapping of 29 Rohingya camps recorded 248 of 280 functional facilities: 152 health posts, 31 primary healthcare centres, 17 labour room/sexual and reproductive health units, seven secondary health facilities and 41 specialised-care centres. The service network was a collaboration of non-governmental organisations, United Nations agencies and the Bangladesh government; labour room/sexual and reproductive health units were intended mainly for uncomplicated labour and childbirth, whereas secondary facilities were intended to manage both uncomplicated and complicated childbirth.[79] The mapping reported one facility of any type per 3,300 people, one secondary facility per 90,000 people, and at least one labour room/sexual and reproductive health unit within 1,000 metres in most camps.[79]

The 2019 Cox’s Bazar assessment described 11 BEmONC facilities for roughly 900,000 people and one in-camp CEmONC facility that was not open 24 hours a day. It named Friendship Hospital, Turkish Field Hospital and Malaysian Field Hospital as hospitals near the camps offering caesarean delivery and blood transfusion; Ukhia Health Complex, Cox’s Bazar District Hospital and Hope Ramu Hospital also provided caesarean delivery.[83] Ukhia Health Complex was reported as 10 minutes by motor vehicle from the Kutupalong megacamp, whereas hospitals in Cox’s Bazar were roughly 2.5 hours away by motor vehicle.[83]

In August 2023, all 72 health posts and 45 primary healthcare centres remained functional, but the HOPE Foundation CEmONC centre in Camp 4 Extension ceased operating, leaving one reported CEmONC centre while UNFPA planned to expand nearby capacity.[70] In September 2023, UNFPA partnered with Friendship Hospital in Ukhiya after HOPE Field Hospital halted CEmONC provision, adding staff, equipment and supplies; Friendship Hospital performed an average of 202 caesarean sections monthly during September–December, compared with the earlier response-wide monthly average of 115.[44] CEmONC services were established at Teknaf Upazila Health Complex late in 2023 after being unavailable for most of that year; it performed 41 caesarean sections in November–December, including six for Rohingya refugees.[44]

Mae La’s principal health provider was Première Urgence–Aide Médicale Internationale working with SMRU. SMRU operated the in-camp obstetric department and special-care baby unit, while complicated cases were referred to Mae Sot Hospital in Thailand’s public system.[16]

### What cultural mediation practices involving traditional birth attendants were documented

Traditional birth attendants (TBAs), locally termed Doronis or dai ma, remained trusted community birth helpers. Camp 22 qualitative research in 2021 found that husbands and parents-in-law often made delivery decisions, and that trust in TBAs, privacy, familiarity and home-based cultural practices shaped preference for home delivery.[60] A separate August 2021 interview study found that seven of eight interviewed mothers preferred hospital birth despite most having delivered at home, showing that stated preference and actual delivery location could diverge when transport, timing and household decision-making intervened.[84]

Referral-hub community volunteers worked door to door with *majhis*, imams and other local leaders to identify pregnancies and persuade families to accept referral; traditional birth attendants were initially outside the formal hub network.[25][105]

The response increasingly used TBAs as mediators rather than clinical providers. MSF worked with Rohingya TBAs to identify pregnant women, explain available antenatal and postnatal services, promote facility delivery, recognize complications and help women reach hospital care.[85] CPI began training TBAs in Kutupalong in 2021 to provide non-clinical pregnancy and labour advice and refer women in labour to facilities.[86] By Q1 2023, IOM partners supported 95 TBAs as community referral agents while the SRH and Community Health Working Groups reviewed their use in the obstetric-referral pathway.[5]

Facilities also adapted to cultural concerns by allowing accompanying TBAs during delivery and strengthening privacy through separate beds and curtains.[10] Traditional birth attendants, trained or untrained, are excluded from the skilled-birth-attendant definition because they are not the cadre trained to manage normal births and diagnose and manage obstetric emergencies.[20][87] **From 2018 to 2023, Cox’s Bazar shifted TBAs toward trusted non-clinical education, identification and referral roles, but no outcome series attributes maternal or newborn changes to those programmes.**

No TBA cultural-mediation programme specifically for Karen refugees in Mae La camp during 2017–2023 is identified. Thailand–Myanmar-border programmes during 2020–2021 revised TBA curricula, retrained and re-skilled 209 TBAs in maternal care, risk assessment and referral, and distributed 1,500 clean delivery kits for circumstances in which hospital travel was difficult.[64][61]

In a historical Mae La account, roughly one quarter of births occurred at home with traditional birth attendants. The account also described attendants as neither formally supported nor excluded: they could attend at a woman’s request, and observations of 20 deliveries found attendants or other support people helping during first-stage labour while SMRU midwives conducted later stages.[107][110]

The wider border model combined promotion of facility birth with a home-birth contingency where geography, rain or night travel prevented hospital access.[64][61] Qualitative context from an unspecified Karen refugee camp reports preference for home birth with traditional midwives because women feared shame from vaginal examinations, uncovered legs and male staff, while family and friends offered greater comfort in traditional delivery.[88]

Described border-care mediation also used Karen- and Burmese-speaking midwives, refugee assistant nurses, peer educators, interpreters, and verbal or pictorial counselling for people with limited literacy; this is distinct from a dated Mae La-specific traditional-birth-attendant referral programme.[109][110]

Separate from 2017–2023 in-camp mediation in Mae La, SMRU’s historical report, with data through 2010, describes verbal explanation in Sgaw Karen, Poe Karen, Burmese and English.[37] A separate SMRU training evaluation reports that three former TBAs completed skilled-birth-attendant training, joined the SMRU team and encouraged women to choose facility births; it does not place this activity in Mae La during 2017–2023.[89] **The documented Thai-border approach connected TBAs and families to risk recognition and referral, but it cannot be assigned as a Mae La-specific practice or outcome determinant.**

Derived — Reported home delivery fell from 88% in 2018 to 30% in July–September 2021: a **58-percentage-point**, or **65.9% relative**, reduction.[51]

The 2020 MPMSR report found that most (70%) facility-based maternal deaths followed a home trial and prolonged labour before the woman reached a facility.[7] A thesis citing UNFPA (2020) likewise reported that 70% of maternal deaths occurring in facilities followed home trials and lengthy labour.[90]

Community leaders known as majhis persuaded women reluctant to deliver at facilities to use referral services. At night, when people living outside the camps could not enter, a majhi relayed emergencies to the referral hub and arranged for the patient to reach the ambulance.[26]

MPMSR recommendations in Cox’s Bazar included allowing TBAs and female family members into facilities during delivery for the mother’s moral support, using local languages or interpreters, and disseminating information about free 24-hour ambulance services.[16]

A Cox’s Bazar study found that community trust shaped collective delivery decision-making and that trust was higher in TBAs than in formally trained midwives, negatively affecting perceptions of midwife competence.[91] Providers reported that social and religious norms concerning veiling and family or community pressure contributed to home birth with TBAs; they also described cases in which a TBA attempted to treat a complication at home and worsened it before facility care.[25]

### Synthesis —

Cox’s Bazar had the more explicitly documented emergency transport network and TBA-referral activity, but also repeated constraints in midwife retention, roads, communications, blood availability and dependable 24/7 CEmONC capacity.[1][52][5][7] Mae La had an SMRU facility-and-referral model with BEmONC, emergency vehicle transfer and public-hospital links, yet no two-hour coverage or midwife ratio can be calculated for the camp.[34][3][56]

**The comparative direction is clear: Cox’s Bazar documented more fragmented but extensively coordinated humanitarian referral infrastructure, while Mae La documented a more stable clinical-and-public-hospital linkage; neither setting has the matched staffing, travel-time and TBA-intervention measures needed to assign differences in maternal or newborn outcomes to these service models.**[5][49][34][3][64]

1. Treat reported staffing figures as workforce inputs, not as comparable midwife-density trends.
2. Use two-hour EmONC access only when measured from households or camps to a functioning CEmONC facility, not when inferred from facility counts or ambulance availability.
3. Distinguish TBA-supported referral and cultural mediation from skilled birth attendance and from clinical obstetric care.

## UNFPA funding and maternal-service continuity

Judgment — Funding continuity means whether essential care remains available without a preventable break in staffing, supplies, referral transport or facility services. A fall in a whole-sector appeal’s financing is not the same measure as a reduction in UNFPA funding for maternal care; the two must be separated before attributing an interruption to UNFPA.

### Did the reported 2021 UNFPA funding reduction apply to maternal-health

UNFPA-supported services operated in the registered Nayapara and Kutupalong camps during 2021. RTMI provided comprehensive sexual and reproductive health and rights services, including antenatal care (ANC, care during pregnancy), facility-based delivery (FBD, birth in a health facility), postnatal care (PNC, care after birth), family planning and information interventions.[54]

At the start of the Rohingya emergency, UNFPA distributed clean-delivery kits and operated women-friendly spaces that referred women to health and protection services. Through the Minimum Initial Service Package for priority reproductive-health services, it supported emergency obstetric care, community awareness, clean-delivery supplies and maternal-mortality-surveillance partnerships.[99][83][25]

UNFPA-linked maternal-health support in Cox’s Bazar also included clean-delivery kits, women-friendly referral spaces and leadership of the Minimum Initial Service Package.[99][83][25]

The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs Financial Tracking Service recorded two incoming paid 2021 Joint Response Plan contributions to UNFPA—US$1,286,344 in the Health cluster and US$718,989 under a multi-sector flow—for sexual and reproductive health and rights and gender-based-violence services for women and girls in Bangladesh. It also recorded US$106,758 for a 2021–2024 regional sexual-and-reproductive-health-supplies initiative with Bangladesh as destination country.[92]

WHO’s July–September 2021 Cox’s Bazar Health Sector Bulletin reported US$27,722,211 received against a US$135 million sector request, or 20.5%.[51] Health-sector funding reports recorded low appeal receipts from the 25% funded against a US$172 million request as of 23 January 2021, through the July–September 2021 20.5% funding snapshot, to US$2,112,676, or 1.9% of the US$110 million 2022 appeal, received by the end of the first quarter of 2022.[55][51][72] The United Kingdom’s April 2021 decision cut 85% of its UNFPA funding as part of broader foreign-aid reductions.[93] UNFPA calculated that UK aid-budget cuts reduced its net revenue by nearly £150 million during 2021–2022 and estimated that the promised funding could have averted 47 million unintended pregnancies, 813,000 maternal and child deaths, and 14.4 million unsafe abortions.[94] Cox’s Bazar records nevertheless document continuing UNFPA-linked SRHR activity and incoming Bangladesh funding.[54][92]

Thailand’s 2021 UNFPA programme recorded US$574,728 in spending for integrated sexual and reproductive health services. UNFPA implemented US$286,902 (50%), non-governmental organizations US$20,748 (4%), and government (GOV) US$267,078 (46%); 47% came from core resources and 53% from non-core resources.[95]

The same Thailand programme recorded US$5,903 for health-workforce-capacity activity, entirely government-implemented and core-funded, with an emphasis on midwives’ management and clinical skills for integrated sexual and reproductive health services in humanitarian settings.[95] These are Thailand-wide programme entries rather than Mae La-specific maternal-service accounts.

**For Mae La, exposure of Karen maternal services to the alleged 2021 UNFPA funding reduction is unestablished: the reported Thailand-wide expenditure identifies neither Mae La, Karen refugee beneficiaries, maternal-health services, nor a funding cut.** The relevant geographic unit remains Thailand, whereas the requested unit is Karen refugees in Mae La camp.[95]

UNFPA historically supported a Planned Parenthood Association of Thailand (PPAT) sexual and reproductive health clinic in Mae La.[109]

The reported 2021 reduction was the United Kingdom’s April 2021 decision to cut 85% of its funding to UNFPA. UNFPA calculated that United Kingdom aid-budget cuts reduced its net revenue by nearly £150 million during 2021–2022.[93][94]

### How did the reported 2021 UNFPA funding reduction affect continuity

In 2021, UNFPA and implementing partners developed a harmonized obstetric referral service operating 24 hours per day, seven days per week. It covered community-to-facility and facility-to-community referral and emergency transport between community and facility or between facilities.[23] The Maternal and Perinatal Mortality Surveillance and Response (MPMSR) system—surveillance followed by action to prevent further maternal or perinatal deaths—had operated in Rohingya camps since 2019 and began its response activities in 2021.

Training in March, September and late November–December 2021 strengthened notification of deaths among women of reproductive age and reporting of community- and facility-based maternal deaths.[23]

The health sector nevertheless faced broad financial pressure. Its 2023–2024 strategic plan reports that health response financing received only 35–45% of requirements during 2018–2021, while the refugee population targeted for health assistance remained approximately 850,000 in 2020 and 884,000 in 2021.[1] The plan identifies inadequate financing for referrals, short funding cycles, costs at receiving facilities and occasional camp approval barriers for elective external referrals as constraints on universal referral capacity.[1]

**Cox’s Bazar, 2022 —** From January through March 2022, UNFPA supported 22 health facilities serving Rohingya refugee camps and host communities. Those facilities recorded 23,616 ANC visits, 2,442 facility-based deliveries attended by skilled birth attendants, 6,474 PNC visits and 32,482 family-planning visits. UNFPA also planned distribution of clinical-management-of-rape kits by the end of January 2022 to address supply-chain challenges.[19][52] **Cox’s Bazar, 2023 —** In September 2023, after HOPE Field Hospital stopped providing comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn services, UNFPA partnered with Friendship to prevent a service gap.

UNFPA added human resources, equipment and supplies at Friendship Hospital in Ukhiya; the facility performed an average of 202 caesarean sections monthly from September through December 2023, compared with the earlier response-wide monthly average of 115.[44] In 2023, MSF completed reconstruction of its Balukhali clinic after a 2021 fire destroyed the facility; it reopened as a specialist provider of sexual and reproductive health, mental-health and sexual- and gender-based-violence care, while emergency-room and outpatient services ended at that site.[2]

MSF’s Cox’s Bazar programme recorded 57,213 antenatal-care patients in 2023; this is its patient volume, not Rohingya population-wide antenatal coverage.[2]

Before the 2021 fire, MSF’s Balukhali clinic provided antenatal care, postnatal care, family planning, emergency-room services and general outpatient care; its reconstructed 2023 facility did not restore the emergency-room or general outpatient configuration.[2]

Cox’s Bazar experienced substantial sector-wide funding and referral vulnerabilities, but the documented 2021–2023 continuity events are a 24/7 obstetric-referral intervention, mortality-surveillance response, planned supply action and post-fire specialist-facility reopening—not a measured maternal-service interruption caused by a specified UNFPA reduction.[23][1][52][2]

WHO reported that referrals in Cox’s Bazar increased from 10,400 in 2022 to 22,374 during January–October 2023.[104]

Thailand-wide integrated sexual-and-reproductive-health expenditure increased from US$342,415 in 2020 to US$574,728 in 2021. The delivery mix also changed: government implementation rose from US$2,388 (1%) in 2020 to US$267,078 (46%) in 2021, while UNFPA implementation declined in dollar terms from US$308,631 to US$286,902.[95] These changes describe the national programme category, not care delivery in Mae La.

UNFPA’s Asia-Pacific regional report states that it supported national and local health-system continuity for integrated sexual and reproductive health, including safe delivery, family planning, mental-health and psychosocial support, and gender-based-violence services.[96] In 2023, The Border Consortium reported continuing maternal, infant and young-child-feeding activities across Thailand’s refugee camps; approximately 2,000 caregivers of children aged 6–24 months attended monthly nutrition-practice sessions.[33]

The measured Thailand-wide programme total rose in 2021, but it neither measures nor demonstrates continuity of antenatal, delivery, emergency-obstetric or postnatal services for Karen refugees in Mae La. Continuing caregiver nutrition activity in 2023 indicates ongoing camp-programme support, not continuity of Mae La obstetric care.[95][33]

Consultations in 2022–2023 described declining foreign funding and assistance barely sufficient for daily needs in Mae La, but did not identify a UNFPA funding amount or a maternal-service consequence.[118]

During COVID-19 restrictions, NGO personnel and refugees faced severe limits on movement into and within Mae La, although medical evacuation remained possible for serious illness; camp committees and community leaders helped sustain assistance.[118]

Mae La antenatal care was adapted to a minimum of eight visits instead of the usual pattern of visits every two to four weeks when feasible; telephone consultations were impractical for many women who lacked phones.[116][118]

Derived — Thailand-wide UNFPA integrated sexual-and-reproductive-health programme spending increased by US$232,313, from US$342,415 in 2020 to US$574,728 in 2021—an increase of **67.8%**.[95]

Derived — In Cox’s Bazar in 2023, the reported share of mothers with ANC4 or more visits was 20 percentage points higher in UNFPA-supported facility deliveries than in the health-sector aggregate (more than 95% versus 75%), or **1.27 times** the aggregate percentage.[49]

In 2021, the United States planned US$2.6 million in extrabudgetary funding for UNFPA programmes responding to the Rohingya refugee crisis. The United States also resumed UNFPA funding in FY2021, appropriating US$32.5 million in core funding in each of FY2021 and FY2022 and US$20 million for COVID-19 response and recovery in FY2021.[93]

Cox’s Bazar, 2021 — RTMI provided UNFPA-supported comprehensive sexual and reproductive health and rights services in Nayapara and Kutupalong registered camps, including ANC, FBD and PNC. UNFPA and implementing partners developed a 24/7 harmonized obstetric referral service covering community-to-facility and facility-to-community referral and emergency obstetric transport between community and facility or between facilities.[54][23]

### Synthesis —

The two settings cannot be compared as though both experienced the same 2021 UNFPA funding event. Cox’s Bazar had documented UNFPA-linked reproductive-health services, paid 2021 funding flows, round-the-clock obstetric referral and mortality-surveillance response amid a health-sector financing shortfall and referral-cost pressures.[54][92][23][1] Thailand had national UNFPA sexual-and-reproductive-health and midwifery-capacity expenditure, with higher aggregate integrated-SRH spending in 2021 than 2020, but no Mae La-specific funding or continuity measure.[95] The documented comparison is asymmetric: the United Kingdom cut 85% of its UNFPA funding in April 2021, Cox’s Bazar experienced health-sector funding pressure and UNFPA maintained and adapted specified maternal-service functions, including 24/7 referral and the September 2023 transfer of comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn capacity to Friendship Hospital; no camp-specific funding-change effect can be assigned to Mae La maternal services.[93][72][23][44][95]

## Outcome differences and strength of comparison

Maternal and newborn outcomes must be read alongside the pathway by which women reach care, receive treatment, and are referred onward. A high proportion of women reaching antenatal care (ANC, pregnancy care before birth) or a skilled attendant at delivery can coexist with lethal delays in transport, referral, blood supply, staffing, diagnosis, or treatment. The two settings therefore support a comparison of documented outcomes and service pathways, but not a simple ranking of NGO care against public-sector integration.

### How do documented maternal and newborn outcomes among Rohingya refugee

Cox’s Bazar operated a multi-partner humanitarian maternity system rather than a system defined solely by NGO field hospitals. Its dominant mode was static, facility-based care supported by about 1,400 community health workers; BEmONC (basic emergency obstetric and newborn care) was intended at primary health centres, while higher-level referrals connected camps to IOM-supported Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes, Friendship Maternity Centre, UNFPA-supported Hope Field Hospital, Cox’s Bazar Sadar Hospital, and Chittagong Medical College Hospital.[1][5] The system also used referral hubs, community health workers, ambulances, stretcher carriers, wheelchairs, and local transport agents to identify and transfer women with obstetric emergencies.[5]

The 2023 Cox’s Bazar Health Sector Bulletin reported a maternal mortality rate of 295 per 100,000 live births, four-or-more ANC coverage of 75% among Rohingya women with live births, and births assisted by a skilled attendant of 82% of births for January–December 2023.[43] Under the UNHCR Health Information System (HIS) definition, four-visit ANC coverage is pregnant women with at least four visits by delivery divided by live births, while skilled birth attendance excludes traditional birth attendants and includes trained doctors, nurses, and midwives who can manage normal birth and identify, manage, or refer complications.[20] **The 2023 Cox’s Bazar pattern is substantial recorded maternity-service use alongside a maternal mortality rate of 295 per 100,000 live births; service-contact indicators alone therefore cannot be treated as mortality-prevention measures.**[43][78][97]

The maternal mortality figures themselves require careful separation by source and method. A 2017–2018 Reproductive Age Mortality Study (RAMOS) estimated a maternal mortality ratio of 179 per 100,000 live births, with a 95% confidence interval of 130–228, from 52 maternal deaths among 82 pregnancy-related deaths.[24] The January 2024 Health Sector Bulletin reported 321 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births in 2022 and 295 in 2023, with 83 and 84 reported maternal deaths respectively.[43] A WHO page later reported 229.17 per 100,000 live births and 59 deaths in 2022, and 200.39 per 100,000 live births and 57 deaths in 2023.[98] The 2023 reports therefore provide incompatible Cox’s Bazar maternal-mortality values: the sources do not provide a shared denominator, case ascertainment method, or definition that resolves the discrepancy.[43][98][62]

The staffing evidence shows a shortage in the wider skilled-birth-attendant workforce, not a midwife-to-population ratio. The April 2022 facility assessment counted 1,055 skilled birth attendants—392 medical officers, 369 nurses, and 294 midwives—equivalent to 23 skilled birth attendants per 19,699 people, or 51% of the plan’s expected 2,078 staff under its Sphere benchmark.[1] The same strategic plan listed 338 female midwives for January–March 2022, including 82 at health posts and 256 at primary health-care centres, while Q4 2021 monitoring listed 294 female midwives.[1][52] Midwife attrition as staff joined public service was identified as a service-delivery problem, and the response used recruitment, training, deployment, mentoring, and supervisory support to sustain midwifery services.[52][54] This evidence supports a workforce-shortfall interpretation, while neither workforce count supplies the population denominator required for a midwife-to-population ratio.

Emergency obstetric access depended on several referral layers. In Q1 2021, 21 facilities reportedly provided six BEmONC signal functions without assisted vaginal delivery; 41 provided three signal functions, 22 conducted manual placental removal, and 60 provided post-abortion care.[1] By Q1 2023, four secondary referral facilities were described as providing CEmONC (comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care): the two IOM-supported Upazila Health Complexes, Friendship Maternity Centre, and Hope Field Hospital, all within established 24/7 referral pathways.[5] This differs from the 2020 MPMSR review, which stated that Ukhiya and Teknaf Upazila Health Complexes were not yet functional for 24/7 CEmONC and that round-the-clock facilities around the camps were very limited.[7] The different descriptions concern different dates and distinguish pathway designation from fully functional clinical capacity.

Ambulance and referral support were extensive but did not remove delay risks. IOM’s Dispatch and Referral Unit provided 24/7 dispatch and supported 14 ambulances for inter-facility emergency transfers; UNFPA-supported Emergency Referral Transport Services operated 24 hours a day with eight ambulances staffed by a midwife and referral facilitators; UNFPA also supported IRC with 12 ambulances attached to 12 referral hubs.[5] Yet poor roads, hilly terrain, rain, mud, weak mobile coverage, ambulance shortages, security restrictions, and a common arrangement of one ambulance serving two hubs were reported barriers to timely transfer.[27][25] No source measures the proportion of Rohingya refugees who could reach functioning EmONC within two hours; the documented pathway is therefore a referral network, not a two-hour coverage estimate.

The 2023 MPMSR maternal-death reviews classified delays among the 57 reviewed Rohingya maternal deaths using the three-delay model: first delay in deciding to seek care, second delay in reaching care, and third delay in receiving appropriate facility care. These classifications describe circumstances among reviewed deaths and do not estimate the effects of the delivery model, staffing, transport, or referral services on camp-wide maternal mortality.[24] In 2023, 55 of 57 maternal deaths had at least one recorded delay: 29 first delays, three second delays, and 23 third delays.[24] Among 43 facility or inter-facility deaths, 23 involved third delays; reported contributors included failure to follow BEmONC/CEmONC protocols, failure to perform basic ANC laboratory tests, and delayed initial stabilization at BEmONC facilities.[24] The MPMSR system also identified inadequate blood-transfusion access, laboratory gaps, shortages of skilled personnel, inadequate provider competency, and lack of standard protocols.[24] The 2023 maternal-death reviews classified delays in seeking, reaching, and receiving appropriate care among women who died and recorded protocol non-adherence, missing basic ANC laboratory tests, and delayed initial stabilization at BEmONC facilities among the recorded third-delay factors.[24]

Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH, excessive bleeding after birth) was a prominent emergency but not a measured incidence series. It was among commonly referred obstetric conditions, one of the leading causes of managed obstetric complications in 2022, and a condition included in midwife mentoring and lifesaving-skills training.[5][53][54] In 2023, obstetric haemorrhage caused 20 of 57 maternal deaths, or 35%; this is a cause-of-death proportion, not PPH incidence among births.[24] Perinatal surveillance recorded 304 perinatal deaths from August through December 2023, with surveillance beginning on 1 August; that count is neither a full-year measure nor neonatal mortality within 28 days.[24] Earlier prospective surveillance in 29 camps from September 2017 through December 2018 estimated neonatal mortality at 27.0 per 1,000 live births, based on 530 reported neonatal deaths and an estimated 19,477 live births.[48] Its denominator used an assumed crude birth rate and may have overestimated the rate.[12]

Cultural mediation was part of the Cox’s Bazar referral model. Traditional birth attendants known as Doronis were recognized as trusted community resources, and IOM supported 95 as community referral agents while the SRH and Community Health Working Groups reviewed their role in referral pathways.[5] MSF also worked with Rohingya traditional birth attendants to identify pregnant women, provide information on antenatal and postnatal care, recognize complications, and promote delivery at MSF facilities.[85] A Kutupalong pilot trained TBAs in non-clinical pregnancy and labour advice and referral; its operator reported that facility birth in its catchment rose from 33% in 2020 to more than 95% in 2022.[86]

Home birth nevertheless remained linked to first delays. Rohingya women and families described trust in TBAs, privacy, birthing ropes, concerns about male staff and undressing, fear of surgery or onward referral, family decision-making, insecurity, and lack of nighttime transport as influences on place of birth.[60] In 2023 maternal-death reviews, preference for home delivery accounted for seven recorded first-delay factors, while home treatment with pharmacy medicines, unawareness of danger signs, and late decisions to seek facility delivery also appeared.[24] **Cultural acceptability materially shaped care-seeking in Cox’s Bazar, and TBA engagement created a bridge to referral, but the reported outcomes do not quantify a mortality or PPH reduction attributable to that mediation.**[5][24][86]

The Cox’s Bazar system therefore makes possible high levels of recorded ANC4 and skilled-attendant delivery, but it does not eliminate the first, second, and third delays reflected in maternal-death reviews.

Among the 57 women classified as maternal deaths in 2023, 29 (51%) had received at least four ANC visits, compared with 42% reported in the previous year.[24]

### How do documented maternal and newborn outcomes among Karen refugee

Mae La’s documented model is primarily SMRU facility-based maternity and newborn care, not an established Thai Ministry of Public Health-integrated model. SMRU provided ANC, intrapartum care, delivery care, postpartum care, and newborn services through facilities with labour and delivery wards equipped for BEmONC life support; staff received newborn-resuscitation training and routine BEmONC exercises.[34] Its teams included locally trained medics, nurses, midwives, sonographers, and laboratory technicians supervised by qualified doctors.[34] A special care baby unit has operated in Mae La since 2008 for complications including neonatal jaundice, sepsis, transient tachypnoea, and feeding difficulties.[3]

UNHCR’s 2018 Mae La site report recorded 0 maternal deaths, ANC coverage of 91%, skilled-attendant births of 95%, and a neonatal mortality rate of 6 per 1,000 live births.[30] The report also listed 13% of births by caesarean section, 11% low birth weight, and stillbirths at 2 per 1,000 total births per month.[30] Its ANC indicator was not defined as four or more visits, and its neonatal-mortality entry did not state the age window; it therefore cannot be treated as a direct ANC4 or 28-day neonatal-mortality match to the Cox’s Bazar indicators.

Mae La’s available maternity workforce counts in 2018 were three medical doctors, 72 qualified nurses, and 64 community health workers.[30] SMRU’s staffing descriptions identify midwives as part of the care team, and its historical training programme converted some former TBAs into skilled birth attendants, but neither source supplies the number of midwives serving Mae La’s population.[34][89] Staff retention was also a recognised issue in the SMRU model: attrition of skilled-birth-attendant graduates reduced staff numbers and morale and could reduce capacity for safe maternity care.[89] These facts support an interpretation of an organized maternity workforce with retention pressures, not a ratio-based explanation of Mae La’s outcomes.

The referral system included transfer by emergency standby SMRU car to the nearest hospital for women requiring caesarean delivery; SMRU described this pathway as applying to 5–10% of women over time.[3] The service description does not state journey duration, CEmONC functions at each receiving hospital, or the share of camp residents within a two-hour journey.

The operational comparison is therefore not Thai Ministry of Public Health integration versus NGO field hospital care; it is a mature SMRU clinic-and-referral model in Mae La versus Cox’s Bazar’s multi-agency camp, referral-hub, ambulance, field-hospital, and government-hospital network.[34][3][5]

The strongest outcome evidence associated with SMRU’s specialized newborn services predates most of the requested period and concerns preterm infants rather than all newborns in Mae La. In SMRU chart records for preterm neonates born to mothers attending its ANC clinics from 2008–2017, mortality fell from 14.4% in 2008–2011 to 10.5% in 2012–2014 and 5.5% in 2015–2017 as special-care-baby-unit care expanded and became routine.[34] Mortality fell 68% among very preterm neonates and 53% among moderately preterm neonates, while it did not change among extremely preterm neonates.[34] The cohort excluded home-born neonates not subsequently hospitalized, tertiary-hospital births, transfers to tertiary care, and palliative-care cases.[8] This selected preterm-neonate chart cohort, covering January 2008–December 2017 and therefore largely preceding the requested period, showed a temporal association between the expansion and routinisation of special-care-baby-unit care and lower mortality; it is not a 2017–2023 Mae La population neonatal-mortality estimate.[34][8]

Cultural mediation in Mae La is documented most clearly through language-sensitive care and the earlier integration of former TBAs into SMRU’s skilled-birth-attendant workforce. SMRU neonatal services communicated with mothers in their native tongues, and mothers described staff explanations as helping them understand the need for special-care treatment.[8] Former TBAs who completed SMRU’s skilled-birth-attendant training became community influences encouraging facility birth.[89] A Ministry of Public Health refresher-training example involved 50 TBAs in Mae Hong Son Province, but the named location was Mae Sariang rather than Mae La camp.[61] Mae La’s documented cultural pathway is therefore culturally sensitive clinical communication and locally trained maternity personnel, not a camp-specific TBA-referral programme with measured maternal or newborn outcomes.

The Mae La record supports a 2018 snapshot of high ANC coverage and skilled-attendant delivery with low recorded neonatal mortality, while its service descriptions identify a facility-based continuum and hospital referral rather than a quantified two-hour emergency-access system.

The indicator-by-indicator verdict, using the single comparison table in section 3, is as follows. **Maternal mortality rate: no comparison verdict.** Cox’s Bazar reports rates per 100,000 live births for 2017–2018 and 2023, whereas Mae La reports zero maternal deaths in 2018 without a live-birth denominator; the matched population, period, maternal case definition, and live-birth denominator required for a rate comparison are absent.[43][30][24] **Prenatal-care access: no comparison verdict.** Cox’s Bazar reports ANC4 coverage of 75% among Rohingya women with live births in January–December 2023, while Mae La reports 91% ANC coverage in 2018 without a visit threshold or stated denominator.[43][30] **Four-or-more ANC completion: no comparison verdict.** The Cox’s Bazar numerator is women with at least four ANC visits by delivery and its denominator is women with live births; Mae La provides no four-visit measure.[43][20][30] **Skilled birth attendance: no comparison verdict.** Cox’s Bazar reports 82% of births assisted by a skilled attendant in 2023 and Mae La reports 95% in 2018, but the population and period do not match; a matched period, population coverage, skilled-attendant case definition, and total-birth denominator are required.[43][30][20] **Postpartum-haemorrhage incidence: unavailable for both settings.** Neither setting reports postpartum-haemorrhage cases with a delivery denominator, so no incidence comparison is possible.[5][24][20] **Neonatal mortality within 28 days: no comparison verdict.** Cox’s Bazar reported 27.0 deaths per 1,000 estimated live births across 29 camps during September 2017–December 2018, while Mae La reported an NNMR of 6 per 1,000 live births in 2018 without stating the neonatal age window; the population coverage, period, 0–27-day case definition, and directly measured live-birth denominator do not match.[12][30]

Cox’s Bazar recorded PPH among emergency referrals and obstetric haemorrhage as 35% of maternal deaths in 2023, rather than PPH cases per deliveries.[5][24] The available Thai figure—265 PPH cases among 4,774 vaginal births, or 5.55%—comes from Maesai Hospital during 2014–2018 and is not a Mae La Karen-refugee estimate.[75]

No overall maternal-health winner can be identified from the documented outcomes because the camp-specific measures are from different periods and do not align on maternal-mortality denominators, ANC4 completion, postpartum-haemorrhage incidence, or a defined 28-day neonatal-mortality measure.[30][12]

### Synthesis —

**The defensible comparison is of systems and documented snapshots, not a ranked causal verdict.** In Cox’s Bazar, maternal-death reviews recorded delays from home decision-making through transport to treatment at facilities, alongside shortages of blood, laboratory support, protocols, and skilled staff.[24] Mae La’s 2018 indicators—91% ANC coverage, 95% skilled attendance, zero recorded maternal deaths, and NNMR of 6 per 1,000—sit within an SMRU clinic, special-care-baby-unit, and hospital-referral model, but do not provide matching ANC4, PPH-incidence, two-hour-access, or 28-day neonatal-mortality measures.[30][34][3] Cox’s Bazar documented a multi-agency referral network and third-delay classifications among women who died, while Mae La documented an SMRU facility continuum and hospital referral; no overall maternal-health winner can be identified because the available outcomes are not aligned across the full 2017–2023 period.[5][24][34][3][30][12]

### Open questions

Open questions remain for annual, camp-specific maternal mortality ratios; four-or-more-ANC completion; skilled-birth-attendance percentages; postpartum-haemorrhage incidence; and neonatal mortality within 28 days in each population. Also unresolved are comparable midwife-to-population ratios, the proportion of women able to reach functioning comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care within two hours, the measured effects of traditional-birth-attendant mediation, and the location-specific effect of the 2021 UNFPA funding change on continuity through 2023. These questions require indicator series with a stated population denominator, identical case definitions and surveillance coverage, plus dated measures of staffing, travel time, service availability and continuity.
