# Quantum Computing’s Progress Toward Practical Applications

## Executive summary

Quantum computing has real but still narrow practical capability: it can run small quantum-chemistry and optimisation experiments, while error-corrected quantum memory has begun to improve as its code size grows.[1][2][3] Drug discovery has the strongest long-term fit because molecules are quantum systems, but no quantum computer has yet shown that it improves a drug-discovery pipeline.

Cryptography faces a clear future risk because Shor’s algorithm could break RSA and elliptic-curve systems, but current factoring demonstrations are tiny and no cryptographic-scale key has been broken.[4][5] Optimisation has many working prototypes, including quantum annealers and hybrid algorithms, but it has not shown dependable advantage over strong classical solvers on important industrial problems.[6][7] In practice, organisations should treat quantum computing today as a research and preparedness technology, use post-quantum cryptography for the security risk now, and require application-level comparisons with the best classical alternatives before claiming value.

## Quantum computing and practical use

**Reporting date: 19 November 2026.** This section assesses progress toward practical quantum-computing applications in the three requested areas: drug discovery, cryptography, and optimisation problems. Quantum computing is a different way of processing information, not a faster replacement for every ordinary computer.[8][9] Its promise is concentrated in a small number of problem types—especially modelling quantum physical systems, certain number-theory problems, and some search tasks—while its present machines are still limited by errors. That distinction is essential for judging claims about drug discovery, cryptography, and optimisation.

### What is quantum computing?

Quantum computing is computation performed with **quantum bits (qubits)**: physical systems that encode quantum information. An ordinary classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can be prepared in a **superposition**, a quantum combination of the basis states written |0⟩ and |1⟩. The state assigns each possible measurement result a **probability amplitude**: a complex number whose squared magnitude is that result’s probability, with all of the probabilities adding to one.[8][10][11]

A typical **gate-based quantum computer** follows an input–process–output workflow shared with an ordinary computer. A classical computer prepares the input, compiles the quantum program into the target processor’s supported gates and instructions, and maps the program’s qubits onto physical qubits. The processor applies the resulting sequence of quantum operations—**quantum gates**—collectively a **quantum circuit**, then performs a **measurement** that converts the quantum state into ordinary output bits. Because a measurement result is probabilistic, the classical computer commonly runs the circuit repeatedly, collects the measurement results, and processes their distribution into an estimated answer.[11][10]

The unusual resource is not that a quantum computer can read every possible answer simultaneously. A register of n qubits has a state described by 2ⁿ amplitudes, so the mathematical state space grows rapidly as qubits are added. But a final measurement extracts only a limited classical result, not a list of all 2ⁿ possibilities. NIST explicitly warns that this does not make quantum computers efficient brute-force machines for every search problem.[12][8]

Instead, quantum algorithms seek to control **interference**. Amplitudes can reinforce one another—constructive interference—or cancel—destructive interference. A well-designed quantum circuit makes the amplitudes associated with useful answers more likely to appear when measured and suppresses unhelpful answers. Ordinary probabilities cannot cancel in this way; complex amplitudes can.[13][14][15]

**Entanglement** is the other central quantum feature. Entangled qubits have joint states that cannot be described as independent states for each qubit. Their measurement outcomes show correlations stronger than those achievable by separate classical systems. Entanglement is a resource for building algorithms and for encoding information across multiple qubits in quantum error correction.[11][12]

Judgment — **A quantum algorithm earns an advantage only when it uses superposition, entanglement and, above all, interference to make a useful measured outcome likely. Superposition by itself is not parallel readout of all candidate solutions.**

Quantum computers therefore differ qualitatively from classical computers, but not universally in their practical usefulness. By 2016, quantum algorithms with exponential speedups had been found for problems in physics simulation, number theory and topology, but the list of such problems remained relatively small. Broader classes of problems, including search, collision finding and Boolean-formula evaluation, have more modest speedups; Grover’s algorithm gives a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.[9][16]

This matters for the three applications considered later. Molecular simulation aligns with quantum computers’ ability to represent quantum systems. Cryptographic risk rests on a particular number-theory algorithm, Shor’s algorithm. Optimisation is more uncertain because many proposed quantum methods lack either a proven speedup or a convincing advantage over strong classical solvers. **The practical question is not whether a task can be expressed as a quantum circuit, but whether a quantum algorithm exploits the structure of that task better than the best relevant classical alternative.**[17][18]

A quantum linear-system algorithm can produce a quantum state proportional to a mathematical solution, but practical use also requires efficient input loading, matrix implementation and extraction of a decision-relevant output. The number of calls needed can depend on the system’s condition number—a measure of numerical instability—and the required accuracy.[119]

### What quantum-computing capabilities and applications currently exist?

Today’s devices can execute real quantum circuits, prepare superpositions and entangled states, carry out repeated measurements, and run small versions of algorithms for chemistry, simulation, factoring, optimisation and generative modelling. IBM’s Quantum Platform provides access to 100+ qubit quantum processing units (QPUs) and 10 free minutes of execution time per month.[19][20][21][22]

For example, IBM’s introductory hardware exercise creates a two-qubit **Bell state**, an entangled state whose expected measurements are 00 and 11. On a real device, those expected outcomes occur most often, but other outputs appear because of noise and **decoherence**—loss of the fragile quantum state through unwanted interaction with the environment.[11] This simple example captures the field’s central engineering problem: quantum states can be created and manipulated, but their information is easily damaged.

Current hardware includes several physical approaches:

- **Superconducting circuits:** These use electrical circuits cooled to very low temperatures. IBM’s gate-based processors use superconducting transmon qubits. Their strengths include fast gates and mature control electronics; limitations include restricted connectivity and frequency crowding. [21][23]

- **Trapped ions:** These use electrically confined charged atoms manipulated with laser pulses. They offer long coherence times and all-to-all connectivity within one trap, but their gates are slower and large systems require modular architectures. [23][24]

- **Neutral atoms:** These arrange atoms in configurable arrays. Their flexible geometry and potentially large arrays are useful features, while atom loss and laser noise remain engineering constraints. [23]

- **Photonic systems:** These encode information in particles of light. Photonic experiments have demonstrated small compiled factoring circuits and boson-sampling tasks, although those demonstrations do not make a scalable general-purpose computer. [25]

- **Quantum annealers:** These are specialised analogue machines designed to search for low-energy configurations of a mathematical model, often used to represent optimisation problems. They are not general gate-based computers in the same practical sense; they target minimisation tasks. [9][26]

Hardware scale has progressed from demonstrations involving a few qubits to cloud-accessible processors with more than 100 programmable qubits. IBM’s current public hardware page lists Heron processors with 133 or 156 programmable qubits and Nighthawk processors with 120 programmable qubits and four-degree connectivity. It also says Nighthawk r2 executes more than 100,000 samples per second and returns accurate results on certain circuits exceeding 7,500 two-qubit operations. These are vendor-reported hardware metrics, not demonstrations of practical advantage in the three application areas.[27][28]

Quantum-computing benchmarks require particular care. Google reported in 2019 that its Sycamore processor completed a selected task in 200 seconds that it estimated would take a supercomputer about 10,000 years. IBM contested the classical comparison, arguing that its Summit supercomputer could perform the task in 2.5 days. In 2020, a University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) group announced quantum supremacy on the photonic quantum computer Jiuzhang. The disagreement over Sycamore concerned the estimated classical runtime, not whether Sycamore ran the experiment.[10] Such disputes matter because a quantum advantage claim is meaningful only relative to the best realistic classical method, not merely a brute-force simulation of the quantum circuit.[35][17]

A volumetric benchmark varies circuit width—the number of qubits used—and circuit depth independently, rather than assessing only square circuits; this reflects that useful workloads can make different space–time trade-offs.[120]

A volumetric benchmark evaluates circuit width (the number of qubits) and depth (the number of sequential operations) independently, unlike quantum volume, which uses square circuits with equal width and depth. This allows a processor to be assessed against the width–depth trade-off of a particular workload.[120]

Judgment — **The dividing line between technical progress and practical use is not whether a device runs a quantum circuit. It is whether it returns a useful, verifiable result faster, more accurately, or at lower relevant cost than the best available classical method.**

Quantum computers are likely to be used as specialised co-processors rather than replacements for conventional machines. A classical computer prepares data, compiles circuits for a device’s native gates and physical connectivity, orchestrates repeated runs, and processes measured outputs. The variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) are hybrid algorithms that interleave classical computation and quantum processing to reduce circuit size, depth and qubit requirements in response to limited coherence time and qubit fidelity.[10][36][27]

The near-term examples are therefore mostly research infrastructure and narrow workloads. IBM provides cloud access to QPUs, simulators, circuit tools, error-mitigation tutorials, and software for chemistry and optimisation research. Simulation is useful for designing and debugging circuits but is not the same as hardware execution; classical simulation itself becomes difficult as circuit size grows.[20][21][10] Existing practical capability is real but narrow: quantum computers can manipulate quantum information and explore hard-to-simulate systems, while conventional computing still supplies most of the data handling, optimisation, validation and operational workflow.

They use quantum physics respectively to generate randomness or establish shared encryption keys, rather than to run Shor’s algorithm or solve general computational problems.

There is one more direct, though narrow, cryptographic application demonstration. On March 26, 2025, researchers from JPMorganChase, Quantinuum, Argonne National Laboratory, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and the University of Texas at Austin reported the first successful demonstration of certified randomness: a protocol that produces random bits whose unpredictability is mathematically checked. Using a remotely accessed 56-qubit trapped-ion Quantinuum H2 processor, the team used RCS to expand randomness, then used classical supercomputers to certify 71,313 bits of entropy—the quantity measuring unpredictable random information. This was a hybrid quantum-classical result: the quantum processor supplied samples and the classical machines performed the certification.[32]

Progress is also occurring in logical operations, not only logical memory. In February 2025, Quantinuum reported a fault-tolerant controlled-Hadamard gate—a logical operation between two error-protected qubits—on trapped-ion hardware. The experiment encoded the two logical qubits in six physical qubits, used two additional flag ancillas to detect errors, and reported a logical-gate error of at most 2.3×10⁻⁴, compared with about 1×10⁻³ for the corresponding unencoded physical gate; it post-selected the result by discarding about 15% of runs after ancilla checks.[34]

Neutral-atom systems have also demonstrated larger logical circuits. A programmable processor based on reconfigurable atom arrays operated with up to 280 physical qubits, created fault-tolerant logical GHZ states—multi-qubit entangled states—and performed feedforward entanglement teleportation, in which later operations use earlier measurement results. It executed sampling circuits with 48 logical qubits, 228 logical two-qubit gates and 48 logical CCZ gates, where CCZ is a controlled-controlled-Z operation on three qubits. Separate neutral-atom demonstrations used up to 448 atoms to perform logical magic-state distillation, a procedure that prepares a high-quality resource state needed for universal fault-tolerant computing, and key elements of a universal fault-tolerant architecture, including below-threshold performance in a four-round characterisation circuit.[37]

These results broaden the evidence that error-corrected components can preserve information and perform selected encoded operations. They remain distinct from a complete fault-tolerant computer: fault tolerance requires keeping logical error rates controlled as computations become deeper and longer.[37][34]

### What in quantum computing’s development from error-prone physical devices toward

The central development path is from **physical qubits**, the error-prone hardware components, to **logical qubits**, error-protected computational units encoded across many physical qubits. Quantum information is fragile: imperfect gates, imperfect measurement, unwanted environmental interactions, finite coherence times and limited qubit connectivity all cause errors. Those errors accumulate as a circuit becomes deeper—that is, as more sequential operations must succeed.[10][11][1]

NIST describes today’s best quantum computers as having hundreds of interconnected qubits and making an error roughly once in every thousand operations. It contrasts this with an ordinary computer’s approximate rate of one error in a quintillion calculations.[8] That enormous reliability gap explains why present machines favour shallow circuits and repeated sampling rather than long, exact calculations.[11][38]

**Quantum error correction (QEC)** protects information by spreading one logical qubit across an entangled group of physical qubits. The key theoretical result is the **threshold theorem**: if physical operations are sufficiently reliable—below a specified error threshold—adding suitable redundancy and correction steps can make logical errors arbitrarily small. This does not make error correction free. It trades a small number of fragile qubits for a far larger system of physical qubits, additional measurements, fast classical decoding and extra gates.[9][1]

The reported progress is important. Google’s Willow experiment demonstrated a surface-code quantum memory operating below threshold: increasing the code distance by two reduced the logical error rate by more than half, culminating in a 101-qubit distance-7 memory with a 0.143% ± 0.003% error rate per correction cycle. It also demonstrated a real-time decoder: for a distance-5 surface code, latency from receipt of the final syndrome-measurement cycle to return of a correction averaged 63 ± 17 microseconds and remained roughly constant as experiments increased to one million correction cycles and 1.1 seconds.[1] But the same work recorded six large, unexplained, spatially localized correlated error bursts during 2 × 10¹⁰ error-correction cycles.

Willow’s 101-physical-qubit, distance-7 logical memory also had a reported lifetime of 291 microseconds.[121]

In Google Quantum AI’s 2023 surface-code experiment, a 49-physical-qubit distance-5 logical qubit had a 2.914% logical error rate per 921-nanosecond correction cycle, compared with 3.028% for distance-3 comparison codes—a relative reduction of about 4%.[122]

Google’s later Willow experiment reported that its 101-qubit distance-7 logical memory lasted 291 ± 6 microseconds, compared with 119 ± 13 microseconds for its best constituent physical qubit.[121]

The distance-5 decoder’s 63 ± 17-microsecond average latency exceeded its 1.1-microsecond error-correction cycle, requiring a pipeline that processed syndrome information while further cycles occurred.[121][123]

In Google Quantum AI’s 2023 surface-code experiment on a 72-qubit superconducting processor, a distance-5 logical qubit used 49 physical qubits—25 data qubits and 24 measurement qubits. Its logical error rate was 2.914% ± 0.016% per 921-nanosecond correction cycle, versus 3.028% ± 0.023% for the distance-3 comparison, a 4% relative reduction.[122]

In a 2023 surface-code experiment, a distance-5 code using 49 physical qubits had a logical error rate of 2.914% ± 0.016% per 921-nanosecond correction cycle, compared with 3.028% ± 0.023% for a distance-3 code.[122]

Willow’s distance-7 logical memory lasted 291 ± 6 microseconds, compared with 119 ± 13 microseconds for its best constituent physical qubit—a lifetime improvement of 2.4 ± 0.3 times.[121]

They affected neighbourhoods of roughly 30 qubits, occurred approximately once an hour, and decayed with an exponential time constant of around 400 microseconds; mitigating them remains vital to fault-tolerant computation.[1]

In high-distance repetition-code tests, the Willow researchers found rare correlated faults that produced an apparent logical-error floor of 10⁻¹⁰; repetition codes protect against only one type of error and are not equivalent to a full surface code.[121]

The repetition-code tests reached distance 29, but rare correlated events still occurred approximately once per hour, or once every 3 × 10⁹ correction cycles.[123]

The resource cost remains substantial. The Willow study projects that achieving a logical error rate of 10^-6 would require a distance-27 logical qubit using 1,457 physical qubits, and that decoding workload increases quadratically with code distance.[1] Another review estimates roughly 1,000 physical qubits per logical qubit at current physical error rates.[39] Thus, a processor with hundreds of physical qubits is not equivalent to a machine with hundreds of reliable logical qubits.

The field has crossed an important reliability milestone—larger error-corrected memories can now outperform smaller ones in the reported surface-code experiments—but it has not yet crossed into application-scale fault-tolerant computation.[1]

This progression has different implications for the three application areas.

For molecular modelling, the fit is direct. Molecules are quantum systems, and exact representations of their interacting electrons become expensive on classical computers as the system grows. Quantum computers can in principle represent those electronic states more naturally. Quantum chemistry uses molecular-energy calculations, reaction energies, electronic structure, and related properties as inputs to many stages of drug research.[40] (Aspuru-Guzik et al., 2005) The relevant near-term method is often the **variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)**, a hybrid algorithm in which a quantum device prepares trial quantum states and estimates their energies while a classical optimiser changes parameters to seek the lowest-energy state.[40][2][41] (Peruzzo et al., 2014)[42][43]

Real hardware has performed small molecular calculations. A 2017 superconducting experiment determined ground-state energies up to beryllium hydride, BeH2, using a six-qubit Hamiltonian with more than 100 Pauli terms.[2] Later work reported proof-of-concept H2 ab initio molecular dynamics on IBM Q Vigo hardware, using 295 × 10³ measurements and measurement-error mitigation.

Researchers also ran VQE calculations for PtCO on IBM’s ibm_canberra device at Pt–C distances of 1.846 and 3.0 Å; the measured energies qualitatively reproduced complete active space configuration interaction (CASCI) data at those two structures.[18][44] These are molecular-chemistry demonstrations, not evidence that a quantum computer has discovered a drug or improved a full drug-discovery pipeline. The detailed implications belong in **Drug discovery**.

For cryptography, the most consequential prospective capability is not a current deployed application but the future use of **Shor’s algorithm** to factor large integers and calculate discrete logarithms. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer would consequently make RSA and public-key systems based on discrete logarithms, including elliptic-curve cryptography, no longer secure.[9] Those mathematical problems underpin widely used public-key systems such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography.[9] Experiments have factored 15—for example, a 2015 ion-trap implementation used seven effective qubits plus four cache qubits and reported success probabilities above 90%.[4] Factoring 15 proves that algorithmic components can run on quantum hardware; it is not remotely comparable with factoring a cryptographic key.

The gap is governed by both scale and reliability. One estimate says a machine would need about 20 million qubits to run Shor’s algorithm, compared with around 1,100 qubits for the largest machines cited in the same 2024 article.[45] A 2025 resource estimate projects that factoring an RSA-2048 integer could use fewer than one million physical qubits and take under a week, assuming one error per 1,000 gates, a one-microsecond surface-code cycle, ten-microsecond control-system reaction time, a square grid with nearest-neighbour connectivity and a uniform depolarising-noise model.[5] These are design estimates, not measurements on an existing machine. The practical present-day response is therefore conventional **post-quantum cryptography**, not quantum codebreaking; that subject is addressed in **Cryptography**.

For optimisation, existing approaches include gate-based hybrid algorithms such as the **Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)** and analogue quantum annealers. QAOA maps a combinatorial problem such as MaxCut into a mathematical energy function, then alternates quantum operations while a classical optimiser tunes the circuit parameters.[46][36] Quantum annealers seek low-energy configurations directly for a restricted class of optimisation formulations, especially binary quadratic models.[26][47]

These methods run on hardware. Google applied QAOA to graph problems on Sycamore, using 23 physical qubits from its 54-qubit processor.[3][48] D-Wave annealers have thousands of physical qubits, and transport and logistics studies have formulated bin packing, routing, scheduling and loading problems for them.[6][47] Yet hardware qubits do not translate directly into decision variables: limited connectivity often requires **minor embedding**, where one logical variable is represented by a chain of physical qubits.

D-Wave’s Advantage2 has more than 4,400 active qubits, but a review says common transport encodings effectively fall to only a few thousand binary variables after embedding overhead.[47]

Classical competition is particularly demanding in optimisation. For the heterogeneous vehicle routing problem (HVRP), modern high-performance classical optimisers can find near-optimal solutions for instances exceeding 1,000 customers, typically in polynomial or pseudo-polynomial time; the cited quantum analysis estimates that comparable instances would need at least millions of controllable qubits.[6] In QAOA, quantum hardware is the largest bottleneck for real-world performance: current superconducting and trapped-ion qubits have two-qubit gate error rates on the order of 0.1% to 1%, and these errors accumulate as gates are composed into circuits.

Readout errors can flip a measured 0 to 1 or a 1 to 0; in Google’s primary Figure 3 data, the median isolated readout error was 4.4%. Limited connectivity, parameter tuning and the need to map problem interactions onto hardware also reduce scalable performance.[7][3][49] Optimisation demonstrations show that methods can be encoded and run; they do not yet show dependable superiority on industrially important tasks.[50]

A 2024 Scientific Reports study constructed a hybrid quantum-computing pipeline for two drug-discovery tasks: determining Gibbs free-energy profiles for prodrug activation involving covalent-bond cleavage, and simulating covalent-bond interactions.[51] Moderna and IBM researchers reported in a May 2024 preprint that a conditional value at risk (CVaR)-based variational algorithm, run with minimal error mitigation on IBM Eagle and Heron processors, produced minimum-free-energy mRNA secondary structures matching the classical CPLEX solver for sequences up to 60 nucleotides, mapped to problems of 10 to 80 qubits.[52] A review of quantum drug-discovery research found that most studies use simulation, limited hardware verification or benchmark-level evaluation; only a few reach prospective screening, while a KRAS-inhibitor study progressed to compound synthesis and experimental verification.[53]

A quantum-computing application to cryptography also demonstrated a hybrid randomness protocol: a quantum computer generated samples, then classical supercomputers mathematically certified that the randomness was genuine and could not be mimicked by classical methods.[32]

In an Einride shipment-selection study submitted in April 2026 and revised in August 2026, Iterative-QAOA produced a proposed shipment selection that Einride used as a warm start for its routing solver.[54] The study used anonymized real customer data for instances reaching 130 qubits; most results came from noiseless simulation, while the 20-to-35-qubit subset ran on trapped-ion hardware and closely matched the simulation.[54]

For drug discovery, a more accurate quantum calculation would be only one input to a much larger process. It would still need to achieve chemical accuracy where relevant and be followed by laboratory testing and, for medicines, clinical validation; it cannot replace the drug-development pipeline.

Cryptography also has a second, less dramatic quantum implication. **Grover’s algorithm** can in principle speed up brute-force search by a square-root factor, affecting symmetric encryption and hash functions, whereas Shor’s algorithm directly breaks the mathematical foundations of RSA and discrete-logarithm systems; appropriate symmetric key lengths can counter Grover’s reduced advantage.

In optimisation, QAOA and annealing are generally **heuristics**: methods intended to find a good solution without a guarantee that it is the best one. Real business constraints can also make the mathematical encoding much larger than the original decision problem, so an apparently small application may exceed the useful capacity of available hardware.

### Synthesis

Quantum computing has progressed from basic qubit control to cloud-accessible processors, deep selected circuits, benchmark advantages, scientific simulations and below-threshold quantum-memory demonstrations. The most important progress is reliability: logical error can now decrease as error-correcting codes grow, but one useful logical qubit can still demand hundreds or thousands of physical qubits and substantial classical control.[1][39]

The application outlook differs accordingly. Molecular simulation has the strongest structural rationale because the target system is itself quantum, although current chemistry demonstrations remain small. Cryptography has a clear future threat from Shor’s algorithm but no current cryptographic-scale attack. Optimisation has abundant prototypes and specialised annealing hardware, but it faces the strongest classical baselines and the least settled evidence of broad speedup.[17][2][4][6]

## Drug discovery

Judgment — Drug discovery asks which molecules might act on a biological target, which can become safe medicines, and how they behave in the body. Quantum computing matters here because the hardest parts of molecular behaviour—the motions and correlations of electrons—are themselves quantum-mechanical. Its practical role today is narrow: it can be inserted into selected calculations inside a much larger classical drug-discovery workflow.

### How could quantum computing be used in drug discovery?

Quantum computers could calculate **electronic structure**: the arrangement and behaviour of electrons that determines molecular energies, chemical reactions, charge transfer and some forms of binding. That is valuable where standard computational drug-design methods use simplified force fields—mathematical models of atoms and bonds—that can miss polarization, electron correlation, metal-ion behaviour and covalent-bond formation. These effects are particularly relevant at catalytic or metal-containing protein binding sites and for covalent inhibitors.[55][23]

Cytochrome P450 enzymes metabolize many drugs; Google’s analysis reports multireference character—where no single electron arrangement adequately describes the system—in P450 models studied with classical DMRG+NEVPT2 and CCSD(T) calculations. It identifies suitably sized P450 models as a potential quantum-advantage problem, conditional on advances in both classical chemistry and quantum algorithms.[124]

A principal near-term method is the **Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)**. VQE is a hybrid quantum–classical algorithm: a quantum processor prepares trial molecular states and measures their energy, while a classical optimiser repeatedly changes circuit parameters to seek the lowest-energy state. This makes VQE suited to noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, meaning present-day machines that have useful numbers of qubits but no full error correction.[56][57][39]

The most credible immediate pattern is **modular coprocessing**. Classical systems retain large-scale work—compound-library processing, geometry optimisation, docking, molecular-dynamics sampling, candidate generation, scoring and validation—while the quantum processor tackles a selected chemistry or discrete-search bottleneck. Reviews identify three principal insertion points:

- **Machine-learning screening and molecular generation:** quantum circuits can act as feature extractors in predictive models or as priors and generators in models that propose new molecules. The intended value is better ranking or more chemically useful candidate generation, rather than replacing scalable classical deep learning. [53][18]

- **Molecular docking:** docking predicts how a candidate molecule may fit into a protein binding pocket. Under noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) constraints, quantum computing can be a modular plugin for selected docking steps—position recognition, pose search or fragment-based flexible docking—under a limited quantum-call budget, after the problem is translated into a discrete optimisation form such as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation (QUBO) problem. Classical docking still generates poses, processes geometric constraints, performs large-scale enumeration and rough scoring. [53][18]

- **Molecular dynamics and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM):** molecular dynamics follows atomic motion over time. In QM/MM, a chemically important region—such as a reactive bond or enzyme active site—is modelled quantum mechanically, while the protein, solvent and broader environment use classical molecular mechanics. Quantum hardware could provide local electronic energies or forces for bond formation, bond breaking, proton transfer, metal coordination or strong polarization; classical engines would continue long-timescale sampling and trajectory propagation. [53][58][18]

This division of labour follows directly from the physical problem. Reliable binding affinity depends on an **ensemble** of molecular conformations, protonation states, tautomers, solvent interactions and protein flexibility, not merely on the energy of one optimised molecular geometry.[39] **Thus, quantum computing’s plausible first use is as a specialist evaluator of local, electronically difficult decisions, rather than as an all-quantum system for discovering a medicine.**[53][39]

A difference of about 1.4 kcal/mol in binding free energy corresponds to a tenfold change in binding strength, making errors near 1 kcal/mol consequential for candidate comparisons. No efficient quantum algorithm is known for the broader conformational and free-energy sampling bottleneck.[125]

Some drug-design tasks can also be cast as optimisation problems, in which the aim is to select the best combination from many discrete possibilities. Docking pose search can be expressed as graph search, group search or a matching problem, and encoded as a QUBO instance; the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and digitized counterdiabatic QAOA (DCQAOA) have been applied to a combined interaction diagram. QAOA and quantum annealing are also proposed for simplified protein-folding and conformational-search problems.[18][59]

Drug discovery is a longer process than molecular modelling: after researchers identify a biological target and find initial hits, they optimise leads, assess properties such as toxicity and metabolism, and then conduct laboratory, animal and human studies. A quantum-computed result would be one input to those decisions, not a replacement for experimental testing or clinical trials.

### What has been demonstrated in drug discovery?

Quantum hardware has performed chemistry calculations, including energies of small molecules; NIST describes these as early demonstrations rather than useful drug-development applications.[8] A landmark 2017 VQE experiment calculated ground-state energies and potential-energy curves for H2, LiH and BeH2 using 2, 4 and 6 qubits respectively. Those calculations used simplified molecular models, including a minimal STO-3G basis and frozen inner orbitals for LiH and BeH2.[2][43]

A VQE benchmark covered seven molecules from water to 68-atom imatinib, including aspirin, oseltamivir, morphine and lovastatin, using reduced (2e,2o) and (4e,4o) active spaces and comparing results with Hartree–Fock and density-functional theory.[126][127]

In that benchmark, superconducting hardware performed end-to-end two-qubit VQE optimizations for water, benzene and aspirin: eight runs converged in 6–14 iterations but took 1,933–7,429 seconds each. The two-qubit circuits represented reduced active spaces, not the full electronic structure of those molecules.[126][127]

The benchmark’s active-space energies were correlation contributions relative to Hartree–Fock, rather than complete molecular energies; they cannot alone establish chemical accuracy for an entire drug molecule.[127]

Drug-proximate demonstrations have progressed beyond those toy molecules, though they remain narrowly bounded. In the 2024 *Scientific Reports* study *A hybrid quantum computing pipeline for real world drug discovery*, Li, Yin, Li and colleagues used a superconducting device in two hybrid case studies: Gibbs free-energy profiles for prodrug activation by carbon–carbon bond cleavage, and a QM/MM simulation of the covalent inhibitor Sotorasib binding KRAS(G12C). The quantum calculation used a two-electron, two-orbital active-space approximation represented on two qubits, while the surrounding workflow remained classical.[56][60]

In a benchmark covering drug-like molecules of 3 to 68 atoms, end-to-end VQE runs for water, benzene and aspirin used a two-qubit hardware-efficient circuit on 13- and 60-qubit superconducting processors and took 1,933–7,429 seconds per run.[126][127]

For the prodrug case, the calculated reaction barrier and Gibbs-energy change agreed with earlier experimental and theoretical results. For the KRAS(G12C)–Sotorasib case, the authors placed five atoms in the quantum-mechanical region: the cysteine-side SG atom and Sotorasib’s C18, C17, O16 and C15 atoms. The covalent bond joined Sotorasib’s C18 atom to cysteine’s SG atom, and the first 1,600 quantum-computer molecular-dynamics steps closely followed the classical QM/MM baseline.[60][56] The simulation thereby demonstrated that a quantum calculation can be integrated into a drug-target modelling pipeline; it did not turn the quantum processor into a faster replacement for the classical workflow.

In a fragment-based binding study, researchers used a 64-qubit superconducting system but allocated only four qubits to the VQE fragment. Resource limits prevented full VQE optimization, so they measured error-mitigated single-point energies; hardware binding-energy results differed from noiseless simulations by 0.5–6.5 kcal/mol across five ligands, exceeding the approximately 1 kcal/mol decision target. [128]

Other demonstrations target individual discovery subtasks. A modified Grover search—an algorithm designed to amplify promising answers in a structured search—was run in a simulator and on a real quantum computer for protein binding-site localization. It used a simplified representation containing only selected interaction types and ended before full docking-pose prediction.[53] QAOA and digitized counterdiabatic QAOA (DCQAOA) have selected docking poses by finding a maximum-weight clique in a protein–ligand interaction graph.

The reviewed cases were simulation-only 6–12-qubit instances in MindQuantum, used 5,000 samples and 500 trials, had no explicit quantum error mitigation, and relied on substantial pruning; DCQAOA with p = 8 layers worked best in the 8-qubit case, while scaling and local-optima challenges remained.[53][18]

The most experimentally connected example is Vakili and colleagues’ hybrid KRAS-inhibitor generation workflow. Its QCBM used an IBM Guadalupe 16-qubit Falcon r4P processor, with 16 qubits, four circuit layers and 96 parameters, to generate a prior for a classical LSTM; the methods report that the QCBM was run with an error-correction circuit.[61] The workflow trained on a 1.1-million-item dataset assembled from approximately 650 experimentally confirmed KRAS inhibitors, 250,000 top-scoring compounds from a classical virtual screen of 100 million molecules, and 850,000 synthesizability-filtered molecules generated with STONED–SELFIES. It sampled one million compounds from each prior model, then used Chemistry42 filtering and docking-score ranking to select 15 novel molecules for synthesis, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding assays and cell-based assays.[62] Two synthesized hybrid-workflow candidates were highlighted.

ISM061-018-2 bound KRAS-G12D with a reported affinity of 1.4 μM and produced dose-responsive, micromolar-range inhibition in cell assays, but its activity also extended to wild-type KRAS, NRAS and HRAS interactions.[61][63] ISM061-022 showed micromolar-range, concentration-dependent inhibition and greater activity against some mutants, especially G12R and Q61H, but did not bind KRAS-G12D by SPR and showed at least some nonspecific activity in an artificial control assay.[62][53][23][18]

Small-system molecular dynamics has also reached hardware. VQE supplied electronic energies and numerical gradients for ground-state ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) trajectories of H2 on IBM Q Vigo hardware, while the nuclei were propagated classically; H2 was mapped from four qubits to two and then one qubit. Larger molecules in that AIMD work were assessed mainly with classical full-configuration-interaction reference wave functions. An annealer-assisted framework propagated H2 vibrational trajectories; and excited-state demonstrations covered H2 and CH2NH, with agreement degrading near a conical intersection, a point where molecular energy surfaces meet.[53][18] **The demonstrated progression is from small molecular-energy calculations to selected drug-discovery subtasks, not to an end-to-end quantum discovery programme that has outperformed its best classical counterpart.**[8][53]

A separate protein–ligand calculation used a 64-qubit superconducting device but allocated only four qubits to a fragment calculation. Resource limits restricted the work to single-point energies rather than full VQE optimization across its five ligand systems.[18][128]

For the 1,600-step KRAS–Sotorasib QM/MM sanity check, the quantum-hardware run took 30.8 times the wall-clock time of the classical baseline (3,820.0 versus 124.0 minutes). This quantifies that the reported agreement in trajectory came with substantially slower execution in this reduced demonstration.[56]

In 2016, O’Malley and colleagues demonstrated a calculation of the hydrogen molecule’s energy on superconducting quantum hardware. This was an early small-molecule quantum-chemistry demonstration, not a drug-discovery result.

For the prodrug case, Li, Yin, Li and colleagues’ 2024 pipeline calculated the solvent-inclusive carbon–carbon-cleavage barrier as 7 ± 5 kcal/mol and the Gibbs free-energy change as −51 ± 11 kcal/mol with VQE on a superconducting quantum computer. These are means and standard deviations from four independent experiments using the (2e, 2o) active space. The same table reported classical Hartree–Fock (HF), complete active-space configuration interaction (CASCI) and density-functional-theory (DFT) comparisons: with solvent, CASCI gave 11.8 kcal/mol for the barrier and −49.5 kcal/mol for the free-energy change, while DFT gave 8.3 and −10.8 kcal/mol, respectively.[64]

In a thermolysin quantum-hardware free-energy-perturbation example, the reported LUCJ-SQD-extSQD result deviated from experiment by 2.54 kcal/mol; the reported deviations were 6.05 kcal/mol for classical molecular mechanics, −9.39 kcal/mol for Hartree–Fock and 6.18 kcal/mol for classical heat-bath configuration interaction. The quantum-involved result remained above an approximately 1 kcal/mol accuracy target.[129][125]

The QCBM–LSTM model improved filter-passing performance for synthesizability and stability by 21.5% in the Tartarus benchmark. Its generated molecules had the highest scores for target PDB 4LDE, while classical algorithms achieved higher docking scores for the other two tested targets, PDB 1SYH and 6Y2F. On the study’s KRAS dataset, models with a quantum prior had higher local-filter and Chemistry42-filter success rates than models without one, and the local-filter success rate correlated roughly linearly with the number of qubits used to model the prior.[61]

In October 2025, Google Quantum AI reported that its Willow chip ran its Quantum Echoes algorithm 13,000 times faster than the best classical algorithm on one of the world’s fastest supercomputers. In a separate proof-of-principle experiment with UC Berkeley, it applied the algorithm to 15- and 28-atom molecules and reported agreement with traditional nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements; Google identified quantum-enhanced NMR as a possible future drug-discovery use.[52]

On August 18, 2026, Quantinuum, NVIDIA and Pfizer described transformer models that propose circuits to prepare the ground state of imipramine, a benchmark molecule. Quantinuum stated that the method was validated on its Helios hardware.[52]

Hybrid quantum neural-network proof-of-concept studies have combined classical feature extraction with variational quantum circuits to predict IC50 drug response from small genomics-based drug–cell-line datasets. Other proof-of-concept work integrated gene-expression, miRNA and DNA-methylation cancer multiomics data with variational quantum neural networks for lung-cancer subtype classification against classical machine-learning benchmarks.[65]

An active space is a reduced molecular model that retains the electrons and orbitals judged most important to the chemical question, while leaving the rest out or treating it approximately. This reduction makes early hardware experiments possible, but also means their result is not a calculation of the full drug-target system.

In active-space notation, “(6e,6o)” retains six electrons in six orbitals; under a common Jordan–Wigner mapping, it needs 12 qubits, whereas a full orbital description of even a small drug molecule requires more than 50 qubits.[126]

In computational benchmarks, the authors reported that QCBM–LSTM improved the rate at which generated molecules passed their synthesizability and stability filters by 21.5% compared with a fully classical LSTM; five repeated filter comparisons used 1,000 samples per repetition, and the qubit-count comparison used 5,000 samples per repetition. The results were mixed on docking: in the Tartarus benchmark, QCBM–LSTM generated the highest-scoring molecules for target PDB 4LDE, while classical algorithms obtained higher docking scores for PDB 1SYH and 6Y2F.[62]

Molecular-generation evidence also has task-specific limits. The KRAS model did not incorporate target structural data while generating molecules; it evaluated possible binding afterward.[66] Its training depended on unusually extensive KRAS data, including known inhibitors, docking-screened molecules and generated analogues, whereas comparable data density is seldom available for truly undrugged targets.[66]

ISM061-022 showed concentration-dependent inhibition and greater activity against KRAS-G12R and KRAS-Q61H, but did not bind KRAS-G12D and showed evidence of some nonspecific activity in an artificial control assay.[61]

In computational benchmarks, adding the QCBM prior improved the rate at which generated molecules passed synthesizability and stability filters by 21.5% relative to a fully classical LSTM. The QCBM–LSTM had the highest success rate across the Tartarus tasks but a marginally lower docking score than classical approaches.[67][63]

Its unusually extensive training data make this approach difficult to transfer directly to truly undrugged targets, for which comparable data density is seldom available.[66]

### What practical applications in drug discovery remain proposed or unproven?

The long-term ambition is more accurate simulation of drug-relevant electronic structure: binding affinities, reaction pathways, protonation states, metal-centre chemistry, molecular conformations and reactions. A larger fault-tolerant quantum computer could run **Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)**, an algorithm that estimates an energy-related eigenvalue of a molecular Hamiltonian—the mathematical object representing a molecule’s energy and electron interactions—with high precision. QPE requires deep, reliable circuits and a prepared starting state that overlaps strongly with the desired molecular state. The pipeline mapping is NISQ-era VQE for small active-space electronic-structure calculations, QPE on fault-tolerant quantum-computing (FTQC) hardware for full-active-space accuracy on drug-sized molecules, and QML classifiers as research-stage tools for property and activity prediction.[57][39]

Resource estimates illustrate both progress and distance. For an Ibrutinib–Bruton’s tyrosine kinase model, a 2022 *Journal of Chemical Theory and Computation* perspective estimated that sparse qubitization—an algorithmic technique that reduces the cost of simulating a molecular Hamiltonian—could calculate a (42-electron, 42-orbital) active-space ground-state energy in about 7.6 days under stated fault-tolerant assumptions, versus more than 1,000 years using Trotterization. Its resource estimates covered active spaces from (14e,14o) to (100e,100o), assumed future fault-tolerant hardware, and targeted 1.6 mHa precision: textbook QPE used Trotterization, while Heisenberg-limited QPE used sparse qubitization with a truncated Hamiltonian.[55] The same perspective estimated 1.9 days for sparse qubitization of a (32e,32o) space, compared with roughly 250 years for its Trotterized version at a physical error rate of 10^-4.[55]

For a drug-metabolism-relevant cytochrome P450 CYP3A4 model, resource estimates for fault-tolerant QPE on the full CAS(50e, 50o) Hamiltonian place the calculation at 2,000–10,000 logical qubits and 10^8–10^10 T gates. Newer tensor-factorisation and active-volume compilation may reduce T-gate counts by one to two orders of magnitude, but the analysis still requires millions of physical qubits, treats only the compound I iron-oxo intermediate at one molecular geometry, and identifies full CYP3A4 QPE as a post-2030 target.[39] This is why fault-tolerant molecular simulation remains a prospective capability rather than a routine pharmaceutical tool.

Quantum machine learning (QML) remains similarly unsettled. Proposed uses include quantum kernels—circuits that calculate similarity measures for machine-learning models—for structure–activity relationships, binding-affinity prediction and drug–target interaction prediction. But classical graph neural networks and transformers currently outperform demonstrated QML approaches on standard drug-discovery benchmarks.[23][39] Loading large classical molecular datasets into a quantum processor, often called the quantum-RAM problem, is an additional bottleneck.[39]

For virtual screening, Grover-style database search offers a theoretical quadratic speed-up, but it requires fault-tolerant hardware and a structured **oracle**, meaning a quantum procedure that marks which candidates satisfy the search criterion. Classical docking already screens millions of compounds per hour. Near-term quantum workloads instead include small active-space electronic descriptors such as local correlation or protonation indicators, variational or imaginary-time estimators, kernel-based feature maps for niche classification, and modest combinatorial encodings; these complement rather than replace high-throughput docking or ADMET—absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity—assessment.[39][23] For docking and molecular generation, therefore, the near-term proposal is not a quantum replacement for high-throughput systems but a physics-aware quantum evaluator for a small candidate set or a limited discrete decision.[23]

**Practical value remains unproven until a quantum module delivers repeatable, decision-relevant improvement over strong classical baselines under real runtime and resource limits.** That threshold matters because a valid molecule, a reproduced energy curve or a synthesised hit is valuable progress, but each answers a different question from whether the quantum component improved the discovery decision.[18][23]

Proposed molecular-simulation uses also include estimating protein–ligand binding affinities, evaluating drug metabolism and possible adverse effects through atomic-level reaction simulation, and studying molecular interactions relevant to safety and efficacy. In this division of labour, artificial intelligence prioritises candidate molecules and quantum simulation evaluates selected candidates more precisely.[68]

Protein-folding work consists of algorithmic proposals and theoretical frameworks for quantum optimisation or simulation; no biologically realistic protein folding has been executed on quantum hardware.[65]

### What still limits practical use in drug discovery?

The first constraint is hardware reliability. Quantum gates and measurements are imperfect, and errors accumulate as circuits become deeper. A 2024 *Nature* error-correction study contrasted recently demonstrated 99.9% entangling-gate fidelity with error rates below 10^-10 required for many demanding applications.[1] The same study observed six large, localized error bursts across 2 × 10^10 error-correction cycles; each affected roughly 30 qubits, occurred about once an hour and decayed with a time constant of around 400 microseconds.[1]

**Quantum error correction** creates a more reliable **logical qubit** from many error-prone physical qubits, but it imposes major overhead. The 2024 study projected that a logical error rate of 10^-6 would require a distance-27 logical qubit composed of 1,457 physical qubits.[1] Drug-relevant resource estimates commonly require thousands of logical qubits, while one review puts the current physical-to-logical ratio at roughly 1,000:1.[39]

The second constraint is algorithmic. VQE avoids the long circuits of QPE but requires repeated measurements and repeated classical optimisation. Its success depends on the **ansatz**, the parameterised trial wavefunction implemented by the circuit. As molecular systems grow, VQE can encounter **barren plateaus**: nearly flat optimisation landscapes in which parameter changes produce too little signal for efficient learning. Chemical accuracy—often defined as an energy error below about 1 kcal/mol or 0.0016 Hartree—has not been shown for drug-relevant active spaces on NISQ hardware.[39][43]

Measurement overhead is a related obstacle. To estimate molecular energy, a quantum device must sample many observables repeatedly; in one VQE resource simulation for H2, LiH and BeH2, chemical accuracy required approximately 10^6 samples for each molecule.[43] Better measurement grouping can sharply reduce the number of circuit repetitions for selected benchmark systems, but it does not remove the need for high accuracy, repeated sampling and reliable circuits.[69]

The third constraint is modelling completeness. An accurate quantum calculation of a local active site can be overwhelmed by errors in the classical environment unless the embedding between the two is balanced. Whole proteins can contain tens of thousands of atoms, operate in water at finite temperature, and sample conformations over timescales from femtoseconds to milliseconds.[58][70][55] Even future fault-tolerant biomolecular modelling is therefore likely to remain hybrid: quantum resources for the hardest local electronic problem, and classical resources for the rest.[70]

No efficient quantum algorithm is known for the broader free-energy sampling bottleneck in protein–ligand binding, even if quantum hardware eventually improves local electronic-energy calculations.[125]

Faster local electronic-structure calculations would not eliminate the need to sample many molecular configurations, and no efficient quantum algorithm is known for the full free-energy sampling bottleneck.[125]

Finally, existing classical methods set a demanding benchmark. GPU-accelerated free-energy perturbation can achieve roughly 1 kcal/mol accuracy for related compounds in lead optimisation, while current NISQ VQE methods cannot compete with it in production workflows.[39] Classical docking, deep learning and molecular dynamics also remain fast and scalable, despite their known trade-offs in scoring accuracy, distribution shift and sampling cost.[53][18] **The practical hurdle is therefore not merely building a larger quantum computer; it is producing a better molecular decision than mature classical tools at a cost and speed that fit drug-discovery practice.**[55][18]

Free-energy perturbation estimates the free-energy difference between related molecules by sampling intermediate states. No efficient quantum algorithm is known for the full protein-and-solvent free-energy-sampling bottleneck, and no hardware demonstration has calculated a real protein–ligand binding affinity to chemical accuracy.[125]

Reproducibility and validation are further requirements for practical use. Environmental noise, limited coherence times and gate errors cause fluctuations on NISQ devices, and differences between superconducting-qubit and trapped-ion implementations can produce cross-platform variability.[68] Quantum predictions therefore need comparison with density functional theory, molecular-dynamics simulations and experimental data; proposed standard benchmarks cover energy-calculation accuracy, binding-affinity prediction and quantum-noise quantification.[68]

Error mitigation consists of measurement, calibration or post-processing techniques that reduce the apparent effect of hardware errors without fully correcting them during computation. It can improve selected small experiments, but it does not scale as a substitute for fault-tolerant error correction.

### Synthesis

Quantum computing has moved drug discovery from an abstract promise to targeted experiments: small-molecule energy calculations, limited quantum-hardware chemistry, local QM/MM integration, binding-site localization and one KRAS workflow that reached compound synthesis and assays.[8][60][53] The evidence points to hybrid coprocessing as the durable near-term direction, because classical systems still supply the scale, sampling and validation that quantum devices cannot yet provide.[53][23]

The strongest current opportunity is high-accuracy treatment of difficult local electronic chemistry, especially covalent reactions and metal-containing active sites. The weakest current claim is broad acceleration of screening or end-to-end drug development, because present devices remain noisy, active spaces remain small, and leading classical methods remain formidable.[23][39]

## Cryptography

Cryptography protects information and identity: encryption keeps data secret, while digital signatures let a recipient verify who signed a message. Quantum computing matters here chiefly because it could eventually break some of the mathematical problems on which today’s public-key systems rely—yet it is also prompting a practical defensive transition that runs on ordinary computers.

Judgment — Three ideas must remain separate. Quantum *cryptanalysis* uses a quantum computer to attack conventional cryptography. Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is conventional software-based cryptography designed to resist such attacks. Quantum key distribution (QKD) uses quantum communication equipment to distribute keys; it is not a general-purpose quantum computer and is not itself an encryption algorithm.

### How could quantum computing be used in cryptography?

A public-key cryptosystem uses a public key that can be shared openly and a private key that is kept secret. This supports encryption, key establishment—agreeing a shared secret over an insecure network—and digital signatures. RSA relies on integer factorization; Finite Field Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, DSA, ECDSA and EdDSA rely on the discrete-logarithm problem. The National Cyber Security Centre calls a quantum computer able to run attacks on these traditional public-key algorithms a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC).[71] Peter Shor’s 1994 algorithm showed that a sufficiently capable quantum computer could solve both problems efficiently, putting RSA, Diffie–Hellman, ECDH key exchange, ECDSA signatures and related public-key systems at risk.[9][71]

In the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem, an attacker knows a generator point P and another point Q that is a secret multiple of P, and seeks that multiplier.[130][131]

Shor’s algorithm is not simply “trying every key at once.” Its quantum part finds periodic structure in modular arithmetic through phase estimation and a quantum Fourier transform; classical post-processing then turns that result into factors or a discrete logarithm. In factoring, the intermediate task is *order finding*: finding the smallest positive number r for which a raised to r is equal to one modulo N. Some runs fail statistically, so the computation must be repeated.[72]

Grover’s algorithm presents a different risk. It accelerates unstructured search, such as brute-forcing a secret key, quadratically: searching a space of size 2 to the n requires roughly 2 to the power n divided by 2 operations rather than 2 to the n. Cisco gives AES-128 as approximately 2 to the power 64 operations and AES-256 as approximately 2 to the power 128 operations under Grover’s algorithm, equivalent to 64-bit and 128-bit keys respectively. That weakens symmetric-key encryption, where both parties use the same secret key, and hash functions, which map arbitrary messages to fixed-size values. It does not give Shor-like exponential advantage, so the usual mitigation is larger parameters; IBM and NIST describe doubling the symmetric-key length as the general response if Grover becomes practically relevant.[9][73][74]

[9][16] | A sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer would make these schemes no longer secure for encryption, key exchange and many signatures. [9][75] |
| **Symmetric encryption and hashes: AES, SHA-256** | Grover’s algorithm supplies quadratic rather than exponential search acceleration. [9][73] | Larger keys or outputs can address the known generic effect; the threat is materially less severe than for vulnerable public-key systems. [9][16] |
| **Symmetric encryption and hashes: AES, SHA-256, SHA-3** | Grover’s algorithm supplies quadratic rather than exponential search acceleration. [9][73] | Larger AES keys and larger SHA-2 and SHA-3 outputs can address the known generic effect; the threat is materially less severe than for vulnerable public-key systems. [9][16] |

PQC is the practical response to this prospective threat. It uses mathematical problems believed hard for both conventional and quantum computers and runs on present-day hardware; it should not be confused with quantum cryptography or QKD. NIST’s ML-KEM is a *key-encapsulation mechanism* (KEM): it lets two parties establish a shared secret over a public channel, after which they can use symmetric encryption and authentication.[9][76][77][78]

QKD is instead a specialised way to generate and distribute keying material using quantum states. It can detect some interception attempts because measuring those states disturbs them, but it does not authenticate the transmission source; a complete QKD system still requires authentication through asymmetric cryptography or preplaced keys. **The principal prospective quantum-computing use in cryptography is therefore attacking public-key mathematics, while the immediate use is replacing vulnerable public-key algorithms with PQC.**[78][71]

A currently marketed cryptography-related quantum product is a quantum random-number generator (QRNG), which produces randomness for conventional encryption rather than running Shor’s algorithm: Quantinuum describes Quantum Origin as a QRNG designed to strengthen encryption and protect data.[79]

### What has been demonstrated in cryptography?

Quantum computers have executed small demonstrations of Shor-style factoring. In 2001, Vandersypen and colleagues used seven nuclear-spin qubits in a molecule to factor 15 into 3 and 5. A later two-photon compiled experiment factored 21, and an ion-trap experiment demonstrated a scalable form of the algorithm for 15 using effective control of seven qubits and four cache qubits, reporting success probabilities above 90%.[80][81][4]

These are meaningful demonstrations of quantum control, modular arithmetic and period-finding, but they are not demonstrations of cryptographic codebreaking. Some small experiments use *compiled* circuits, meaning that the circuit is simplified for a particular input; the four-photon factoring-15 experiment, for example, selected an easy case and omitted qubits that evolved trivially. IBM’s current hardware tutorial ran order finding for N equals 15 and a equals 2 with 1,024 shots; its phase of 0.25 estimated the order as r equals 4 and recovered non-trivial factor 3. IBM reports that noise leaked measurement counts to other bitstrings, which it filtered statistically.[25][72]

A May 2024 hybrid quantum–classical method reportedly factored integers up to 50 bits. That is still far below RSA-2048, which is a real-world RSA scale; RSA’s own technical explanation states that current quantum computers cannot break 2048-bit RSA in practical cybersecurity settings.[82][72]

QKD has also moved beyond laboratory-only concepts into communications field tests, including a 2023 aerial-fibre QKD test. This is a demonstration of quantum communication for key distribution, not a quantum-computer attack on conventional encryption. Its practical security depends on the physical implementation: the NSA reports that weaknesses in specific QKD hardware have enabled several publicized attacks on commercial systems.[83][78]

A distinct practical result is defensive rather than quantum-computational: in August 2024, NIST finalized its first three PQC standards—FIPS 203 for ML-KEM key establishment, FIPS 204 for ML-DSA digital signatures, and FIPS 205 for SLH-DSA digital signatures—and says the algorithms are ready for use.[77][74] On March 26, 2025, a team from JPMorganChase, Quantinuum, Argonne National Laboratory, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and the University of Texas at Austin reported the first successful certified-randomness protocol on a quantum computer.

NIST finalized FIPS 205, the SLH-DSA digital-signature standard, on 13 August 2024.[132]

Their protocol used Random Circuit Sampling (RCS), in which the remote quantum computer sampled challenge random circuits, and classical supercomputers then certified the output; the team certified 71,313 bits of entropy, meaning measured unpredictability, using combined sustained classical computing of 1.1 × 10 to the power 18 floating-point operations per second (1.1 ExaFLOPS).[32] The experiment used Quantinuum’s 56-qubit trapped-ion System Model H2 and accessed it remotely over the internet.[77][71][76][32] **Demonstrated quantum cryptanalysis remains small-instance factoring.

Demonstrated practical progress consists chiefly of standardized and beginning deployments of quantum-resistant defenses, with certified randomness a separate quantum-computing result that can support cryptographic applications.**[77][32]

A 2019 attempt to factor 35 on IBM Q System One achieved a 14% success rate, which its later review attributes to accumulated noise. That review also describes an IBM-based hybrid quantum–classical variational method for factoring 253, an 8-bit composite.[84] In newer experiments on an IBM processor, authors detected statistically significant phase-estimation signals for 15 and 21; for two tested bases of 35, one yielded only marginal evidence at the stated threshold and the other a weaker statistically significant signal. The authors tested whether small-instance histograms contained a quantum signal, not the end-to-end runtime scaling needed for cryptographic inputs.[84]

A separate proposed hybrid method, Jesse–Victor–Gharabaghi (JVG), computes modular exponentiation classically, encodes those values into a quantum state, and uses a Quantum Number Theoretic Transform (QNTT) for the quantum spectral-analysis stage. Its authors report a 309-digit test-number factorization, which they call RSA-1024-scale, in simulated and experimental frameworks; their real-device circuit used 110 qubits on IBM’s 133-qubit ibm_torino processor. This is a hybrid workflow, unlike standard Shor factoring, whose controlled modular exponentiation is performed quantumly.[85]

For a tested four-digit coprime number on hardware, the JVG authors report 2 seconds of backend occupancy per run versus 68 seconds for their Shor baseline, a 97% reduction in effective backend occupancy time per run; in their simulations, they report runtime declining from 174.1 seconds to 5.4 seconds, memory from 12.5 GB to 0.27 GB, and quantum gate counts by approximately 99%.[85]

A reported experiment by researchers from armasuisse Wissenschaft und Technologie, ETH Zürich and the Paul Scherrer Institut ran Shor’s algorithm on IBM’s 133-qubit system. Calibration drift made some runs unusable; hardware-specific circuits did not transpile through generic pipelines to IonQ and Quantinuum systems; and, for N = 35, circuit depth exceeded what the hardware could execute reliably. With an unfavourable base, noise prevented a statistically significant signal.[86]

### What practical applications in cryptography remain proposed or unproven?

The central proposed application is cryptanalytic: run Shor’s algorithm at a scale that breaks deployed public-key keys. It is theoretically well founded, but not operationally demonstrated. NIST calls the needed machine a *cryptographically relevant quantum computer* (CRQC), meaning a sufficiently powerful quantum computer able to threaten current encryption; no CRQC is known to exist.[77][87]

Resource estimates quantify the gap, but they are models rather than completed attacks. Gidney and Ekerå’s 2021 estimate projected factoring a 2048-bit RSA integer in eight hours with 20 million noisy physical qubits, assuming a planar superconducting grid, 0.1% physical gate error, one-microsecond surface-code cycles and ten-microsecond reaction time. A 2025 estimate reduced the projection to fewer than one million physical qubits and less than a week under stated assumptions, including the same 0.1% error rate and one-microsecond cycle; it estimated 897,864 physical qubits before rounding to one million for slack.[88][5]

The 2025 sub-million-qubit RSA-2048 resource estimate traded more logical operations for fewer magic-state factories, specialized components that supply fault-tolerant non-Clifford operations.[5]

These projections use different units from estimates expressed in *logical qubits*, the error-corrected qubits used by an algorithm. For example, Cisco reports an estimate of roughly 4,000 logical qubits for an RSA-2048 attack taking about one day, while NIST’s general description says many thousands of qubits would be needed to break present-day encryption. Physical-qubit and logical-qubit counts cannot be substituted for each other because error correction turns many noisy physical qubits into one logical qubit.[74][77][10]

Other proposed quantum-cryptography applications include blind quantum computing, where a remote quantum server computes without learning the client’s input; quantum secret sharing; quantum money; quantum blockchain; and delegated learning on encrypted data. A 2023 paper is titled 《Quantum secret sharing protocol using GHZ state: implementation on IBM qiskit》; a separate 2023 paper is titled 《Improving the implementation of quantum blockchain based on hypergraphs》; and a 2024 paper studies 《Hardware requirements for trapped-ion-based verifiable blind quantum computing with a measurement-only client》. These are distinct protocols and research programmes, not evidence that general-purpose quantum computers have replaced deployed encryption systems.[57][83]

QKD is also a limited-purpose alternative rather than a universal replacement. It distributes keys, but conventional encryption still uses those keys, and the required authentication remains external to QKD itself. The NSA judges quantum-resistant cryptography capable of providing QKD’s confidentiality function at lower cost and with a better-understood risk profile. **The unproven step is not whether Shor’s and Grover’s algorithms exist, but whether fault-tolerant machines can execute them against real cryptographic targets at useful cost and scale.**[78][89]

Using the stated physical-qubit counts, the newer RSA-2048 resource projection is 22.3 times lower than the earlier 20-million-qubit projection: 897,864 physical qubits before rounding rather than 20 million. The newer modeled architecture estimates 1,537 total logical qubits, including idle hot patches.[5] This is substantial progress in the projected resource estimate, not a measured cryptanalytic result.[88][5]

Alternative factoring approaches remain research proposals rather than established attacks. A December 2022 preprint reported that the Sublinear Quantum Integer Factorization (SQIF) algorithm factored the 48-bit integer 261,980,999,226,229 on a 10-qubit superconducting processor after mapping factoring to the Closest Vector Problem on a lattice. Its reported factors were 15,538,213 and 16,860,433.[90] Its reported factors multiply to the stated integer, but the reported quantum speedup is unknown, and the result does not threaten real-world cryptosystems because a 48-bit integer is easy for a classical computer to factor.[90]

For RSA-2048, the JVG study projects years of quantum execution time per order-finding attempt for its Shor method and around minutes per attempt for JVG; 10,000 JVG attempts scale to roughly 29 hours under its regression equations.[85] Their reported resource reductions compare complete hybrid and fully quantum pipelines with different register sizes, not an isolated transform or per-qubit comparison.[85]

Resource estimates for standard Shor implementations also remain architecture-dependent. One cited comparison gives an earlier RSA-2048 estimate of around 20 million qubits and eight hours, a later estimate of fewer than one million qubits and roughly five days, and an architecture estimate of approximately 13,436 qubits using novel qubit designs and multimode memories. A separate cited estimate is approximately 8.4 million physical qubits for a relatively direct RSA-2048 implementation with sufficiently low errors and long coherence.[84] These projections are distinct from both the 20-million-physical-qubit, eight-hour estimate and the later sub-million-qubit projection already described above.[84]

### What still limits practical use in cryptography?

The immediate technical limit is quantum error. Physical qubits lose information through *decoherence*, interaction with their environment, and gates introduce errors that accumulate through long computations. Quantum error correction protects one logical qubit by encoding it across many entangled physical qubits, but this produces substantial hardware and control overhead.[10]

Error correction has made important progress without yet reaching cryptanalytic scale. A 2024 surface-code experiment demonstrated real-time decoding with average 63-microsecond latency at code distance 5 for up to one million cycles, with a 1.1-microsecond processor cycle time. The same study stated that present logical error rates remain orders of magnitude from those needed for practical quantum computation and preclude larger fault-tolerant circuits.[1]

Cryptanalytic circuits are especially demanding because they require lengthy, reliable arithmetic. IBM estimates that factoring a 2048-bit number with Shor’s algorithm needs millions of qubits including error-correction overhead and circuit depth on the order of one billion; its tutorial identifies optimized circuit construction and robust error correction as requirements for practical attacks on modern systems.[72]

The QKD route has different limits. QKD needs dedicated fibre links or managed free-space transmitters, special-purpose hardware and sometimes trusted relays. Trusted relays add secure-facility cost and insider-risk exposure; QKD is also susceptible to denial-of-service because disruption can prevent key generation.[78]

PQC migration is practical but difficult. PQC algorithms are not always drop-in replacements: key and signature sizes, computing time, bandwidth, interoperability and legacy-device limitations all matter. NIST says integration of a new standardized algorithm across information systems has historically taken 10 to 20 years, while the NCSC warns that robust final-standard implementations—not draft protocol implementations—are needed for operational systems.[77][87][71]

This migration is urgent for long-lived secrets because of the *harvest now, decrypt later* threat: NIST states that an adversary can collect encrypted data now and retain it in the hope that a future quantum computer will decrypt it.[77] That threat applies most directly to data requiring long-term confidentiality; a future CRQC could also forge signatures and impersonate their legitimate owners.

**Practical cryptography is therefore advancing faster on defensive migration than on quantum codebreaking, because PQC can run now while a cryptographically relevant quantum computer still requires major gains in reliable scale.**[77][71]

Current limitations include not only average error rates but operational stability and portability. In experiments on ibm_torino, authors report 133 physical qubits, single-qubit SX-gate error of 2.876×10⁻⁴, two-qubit CX-gate error of 2.733×10⁻³, and readout error of 2.917×10⁻², all without quantum error correction. They also observed day-to-day calibration changes that made some runs unusable and required their removal as outliers.[84]

A circuit that works on one platform is not automatically usable on another: the same authors report that their IBM-oriented Shor circuits ran in simulation but did not transpile to the available non-superconducting hardware they tried through Amazon Braket and Microsoft Azure Quantum. They identify hardware-specific circuit design and optimization as necessary because platforms differ in qubit topology and native gates.[84]

Hardware platforms impose different trade-offs. Superconducting processors have fast gates but relatively short coherence and sensitivity to noise; trapped-ion systems have long coherence and low gate-error rates but slower gates and scaling challenges; photonic processors face photon loss, probabilistic entanglement generation, and demanding detector requirements. No platform dominates simultaneously in qubit count, fidelity, connectivity, speed, and compatibility with quantum error correction.[84]

For fault-tolerant computation, effective speed also depends on the quantum-error-correction cycle and decoding latency, not only on physical gate time; connectivity affects how efficiently those correction cycles can run. Qubit count by itself is therefore an incomplete measure of readiness for cryptanalysis.[84]

Standardization does not prove that PQC is permanently secure: its algorithms still require continuing cryptanalysis, secure implementations and review for vulnerabilities in the surrounding systems. Migration also requires organisations to inventory where cryptography is used, change protocols, test interoperability and performance, and update key and certificate management.

### Synthesis

Quantum computing creates an unusually clear but still future-facing cryptographic risk. Shor’s algorithm would directly defeat the public-key systems based on factoring and discrete logarithms, whereas Grover’s algorithm creates the less severe quadratic search threat to symmetric encryption and hashes. Small factoring demonstrations establish that the algorithmic ingredients work, but their inputs—15, 21, and reported hybrid factoring up to 50 bits—are far from deployed cryptographic sizes. Resource estimates are improving, yet they still describe hypothetical fault-tolerant machines with thousands of logical qubits or very large physical-qubit fleets.[9][80][81][82][5]

The practical consequence is already real: PQC standards and hybrid deployments are progressing on classical infrastructure, while QKD remains a specialised key-distribution technology with significant engineering, authentication and deployment constraints.[77][71][78]

## Optimization problems

### How could quantum computing be used in optimization problems?

Judgment — Optimization means choosing the best permissible option from many possibilities. A delivery company may seek the cheapest set of routes while respecting vehicle capacity and delivery times; a factory may seek the shortest feasible production schedule; an investor may seek the best return–risk balance. These are often **combinatorial optimization** problems: the computer chooses among discrete combinations, such as yes/no decisions, whose number can grow explosively.

A common route into quantum optimization is to translate a decision problem into an energy-minimization problem. In a **quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO)** model, each decision is a binary variable—zero or one—and the objective and penalties for broken constraints are expressed as a quadratic function. The equivalent physics formulation is an **Ising model**, in which two-state spins interact and the lowest-energy configuration represents the preferred solution. QUBO and Ising forms can be converted into one another by changing between binary variables and spins.[91][92][26]

Two main quantum approaches use this representation:

- **Quantum annealing:** a specialised analogue quantum computer starts in an easily prepared quantum state and gradually changes its energy landscape so that a low-energy final state should encode a good solution. It is designed principally for Ising and QUBO inputs, and repeated anneal-and-readout runs return candidate solutions because an anneal has only a non-zero probability of reaching the ground state, the true lowest-energy answer. [93][91]

- **Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA):** a gate-based, hybrid quantum–classical method. A quantum circuit alternates a **cost Hamiltonian**, representing the objective, with a **mixer Hamiltonian**, which moves probability among candidate bit strings. A conventional optimizer repeatedly adjusts the circuit’s parameters using measurements from the quantum processor. [92][7][3]
- **Other gate-model approaches:** quantum walks and amplitude amplification are also investigated for optimization, alongside variational methods.[133][134]

A third near-term pattern is the broader **hybrid workflow**: a classical computer preprocesses the problem, the quantum device explores or samples selected subproblems, and classical software repairs, scores, or improves the result. D-Wave’s Hybrid Solver Service, for example, combines CPU/GPU heuristic search with queries to an Advantage quantum processing unit, explicitly to tackle problems larger than fit directly on the annealer.[94]

These methods have been formulated for Max-Cut, graph colouring, portfolio selection, scheduling, bin packing, vehicle routing, facility location, traffic control, and energy dispatch. A vehicle-routing problem can be expressed as choosing routes from a depot to customers and back while minimizing distance, time, or another cost under constraints such as vehicle capacity and delivery windows.[92][7][95][96] This makes quantum optimization a plausible component of logistics, finance, manufacturing, and resource-allocation systems—not because every hard problem becomes easy, but because many can be represented as binary choices with costs and constraints.

**What follows:** quantum computers can already run optimization encodings and hybrid search routines, but the translation from a real decision problem into a QUBO or Ising model is itself a central technical task and can determine whether the result is useful.[97][94][98]

QAOA was introduced by Edward Farhi, Jeffrey Goldstone, and Sam Gutmann in 2014. It is one of the best-known variational algorithms, meaning that it uses adjustable circuit settings that a classical optimizer tunes from measurement results.

QAOA is also a **variational quantum algorithm**: the processor prepares a parameterized quantum state, measures the expected value of the encoded cost, and a classical optimizer updates the parameters. It has repeated cost and mixer layers; the number of repetitions is denoted by p. In the theoretical limit p → ∞, QAOA can exactly approximate adiabatic quantum evolution and find the optimum, whereas present devices use shallow circuits.[99][100]

The range of proposed mappings is wider than logistics and finance. QUBO examples include MaxCut, number partitioning, and graph colouring; MaxCut divides a graph’s vertices into two groups so that the total weight of edges between the groups is maximized. Proposed QAOA applications include constrained optimization; vertex-k-colouring for scheduling, mobile radio-frequency assignment, and register allocation; maximum k-vertex cover for network security and social-network analysis; aircraft tail assignment; vehicle routing; portfolio optimization; partially occluded-object detection; and machine-learning training. Drug-design problems can likewise be cast as optimization problems for QAOA or quantum annealing.[99][51][101]

Current use falls into two distinct regimes. In the near-term regime, noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors serve as heuristic co-processors in hybrid workflows; the longer-term objective is fault-tolerant algorithms with provable asymptotic speedups on large problems. Quantum routines can refine candidate neighbourhoods, generate initial samples for stochastic search, or explore small locally difficult subgraphs within classical tabu search or simulated annealing. Quantum annealers are specialised for sparse Ising or QUBO models, particularly where the problem landscape maps naturally to device topology; neutral-atom arrays can directly model Maximum Independent Set with a coupling graph constrained by atom geometry.[100]

Several QAOA adaptations target particular structures rather than providing one universal optimizer: ADAPT-QAOA selects mixers from a pool of operators; RQAOA iteratively reduces problem size; WS-QAOA starts from relaxed-QUBO solutions and can retain classical guarantees such as the Goemans–Williamson bound for MaxCut; and Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatzes use more flexible operators for hard and soft constraints. Selecting and tuning parameters remains part of the computation: gradient-free methods are more computationally efficient but struggle with many layers and noise, while gradient-based and machine-learning methods can perform better but are computationally expensive.[99]

Max-Cut divides the nodes of a graph into two groups so that as many connections as possible, or the greatest total connection weight in a weighted graph, cross between the groups.[7]

### What has been demonstrated in optimization problems?

Quantum optimization has moved well beyond abstract proposals. Gate-based processors have executed QAOA circuits on graph problems, while quantum annealers have been programmed for portfolio, scheduling, routing, and logistics benchmarks. These demonstrations establish that present hardware can produce candidate solutions and support closed-loop hybrid optimization; they do not, by themselves, establish a general practical advantage over leading classical solvers.

Google’s Sycamore experiment applied QAOA to combinatorial optimization using 23 physical qubits from a 54-qubit superconducting processor. It covered hardware-grid problems, three-regular Max-Cut graphs, and fully connected Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin-glass instances. For one 23-bit hardware-grid instance, the study reports that random sampling could find a ground-state bit string with roughly a 20% chance. In a separate experimental angle-optimization procedure, it used six executions of 25,000 samples for gradient estimates and a further 25,000 repetitions at the proposed angles, for 175,000 bit strings in total; the reported cumulative probability of finding a ground-state bit string was nearly 100%.[3] The result demonstrates that tuning QAOA parameters can materially improve a small hardware-native task.

The same Sycamore study also shows why a successful hardware run is not yet a practical win. At the studied sizes, all its problems remained exactly solvable by classical algorithms. For compiled, non-native Max-Cut and spin-glass problems, circuit depth increased with problem size, errors propagated between qubits, and solution quality declined; performance remained above random guessing only up to 17 bits in those challenging cases.[3] In contrast, shallow QAOA on problems matching the chip’s connectivity showed normalized solution quality that was independent of problem size at fixed circuit depth.[3]

A QAOA simulation for random three-regular Max-Cut graphs estimated a runtime crossover only at several hundred to several thousand qubits; the estimate extrapolated from small-system simulations rather than measuring an advantage at that scale.[135]

A Rydberg-atom optimization study simulated systems of up to 26 atoms and extrapolated approximation ratios of about 0.72 under then-recent hardware noise and 0.84 under a near-future noise model. It reported experimental repetition rates of 3–5 Hz, dominated by atom loading and measurement.[136]

IBM’s current QAOA tutorial likewise demonstrates real-hardware execution at two very different scales. Its five-node Max-Cut example returned a cut value of 5, matching the brute-force classical optimum of 5. Its 100-node weighted-graph example returned a cut value of 78.0, but the tutorial gives neither the graph’s known optimum nor a classical comparison for that larger case.[92] The former validates the workflow on a toy instance; the latter shows that a larger circuit can be run, rather than proving its answer is competitively good.

Quantum annealers have also produced real-device optimization results. A 2021 study benchmarked a D-Wave 2000Q annealer on Markowitz portfolio selection, comparing results against brute-force ground truth for generated portfolio instances. It evaluated forward and reverse annealing, solution success probabilities, and broken chains—physical-qubit chains used to represent one logical variable.[102] On the tested 8-to-20-variable instances, estimated success probability decayed exponentially with problem size for both embedding methods.[102] Reverse annealing from the best state found by forward annealing produced lower-energy solutions on average for the 94 cases where forward annealing had not found the ground state.[102]

Application studies now cover practical-looking tasks rather than only textbook graphs. A transport-and-logistics review reports a hybrid quantum-annealing facility-location solver that came within 1% of the best-known objective on 12 benchmarks and used about one-third fewer iterations than tuned tabu search or simulated annealing. It also reports a D-Wave hybrid solver reducing the number of transit facilities by 40% while maintaining the same accessibility in a Vancouver metropolitan-area case.[47][26] A car paint-shop sequencing study used instances derived from real-world data, from 10 to 3,000 cars, and found that quantum hardware and hybrid algorithms produced adequate small-instance solutions but approached the performance of a simple greedy algorithm as size increased.[26]

There are also demonstrations whose quantum component is embedded in a larger classical method. A 2026 preprint benchmarked a hybrid sequential quantum computing pipeline on 20 higher-order binary optimization instances with 156 variables. The pipeline combined classical simulated-annealing warm starts, short quantum evolutions on IBM Heron r3 processors, and classical memetic tabu search. It achieved geometric-mean time-to-solution improvements over simulated annealing—3.3 times for one instance family and 1.9 times for another—but enhanced parallel tempering was faster than the hybrid pipeline across the benchmark.[103] The authors state directly that the result does not establish quantum advantage or an asymptotic separation from classical optimization, and the design did not isolate the contribution of the quantum stage.[103]

**What follows:** demonstrated progress is broad experimental capability across graph problems, finance, logistics, scheduling, and allocation—not a demonstrated, general quantum win over strong classical optimization on an operationally useful workload.[92][102][26][103]

A newer gate-based demonstration uses quantum-enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo (QeMCMC), which uses a quantum routine to help sample candidate solutions within a Markov-chain search, together with parallel tempering, a classical technique for escaping difficult energy landscapes. IBM researchers reported solving a 123-node Maximum Independent Set problem on IBM hardware using 123 qubits. Maximum Independent Set asks for the largest set of graph nodes with no links between any two selected nodes.[104] The authors describe this as a capability demonstration on a hard optimization problem and identify larger devices and improved error rates as the path toward quantum advantage in optimization.[104]

Hybrid demonstrations are also reaching application-specific formulations. In 2026, Allstate and IBM reported a hybrid workflow for a chance-constrained knapsack problem in insurance underwriting: choose the most profitable portfolio of policies while remaining within a risk-and-loss limit. A knapsack problem selects items subject to a capacity limit; here the limit is a probabilistic risk budget. The workflow ran a knapsack-specific QAOA circuit on an IBM Heron processor to generate candidate portfolios, then used classical post-processing to repair candidates that exceeded the risk budget and feed information from successful portfolios into later quantum rounds.[105] This demonstrates a quantum component within an underwriting optimization workflow.[105]

Quantum annealing hardware has also progressed in scale and connectivity. On 20 May 2025, D-Wave made its Advantage2_system1.1 annealing solver available through its Leap service. Advantage2 has more than 4,400 qubits, more than 40,000 couplers—programmable links between qubits—and a Zephyr topology in which each qubit connects to 20 others, compared with 15 on the previous Advantage processor.[106] D-Wave benchmarked this system on tunable-precision three-dimensional lattice spin glasses and reported improved solution quality across its test scenarios; in some cases it obtained better-quality solutions with anneal times several orders of magnitude shorter than on the previous-generation Advantage system.[107]

### What practical applications in optimization problems remain proposed or unproven?

The proposed applications are extensive. Quantum methods have been studied for vehicle routing, job-shop scheduling, bin packing, transit-facility allocation, power-system unit commitment, railway conflict management, portfolio optimization, customer-data applications, and cash handling.[83] Their potential value is straightforward: a better route, schedule, allocation, or risk-adjusted portfolio can save time, fuel, capital, or scarce capacity.

However, a candidate application and a practical deployment are different achievements. The 2024 heterogeneous vehicle-routing study is especially clear. It encoded routes and vehicle capacities in one Ising Hamiltonian, but its QAOA results came from exact, noise-free state-vector simulations of 11-, 19-, and 21-qubit cases—at most three customers and two trucks.[6] Even in that idealised setting, the authors found performance was not competitive with modern classical heuristics, which can solve routing instances with more than 1,000 customers; they estimated that a quantum computer addressing that scale would need at least millions of controllable qubits.[6]

In that London routing study, QAOA was reliable only through four-customer, 16-qubit subproblems, and its accepted results did not improve the classical baseline: fleet distance rose by 24.63% and 2.58% in two cases.[137]

The limitation was not merely hardware scale. In the routing simulations, QAOA shifted probability toward lower-energy answers but failed to reliably distinguish among feasible routes with different costs. A valid route is not necessarily a good route, and a method that samples the worst and best valid routes equally is not useful for the stated optimization objective.[6] Similarly, increasing QAOA depth can improve solution quality in principle, but the deeper circuit has more gates and therefore more exposure to noise.[7]

Some narrower algorithmic comparisons are promising but should be read at their stated scope. Warm-started QAOA with custom mixers was reported to computationally beat the Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut result at low circuit depth, while recursive QAOA was reported to outperform original QAOA on complete graphs.[83] These are advances in algorithm design and in comparisons between specified methods or graph classes; they are not demonstrations that quantum hardware beats the best classical solvers on large operational workloads.

The relevant standard is **end-to-end practical advantage**: a quantum-plus-classical workflow must return a useful classical decision, on realistic input data, with solution quality, reliability, time, and cost better than the best applicable conventional alternative. An end-to-end assessment of a proposed quantum interior-point method for portfolio optimization illustrates the gap. For a simulated portfolio with 100 assets, the method was estimated to require 8 million logical qubits, T-depth of 2 times 10 to the 24th power, and T-count of 7 times 10 to the 29th power; even an optimistic GHz rate for T-gate layers implied millions of years of runtime.[108] This does not condemn all quantum portfolio methods, but it rules out this formulation as currently practical.

Quantum linear-system algorithms and quantum interior-point methods have been proposed for second-order cone programming, semidefinite programming, portfolio optimization, network-flow-like subroutines and power-grid optimization. A quantum linear-system algorithm outputs a quantum state proportional to A⁻¹b rather than an automatically readable solution vector, and requires procedures to encode the matrix A and prepare b.[119]

**What follows:** quantum optimization has progressed from mathematical mappings to hardware experiments and industrially framed case studies, but its prospective benefits in routing, scheduling, energy, finance, and allocation remain conditional on outperforming mature classical optimization workflows end to end.[26][108]

The range of proposed operational uses continues to expand. QuEra lists clinical-trial optimization—selecting cohorts and parameters to improve trial success and reduce delays—portfolio optimization balancing return, risk and liquidity, and manufacturing process optimization that schedules production to raise throughput and reduce downtime.[109] These examples illustrate that the same optimization techniques could support decisions in pharmaceutical development, finance, and production, not only graph benchmarks.[109]

A practical development path is to test small, relevant instances against classical approaches, co-design and simulate the application, validate it on quantum systems under operating conditions, and then estimate the resources and performance required for life-sized problems.[109] This sequence separates a successful small-instance experiment from scaled deployment.[109]

The 156-variable HSQC study is also explicitly a benchmark rather than an industrial deployment. Its authors state that adapting the workflow to industrially relevant problems and to particular hardware and classical solvers remains future work.[110]

A proposed benefit may be to find a better feasible plan in a fixed time, find an acceptable plan faster, or sample several useful near-optimal plans for a human or classical system to assess. Longer-term fault-tolerant quantum algorithms may speed up selected optimization-related tasks, but there is no general result showing that quantum computers efficiently solve every practically hard optimization problem.

For NP-hard optimization problems, efficient exact solutions are not expected in the worst case; investigated quantum targets instead include structured instance families, average-case improvements and better approximations.[138][139]

### What still limits practical use in optimization problems?

The first limit is **reliability**. Current processors suffer gate errors, decoherence—the loss of delicate quantum states—and readout errors. In the Sycamore QAOA experiment, average two-qubit gate fidelity was 99.4% and average per-qubit readout fidelity was 95.9%; errors become more damaging as circuits get deeper and as routing operations are added to accommodate a problem whose connections do not match the chip’s layout.[3] Error mitigation can improve estimates or samples, but it adds repetitions and classical processing rather than removing the underlying hardware limit.[111][50]

The second limit is **connectivity and embedding**. An annealer’s physical qubits are connected only in a fixed hardware graph. If a model requires interactions absent from that graph, one logical variable may need to be represented by a chain of several physical qubits—a process called **minor embedding**. This consumes qubits and can degrade answers when chains break or when chain strength is poorly chosen.[112] D-Wave documentation gives a simple example: a fully connected five-variable problem requires six physical qubits on an Advantage processor because one logical variable must become a two-qubit chain.[112]

The third limit is **model growth**. Operational optimization contains constraints—capacities, time windows, precedence relationships, budgets, fairness rules, and legal requirements. Converting a mixed-integer model into QUBO often increases the number of variables, the density of interactions, and the range of coefficients.[98] Constraint penalties can also create invalid solutions if they are inadequately designed; basic QAOA may sample invalid answers for problems with hard constraints, motivating specialised constraint-preserving mixers.[7]

A quantum optimization method also needs an efficiently implementable cost-function oracle—a circuit that evaluates or encodes its objective and constraints. Realistic constraints can make this circuit a bottleneck when they require slack variables or dense interactions.[139]

The fourth limit is the quality of the classical comparison. Classical optimization is not brute force alone: it includes exact methods, mixed-integer programming, constraint programming, local search, metaheuristics, and highly tuned application software. Classical interior-point methods can solve portfolios with thousands of stocks reasonably quickly on standard computing resources, while modern routing heuristics can handle more than 1,000 customers.[108][6] A quantum result against random guessing, a weak heuristic, or another quantum algorithm therefore does not decide practical value.

Relevant classical comparisons can also include branch-and-bound, decomposition, dynamic programming and machine-learning-guided heuristics, rather than brute-force search alone.[139]

Finally, total workflow cost matters. A 2025 transport review found that no reviewed study reported an end-to-end time spanning submission, quantum processing, and classical decoding; in two annealing studies, embedding, coefficient scaling, and decoding took longer than quantum sampling, sometimes by a factor of two.[47] The same review found that virtually all empirical transport demonstrations remained proof-of-concept instances and that none validated quantum-optimized schedules or signal plans in a live operational environment.[47]

**What follows:** the decisive test is not whether a quantum device can return a low-energy bit string, but whether the complete quantum–classical system produces reliably better feasible decisions than a strong classical solver after encoding, queueing, repeated sampling, decoding, and operational constraints are counted.[108][47][98]

Variational algorithms such as QAOA can also encounter barren plateaus: regions of the parameter landscape in which the measured signal or gradient is too small to tell the classical optimizer how to improve the circuit. Repeated circuit evaluations, cloud queueing, and data-transfer overhead can therefore make a hybrid workflow slow even if each individual quantum circuit is short.

Fault-tolerant quantum computing could reduce hardware-noise limitations, but it would not automatically remove the cost of formulating the problem or establish a speedup for a particular operational use case.

### Synthesis

Judgment — Quantum optimization is progressing in breadth faster than in proven economic usefulness. Quantum annealers, gate-based QAOA, photonic processors, and hybrid systems can now encode and run small-to-moderate optimization benchmarks across logistics, finance, scheduling, graph problems, and energy applications. The strongest current evidence is for experimental feasibility, parameter-tuning methods, and selected benchmark improvements.[3][102][111][47]

The limiting pattern is equally consistent: realistic problems expand during encoding, present hardware introduces noise and connectivity overhead, and classical solvers remain formidable competitors. Hybrid workflows are therefore the practical near-term route, but their value must be measured as a whole workflow rather than credited to a quantum subroutine alone.[98][3][103] The field’s direction is clear—toward better encodings, lower-error processors, error mitigation, and fair benchmarks—while a general practical quantum advantage for optimization remains unproven.[98][50][103]

## What the progress means

### How is quantum computing progressing toward practical applications in drug

Judgment — Quantum computing is progressing on two tracks that must be kept separate. First, engineers are making quantum processors larger, faster, and more reliable. Second, scientists and companies are testing where those processors might improve a real workflow. Progress on the first track is real; practical value on the second differs sharply among drug discovery, cryptography, and optimization.

A quantum computer processes information with **quantum bits (qubits)**. Unlike an ordinary bit, which is either 0 or 1, a qubit can be prepared in a quantum state that combines possible outcomes. Quantum gates manipulate those states, and measurement produces ordinary data at the end. Real devices can run such circuits: IBM’s learning material, for example, shows a two-qubit Bell-state circuit executed on hardware, with the expected correlated results most frequent but with additional outcomes caused by noise and **decoherence**—loss of quantum information through unwanted interaction with the environment.[11]

The field calls current devices **Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ)** computers: processors with useful numbers of physical qubits but without full error correction. Their defining constraint is that operations are imperfect. Circuit depth—the largest number of sequential gates along a route through a circuit—therefore matters: shallow circuits accumulate fewer errors than deep ones. Hardware connectivity also matters because two qubits that need to interact may not be directly connected, requiring extra routing gates that increase depth and error exposure.[11][83]

This means that a headline qubit count is not the same as usable computing power. IBM’s documentation shows 100-qubit GHZ-state experiments, but reports that the correlation signal falls with qubit separation because of noise. IBM also describes Nighthawk r2 as having 120 programmable qubits, 458 physical qubits, and accurate results on circuits exceeding 7,500 two-qubit operations; these are meaningful hardware metrics, but they do not by themselves show an advantage in drug discovery, cryptography, or optimization.[38][28]

**The central measure is practical quantum advantage:** outperforming the best available conventional method on a real problem with known scientific or commercial relevance. That definition is much stricter than showing that a quantum circuit works, or even that it exceeds brute-force classical simulation on a specially designed benchmark.[31][29] NIST similarly notes that early demonstrations have not yet proved truly useful and that classical computers have sometimes matched or exceeded quantum processors on particular tasks.[8]

The main route beyond NISQ is **fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC)**. Fault tolerance encodes one error-protected **logical qubit** across many imperfect **physical qubits**, repeatedly detects errors, and corrects them fast enough that a long algorithm can finish reliably. This is not a cosmetic upgrade: algorithms for chemically demanding simulation and cryptographically significant factoring require deep circuits and long calculations that current uncorrected processors cannot sustain.[83][10][1][8]

There is genuine movement toward that goal. Google’s Willow surface-code-memory experiment reported a 101-qubit distance-7 code with a logical error rate of 0.143% ± 0.003% per error-correction cycle. Increasing code distance by two suppressed logical error by a factor of 2.14 ± 0.02, and the study also demonstrated real-time decoding on a 72-qubit processor with a 1.1-microsecond correction cycle.[1]

The same research quantifies why this is progress rather than completion. Its extrapolation estimated that a logical error rate of 10 to the minus 6 would require a distance-27 logical qubit using 1,457 physical qubits, while syndrome measurements and decoding demands rise sharply as codes grow. The processor also suffered six unexplained correlated error bursts over 2 × 10¹⁰ correction cycles; these bursts affected neighborhoods of about 30 qubits and remain an obstacle to large fault-tolerant circuits.[1]

The result is a useful **capability gap**: quantum processors can now execute increasingly complex, error-managed calculations, while many high-value applications require thousands of logical qubits and billions of reliable operations. Progress can close this gap from both sides—better hardware reduces the number of physical qubits needed per logical qubit, while better algorithms and compilation reduce the logical resources required.[17][39][23] The sections on drug discovery, cryptography, and optimization should therefore be read as different tests of how far that gap still matters.

#### Drug discovery: direct scientific rationale, but early practical use

Drug discovery has perhaps the most natural long-term rationale for quantum computing because molecules are quantum systems. Their chemical behaviour depends on electrons: how electrons are distributed, correlated, transferred, and polarized determines bond formation, reactions, conformations, and molecular interactions. Classical computational chemistry already provides powerful tools, including molecular dynamics, machine learning, docking, density-functional theory, and high-level wavefunction methods. But some electron-correlation problems become expensive as molecular systems grow; coupled-cluster calculations, for example, can scale as the seventh power of electron number or worse.[23][57][39]

Quantum computers might eventually help by simulating the electronic structure of a selected molecular region more directly. **Electronic structure** means the arrangement and behaviour of electrons in a molecule or material. A likely quantum role is not to simulate an entire drug-development programme, or even a whole protein–drug system, on a quantum chip. It is to calculate a difficult local quantity—such as an electronic energy, a reaction barrier, protonation state, or metal-centre correlation—then send that result into a classical computer-aided drug-design workflow.[53][23][39]

The leading near-term chemistry method is the **Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)**. VQE is a hybrid quantum–classical algorithm: a quantum processor prepares a parameterized trial quantum state and measures its energy, while a classical optimizer adjusts the parameters repeatedly to search for the lowest-energy state. This design aims to use circuits shallow enough for NISQ hardware, but it requires many measurements and a classical optimization loop.[70][57][39]

Quantum chemistry has moved beyond pure theory. Quantum computers have calculated energies of small molecules, and hardware experiments have performed molecular-energy calculations including LiH and BeH2. A 2017 VQE experiment optimized Hamiltonian problems with up to six qubits and more than 100 Pauli terms, determining ground-state energies up to BeH2; its measured results agreed with a classical noise model of the device.[8][2]

More recent work has extended the range of molecular subproblems. Quantum-hardware studies have estimated protonation-state energetics in model fragments related to staphylococcal nuclease and α-lactalbumin, and have mapped dissociation behaviour in strongly correlated small-molecule test systems. IBM and RIKEN also used quantum hardware together with classical computing to model the electronic structure of iron sulfides, while Cleveland Clinic researchers studied intermolecular interactions on an IBM Quantum System One as a possible foundation for future drug-discovery and design work.[23][29]

These achievements matter because they test the core scientific premise: a quantum processor can contribute to a chemistry calculation that a classical workflow can use. They also show the current scale. Published NISQ demonstrations highlighted in a 2026 drug-discovery review remain toy systems such as H2, LiH, and BeH2, typically with active spaces of 12–16 orbitals mapped to 12–16 qubits.[39]

A **hybrid workflow** is already the practical model. Classical systems handle molecular representations, geometry optimization, large-scale screening, pose generation, scoring, molecular-dynamics propagation, sampling, and validation. Quantum processors are tested on carefully selected subroutines: local electronic descriptors, molecular-energy corrections, limited docking choices, niche machine-learning layers, or modest combinatorial encodings.[53][23][57]

This division of labour is important in molecular dynamics. A full molecular-dynamics simulation follows atoms through time and generally requires long trajectories, solvent treatment, temperature control, and repeated sampling. Current proposals place a quantum calculation only in an electronically difficult local region—such as bond breaking, metal coordination, proton transfer, or strong polarization—while classical engines propagate the rest of the system.[53]

Several hardware demonstrations illustrate this pattern:

- **Ground-state molecular dynamics:** Fedorov and colleagues used VQE electronic energies on IBM hardware while propagating H2 nuclei classically on the resulting potential-energy surface. The demonstration was a proof of concept for hydrogen, with numerical gradients. [53]

- **Annealer-assisted dynamics:** a D-Wave 2000Q annealer, followed by classical greedy post-processing, propagated H2 vibrational trajectories in harmonic, anharmonic, and dissociative regimes. The system used 2,048 physical qubits and up to 64 fully connected logical qubits, but the calculation addressed trajectory propagation rather than complete electronic-structure dynamics. [53]

- **Excited-state dynamics:** restricted VQE methods modeled H2 and CH2NH trajectories. The simulations agreed with exact adiabatic results except near a conical intersection, a region where electronic states come close together and the method became effectively diabatic. [53][18]

Drug-design researchers are also testing quantum optimization and quantum machine learning. A modified Grover search was run on a simulator and real quantum hardware for binding-site localization before docking. QAOA and DC-QAOA have been used in simulation for tiny docking-pose-selection problems reduced to 6, 8, or 12 qubits.[53] These are genuine algorithmic demonstrations, but they address reduced upstream tasks rather than whole-library screening or complete prediction of how a drug binds.

The most concrete drug-discovery example in the findings is a hybrid generative pipeline for KRAS inhibitors. It combined a quantum circuit Born machine prior with a classical LSTM molecular generator and Chemistry42 reward and filtering stages. The workflow generated one million compounds per model, synthesized the top 15, and tested them with surface-plasmon-resonance and cell assays; a later account reports that two molecules showed promising activity.[53][23]

This is progress from an algorithmic prototype to laboratory testing. The quantum component was one part of a larger hybrid system, and the review notes that the workflow remained biased toward known KRAS chemistry and docking-based proxy measures. **It shows that quantum-assisted candidate generation can reach synthesis and assay, not that quantum computing has yet improved the probability of discovering a drug relative to the strongest classical alternatives.**[53]

A 2024 Scientific Reports study provides an equally useful practical lesson. Its hybrid workflow modelled a covalent bond between Sotorasib and mutant KRAS(G12C), using a small five-atom quantum-mechanical region. The first 1,600 quantum-computer simulation steps followed the classical QM/MM baseline closely, and the simulated covalent bond remained near 1.86 Å with a standard deviation below 0.1 Å.[56][60]

The same study did not report a speed benefit from the quantum calculation. For four active spaces, VQE wall times were 424, 63, 161, and 424 seconds, compared with 358, 3, 97, and 360 seconds for the corresponding classical CASCI calculations. Its 1,600-step hardware-involved simulation took 3,820.0 minutes, versus 124.0 minutes for the classical simulation and 126.0 minutes for noiseless quantum emulation.[56]

That comparison identifies the current near-term objective: validate a quantum subroutine’s physical and chemical output before expecting it to accelerate the workflow. The study’s authors described the first 1,600 hardware steps as a sanity check, and the results support feasibility for a limited hybrid calculation.[60][56] The cost is that hardware execution, measurement repetition, queueing, and classical control can currently outweigh any theoretical advantage.

Measurement is a major constraint in chemistry. Quantum algorithms estimate energies statistically by repeating circuits many times, or **shots**. Required shot counts rise sharply as the desired precision tightens: one chemistry review states that the number of measurements required for accuracy ε scales as ε to the minus 2.[70] For a projected 630-orbital molecular calculation, the 2024 hybrid-pipeline study estimated around 300,000 Hamiltonian terms, roughly 100 million shots for 0.01-Hartree precision, and 100,000 seconds on one quantum processor for the energy measurement alone under its assumptions.[60]

Drug-relevant chemistry also requires more than a single energy calculation. Binding affinity is a free-energy problem involving multiple conformations, protonation states, tautomers, solvent effects, and protein flexibility. A quantum result for one molecular geometry can become useful as a high-accuracy local electronic reference, but it must be combined with classical molecular dynamics or free-energy perturbation to estimate a complete binding affinity.[39][70]

Fault-tolerant estimates show why researchers still pursue the field. For a full 50-orbital CYP3A4 active-space calculation, one 2026 review reports earlier estimates of 2,000–10,000 logical qubits and 10⁸–10¹⁰ T gates, while newer compilation methods may reduce the T-gate count by one or two orders of magnitude. The same review still says millions of physical qubits would be required and places the calculation beyond 2030.[39]

Other resource analyses show similar algorithmic progress without current execution. For an active-space calculation involving Ibrutinib bound to Bruton’s tyrosine kinase, an ACS perspective estimated a change from more than 1,000 years using Trotterization to a few days using sparse qubitization on a sufficiently large error-corrected quantum computer. Sparse qubitization is an algorithmic method designed to simulate a molecular Hamiltonian with far fewer costly logical operations; the result is a projection for a future machine, not a completed drug calculation.[55]

The immediate implication is clear. Drug discovery is the closest of the three areas to a scientifically natural quantum application because molecular electronic structure is itself quantum mechanical, and the field has already demonstrated molecular subcalculations, hybrid pipelines, and experimentally tested generated candidates. But industrial use depends on repeatable gains at actual decision points—better ranking, calibration, or reduction in downstream synthesis and assay burden—against strong classical baselines and within realistic time and resource budgets.[23][53]

#### Cryptography: the practical application today is preparation, not quantum code-breaking

Cryptography differs fundamentally from drug discovery. The most consequential quantum-computing application is not better cryptographic software running on a quantum processor. It is **quantum cryptanalysis**: using a sufficiently large, fault-tolerant quantum computer to break some public-key cryptographic systems. Public-key cryptography uses a public key for encryption or signature verification and a private key for decryption or signature creation; RSA is a widely used example.[82]

The threat comes from **Shor’s algorithm**, which can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a sufficiently capable quantum computer. Factoring is central to RSA, while discrete logarithms underpin Diffie–Hellman, DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, and related systems.[75][71][113] In contrast, symmetric cryptography such as AES faces a different and less severe quantum effect: Grover’s algorithm provides at most a quadratic search speed-up rather than Shor’s transformative attack on public-key mathematics.[87][75]

Small demonstrations of quantum factoring exist. A trapped-ion experiment reported a scalable Kitaev-style implementation of Shor’s algorithm factoring 15, using seven active qubits and four cache qubits, with success probabilities above 90%. IBM’s current tutorial also runs the order-finding step for N = 15 on real hardware, where noise caused leakage into unwanted bitstrings.[4][72]

These demonstrations prove that the algorithmic components can run on hardware at very small scale. They do not approach cryptographic relevance: RSA commonly uses 2,048- to 4,096-bit keys.[72] **The gap is not between factoring nothing and factoring something; it is between factoring 15 and performing fault-tolerant arithmetic across the billions of logical operations needed for RSA-2048.**[4][45][5]

Resource estimates make this scale concrete. A 2025 estimate for factoring a 2,048-bit RSA integer projected fewer than one million physical qubits and less than a week, assuming a square nearest-neighbour layout, a 0.1% gate-error rate, a one-microsecond surface-code cycle, and a 10-microsecond control-system reaction time. Its logical-cost table estimated 1,399 logical qubits, 6.5 × 10⁹ Toffoli gates, and 9.2 expected shots; the proposed architecture’s physical-qubit estimate was 897,864 before rounding to one million.[5]

That is significant algorithmic progress from an earlier 2021 estimate of 20 million physical qubits and roughly eight hours for RSA-2048 factoring. It is not a forecast that an attack-capable machine already exists: the later estimate relies on specified hardware and error-correction assumptions, and its author states that the qubit count cannot plausibly be reduced to 100,000 without changing those assumptions.[89][5] The National Cyber Security Centre states that today’s machines remain limited and have relatively high error rates per operation, and that the date at which a large general-purpose quantum computer might emerge is hard to predict.[71]

Judgment — Cryptography therefore has the clearest theoretical quantum advantage and the least tolerance for approximation. A drug-design calculation can sometimes be useful if it improves an estimate or ranking; an optimization solver can be useful if it finds a good-enough feasible solution. A cryptanalytic attack must recover the relevant key or break the security property reliably enough to matter. That requirement pushes the main cryptographic threat firmly into the fault-tolerant era.

The practical consequence is already substantial because cryptographic migration takes years. The United States government’s 2024 report says that a **cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer (CRQC)** is not known to exist, while stating that one may emerge in the coming decade. It also distinguishes this uncertainty from the need to prepare now, because data encrypted today can be collected and decrypted later if a capable machine becomes available.[87][113]

The present application is **post-quantum cryptography (PQC)**. PQC consists of classical cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks from both conventional and future quantum computers. It is not cryptography performed by a quantum computer, and it is not the same as **quantum cryptography**, which uses quantum physical effects for security.[77][73]

NIST released its first three finalized PQC standards in August 2024: FIPS 203 for ML-KEM key establishment, FIPS 204 for ML-DSA digital signatures, and FIPS 205 for SLH-DSA digital signatures. NIST says these algorithms are ready for use.[77][71] The standards result from a public process begun in December 2016 that received 82 candidate submissions, admitted 69 to the first round, and selected four algorithms for initial standardization after successive review rounds.[87]

PQC is already entering operational internet infrastructure. Major providers including Google and Cloudflare have supported X25519 plus ML-KEM-768 hybrid key establishment in TLS 1.3. A hybrid method combines a conventional and a post-quantum scheme so that an attacker must break both; NIST also describes this approach as more conservative but more computationally demanding.[114][115] One reported early-2024 measure from Cloudflare put PQC-secured TLS 1.3 connections at nearly 2%.[116]

OpenSSH has offered post-quantum hybrid key agreement by default since April 2022.[140]

Migration remains an engineering and governance problem as much as a mathematics problem. PQC must fit protocols such as TLS, and organizations must handle larger keys and signatures, encryption and verification times, bandwidth, certificates, key management, software updates, and devices that cannot be upgraded.[113][87] For ML-KEM, NIST specifies parameter sets that trade increasing security strength for decreasing performance: ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768, and ML-KEM-1024.[76]

The scale is material. A 2024 White House report projected approximately $7.1 billion in 2024 dollars to migrate prioritized US federal information systems to PQC during 2025–2035, excluding national-security systems. It described this as a rough-order-of-magnitude estimate with high uncertainty and said replacing systems incompatible with PQC forms a significant part of the cost.[87]

NIST’s October 2026 transition roadmap, NISTIR 8547, gives a staged direction rather than predicting when a quantum computer will break encryption. National Security Memorandum 10 set 2035 as the primary federal migration target, while Executive Order 14412 requires high-value assets and high-impact systems to migrate key establishment by 2030 and digital signatures by 2031.[115] This makes cryptography the area with the strongest current practical response to quantum computing, even though the feared quantum computation itself has not been demonstrated at cryptographic scale.

Quantum communication is a separate path. **Quantum key distribution (QKD)** distributes keying material using quantum states. The US National Security Agency notes that QKD does not authenticate the source of a transmission, so authentication still requires asymmetric cryptography or preplaced keys; it considers quantum-resistant cryptography typically less expensive and easier to maintain for its security context.[78] The practical decision is therefore not simply quantum versus non-quantum security, but which security architecture solves the full operational problem.

#### Optimization: broad experimentation, no general practical advantage

Optimization means selecting the best solution from many possible choices while obeying constraints. Scheduling workers or machines, routing vehicles, selecting assets for a portfolio, assigning power generation, designing supply chains, and choosing molecular conformations are all optimization problems. The difficulty arises when the number of possible combinations grows so rapidly that exhaustive search is impossible.[50][8][47]

Quantum optimization has two prominent approaches. **Quantum annealing** encodes an optimization problem into an energy landscape and attempts to guide a physical system toward its lowest-energy configuration. **Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)** is a hybrid quantum–classical method in which a parameterized quantum circuit alternates problem-specific and mixing operations while a classical optimizer tunes the parameters.[35][7][36]

QAOA has been demonstrated on hardware. Google’s Sycamore experiment applied it to combinatorial optimization using 23 of a 54-qubit superconducting processor. In one 23-bit hardware-grid problem, the experiment used 175,000 measured bitstrings during parameter optimization; random sampling found a ground-state string with roughly 20% probability, while the cumulative probability after optimized sampling rose to nearly 100% for that particular instance and procedure.[3][48]

That result shows a quantum optimizer can improve its own search distribution on a physical device. It does not establish a general practical advantage, because optimization performance depends on instance structure, encoding, parameter training, sampling cost, hardware connectivity, and the strength of the classical comparator. IBM states directly that it remains unsettled which quantum methods and problem classes can deliver practical quantum advantage.[3][50]

The catalogue of investigated applications is wide:

- **Transport and scheduling:** quantum approaches have been studied for smart charging of electric vehicles, job-shop scheduling, vehicle routing, railway conflict management, transit-facility allocation, and car paint-shop sequencing. [83][26]

- **Industry and infrastructure:** research has examined unit commitment in microgrids, power-system optimization, logistics-network design, waste-transport planning, and supply-chain-related tasks. [83][26]

- **Finance and decisions:** quantum and hybrid methods have been tested for portfolio optimization, including real datasets and quantum annealing controls. [83][102]

- **Scientific and molecular tasks:** drug-design problems can be cast as optimization problems, including protein folding, conformational search, docking-pose selection, and molecular-structure optimization. [60][53][57]

This breadth is valuable because it identifies reusable formulations and bottlenecks. Yet broad experimentation is not the same as a general speed-up. Theoretical work states that quantum computers will not offer exponential speed-ups for every instance of every optimization problem; the best-known exponential example involves optimization reducible to integer factoring, which is unlikely to be an ordinary industrial workload.[50]

Classical optimization is also a demanding benchmark. Modern high-performance classical optimizers can find near-optimal heterogeneous vehicle-routing solutions with more than 1,000 customers, typically in polynomial or pseudo-polynomial time. A 2024 quantum-routing analysis estimated that a quantum computer would need at least millions of controllable qubits to address instances of that size.[6]

Small QAOA simulations show why scale is difficult. For a vehicle-routing formulation at QAOA depth p = 1, an 11-qubit instance generated valid bitstrings with about 3% probability; 19- and 21-qubit instances were about an order of magnitude lower. At depths up to p = 5, the chance of sampling the optimal tour was 10% for the 11-qubit problem and around 1% or below for the two larger cases.[6]

Hardware layout creates another constraint. Many optimization problems have dense interactions: a decision about one variable depends on many others. Superconducting chips generally connect each qubit only to nearby neighbours, so compilers add swap operations to move information around. Research on dense QAOA mappings shows that the best routing strategy depends on whether the chip uses a line, grid, heavy-hex, or three-dimensional grid layout.[49]

Quantum annealers face a related issue called **minor embedding**. If the mathematical optimization model requires interactions that the physical chip does not directly provide, one logical variable may be represented by a chain of physical qubits. D-Wave Advantage2 has more than 4,400 active qubits and 20-way native connectivity, but a transport-and-logistics review reports that embedding overhead commonly reduces effective problem sizes to a few thousand binary variables.[47]

Embedding can affect answer quality as well as scale. In one D-Wave documentation example for a 30-node signed social-network problem, 17.5% of 1,000 samples had at least one broken chain. Inadequately tuned chain strengths can make physical qubits intended to represent one logical variable disagree.[112][47]

Some application results are useful but narrow. A logistics-network-design study translated costs and constraints into an Ising problem and reported average error below 1% against published optima, plus fewer iterations than its classical comparators. A car paint-shop study using real-world-derived data found that quantum annealing hardware and hybrid algorithms produced adequate small-instance solutions but approached the performance of a simple greedy algorithm at larger sizes, across sequences of 10 to 3,000 cars.[26]

These examples point to the right near-term role: quantum optimization can be a component in a hybrid heuristic, particularly where a good feasible solution has value and the problem structure maps well to the hardware. They do not support treating quantum annealing or QAOA as a replacement for mature classical operations-research software.[102][50]

Hybrid results must be interpreted as hybrid results. In one portfolio-optimization benchmark, researchers first drew 1,000 forward-annealing samples, then used the best candidate as a starting state for 1,000 reverse-annealing samples. Selecting the better forward or reverse result for each of 100 problems improved estimated success probability by an order of magnitude for sizes 16–20; the improvement came from the combined selection rule, not reverse annealing alone.[102]

The same principle applies to gate-based algorithms. A 2025 comparative study used classical optimizers with up to 5,000 evaluations, 4,000 quantum shots per circuit execution, and classical multi-bit-swap post-processing. For multidimensional-knapsack cases, its PCE approach found feasible solutions across the listed instances using 6–10 qubits, but reported optimality gaps from 11.87% to 50.98%; feasibility and closeness to the optimum are different outcomes.[117]

Noise is particularly damaging in variational optimization because it affects both the quantum evaluation and the classical training loop. Error propagation in NISQ optimization is an active research topic, as are noise-induced barren plateaus—regions in which an optimizer receives an extremely weak gradient signal—and error-mitigation techniques.[83] The practical cost includes repeated circuit evaluations, shot counts, parameter searches, and compilation overhead, not only the time spent on the quantum chip.

Judgment — Optimization is therefore progressing through experimentation rather than convergence on a single winning quantum method. It has the broadest range of real-world-flavoured case studies because many business and scientific problems can be translated into QUBO, Ising, or graph formulations. It has the weakest general claim to advantage because strong classical solvers, problem-specific heuristics, and hardware-encoding overheads often determine performance more than the nominal quantum algorithm.

#### What practical progress means now

The three application areas have different readiness profiles. Drug discovery has the strongest scientific match between quantum hardware and the target problem: molecular electronic structure is quantum mechanical, and quantum processors have already executed small molecular and hybrid chemistry calculations. Cryptography has the clearest eventual disruptive algorithm—Shor’s—but practical quantum code-breaking remains far beyond present hardware; its immediate real-world impact is accelerating adoption of PQC. Optimization has the widest collection of pilots across transport, finance, energy, manufacturing, and molecular design, but no established general-purpose quantum advantage over leading classical solvers.[8][23][87][77][50][26]

The common constraint is reliability. Current devices must limit circuit depth, manage connectivity, repeat measurements, mitigate errors, and rely heavily on classical computation. Fault-tolerant logical qubits are the route to more consequential molecular simulation and cryptanalysis, while optimization may continue to depend on problem-specific hybrid methods even after hardware improves.[11][1][53][89][49]

The practical implication for a reader assessing claims is to ask four questions. First, was the result run on real quantum hardware, a classical simulator, or a projected fault-tolerant machine? Second, what exact problem size and quality target were used? Third, what classical baseline was compared on the same task? Fourth, did the quantum component improve a downstream decision, cost, runtime, or scientific measurement rather than only an intermediate benchmark?[23][53][31]

1. Treat current quantum computing as a specialized experimental coprocessor, not a replacement for classical computing. [8][53]
2. In drug discovery, prioritize narrowly scoped hybrid tests with strong chemical and decision-relevant classical baselines. [53][23]
3. In cryptography, prioritize inventory and migration to standardized PQC now; do not wait for a cryptographically relevant quantum computer to be built. [77][118][115]
4. In optimization, require end-to-end comparisons against modern classical solvers on operationally meaningful instances before claiming a practical quantum advantage. [50][98][6]

### Open questions

It remains open when fault-tolerant quantum computers will be large and reliable enough to deliver a validated drug-discovery result, break a deployed cryptographic key, or outperform the best classical optimisation methods on a valuable real-world workload. Those questions would be settled by reproducible end-to-end results that measure useful outcomes, runtime, accuracy and cost against the strongest relevant classical systems.
