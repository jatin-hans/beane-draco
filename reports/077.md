The best-supported verdict is a division of labour, not a methodological winner: process tracing is indispensable for establishing and explaining within-case mechanisms, while Pearl-style causal graphs are indispensable for making identification assumptions about intervention effects explicit.

Bennett’s Bayesian account and Mahoney’s Bayesian and set-theoretic work can discipline comparison among rival explanations, but neither is interchangeable with Pearl’s structural causal models. Across Skocpol’s revolutions, QCA applications, and mixed-methods research on democratic breakdown, the strongest designs specify alternative pathways, map treatment assignment and causal order, and use within-case evidence to test rather than merely narrate the proposed mechanism.

Since 2010, protocols have increasingly required case-selection justification, explicit treatment-selection analysis, causal graphs or equivalent causal-order diagrams, and avoidance of conditioning on variables caused by treatment. The remaining disagreement concerns whether qualitative mechanism evidence can validate causal identification or only probe its plausibility, and how far set-theoretic claims of necessity and sufficiency can travel beyond carefully bounded populations.

## Methodological tensions between process tracing and Bayesian approaches

A **causal mechanism** is a process through which agents with causal capacities operate in specific contexts to transfer energy, information, or matter to other entities; more generally, it is the theorized link that transmits causal forces from a cause to an outcome. Process tracing and causal inference address different parts of the same problem.[1] **Within-case evidence** is information from inside a particular historical or contemporary case—such as documents, interviews, observed decisions, and event sequences—used to assess how a proposed causal process operated.

**Cross-case analysis** compares observations across multiple units, commonly the rows of a dataset, to assess patterns of difference among cases. Process tracing asks how an outcome was produced in a particular case: what actors did, in what sequence, and through what causal process. Causal-inference frameworks ask what would have happened under a different intervention, and what assumptions permit an observed association to be interpreted as causal. **Equifinality** is the possibility that multiple paths or combinations of variables produce the same kind of outcome. A **confounder** is a prior common cause of a proposed exposure or treatment and its outcome. In Pearl’s graphical framework, this problem is represented as a back-door causal path from treatment to outcome; the simplest form is a variable causing both.[2] The tension matters because a detailed historical narrative can mistake a consequence for a cause, while a statistically identified effect can remain a black box about the process that generated it.[3][4][5][2]

**The central conclusion is that Bennett and Checkel (2021, 'Understanding Bayesianism: Fundamentals for process tracers') (2015, 'Process tracing: From metaphor to analytic tool')’s process tracing, Pearl’s causal-graphical framework, and Mahoney’s Bayesian and set-theoretic work are complementary but non-interchangeable tools.** Process tracing supplies evidence about mechanisms in cases; Pearl supplies formal rules for identifying intervention effects from a stated causal structure; Mahoney supplies a framework for evaluating causal-process evidence and alternative explanations.[6] None alone removes the need to state and defend causal assumptions.[5][7][8]

### process tracing as articulated by Andrew Bennett and Jeffrey Checkel

Bennett and Checkel define process tracing as within-case causal inference: analysis of evidence about processes, sequences, and conjunctions of events in order to develop or test hypotheses about causal mechanisms that explain an individual case. A causal mechanism is not simply an intervening variable placed between an independent and dependent variable. It is the productive process through which a cause generates an outcome in a particular context. Bennett defines such mechanisms as processes through which agents with causal capacities transfer energy, information, or matter to other entities.[3][1][5]

Operationally, process tracing begins by turning a causal explanation into observable implications. Researchers specify what must or should have happened if a proposed mechanism operated: which actors interacted, what information they possessed, what decisions they made, what sequence of events occurred, and what documentary, interview, archival, survey, or other traces those actions should have left. Bennett’s later formulation asks investigators to derive concrete, case-specific implications of every alternative theory, gather evidence, assess how likely that evidence would be under each theory, and update confidence in the alternatives.[9][6]

This is why process tracing is not equivalent to chronological narrative. A chronology records that event A preceded event B. A process-tracing argument must show why A plausibly generated B through specified intermediate steps, and why competing explanations would not predict the same evidence. Collier’s formulation captures the point: process tracing examines trajectories involving independent, dependent, and intervening variables, but the explanation fails if the phenomena at each step are not adequately described.[10][4]

Bennett and Checkel therefore treat causal-process observations, or CPOs, as the core evidentiary material. George and Bennett’s 2005 formulation calls process tracing an operational procedure for attempting to identify and verify observable within-case implications of causal mechanisms.[11] CPOs are diagnostic pieces of evidence about context, process, or mechanism.

They may be qualitative, such as cabinet memoranda or interview testimony, but they can also be numerical when a number bears on a particular step in a causal sequence. They contrast with data-set observations, or DSOs, which are rows in a cross-case dataset.[3][4][12]

Collier’s test typology, slightly adapting Bennett (2010), who builds on Van Evera (1997), distinguishes evidence by its diagnostic force.[10] In Collier’s (2011) four-test typology, which slightly adapts Bennett (2010) and builds on Van Evera (1997), a straw-in-the-wind test provides weak support or weak disconfirmation; a hoop test concerns evidence expected if a hypothesis is true, so failing it substantially weakens the hypothesis; a smoking-gun test strongly favors a hypothesis when it is observed; and a doubly decisive test both supports one explanation and rules out rivals.[10] These are not automatic labels attached to evidence. Their force depends on prior knowledge, the formulation of the hypothesis, and the auxiliary assumptions linking observation to theory.[10][4]

Bennett’s Bayesian presentation reframes this logic in terms of likelihoods. An observation is probative when it is much more likely if one explanation is true than if competing explanations are true. Bayesian reasoning thus does not oppose process tracing. It gives process tracers a language for asking how much an observation should update belief in competing explanations, given prior knowledge and the observation’s expectedness under each explanation.[13][14][6]

Fairfield and Charman (2022, 'Social inquiry and Bayesian inference: Rethinking qualitative research') (2018, 'The Bayesian foundations of iterative research in qualitative social science: A dialogue with the data') (Fairfield and Charman, 2019)’s explicit Bayesian process-tracing procedure sets out three steps: first, specify hypotheses and prior probabilities; second, identify evidence; third, assess likelihoods or likelihood ratios under rival hypotheses.[6][15] It recommends comparing concrete rival hypotheses, not a favored explanation against an undifferentiated logical negation. It also requires attention to evidentiary dependence: two interviews repeating the same account are not necessarily two independent confirmations.[9]

Bennett’s use of Bayesian reasoning is therefore principally epistemic rather than statistical. It concerns how an investigator should compare explanations of a case under incomplete information. It does not turn process tracing into a regression estimator, nor does it require every qualitative judgment to be assigned a precise numerical probability. Explicit Bayesian analysis can be useful for identifying where disagreement lies—in priors, likelihoods, background assumptions, or readings of evidence—while numerical probabilities in qualitative research can create false precision.[9][6]

Checkel shares the basic within-case, mechanism-centered definition. He describes deductive process tracing as testing a mechanism theory’s observable implications within a case, and inductive process tracing as using within-case evidence to develop new explanatory hypotheses. He stresses that process tracing is meaningful only where a study has hypothesized mechanisms whose observable implications can be measured.[8][16][17]

Checkel’s distinctive emphasis is on the production of the evidence itself. Archives, interviews, ethnography, surveys, fieldwork, document analysis, and discourse analysis generate the data used to trace mechanisms; Bayesian logic, set theory, and causal graphs do not replace those methods. His recent work argues that process-tracing transparency should disclose not only the material consulted but also how the researcher’s identity, question wording, power position, and relationship with interviewees shaped the evidence produced.[8][16]

This concern is not merely ethical. If an interviewee’s statement is affected by the interviewer’s positionality, incentives, or access conditions, its evidentiary weight cannot be assessed solely by counting it as corroboration. Fairfield and Charman make a compatible point: testimonial evidence should be defined as the fact that a particular source made a particular statement, and its likelihood should be assessed under rival hypotheses in light of the source’s knowledge and incentives.[9][8]

Checkel accepts that Bayesian reasoning can help researchers identify evidence that would discriminate among rival explanations. He does not accept that it supplies a complete process-tracing method. In his account, Bayesianism concerns the analysis of already collected qualitative evidence; it gives no instruction about which fieldwork, interview, archival, or ethnographic practice will generate credible mechanism evidence.[8]

Checkel also criticizes explicit numerical Bayesianism. He argues that Bayesian updating requires researchers to assign a prior probability and likelihoods for observing the evidence if a theory is true or false, and that these estimates are difficult to make transparent. He further argues that deductively specified priors sit uneasily with inductive process tracing, where the explanation is developed through engagement with the case rather than fixed in advance.[8][16]

This is an important internal disagreement, not a dispute between process tracing and Bayesian logic as such. Bennett treats Bayesianism as clarifying the strengths and limits of case-study inference. Fairfield and Charman argue that formalization can discipline judgment and localize disagreements. Checkel agrees that evidence should discriminate among alternatives, but maintains that numerical formalization offers limited improvement if it distracts from evidence collection, source criticism, and reflexivity.[13][18][9][16]

#### Causal mechanisms: productive process versus structural function

The principal methodological tension concerns what counts as a mechanism. For Bennett and Checkel, a mechanism is ordinarily a productive process involving actors, institutions, decisions, information, and events. The researcher should reconstruct that process at a level of detail sufficient to test whether it operated. This focus is designed to bridge structural context and agency: a state structure, class relation, or institutional rule shapes opportunities and constraints, but actors’ actions help transmit those effects to the outcome.[5][1][22]

For Pearl, a mechanism is a stable relation that determines a variable from its direct causes and omitted disturbances. A structural causal model represents the relation through equations, while a directed acyclic graph, or DAG, represents the model’s causal structure. Each arrow asserts a direct functional relation; each missing arrow is an exclusion assumption that a direct causal relationship does not exist.[7]

The difference is substantial. A process tracer may ask how a party leader exploited polarization, which coalition shifted, and why institutions failed to constrain the executive. A Pearl-style model asks which variables are direct causes of which others, what causal paths connect treatment to outcome, and how an intervention changing one variable would alter the distribution of the outcome. The first produces a historically thick account of causal production; the second produces a formal intervention model.[6][7]

Judgment — **Neither definition is reducible to the other.** A DAG can represent a mechanism at an abstract level, but it does not by itself establish that the entities and activities constituting that mechanism occurred in a particular case. Conversely, an event sequence can demonstrate an intelligible process while leaving unclear whether the treatment effect is identified against confounding across a comparison population.

The two approaches do meet around counterfactual reasoning. Pearl’s intervention operator distinguishes observation from action: conditioning asks what is associated with X in the world as observed, while do(X) asks what would happen if X were imposed by intervention. Process tracing also becomes more credible when it asks whether each link in a proposed sequence made a difference, rather than merely whether every link occurred. Runhardt’s 2015 critique makes the latter demand explicit: evidence that intervening events were present should be supplemented by counterfactual evidence that each link was genuinely causal, using tentative comparisons with other case studies.[7][23]

For actor-centered process tracing, the counterfactual inquiry includes whether actors faced genuine alternatives and whether a different decision or event was genuinely possible.[98][99][100]

The process-tracing literature has increasingly moved toward this stricter standard. Waldner’s completeness standard combines causal graphs, event-history maps, and invariant causal mechanisms. An event-history map records the sequence of events; a graph specifies the claimed causal structure; and an invariant mechanism is one expected to operate similarly under stated conditions. This standard is a bridge to graphical causal inference because it forces researchers to distinguish a narrative chronology from a causal model.[24][14][25]

The bridge does not erase the difference in inferential target. Beach (2017, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Achieving Methodological Alignment When Combining QCA and Process tracing in Practice') and Pedersen (2016, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Selecting Appropriate Cases When Tracing Causal Mechanisms') distinguish mechanistic evidence—evidence that a process operated in a particular case—from difference-making evidence—evidence, usually from variance-based or experimental designs, that changing a cause changes an outcome across units.[26][27] Mechanistic evidence can increase confidence in an explanation of a case, but it does not alone estimate an average treatment effect or establish cross-case difference-making.[1]

This distinction matters for comparative politics. A study can establish that a democratic government attacked courts through a recognizable mechanism in one country without proving that electoral victory by a particular kind of party has the same average effect on judicial independence across all democracies. Conversely, a regression can estimate a cross-national association between polarization and democratic regression without showing that polarization worked through the same mechanism in every country.[1][28][2]

#### Equifinality: multiple roads to the same outcome

Equifinality is the possibility that multiple causal paths or combinations of variables produce the same outcome. Bennett and Checkel identify it as a central problem of process tracing because finding an expected mechanism in an outcome-positive case does not establish that it was the only cause, the dominant cause, or the mechanism that operated in comparable cases.[5][20][8]

Bennett’s response is to make alternative explanations explicit. Competing explanations need not be monocausal, mutually exclusive in their variables, or entirely separate. They may share variables but differ over the primary cause, sequencing, interaction, or functional relationship among the same variables. A researcher must therefore ask not merely whether evidence fits the favored mechanism, but whether it is more likely under that mechanism than under alternatives.[6][29]

The advantage is that equifinality becomes a research design problem rather than a reason to abandon causal explanation. Researchers can select outcome-positive cases to identify alternative paths, positive-negative cases to examine scope conditions, and negative-positive cases to investigate pathways that do not involve the focal independent variable. Bennett explicitly treats the latter as cases that reveal routes to the outcome other than the focal cause.[6]

Checkel makes the same point more directly: social causation contains multiple mechanisms, one or more of which may generate the same outcome. He argues that testing for equifinality requires more explicit process tracing. Evidence that establishes one plausible mechanism does not dispense with the need to test alternatives that might generate the same result.[8][16]

Bayesian process tracing offers a disciplined way to do this when hypotheses can be specified adequately. It compares the likelihood of evidence under alternatives, and it warns researchers not to count dependent observations as separate evidence. Yet the method faces a practical problem when explanations operate simultaneously or interact. A simple Bayesian comparison often assumes mutually exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses; real-world mechanisms may combine. Modeling the joint possibilities preserves logical coherence but expands the hypothesis space and makes likelihood assessment harder.[9]

Judgment — **This is the central trade-off in Bayesian process tracing.** Mutually exclusive hypotheses make comparison tractable, but conjunctural causation—the joint operation of several causes—often describes comparative politics more accurately. The appropriate response is not to force a complex case into one exclusive story. It is to compare explicitly specified configurations: mechanism A alone, mechanism B alone, A plus B, and neither A nor B, where theory and evidence make those possibilities substantively plausible.

Set-theoretic methods, especially qualitative comparative analysis, or QCA, are designed around this kind of conjunctural causation. Barrenechea and Mahoney’s 《A Set-Theoretic Approach to Bayesian Process Tracing》 was first published online on July 25, 2017 and appeared in the August 2019 issue of Sociological Methods & Research.[30] They treat outcomes as potentially produced by different sufficient combinations of conditions rather than by a single additive causal effect.

Mahoney’s work with Barrenechea on set-theoretic Bayesian process tracing belongs to this formalization strand, which Checkel distinguishes from causal graphs. It should not be conflated with Pearl’s DAG framework.[30][8]

Mahoney’s process-tracing logic also accommodates equifinality. Collier’s account of auxiliary outcomes follows Mahoney (2010): these outcomes lie outside the main causal sequence but can provide additional inferential leverage for a hypothesized independent variable or intervening mechanism.[10] In the causal-sequence framework, researchers identify independent variables, an outcome, and mechanisms linking them.

Mahoney’s historically bounded counterfactual analysis also distinguishes SUIN-condition claims alongside necessary-condition, sufficient-condition, and INUS-condition claims; SUIN and INUS describe ways a factor contributes within a conjunction.[98][101][102]

Mahoney recognizes a trade-off between a historical counterfactual’s causal importance and its plausibility.[98][99][100]

Mahoney’s configurational counterfactual asks whether changing a condition would negate a claimed necessary or sufficient relationship within a specified configuration; his framework distinguishes SUIN as well as INUS claims.[98][102]

When different causes may generate the same outcome, the task is to establish, with high certainty, that each proposed cause or path produced that outcome. Auxiliary outcomes—consequences outside the main causal sequence—can add leverage because rival explanations may not predict them.[12][10]

Pearl’s framework handles a related but different problem. A DAG can represent multiple direct causes, mediated pathways, and interactions through structural equations. It can therefore encode multiple routes to the same outcome. But Pearl’s key warning is epistemic: the same observational distribution can be compatible with distinct causal structures. Two DAGs are observationally equivalent when they have the same skeleton and v-structures; probabilities alone cannot orient every causal arrow.[7]

Pearl’s notion of observational equivalence resembles equifinality only in the broad sense that multiple causal accounts can fit the same observed evidence. The concepts should not be collapsed. Equifinality is typically an ontological claim that several causal paths genuinely can produce Y. Observational equivalence is an epistemic claim that observed associations do not distinguish among rival causal structures. The first concerns causal complexity in the world; the second concerns limits of inference from observational data.[12][7]

The practical implication is sharp. Process tracing can investigate whether distinct mechanisms operated in France, Russia, China, Hungary, Poland, or the United States. A causal graph can show whether observed variables and temporal ordering suffice to identify one mechanism or effect. Neither the presence of a common outcome nor the fit of a cross-case model selects a unique causal pathway.[1][7]

Process tracers have responded by placing greater weight on causal homogeneity and scope conditions. Beach and Pedersen argue that the same cause and outcome can be linked through different mechanisms in different contexts. They recommend tracing mechanisms in more than one typical case within a bounded, causally similar population rather than generalizing from a single case. If a proposed mechanism breaks down in a deviant case, researchers should trace where it failed and compare that case systematically with a typical case to identify omitted causal or contextual conditions.[1]

This strategy makes a modest but defensible claim: a mechanism found repeatedly across causally similar cases may support cautious generalization within that bounded population. It does not justify a claim that the same mechanism operates everywhere that an outcome occurs. Recent work accordingly recommends treating mechanistic heterogeneity—the possibility that different mechanisms link the same X and Y—as the default assumption until empirical study shows otherwise.[1][28]

#### Confounder identification: alternative explanation, treatment assignment, and adjustment

A confounder is a variable that causally affects both a treatment or putative cause and an outcome. In ordinary language, it is a common cause that can make treatment and outcome appear associated even if the treatment has no causal effect, or make the estimated effect too large or too small. Confounding is therefore not identical to correlation. It is a claim about causal structure.[2][7]

Process tracing addresses confounding first by treating it as an alternative causal explanation. Bennett advises researchers to consider potential confounders identified in research and trace whether they have a causal path to the outcome. His checklist also treats nonrandom selection into a program or population, exogenous historical events, sequencing, and multiple simultaneous treatments as potential threats to causal attribution.[6]

This is valuable because it directs attention to the causal prehistory of the treatment. A researcher studying whether a policy caused an outcome should ask why some units received the policy and others did not. If factors that determined treatment assignment also independently affected the outcome, the observed comparison is confounded. Dunning calls evidence about the assignment process treatment-assignment CPOs.[3]

The point is especially important in observational studies, where treatment assignment is not controlled by an experimenter. Regression and matching typically assume conditional independence: within groups defined by observed covariates, treatment is as-if random. Dunning argues that qualitative evidence about how treatment was assigned is essential for making this assumption credible, because a balance test on measured variables cannot show that unmeasured selection processes are absent.[3]

Lyall develops this insight in a process-tracing framework for civil-war research. He recommends matching treated and control observations to reduce alternative explanations, then using process tracing to investigate the treatment and variables that could not be adequately controlled. He also recommends tracing whether treatment assignment was deliberate, strategic, or plausibly as-if random, because selection bias arises when treated units differ systematically from untreated units for reasons related to potential outcomes.[21]

The post-2010 protocol implied by this work contains four linked steps:

1. **Map treatment assignment.** Researchers trace how units entered treatment and control conditions, including what decision-makers knew, what incentives they had, and whether treatment was targeted or plausibly accidental. [21][3]

2. **Build counterfactual comparisons.** Researchers identify untreated or differently treated observations that could serve as plausible controls, rather than tracing only outcome-positive treated cases. Lyall recommends process tracing in control observations because the same causal process may occur in treated and untreated settings. [21]

3. **Stratify and compare on pretreatment covariates.** Researchers should stratify by relevant covariates before tracing mechanisms, both to investigate conditional effects and to disclose gaps in coverage. [21]

4. **Search actively for disconfirming evidence.** Researchers should seek evidence that would undermine as-if-random assignment, report where and how they searched, and make interviews, documents, and other CPOs inspectable where ethically and practically feasible. [3]

These steps do not turn process tracing into a formal statistical solution to confounding. They improve the credibility of the causal assumptions that statistical or quasi-experimental designs require. The key contribution is diagnostic: within-case evidence can reveal whether a claimed treatment-control comparison rests on strategic selection, private information, spillovers, or other processes not visible in a dataset.[21][3]

Pearl’s causal-graphical framework addresses the same problem with a more formal identification rule. A causal graph represents variables as nodes and hypothesized direct causal relationships as directed arrows. A back-door path is a noncausal path entering the treatment variable from one of its causes and continuing to the outcome. Such paths create confounding.[7][2]

Pearl’s back-door criterion says that an adjustment set must satisfy two conditions: first, it may not contain a descendant of the treatment; second, it must block every path from treatment to outcome that enters treatment through an arrow. When such a set exists, adjustment identifies the causal effect under the graph’s assumptions.[7]

This is a major advance over the common instruction simply to control for “relevant variables.” The criterion explains why some variables should be controlled and others should not. A pretreatment common cause of treatment and outcome may need adjustment. A mediator—an intermediate variable caused by treatment and affecting outcome—usually should not be adjusted for when estimating the total effect, because doing so blocks part of the causal effect of interest. A collider—a common effect of two causes—should not ordinarily be conditioned on, because conditioning on it can induce a spurious association between those causes.[7]

Even a pretreatment variable can be a harmful control: conditioning on collider C2 opens the path A ← U1 → C2 ← U2 → Y, whereas C1 and C3 block the relevant back-door paths in this example.[103][104]

Three distinct selection problems require separate treatment. **Nonrandom treatment assignment** arises when units enter treatment and control conditions through processes related to potential outcomes; treatment-assignment CPOs investigate that process. **Selection into the observed sample** can induce Berkson’s paradox when analysts condition on a common consequence of otherwise independent causes. **Purposive selection of outcome-positive cases** selects cases because the outcome occurred. An outcome-positive case can supply diagnostic mechanism evidence, whereas estimating prevalence or a population effect requires an appropriate comparison population and defensible assignment assumptions.[3][7][25][31]

Pearl’s framework also clarifies the problem of post-treatment conditioning. A post-treatment variable is a variable affected by the treatment. Adjusting for it mechanically can distort the total effect by blocking mediation or creating collider bias. Pearl calls adjustment for a variable between an action and its consequence one of the most serious errors in causal analysis because it interferes with estimation of the action’s total effect.[7]

The rule is not an absolute prohibition on measuring or analyzing post-treatment variables. Pearl shows that, in some graph structures, post-treatment variables are needed for identification through nonstandard multistage methods such as front-door adjustment. The methodological rule is therefore not never use post-treatment information. It is never condition on a variable merely because it is observed; first specify its causal position and the estimand—the total effect, a direct effect, a mediated effect, or a sequential plan effect—being sought.[7]

Judgment — **This distinction has changed what a defensible process-tracing protocol looks like.** A process tracer may investigate post-treatment events as evidence of a mechanism. But those events cannot automatically be treated as covariates that validate a treatment effect. The researcher must distinguish evidence used to explain the pathway from variables used to identify the total causal effect.

Mahoney’s approach is closer to Bennett’s than to Pearl’s on this issue. Mahoney’s causal-sequence framework identifies independent variables, dependent variables, and mechanisms, and uses CPOs to assess the intervening sequence. It supports searching for alternative explanations and auxiliary outcomes. Yet it does not itself provide a graphical algorithm for selecting a sufficient confounder-adjustment set.[12][10][2]

Mahoney’s Bayesian and set-theoretic work instead makes the inferential task comparative: evidence is useful if it is more likely under some theories of the causal process than under others. This is valuable for identifying rival explanations, including a candidate confounder. But omitted variables, measurement error, and probabilistic causal relationships remain recognized difficulties for process tracing.[2][4]

The most productive integration therefore traces backward as well as forward. Backward process tracing starts from the treatment and investigates its causes. It then asks whether any treatment cause also belongs to an autonomous pathway to the outcome that does not pass through treatment. If so, it is a candidate confounder and the statistical model should be redesigned. A complementary, more open-ended search begins from the outcome and looks for processes leading to it that do not involve the treatment.[2]

This protocol makes confounder identification an empirical and theoretical task rather than an exercise in adding all available controls. A variable is a serious candidate for adjustment when evidence supports a causal connection to both treatment assignment and outcome, and when cases differ substantially on it.[2]

Pearl’s framework adds an essential caution: the graph itself does not validate its causal assumptions. Analysts must supply substantive knowledge about parent sets, omitted common causes, and exclusion restrictions. Causal assumptions cannot generally be recovered from the joint observational distribution. Even if a graph is compatible with observed data, unobserved common causes and observational equivalence may leave causal effects unidentified.[7]

Unmeasured sample-selection processes can also defeat observational identification; graphical representations such as maximal ancestral graphs (MAGs) and partial ancestral graphs (PAGs) may leave relevant causal edges unresolved.[103][104]

The methodological conclusion is therefore balanced. Process tracing is indispensable for investigating how treatment was assigned, whether an alleged confounder actually affects treatment and outcome, and whether a purported mechanism occurred. DAGs are indispensable for making clear whether those variables are pre-treatment confounders, mediators, colliders, or descendants of treatment, and for determining whether the causal effect is identified under the stated assumptions.[2][7]

For Bennett, mechanism tracing requires specifying observable implications for every proposed step in a causal process and assessing whether each observation discriminates between rival explanations.[9][6] Bennett treats equifinality as a reason to trace distinct causal sequences that may lead to the same outcome rather than infer a single pathway from a cross-case association.[6] Bennett addresses possible confounding by specifying rival explanations, including candidate confounders, and seeking within-case observations that they predict differently.[6]

Bennett and Checkel’s 2015 best-practice protocol directs researchers to cast the net widely for alternative explanations; evaluate alternatives with equal rigor; consider evidentiary-source bias; assess whether the case is most or least likely for alternatives; make a justifiable decision on when to start and stop inquiry; gather diverse and relevant evidence; combine process tracing with case comparisons when useful and feasible; remain open to and transparently report inductive insights; deduce the specific process that should occur if an explanation is true; and recognize that conclusive process tracing is good but not all good process tracing is conclusive.[32] These practices operationalize their definition of process tracing as analysis of evidence on processes, sequences, and event conjunctions within a case to develop or test causal-mechanism hypotheses.[32]

### Bayesian approaches to causal inference championed by Judea Pearl and James Mahoney

The phrase Bayesian approaches can obscure a crucial distinction. Pearl uses probability, Bayesian networks, structural causal models, and causal graphs, but his signature contribution is causal-graphical identification: explicit representations of causal structure that distinguish observation from intervention. Mahoney uses Bayesian reasoning to assess the evidentiary force of process-tracing observations and, with set-theoretic work, to reason about causal configurations. These are related forms of probabilistic reasoning, but they answer different questions.[7][8][2]

Pearl’s framework begins with a structural causal model. A model contains variables, structural equations showing how each variable is generated from its direct causes and disturbances, and a directed acyclic graph showing those direct causal relations. A causal effect concerns an intervention, represented by the do operator, which modifies the relevant structural equation. This differs from conditioning: observing that X equals x leaves the causal system unchanged, whereas doing X equals x changes it.[7]

Pearl’s structural causal model can be written as M = ⟨U, V, F⟩, where U denotes exogenous variables, V endogenous variables, and F the structural functions. Its modularity assumption allows an intervention such as do(X=x) to replace the equation for X while leaving the other equations unchanged.[81][99][105]

The framework’s central achievement is to make causal identification conditional on visible assumptions. If the graph contains the relevant variables and a set of covariates blocks all back-door paths without conditioning on treatment descendants, the total effect is identifiable by adjustment. If the graph contains an unblocked confounding arc or an unobserved common cause that prevents identification, the data do not identify the effect. That conclusion is substantive: it says no amount of larger-N observational data resolves the causal question without stronger assumptions or different evidence.[7][33]

Pearl can also represent unmeasured confounding. Bidirected edges represent unobserved common causes. Latent variables may be included explicitly, and graphical criteria such as the front-door criterion or do-calculus can sometimes identify effects despite unobserved confounding. But such identification is structure-dependent, not automatic.[7]

Pearl’s treatment of mechanisms follows from this structural view. Each parent-child relation is assumed to be an autonomous mechanism that remains invariant when other mechanisms are perturbed. This invariance gives the model counterfactual and intervention meaning: researchers can ask how Y would change if they intervened on X while holding the rest of the structural system as specified.[7]

Mahoney’s treatment of mechanisms is different in emphasis. A causal mechanism is an unobservable entity or process that, under specified circumstances, is sufficient to generate an outcome. Methodologically, the researcher investigates observable traces of that process. In the causal-sequence framework, mechanisms connect hypothesized independent variables to an outcome; CPOs and auxiliary outcomes provide evidence for or against the hypothesized sequence.[34][12][10]

Mahoney’s Bayesian orientation therefore concerns inference to the best explanation. The relevant question is not only whether a piece of evidence is consistent with a theory, but whether it was expected under that theory and difficult to explain under alternatives. This is closely compatible with Bennett’s Bayesian process tracing. It is also compatible with Mahoney’s work on process-tracing tests, which locates observations in a larger causal sequence.[2][12][25]

The difference from Pearl is clearest in confounding. Pearl asks whether a causal effect is identifiable under a graph, and produces criteria for appropriate adjustment. Mahoney’s process-tracing logic asks whether the evidence supports a causal process and whether alternative explanations, including confounding explanations, remain plausible. The former is a formal theory of intervention identification; the latter is a theory of evidentiary discrimination among causal explanations.[7][2][3]

Judgment — **A researcher should not describe Mahoney as a Pearl-style graphical causal-inference scholar merely because both use Bayesian language.** Mahoney’s Bayesianism concerns updating confidence in causal explanations from case evidence. Pearl’s causal graphical framework concerns whether intervention effects and counterfactuals follow from a specified structural model. The first is especially suited to explaining singular historical cases; the second is especially suited to auditing the causal assumptions required for observational effect estimates.

The frameworks nevertheless overlap in constructive ways. Both reject the idea that correlation alone establishes causation. Both require causal assumptions outside the observed association. Both emphasize alternative explanations and the limits of observational evidence. Both can represent causal pathways and recognize that observed data may underdetermine underlying processes.[7][2]

They differ in what is treated as the central inferential object. Pearl centers the intervention effect, usually expressed as the expected distribution of Y under do(X). Process tracing centers the explanation of an outcome in a case: which mechanism or combination of mechanisms generated it. Mahoney’s set-theoretic work further centers configurations of conditions and causal sufficiency, rather than an average effect of one variable holding all else equal.[7][6][35]

This difference should determine method choice. If the question is whether a policy treatment caused an average change in an outcome across a defined population, researchers need an explicit estimand, a DAG or equivalent causal model, and an identification strategy. If the question is how and why a revolutionary crisis, democratic breakdown, or policy reform unfolded in a particular country, process tracing is indispensable. If the question asks both whether a relationship travels across cases and how it operates, the design should allocate separate tasks to cross-case and within-case evidence.[21][1][6][2]

Charles Ragin’s *Fuzzy-Set Social Science* (2000) provides a concrete QCA application: its analysis of welfare-state outcomes in affluent democracies examines combinations of social and political conditions associated with generous welfare states rather than uniform additive effects. Process tracing can then test whether cases assigned to the same truth-table configuration actually followed the proposed common mechanism, or reached the outcome through distinct pathways.

Equifinality is a many-to-one relationship in which different variables or combinations of variables generate the same effect; multiple paths to democracy are a standard example. It differs from multifinality, a one-to-many relationship in which a variable has different effects because it interacts with another variable.[36]

The practical interview-design implication is that evidentiary value is relative to the specified hypothesis set. In explicit Bayesian process tracing, all hypotheses are compared against all evidence, rather than treating answers elicited by questions about the working hypothesis as independent confirmation. By contrast, approaches that do not require mutually exclusive rivals allow evidence supporting one explanation without automatically undermining others.[37]

Mahoney’s 2012 procedure operationalizes this comparison by combining pre-existing generalizations with case-specific causal-process observations and applying hoop and smoking-gun tests to the X–M and M–Y links, not only to the direct X–Y association.[38] A hoop test requires the specified observation for a hypothesis to remain viable; a smoking-gun observation provides decisive support when present.[38] Mahoney further holds that rigorous hoop tests, easy smoking-gun failures, and a consistent series of straw-in-the-wind tests each shift confidence.[38][39]

Mahoney’s mechanism tests are directed at the causal process’s component links. Mahoney recommends applying hoop and smoking-gun tests to causal-process observations concerning the X-to-mechanism link or the mechanism-to-Y link, rather than only to evidence about a direct X-to-Y relationship.[38]

Mahoney’s account uses hoop and smoking-gun tests for theory testing, while also treating process tracing as a means of theory development through counterfactual analysis or inductive discovery.[40]

Bennett and Checkel (2021) make causal mechanisms observable through within-case evidence about sequences, actors, and intervening processes; they treat equifinality as a reason to compare rival pathways rather than infer uniqueness from an outcome.[18] Mahoney’s Bayesian and set-theoretic reasoning sharpens that comparative evidentiary task.

Pearl’s framework supplies a different discipline: it represents mechanisms as structural relations and determines whether a causal effect is identifiable after confounding, mediation, selection, and post-treatment variables are properly located in a graph. The strongest current position is therefore integration, not substitution: use process tracing to investigate mechanisms, treatment assignment, and rival causal paths; use causal graphs and cross-case designs to state the estimand, identify admissible controls, and expose assumptions about confounding. The unresolved disagreement concerns how far explicit numerical Bayesianism improves qualitative inference, especially when mechanisms interact and priors cannot be specified without contestation.[8][9][7][16][18]

**Skocpol (1979, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'States and Social Revolutions: A Comparative Analysis of France, Russia and China')’s revolutionary comparison illustrates the strengths and limits of all four approaches.** Skocpol explains the social revolutions in France, Russia, and China through political crises in old-regime states: cross-pressures between domestic class structures and international exigencies broke apart autocracies, centralized administrations, and armies, opening the way for revolts from below.[41] The crises occurred in France in 1787–9, Russia in the first half of 1917, and China in 1911–16.[42]

Bennett’s approach directs attention to observable implications of this sequence—state breakdown, the disorganization of coercive controls, and subsequent mobilization—and to rival accounts of each link. Checkel adds that evidence for state breakdown, mobilization, and ideology must be assessed through the practices that produced it: archival materials, interviews, ethnography, surveys, or discourse analysis. Sewell (1994, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'Ideologies and social revolutions: Reflections on the French case')’s critique that Skocpol’s treatment of ideology is inadequate identifies a rival or omitted mechanism requiring precisely this evidentiary scrutiny.[43][11][6][8]

Skocpol’s negative-case contrasts strengthen the configurational explanation without constituting a graph-defined adjustment estimate. She used England, Prussia/Germany, and Japan as controls because they did not undergo successful social revolutions despite structural and historical similarities, and she used distinct contrasts for distinct claims: the Japanese Meiji Restoration and Prussian Reform Movement for state crises, and the English Parliamentary Revolution and failed German revolution of 1848–50 for agrarian structures and peasant revolts. Her positive revolutionary cases received much fuller treatment than the negative cases.[44]

Pearl’s framework would require an explicit graph distinguishing international exigencies, class structures, state crisis, coercive disorganization, mobilization, ideology, and revolutionary transformation, then identifying an admissible adjustment set for a stated intervention effect. Mahoney’s causal-sequence framework instead fits Skocpol’s explanatory structure directly by locating mechanisms between proposed causes and revolutionary outcomes and evaluating auxiliary implications. Neither move converts Skocpol’s contrasts into an identified population-level treatment effect.[7][12][10][1]

Theda Skocpol’s *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) is a foundational comparative-historical study that predates the later vocabularies of process tracing, Bayesian evidentiary assessment, and causal graphs. Her argument treats social revolution as the conjunction of state breakdown under international and domestic pressures and autonomous peasant revolt, rather than as the product of revolutionary ideology or deliberate elite action alone.

Process tracing originated in cognitive psychology in the late 1960s and was adopted in the social sciences in the late 1970s as a way to use case-study evidence for inferences about historical explanations.[45] George and Bennett played a leading role in developing process tracing as within-case analysis.[46] Pearl’s *Causality* was first published in 2000 and substantially revised in its second edition in 2009, developing a framework that distinguishes causal from statistical concepts and links substantive causal theories to observational implications.[47] Mahoney’s article *The Logic of Process Tracing Tests in the Social Sciences* was published in November 2012, and Barrenechea and Mahoney’s set-theoretic Bayesian process-tracing article appeared in August 2019.[48][49]

## Competing claims using three empirical applications

This section tests the rival methodological claims against research designs rather than treating any method as self-validating. The central question is whether cross-case regularities, configurational patterns, and within-case sequences support the same causal conclusion. Across all three applications, they answer different questions: cross-case analysis maps recurrence and variation, while within-case analysis examines how a proposed process unfolded.

### Theda Skocpol (1979, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'States and Social Revolutions: A Comparative Analysis of France, Russia and China') [41]’s analysis of revolutionary causation

Theda Skocpol’s *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) compares France, Russia, and China and explains social-revolutionary transformation through the interaction of state structures, international forces, and class relations. Its object is not simply an uprising, but transformations that reshape state organizations, class structures, and dominant ideologies.[41][50] Skocpol’s structural perspective makes states central as administrative and coercive organizations that may be autonomous from class interests.[41][51]

Skocpol defines a social revolution as a rapid, basic transformation of state and class structures, accompanied and partly carried through by class-based revolt from below.[106][107]

Skocpol specifies a causal sequence. International competition or external threat places old-regime states under pressure; institutional relations with landed upper classes and agrarian economies constrain the state’s response; administrative and military institutions then break down; peasant revolts take advantage of the lapse in governmental supervision; and marginal elites consolidate revolutionary transformation through new centralized, mass-incorporating state organizations.[42][52] This is already close to what process-tracing scholars call a causal mechanism: a sequence of entities and activities linking an initial condition to an outcome. It makes claims about intermediate events, not merely about the correlation between an external threat and revolution.

Skocpol locates international relations and the politically autonomous state in historical “world time”; military backwardness, dependency, and domestic class pressure contribute to administrative and military crisis.[107][108]

The mechanism is visible in the three principal cases but is not identical in every detail. France’s revolutionary crisis made liberal stabilization impossible and was followed by the emergence of centralized bureaucratic state organizations through mass mobilization.[53] In Russia, during 1917–18, mass revolts by industrial workers, peasants, and soldiers undercut the landed and capitalist classes and sealed the dissolution of tsarist state machinery; although organized revolutionary leaders professed socialist equality and proletarian democracy, the revolution produced a centralized bureaucratic party-state committed to rapid industrialization by command and terror.

After March 1917, popular, especially peasant, revolts intersected with urban-based political leaders’ struggles to build new state organizations, and the Russian crisis deepened more rapidly and chaotically than the French.[54] In China, peasants supplied the major insurrectionary force, but they also became the organized popular basis for consolidating revolutionary state power, unlike France and Russia, where state-building relied more on urban support and administrative hierarchies.[55]

**Skocpol’s strongest methodological achievement is the connection of a cross-case structural argument to a sequenced account of state breakdown, peasant revolt, and revolutionary consolidation.** Bennett and Checkel (2021, 'Understanding Bayesianism: Fundamentals for process tracers') (2015, 'Process tracing: From metaphor to analytic tool')’s process tracing would test Skocpol’s within-case sequence from state crisis and breakdown through peasant revolt to revolutionary consolidation against rival sequences, including Sewell’s ideological account of the French Revolution.[42][52][6][43]

A test of Skocpol’s structural sequence would also examine whether Communist organization in China, Bolshevik strategy in Russia, or ideology in either case was a causal link rather than a secondary feature.[106][107]

Skocpol’s comparative design also addresses equifinality, meaning that more than one causal path can produce the same outcome. Her three revolutionary cases share a broad configuration—old-regime crisis and state breakdown combined with agrarian structures conducive to peasant revolt—but they reach crisis through different immediate routes. Russia’s crisis developed under wartime defeat; France and China experienced major crises during formal peace, when reform and resource-mobilization efforts met resistance from dominant classes.[56] China’s route also depended on post-1927 political-military fragmentation and the Communist Party’s reorganization of local politics to generate peasant collective leverage.[56]

Her comparison is not limited to outcome-positive cases. Skocpol and Somers characterize the research as using both Mill’s Method of Agreement, which seeks common hypothesized causes among cases with the outcome, and Method of Difference, which contrasts outcome cases with similar cases lacking the outcome.[44] England, Prussia/Germany, and Japan serve as contrasts because they did not undergo successful social-revolutionary transformation despite important structural and historical similarities to France, Russia, and China.[44] Japan and Prussia adapted to international pressures through reforms from above, while England and Germany lacked the peasant insurrections against landlords that Skocpol treats as crucial to social revolution.[56]

| Comparative feature in Skocpol’s design | Evidence and implication |
|---|---|
| **Agreement among revolutionary cases** | France, Russia, and China are compared as cases in which political crises centered on old-regime states disorganized administrative and coercive control, opening the way for revolts from below. [42] |
| **Difference-oriented contrasts** | Japan and Prussia are used for claims about state crises; England and the failed German Revolution of 1848–50 are used for claims about agrarian structures and peasant revolts. [44] |
| **Alternative routes within the main pattern** | Russia’s crisis followed wartime defeats, whereas France and China experienced reform crises in peacetime; China’s later revolutionary consolidation depended on a distinct Communist-led process. [56] |
| **Limit on causal control** | The positive cases receive extensive treatment, whereas negative cases receive less; Skocpol and Somers also state that perfectly controlled historical comparisons are never feasible. [44] |

The contrasts make Skocpol’s treatment of confounding stronger than a comparison of France, Russia, and China alone. A confounder is a prior common cause that makes an apparent relationship between a proposed cause and outcome misleading. By adding structurally similar nonrevolutionary cases, Skocpol asks why international pressures and political crisis sometimes produced reform rather than social revolution.[44] However, the negative cases are less deeply analyzed, and macro-causal comparative history treats historical controls as approximations rather than as fully equivalent counterfactual worlds.[44]

An identification critique characterizes Skocpol’s negative cases as dissimilar to the revolutionary cases and notes that classifying revolutions relies on retrospective judgments; these concerns weaken claims about general intervention effects.[99][105][107]

Judgment — Pearl’s causal-graphical framework would require an explicit directed acyclic graph: nodes for international pressure, state capacity, landed-class power, agrarian structure, peasant revolt, and revolutionary transformation; arrows representing hypothesized direction; and a defensible adjustment strategy for variables that precede treatment. Skocpol supplies rich theory for such a graph, but her comparison does not estimate a graph-defined intervention effect. Mahoney’s Bayesian process-tracing approach asks a different question: how strongly does a particular piece of evidence discriminate among rival explanations? Skocpol’s narratives generate many potentially diagnostic observations, but they do not provide explicit likelihood comparisons among the structural, ideological, and actor-centered accounts.

A Pearl-style model could treat agrarian structure as modifying the effect of international challenge or administrative weakness, rather than only as a separate cause of revolution.[99][105][107]

The principal substantive challenge comes from the treatment of ideology. Skocpol holds that societal crisis alone was insufficient: administrative and military breakdown inaugurated social-revolutionary transformations in France, Russia, and China.[52] William Sewell (1994, Cambridge University Press eBooks, 'Ideologies and social revolutions: Reflections on the French case') nevertheless describes the book as a model of comparative historical analysis while arguing that its treatment of ideology is inadequate and developing an alternative account of the French case.[43] This disagreement is not a refutation of the state-breakdown mechanism. It identifies a rival or supplementary causal process that within-case analysis should trace alongside structural pressures.

Skocpol’s inference is deliberately scope-bounded. Skocpol and Somers state that comparative-historical causal arguments are largely inductive and cannot readily be generalized beyond the cases actually studied.[44] Mahoney and Goertz describe Skocpol’s 1979 theory as intended to explain all social revolutions among agrarian-bureaucratic states that were not formally colonized; they identify France, Russia, and China as that universe.[57] The appropriate conclusion is therefore a recurring causal configuration within a restricted historical population, not a universal theory of every revolution.

Skocpol’s model can also be stated as a precise conjunctural claim: “social revolution if and only if peasant revolt and state breakdown.” In the set-theoretic formulation, state breakdown AND peasant revolt are jointly sufficient for social revolution, while each condition is separately necessary; testing the model requires coding these core variables and assessing the fit between the coded data and the model.[58]

A set-theoretic reassessment finds that Skocpol’s sufficiency hypothesis fits all three positive cases—France, Russia 1917, and China—because social revolution is greater than or equal to state breakdown AND peasant revolt. For nonoccurrence cases, the sufficiency requirements are met by England and Japan but not by Russia 1905, Germany, and Prussia.[58] The same reassessment finds state breakdown consistent with necessity in all nine cases, but finds falsifying cases for peasant revolt: England and Japan have 0.00 membership in peasant revolt and 0.42 membership in social revolution.[58]

China is a partially falsifying case for peasant revolt because Skocpol argues that the Chinese Communist Party created substantial peasant autonomy and solidarity once the revolution was under way; including those organizational activities permits coding China at 1.00 for peasant revolt.[58] In the necessary-condition framework, a case in the “falsification–substitutability” cell, (0, 1), directs within-case analysis to ask whether a new factor substitutes for the proposed necessary condition; this cell is also an equifinality cell.[58]

A causal graph would also distinguish a mediator from a confounder: state crisis may mediate the effect of international pressure on revolution, so conditioning on state crisis would be post-treatment conditioning if the estimand is the total effect of international pressure. Checkel’s approach additionally requires separating revolutionary actors’ rhetoric and retrospective justifications from evidence that ideas or socialization changed their capacities and opportunities.

### the qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) tradition following Charles Ragin

Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) is a cross-case set-theoretic method that evaluates whether conditions or combinations of conditions are necessary or sufficient for an outcome. In ordinary terms, it asks whether membership in one set of cases—for example, cases with a particular configuration of party competition and demand-side grievances—is consistently contained within the set of cases with an outcome. QCA was developed for small- and intermediate-*N* comparative research and applies Boolean or fuzzy-set logic to cross-case evidence.[59]

QCA expresses conjunctions with Boolean AND and alternative pathways with OR. Crisp-set membership is binary, while fuzzy-set membership runs from 0 to 1 and uses anchors for full exclusion, crossover, and full inclusion; truth tables expose configurations associated with contradictory outcomes.[109][110]

In crisp-set QCA, membership in a condition is binary; Boolean AND denotes a conjunction of conditions, while Boolean OR denotes alternative routes to the same outcome.[109][110]

With k crisp conditions, a QCA truth table has 2^k logically possible rows. When cases share a calibrated configuration but have different outcomes, investigators can revisit calibration, add a condition, or examine the cases more closely.[109][110]

Schneider and Wagemann’s 2006 QCA application examines democratic consolidation by distinguishing remote structural conditions from proximate political conditions.

A concrete application is Barquero, Jiménez, and Ugarte’s 2022 fuzzy-set QCA of radical-right-party electoral success in ten European countries from 2008 to 2019. The study reports four sufficient causal paths to success across diverse contexts.[60] Those paths combine demand-side and supply-side conditions, although the authors judge supply-side conditions more important qualitatively and quantitatively.[60] This is an empirical representation of equifinality: several distinct conjunctions can be sufficient for the same outcome.

| Feature of the radical-right fsQCA application | Reported result |
|---|---|
| **Cases and period** | Ten European countries, 2008–2019. [60] |
| **Causal form** | Four sufficient paths lead to radical-right-party success in diverse contexts. [60] |
| **Conditions combined** | Each path combines demand-side and supply-side conditions. [60] |
| **Substantive emphasis** | Supply-side conditions are more important qualitatively and quantitatively. [60] |

The application supports QCA’s distinctive contribution to equifinality. A conventional additive statistical model may allow many combinations of values to exceed an outcome threshold, whereas QCA seeks to identify a relatively small number of interpretable causal paths within a bounded population.[57] In set-theoretic language, individual conditions can be INUS conditions: insufficient but necessary parts of a conjunction that is itself sufficient but unnecessary because other sufficient paths exist.[2][57]

**The four-path result supports a configurational account of radical-right success: no single demand-side or supply-side condition is treated as the universal route to the outcome.** It therefore fits Mahoney’s broader interest in necessary and sufficient causation better than a single average-treatment-effect model.[60] It also illustrates why equifinality cannot be reduced to a regression interaction: QCA identifies alternative conjunctions and assigns cases to paths.[57]

Yet a QCA solution does not itself reveal the causal mechanism operating within each configuration. Cross-case configurations show that conditions are jointly associated with an outcome; they do not show the actor-level interactions through which those conditions generated it.[28] Schneider and Rohlfing (2013, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Combining QCA and Process Tracing in Set-Theoretic Multi-Method Research') therefore argue that QCA works best when followed by case studies that examine the causal quality of a solution, the mechanisms underlying its terms, and potentially omitted conditions.[61][62] Their case-selection guidance identifies typical and deviant cases as suitable sites for such follow-up analysis.[61]

This is where process tracing and QCA become complementary. QCA maps configurations and identifies cases that instantiate, contradict, or lie near a proposed set relation. Process tracing then examines whether the hypothesized process occurred in those cases. Negative cases are especially valuable because they can explain outcome failure, expose inconsistency between explanations for occurrence and absence, and test whether proposed outcome and failure mechanisms are too similar to generate different results.[63]

Judgment — Pearl’s framework treats confounder identification as a problem of causal structure: researchers specify which pre-treatment variables cause both treatment and outcome, then use the graph to determine whether conditioning on a set blocks noncausal paths without blocking causal ones. QCA does not perform that operation. A sufficient configuration may be associated with radical-right success because of an omitted common cause, a calibration decision, or an unmodelled sequence. The relevant QCA response is substantive theorization, transparent calibration, robustness assessment, and within-case scrutiny—not graphical back-door adjustment.

A sufficient-set relation does not itself establish whether a condition is a cause rather than a mediator, collider, descendant of the outcome, or proxy; configurational analysis also does not by itself resolve limited diversity.[109][104][110]

QCA researchers recognize this vulnerability. Rutten (2020, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Applying and Assessing Large-N QCA: Causality and Robustness From a Critical Realist Perspective') argues that causal inference in QCA depends on dialogue among empirical patterns, theoretical expectations, and case-based knowledge, and that large-*N* QCA weakens the last of these elements.[64] Kuehn and Rohlfing argue that omitted-variable bias affects QCA because solutions depend heavily on included variables, while truth tables lack direct equivalents to regression specification tests.[65] Hug (2013, Political Analysis, 'Qualitative Comparative Analysis: How Inductive Use and Measurement Error Lead to Problematic Inference') further argues that Boolean QCA is justified for deterministic hypotheses with error-free measurement, but that political research rarely meets both assumptions and exploratory Boolean analysis under measurement error can produce dramatically misleading inference.[66]

These critiques make calibration central. Calibration is the process of assigning cases membership scores in sets such as high economic insecurity or strong party-system fragmentation. A case wrongly scored can enter a different truth-table row, altering the solution; Kuehn and Rohlfing recommend changing scores case by case to assess sensitivity.[65] Recent methodological work also recommends thick case descriptions, opening the internal content of QCA conditions, incorporating time, and combining QCA with more time-sensitive methods.[67]

QCA calibration is a substantive theoretical judgment about what counts as membership in a set, rather than a routine statistical transformation of raw observations.[109][110]

There remains a genuine methodological disagreement. Mikkelsen (2015, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Negative Case Selection: Justifications and Consequences for Set-Theoretic MMR') presents combined QCA and process tracing as having strong potential for valid set-theoretic multimethod inference.[63] Beach (2017, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Achieving Methodological Alignment When Combining QCA and Process tracing in Practice') argues, however, that process tracing rests on a mechanism-based conception of causation, whereas QCA makes counterfactual claims about set relations; their differing ontologies can yield contradictory case-selection guidance.[26] The practical resolution is not to call the methods interchangeable. It is to declare whether the study gives analytical priority to conditions and sufficiency relations or to mechanisms and causal processes.[35]

Beach and Rohlfing distinguish condition-centered integration, which starts from cross-case configurations, from mechanism-centered integration, which starts from a theorized process and investigates its scope across cases.[111][35]

The radical-right application operationalizes its 29 cases as national elections, rather than countries, in Spain, Italy, Greece, Germany, Sweden, France, the Netherlands, Austria, Denmark, and Norway during 2008–2019. It defines the outcome as radical-right-party vote share, calibrating 15% as full membership, 8% as the crossover between electoral success and failure, and 5% as full non-membership. Its economic-crisis condition is the change in the harmonized unemployment rate in the two years before an election.[68]

The study’s operationalization makes the configurational claim reproducible. Fuzzy-set membership ranges from 0, total absence, to 1, total presence; the authors report no individually necessary condition for either success or its absence at their 0.9 consistency threshold. They construct a truth table of 18 configurations, use a 0.8 consistency cutoff, and distinguish complex, parsimonious, and intermediate solutions by their treatment of logical remainders—configurations for which no empirical cases exist.[68]

The intermediate solution incorporates theoretically informed easy counterfactuals and positive directional expectations for all five conditions. It identifies four paths, with overall consistency of 0.901 and coverage of 0.652. One path, ~NATIV*EXTREM, combines the absence of strong mass-level nativism with an extremist centre-right position on the GALTAN dimension; it has raw coverage 0.313, unique coverage 0.124, and consistency 0.925, and includes SWE2018, SPA2019_1, SPA2019_2, NET2017, and DENM2011. A third path, ECO*NICHE*~EXTREM, combines economic crisis, programmatic nicheness, and the absence of an extremist centre-right position; it has raw coverage 0.312, unique coverage 0.119, and consistency 0.843, and includes AUST2013, NORW2009, SWE2010, NET2012, FRAN2012, NET2010, NORW2017, and GERM2013. Thus the reported equifinality is not merely a general QCA premise but a result produced by explicitly calibrated memberships and stated solution rules.[68]

The application also operationalizes causal asymmetry: the presence and absence of radical-right success require different explanations. Four outcome-positive elections—ITAL2008, GERM2017, AUST2017, and DENM2015—are not covered by any of the four intermediate-solution paths; the authors state that these unexplained cases require detailed post-QCA analyses. They recommend examining the processes behind the paths with both large-N quantitative and small-N qualitative approaches.[68]

A QCA application of successful training transfer identified eight sufficient pathways, each with consistency of 1.00; each pathway covered either 0.133 or 0.067 of successful cases. Multiple sufficient configurations in that application do not by themselves establish that different within-case mechanisms operated.[112]

A Pearl-style assessment of a QCA configuration asks whether its conditions precede the outcome and whether omitted common causes or conditioning on consequences of the outcome distort the configuration; a conjunction by itself does not reveal the interactions among its conditions.[28]

The Schneider and Wagemann democratic-consolidation application uses alternative combinations of remote and proximate conditions to assess equifinality: democratic consolidation can arise through more than one configuration.

Ragin’s *The Comparative Method* (1987) supplied an early substantive illustration through welfare-state development in advanced capitalist democracies. Its configurational logic treats such conditions as labor strength, left or corporatist political organization, and state-economic structure as potentially consequential in combination rather than as variables with a single additive effect.

Schneider and Wagemann’s two-step fuzzy-set QCA supplies a second concrete application of this logic in research on democratic consolidation. The first step identifies different combinations of remote structural factors that make consolidation possible; the second identifies combinations of proximate factors within those outcome-enabling contexts. Their analysis finds different paths to consolidation, all characterized by a fit between the institutional mix and the societal context in the dispersion of power.[69] The two-step design uses theory to separate remote from proximate conditions and thereby reduces limited diversity: with eight conditions, the stated maximum number of logical remainders falls from 255 in a one-step analysis to 30 when conditions are divided into two groups of four, or to 66 in the specified two-and-six division.[69]

Schneider and Wagemann’s two-step fuzzy-set QCA of democratic consolidation first identifies combinations of remote structural factors that make consolidation possible and then examines combinations of proximate factors within those outcome-enabling contexts.[69]

### recent mixed-methods studies of democratic breakdown

Recent research on democratic breakdown demonstrates how cross-case evidence and within-case analysis can be combined without claiming that either alone answers every causal question. Haggard and Kaufman’s *Backsliding: Democratic Regress in the Contemporary World* (2021) studies 16 backsliding episodes using standardized democracy indicators, regional comparisons, and structured case studies.[31] Kaufman, Kelemen, and Kolcak’s 2024 *Perspectives on Politics* article, *Federalism and Democratic Backsliding in Comparative Perspective*, combines worldwide quantitative analysis with structured, focused case studies of the United States, Brazil, Venezuela, and India.[70]

Haggard and Kaufman define backsliding as incremental erosion of democratic institutions, rules, and norms by elected governments, typically under an autocratic leader.[31] Their measure distinguishes erosion within democracy from outright reversion: a state qualifies as democratic after at least eight consecutive years scoring at least 0.5 on V-Dem’s Electoral Democracy Index, while reversion occurs when that index falls below 0.5.[31] They first generated 29 candidate cases and retained cases identified as eroding or reverting by at least two other measurement sources.[31]

Their mechanism is explicitly sequential. First, polarization weakens confidence in institutions, increases the appeal of anti-system actors, and raises tolerance for departures from democratic rules. Second, party-system realignment and electoral arrangements help autocratic leaders gain executive office and legislative control. Third, incremental derogations weaken checks, rights, media freedom, civil society, and electoral integrity.[31] This is a process-tracing-friendly account because each stage produces observable implications about timing, institutional behavior, and actor strategy.

The study combines these mechanisms with cross-case regularities. Every case displayed an increase on at least one polarization measure before backsliding, and 75 percent showed increased anti-system civil-society movements during the backsliding period.[31] The authors’ 16-case analysis found that 15 cases were relatively polarized against regional benchmarks, and within-case analysis identified clear evidence that polarization caused backsliding in 14 cases.[71] Greece and the Dominican Republic are treated as anomalies: Greece raises a case-classification issue, while the Dominican Republic combined lower polarization with a milder and more transient form of backsliding and alternative processes.[71]

**Haggard and Kaufman use cross-case evidence to identify a strong pattern and within-case evidence to assess whether polarization actually contributed to backsliding in particular countries.** This is closer to Mahoney’s Bayesian process-tracing aspiration than to a simple correlation: case evidence can raise or lower confidence in a proposed causal account, including in anomalous cases.[71] But their evidence is not a formal Bayesian calculation of prior and posterior probabilities.

The cases also show equifinality. Countries polarized in distinctive ways, and Haggard and Kaufman state that polarization is difficult to trace to one underlying cause.[31] Autocratic legislative dominance arose through several routes: collapse or fragmentation of centrist parties, extremist capture of established parties, coordination failures in polarized party systems, and electoral disproportionality that converted pluralities into legislative majorities.[31] Electoral disproportionality is explicitly neither necessary nor sufficient for backsliding.[31]

The content of anti-system appeals differed across Haggard and Kaufman’s cases: Venezuela featured left-populist class appeals, whereas Turkey and Hungary featured religious, rural, and nationalist appeals.[113]

The constituent-assembly cases illustrate both a pathway and its contingency. In Venezuela, a Chavista-dominated assembly displaced Congress and produced a constitution that substantially increased presidential power.[31] In Ecuador, Correa’s allies dominated the assembly, which dismissed Congress and adopted a constitution that expanded presidential authority.[31] In Bolivia, opposition forces retained leverage, negotiated a compromise, and prevented the constituent assembly from directly producing executive overreach.[31] These comparisons make the condition “constituent assembly” analytically incomplete without the balance of opposition power, institutional rules, and international mediation.

Venezuela’s sequence included Chávez’s election in 1998, a constituent assembly in 1999, and a legislative election in 2000.[113]

The analysis of incrementalism similarly combines recurring patterns with within-case sequences. Haggard and Kaufman report constitutional revision or amendment in 12 of 16 cases and efforts to lift executive term limits in seven cases: Bolivia, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Nicaragua, Russia, Turkey, and Venezuela.[31] They argue that attacks on one democratic institution create opportunities for attacks on others, while legally ambiguous incremental steps normalize abuse and impede opposition coordination.[31] Hungary supplies a within-case illustration: after Fidesz’s 2010 parliamentary victory, judicial and electoral changes, pressure on independent media, and regulatory harassment of civil society unfolded alongside declining democracy indicators.[31]

Turkey’s AKP rose to power in 2002 and concentrated executive power more sharply after the late 2000s and the failed coup in 2016.[113]

The design has a clear causal-inference limitation: Haggard and Kaufman explicitly select on the dependent variable by studying backsliding cases.[31] This is appropriate for explaining how backsliding unfolded in the selected population, but it cannot by itself estimate the causal effect of polarization, legislative dominance, or incremental reform relative to a representative set of non-backsliding democracies. That distinction matters because a high proportion of polarized backsliding cases is not identical to evidence that polarization would cause backsliding under intervention.

The federalism study supplies a useful contrast. Its worldwide quantitative analysis finds no systematic relationship between federalism and democratic backsliding.[70] Its structured case studies nevertheless support a conditional argument: federalism can obstruct autocratic threats posed by national rulers under a limited set of conditions.[70] This is not a contradiction. The statistical result rejects a uniform average association, while the case studies specify heterogeneous mechanisms that may operate only in certain institutional settings.

The 16-case analytic population retained 55.2% of the 29 candidate backsliding cases initially identified (16/29). This quantifies how the study’s cross-dataset validation rule substantially narrowed the cases taken forward for within-case analysis.[31][71]

Constitutional revision or amendment occurred in 75.0% of the 16 backsliding episodes (12/16), making it a frequent component of the incremental-backsliding sequence rather than an exceptional institutional tactic.[31][71]

Turkey’s 2002 electoral threshold converted the AKP’s 34% vote share into 66% of legislative seats—a 32-percentage-point seat-share premium. This gives the institutional-disproportionality mechanism a concrete magnitude in one of the study’s illustrative cases.[31]

The federalism study supplies a more fully specified cross-case test than the brief contrast suggests. Its universe comprises 105 democracies from 1974 through 2021, 18 federal and 87 unitary; a democracy has at least eight consecutive years with a V-Dem Electoral Democracy Index score of at least 0.5. It identifies backsliding by first locating significant declines from a prior V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index peak and then retaining cases validated as erosion or authoritarian reversion by at least two other democracy indices; this procedure identifies 21 countries.[72]

Its statistical findings reject a uniform federalism effect across each outcome examined. Backsliding occurred in 22% of federal democracies and 20% of unitary democracies, with no statistically significant relationship. Federalism likewise has no statistically significant relationship with severity—defined as the fall from the pre-episode Liberal Democracy Index score to its episode low—or speed—severity divided by the episode’s years—and no significant relationship emerges when decentralization is measured with the Regional Authority Index in logistic-regression models. Among backsliders, 50% of federal and 53% of unitary cases fell below the 0.5 Electoral Democracy Index threshold; that difference also is not statistically significant.[72]

The structured comparison then specifies the conditional mechanism behind the null aggregate relationship. Federalism can constrain an aspiring national autocrat when state governments possess authority over key functions and sufficient state governments are controlled by opposition parties or by ruling-party actors independent of that autocrat. The United States under Donald Trump (2017–21) and Brazil under Jair Bolsonaro (2019–23) are cases in which those conditions held more strongly; Venezuela under Hugo Chávez and Nicolás Maduro (1999 to present) and India under Narendra Modi (2014 to present) are cases in which they did not.[72]

Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán’s 2013 study of Latin American regimes combines cross-country quantitative analysis of democratic survival, transitions, and breakdown with historical case analysis of regime trajectories and political actors, including intensive testing in Argentina.[73][74]

Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán explain Latin American regime survival and breakdown through political actors’ commitment to democracy and political radicalism alongside structural preconditions, economic performance, institutional factors, regional diffusion, and United States support for democracy. They find that elite commitment to democratic ideals reduces competitive-regime breakdown, while radicalism undermines competitive regimes indirectly by eroding normative preferences for democracy; in Argentina, a chronically weak pro-democracy coalition and radical actors explain recurrent breakdowns.[73][74]

Haggard and Kaufman’s 2021 study examines democratic erosion and reversion across cases including Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and Venezuela, rather than treating democratic breakdown as one uniform sequence.[31]

Other influential recent studies illustrate different degrees of methodological integration: Nancy Bermeo’s 2016 article identifies executive aggrandizement and strategic election manipulation as characteristic contemporary routes; Levitsky and Ziblatt’s *How Democracies Die* (2018) compares the United States, Venezuela, and interwar Europe; and Lührmann and Lindberg’s 2019 study uses V-Dem data with country illustrations to characterize a third wave of autocratization. Together they show that a common decline in democracy indicators can mask distinct sequences of partisan accommodation, attacks on horizontal accountability, media capture, and institutional subordination.

Haggard and Kaufman’s 2016 study of third-wave democratic transitions and reversals combines statistical analysis with process tracing; its distributive-conflict analysis relates Gini-measured income inequality to the probability of democratic transition and traces distributive grievances, mass mobilization, and incumbent overthrow or departure.[75][71] The process-tracing component selects all 52 distributive-conflict transition cases on the dependent variable, so it is suited to examining the proposed process among such transitions rather than estimating its prevalence in a representative sample of all cases.[75]

A global event-history study covering 4,300 country-years, 204 democratic episodes, and 83 breakdowns during 1900–2010 used Weibull models to associate one-standard-deviation increases in civil-society participation and party institutionalization with 68% and 45% lower breakdown hazards, respectively. After propagating V-Dem measurement uncertainty, the respective estimates were 63% and 14% lower breakdown hazards.[114]

Across revolution, QCA, and democratic backsliding, the strongest conclusion is methodological division of labor rather than methodological replacement. Skocpol shows how cross-case contrasts can support a structural configuration while historical sequences specify state breakdown and revolutionary consolidation. QCA maps alternative sufficient configurations, but follow-up case studies examine mechanisms and omitted conditions. Haggard and Kaufman use standardized indicators to identify common patterns and case narratives to assess whether proposed processes occurred, while the federalism study shows why a null global association can coexist with conditional causal effects.[44][62][26][71][70] The unresolved issue is not whether methods may be combined, but whether the researcher has specified the causal estimand, the relevant scope conditions, and the distinct inferential role of each kind of evidence.

## Critiques and process tracing protocols since 2010

Process tracing now faces two design questions before it can support a causal claim: why were particular cases, people, or documents observed, and where do those observations sit in the causal sequence? These questions matter because evidence can illuminate a mechanism while still yielding a biased estimate if treatment assignment, sample inclusion, or statistical controls are causally mis-specified.

### selection bias

Selection bias arises when treated and untreated units differ because entry into treatment, exposure, or the observed sample was not random. A related causal-graph problem occurs when researchers condition on a *collider*—a common effect of two causes—which induces an association between those causes; Pearl calls this selection bias or Berkson’s paradox. Selecting cases because the outcome occurred can likewise bias causal conclusions when the inference concerns the causes of that outcome.[21][7][76]

Since 2010, mixed-method protocols have made the *treatment-assignment causal process observation* (treatment-assignment CPO) an explicit object of process tracing. A treatment-assignment CPO is evidence about how units entered treatment or control categories: who had information, incentives, and capacity to select into exposure. Dunning argues that such evidence is vital for assessing confounding in observational studies, because regression and matching normally assume *conditional independence*: within strata of measured covariates, assignment is as-if random.[3] Lyall accordingly recommends tracing whether assignment was plausibly as-if random, particularly where relevant conditioning information is private and cannot readily be matched across cases.[21]

The protocol joins qualitative and quantitative checks rather than treating either as sufficient. First, researchers examine balance on covariates unaffected by treatment. Second, they trace whether decision-makers or subjects could strategically sort into treatment in ways related to their potential outcomes. The Argentina land-titling example combines qualitative evidence on occupation, allocation, and legal processes with balance tests of pretreatment covariates; Dunning’s London cholera discussion similarly uses institutional facts about renters, landlords, and fixed pipes to evaluate household-level self-selection.[3] **This division of labor makes process tracing a test of the assignment story behind an observational design, while balance tests assess only observed pretreatment differences.**[3][6]

Protocols also increasingly redesign comparisons instead of tracing treated cases alone. Lyall proposes matching treated and control observations, then using process tracing on the treatment and factors that matching could not control; he further recommends counterfactual non-victims, two-control-group comparisons, and stratification by key covariates before fieldwork. Stratification exposes inaccessible strata—such as high-violence areas—and thereby identifies limits on generalization.[21] Out-of-sample tests are preferred for theory testing because they reduce the risk of fitting an explanation to the cases that generated it.[21]

Transparency has become a corresponding procedural norm. Dunning asks researchers to seek evidence that would undercut, as well as support, an as-if-random assignment claim; report where and how they searched; identify interviews and other materials consulted; and, where feasible, catalogue evidence or provide active citations, transcripts, and archival records for scrutiny.[3] Bennett adds that researchers should discount documentary and interview evidence affected by record-makers’ preferences or interviewees’ motivated biases.[6] This protocol makes selective quotation, source bias, and missing counterevidence themselves objects of inference.

Source criticism includes assessing actors’ incentives to disclose or conceal information, distinguishing public or prepared statements from private or spontaneous ones, and consulting competing historiographies. Interviews, documents, or media accounts that repeat one interested actor’s account are not independent evidence streams.[115][39]

Case selection remains contested. A strict comparative-inference view holds that studying only outcome-positive cases violates the logic of explaining why an outcome occurred.[76] Model-based work instead argues that selecting on the dependent variable is not inherently damaging: appropriateness depends on the case distribution and the probative value of available evidence.[25] Systematic selection from a cross-case analysis can reduce selection bias, yet a case typical of a regression or QCA solution is typical only at the cross-case level and need not display the same within-case process.[65] **The practical result is purposive selection for mechanism discovery, paired with an explicit target population, comparison logic, and stated limits on generalization.**[21][2]

Selection scrutiny now extends to the generation of within-case evidence, not only to selection of cases. A 2024 interviewing protocol directs researchers to use a specified causal model and its predicted fingerprints to select actors directly involved in the process, actors sufficiently close to it, and people temporally close to it; direct involvement is relevant but must be assessed for access and reliability.[37] Interview samples should also be selected in light of rival explanations, and an auxiliary sample is required when a sample drawn from the preferred process would exclude respondents able to provide essential evidence for a rival account.[37] Researchers should sequence interviews along the causal chain while guarding against the unconscious biases that sequential collection can create.[37]

Seawright’s simulations favor deviant cases and cases extreme on the principal independent variable for many discovery goals, while questioning the routine preference for typical cases, cases extreme on the outcome, and most-similar designs.[116]

A most-likely case tests a theory under favorable conditions, so failure substantially weakens it; a least-likely case tests it under unfavorable conditions, so success can strengthen claims about its scope. Neither is a representative estimate of an average effect.[115][5][116]

This development sharpens the distinction between two sampling targets. For inference about a hypothesized process in one specified case, purposive, nonrandom respondent sampling prioritizes relevance rather than sample size, representativeness, or diversity. For claims that a mechanism travels to a wider population, generalization rests instead on selecting and comparing additional cases in which to trace the process.[37] Process-tracing protocols accordingly require questions derived from the predicted evidence of both the working explanation and its rivals, and seek observations for which those explanations predict different results.[37] They also require diverse, independent evidence streams, because apparent triangulation from a common source can disguise selection bias.[39]

Recent potential-outcomes critiques have directed comparative case-study design toward explicit statistical case-selection logic; the chapter identifies Lyall (2014), Glynn and Ichino (2015), Nielsen (2016), Herron and Quinn (2016), and Gerring’s 2017 second edition as part of this turn.[58]

An X-centered strategy examines all cases in which the proposed cause is present, including X = 1, Y = 0 cases, to establish how often the mechanism produces the outcome; selecting only Y = 1 cases misses instances in which X fails to operate as theorized.[58] Haggard and Kaufman selected all transition and reversion cases from 1980–2000 and described selection on the dependent variable as central to a design aimed at identifying the causal mechanism leading to regime change.[58]

A critique of transition-focused designs identifies Egypt, Iran, and Sudan as cases in which elite splits did not produce a transition, illustrating what a sample restricted to successful transitions can miss.[117]

Concrete case-selection critiques underscore the protocol’s purpose. Kreuzer (2024, Politics, 'The methodological legacies of Theda Skocpol’s State and Social Revolutions: Locating the three pillars of comparative historical analysis')’s critique of Cusack, Iversen, and Soskice found that they selected 18 of 32 available European pre-1920 electoral-system cases without explaining the exclusion of the other 14, oversampled West European cases, and underrepresented failed democracies by omitting Portugal, Spain, Greece, and all East European democracies except Czechoslovakia.[77][58] Random selection from a remaining long list of case-study or paired-comparison candidates is a hedge against cherry-picking cases favorable to the researcher’s claim.[58]

A review of game-theoretic articles with case studies found that almost all single substantial case studies, and even multiple-case studies, selected (1, 1) cases; paired comparisons combining (0, 0) and (1, 1) cases were extremely rare, and confounders were rarely discussed in case selection.[58] Discussing alternative explanations of individual events or alternative formal models does not itself make confounders relevant to case selection.[58]

A standard mixed-method sequence first estimates a pattern in observational cross-case data and then process traces selected cases to determine whether the hypothesized mechanisms behind population-level correlations are evident.[6] Statistical analysis can also locate outlier or deviant cases, and process tracing those cases can uncover omitted variables.[6]

Within-case sampling is itself a selection problem. Lyall reports that campesino organizations selected Wood’s interviewees and that all but 24 of 200 campesinos interviewed had participated in the insurgency; participant oversampling can overstate emotional mechanisms when state violence and proximity to insurgent forces condition whether emotions can be acted upon.[21] The required corrective is interviews with non-participants both in the five study areas and beyond them.[21]

Within-case sampling is itself a selection problem.

For matching studies, the omitted-variable protocol begins with an inclusive inventory of variables on which members of a matched pair differ greatly, then uses intensive forward process tracing from each candidate variable to seek plausible signs of a path to the outcome.[2] A candidate difference makes a prima facie case for inclusion among matching variables when evidence supports links from it to both treatment assignment and outcome and the matched cases have substantially different values on it.[2]

### post-treatment conditioning

Post-treatment conditioning means controlling for, matching on, excluding, or subsetting units on a variable that treatment may have affected.[78] For a *total effect*—the full effect of a treatment through all of its pathways—such adjustment can block a genuine pathway through a mediator or open a spurious path through a collider, even in a randomized experiment.[7][79][80] Pearl’s back-door criterion therefore defines an admissible adjustment set as one that contains no descendant of the treatment and blocks all back-door paths into it.[81]

Successful observational adjustment also depends on consistency, positivity, temporal ordering, and conditional ignorability or exchangeability, not only on selecting controls from an assumed graph.[118][104]

This critique has altered the use of process evidence in mixed-method research. The first step is to reconstruct the temporally ordered pathway from treatment to outcome. The second is to classify each proposed statistical control by causal role: antecedent confounder, mediator, collider, or another descendant of treatment. The third is to treat within-case evidence that a control lies on the treatment-to-outcome pathway as evidence that it is probably inappropriate for estimating the total effect.[2] This is a causal-ordering audit, not a rule against observing post-treatment events: process evidence is precisely what helps identify them.

The audit must cover diverse cases. A failure to find a control on the pathway in one case offers only weak support for its safety, because the same control may be post-treatment in another subset of cases. Multiple case studies of pathways provide stronger evidence.[2] When case evidence identifies a suspect control, researchers should re-estimate without it, demonstrate that the variable is not post-treatment in the other cases, or substantively challenge the pathway account.

In the example of economic integration and party polarization, evidence that globalization increased inequality in 1980s Turkey makes inequality a potentially inappropriate regression control and triggers precisely those revision choices.[2]

The warning is estimand-specific rather than an absolute ban. Conditioning on a mediator ordinarily biases an estimate of the total effect, but under specified graphical conditions it may identify a *controlled direct effect*: the effect of treatment when the mediator is fixed at a specified value. Identifying that distinct estimand requires blocking treatment–outcome and mediator–outcome back-door paths; merely including the mediator as a control is insufficient.[82] Pearl also identifies causal structures in which treatment-affected covariates must be measured for total-effect identification through nonstandard, multistage adjustment.[33] **Process tracing should therefore distinguish mediators from genuine antecedent confounders and revise models accordingly, rather than mechanically conditioning on a visible mechanism.**[7][2]

The quantitative warning applies to sample restriction as well as regression adjustment: conditioning includes controlling for a post-treatment variable, eliminating observations by a post-treatment criterion, and subsetting on such a variable.[83][84] In experiments published in the *American Journal of Political Science*, *American Political Science Review*, and *Journal of Politics* during 2012–2014, Montgomery, Nyhan, and Torres found post-treatment conditioning in 46.7% of articles.[85] They show that the resulting bias can have any direction and any size and often cannot be bounded or eliminated without strong assumptions.[86][85]

The resulting protocol fixes the temporal status of covariates before data collection and treats controls unrelated to treatment and measured in advance as the appropriate adjustment candidates.[86] For mediation, merely controlling for the mediator does not identify direct or indirect effects without additional assumptions, including sequential ignorability; sensitivity analysis is the recommended procedure when researchers estimate mediation effects under those stronger assumptions.[86]

Causal graphs have become an operational part of this audit. Waldner’s completeness standard requires a causal graph whose nodes are jointly sufficient for the outcome, with every step predicted and causally ordered.[32] The 2025 Qualitative and Multi-Method Inquiry curriculum translates this into process-tracing practice through construction of front-door paths, causal-graph-guided data collection, techniques for missing data, and mitigation of bias from back- and side-door paths.[87] Pearl’s front-door criterion shows why the protocol is graph-specific: an observed treatment-affected mediator can assist identification when treatment affects the outcome only through that mediator and an unobserved common cause of treatment and outcome has no direct effect on the mediator.[88]

Selection-bias critiques have shifted process tracing beyond verifying a hypothesized mechanism toward investigating the prior processes that generated treatment, exposure, case inclusion, and available evidence. Post-treatment critiques have added a second task: auditing whether variables used as controls are actually downstream components of the mechanism being estimated. Together, these developments favor designs that combine explicit assignment histories, counterfactual comparison groups, pretreatment balance diagnostics, pathway reconstruction, and transparent searches for disconfirming evidence.[3][2]

The two critiques also mark the boundary of process tracing’s contribution. Within-case evidence can expose strategic assignment, unmeasured common causes, biased inclusion, or a bad control; it cannot convert an unsupported assignment assumption into randomization or make a mediator’s causal effect identifiable by observation alone. Credible causal inference therefore requires process tracers and statistical analysts to specify the causal question, preserve temporal order, and subject both the cross-case design and the within-case account to adversarial tests.[4][89][6]

Post-2010 protocol writing also makes design-stage specification more concrete: formulate competing hypotheses, state what each predicts if true or false, construct a timeline from the emergence of the proposed cause through the outcome, identify theoretically grounded alternatives at relevant causal-graph nodes, and complete these steps before collecting evidence.[39] When evidence is unavailable, it lowers the maximum probability assignable to an explanation; when contrary evidence leads to a revised hypothesis, the revision must generate and be tested against new observable implications.[39]

Ricks and Liu specify seven steps: identify primary and rival hypotheses, establish a timeline, construct a causal graph, identify alternative choices or events, identify counterfactual outcomes, then collect evidence for the primary hypothesis and for its rivals. The first five steps precede evidence collection.[100][39]

Ricks and Liu advise considering agent-centered, structural, power-based, institutional, normative, and legitimacy explanations as rivals, specifying evidence expected both if each is true and if it is false.[100][39]

## Current methodological consensus on integrating evidence

### Shared aims and complementary evidence

Judgment — The integration literature converges on an integrative multi-method design in which within-case evidence and cross-case analysis are linked to one causal inference: one method may produce the final inference while another designs, tests, refines, or bolsters the assumptions on which it rests.[2] The practical issue is division of labour.

Cross-case analysis asks whether outcomes systematically differ as explanatory factors differ across a population; within-case inquiry asks how, under what conditions, and through which causal process a putative cause produced an outcome in particular cases.

This documented convergence corresponds to a distinction between **difference-making evidence** and **mechanistic evidence**.[1][2] Difference-making evidence comes from variation across cases, as in regression, matching, experiments, or QCA: it bears on whether changing X is associated with or causes a change in Y. Mechanistic evidence comes from tracing the actors, actions, decisions, sequences, and intervening links through which X plausibly produces Y in a case.

Beach (2017, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Achieving Methodological Alignment When Combining QCA and Process tracing in Practice') and Pedersen (2016, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Selecting Appropriate Cases When Tracing Causal Mechanisms') distinguish these explicitly: intensive within-case tracing generates evidence about how a mechanism operated in a case, whereas variance-based and experimental designs generate evidence that a cause makes a difference across cases.[26][27][1] Their corollary is important: finding a mechanism in a real case does not itself demonstrate cross-case difference-making, which requires cross-case variation.[1]

Cross-case statistical models consequently do not merely supply a preliminary correlation to decorate with a narrative. They can map the relevant population, identify robust associations, reveal deviant cases, estimate average effects under stated assumptions, and structure case selection. Within-case research can then test measurement, reconstruct pathways, assess whether the treatment was assigned in a plausibly exogenous way, and search for omitted confounders. Seawright defines an **integrative multi-method design** as one in which methods jointly support a single causal inference: one method may make the final inference, while another designs, tests, refines, or bolsters its assumptions.[2] He identifies measurement validity, omitted variables, interactions, and causal pathways as matters that case studies can investigate to improve a regression-based inference.[2]

The converse design is equally legitimate. A qualitative explanation can identify a weak or consequential link in a causal chain, and a quantitative component—ideally experimental where feasible—can test that link or estimate an effect size crucial to the broader explanation.[2] Seawright therefore rejects a fixed hierarchy in which statistics must always make the final causal inference: some integrative designs are structured by qualitative analysis, with quantitative components testing critical assumptions.[2] In Seawright’s recommended integrative design, integration means making the components answer linked questions, rather than presenting a regression table and a case narrative side by side.[2]

A central recommendation is conditional: a regression coefficient or QCA solution becomes more credible as causal evidence when within-case inquiry tests its assumptions and the reconstructed process could plausibly produce the direction and magnitude of the cross-case result.[2] Seawright recommends tracing not only whether a pathway exists but whether it could account for as much of the effect as the regression estimates.[2] A highly contingent pathway raises doubts about a large, general treatment-effect estimate; repeated evidence of a low-contingency pathway supports such an estimate.[2] This does not reduce process tracing to post hoc illustration. It turns case inquiry into a potential falsifier of causal interpretation.

Dunning’s account of **causal-process observations**—evidence about context, process, or mechanism—captures this role particularly well. He argues that process tracing can help researchers understand selection into treatment categories and validate causal assumptions embedded in quantitative models.[3] In studies that invoke natural or quasi-natural experiments, within-case inquiry can ask how treatment was actually assigned, while cross-case checks can examine balance on observed pre-treatment covariates.

In the Argentine land-titling example, qualitative evidence about occupation, plot allocation, and legal processes was paired with statistical balance tests on pre-treatment covariates.[3] Neither component is redundant: balance tests cannot reveal all strategic motives and private information, while historical evidence does not by itself demonstrate covariate balance across a study population.

The same logic applies to observational regressions. A **confounder** is a factor that creates a back-door path between treatment and outcome—most simply, a cause of both.[2] Seawright’s proposed within-case protocol first traces backward from the treatment to identify its causes, then asks whether any also belong to an autonomous process leading to the outcome without passing through treatment.

Evidence of such overlap identifies a candidate confounder and supplies a basis for redesigning the regression.[2] A complementary, more inductive search begins with other processes leading to the outcome, though it is less structured and more likely to miss confounders.[2] Scholars should therefore combine backward tracing of treatment assignment with open-ended inquiry into alternative outcome processes.

Within-case inquiry also has a specific function in detecting **post-treatment bias**: bias introduced by conditioning on a variable caused by the treatment. Such a control can remove part of the very effect that the researcher seeks to estimate; conditioning on a collider—a common effect of two causes—can introduce bias even where none previously existed.[2] The appropriate protocol is to trace the treatment-to-outcome pathway and inspect whether regression controls appear as steps in that pathway.

Evidence that a control is on the pathway in even one case makes it probably inappropriate; absence of such evidence in one case offers only weak support for retaining it.[2] Multiple, diverse process-tracing studies provide materially better support because a control may be post-treatment only in a subset of cases.[2]

A concrete illustration concerns Ward and colleagues’ time-series cross-sectional regressions of economic integration and party polarization in countries from 1961 to 2010. Their models controlled for economic inequality. Keyder’s within-case account of Turkey in the 1980s described globalization as increasing inequality, implying that inequality could be a post-treatment control rather than a confounder in this setting.[2] Seawright identifies three appropriate responses: show that Turkey is exceptional and that inequality is not post-treatment elsewhere; re-estimate without inequality; or rebut the within-case account on its merits.[2] The point is not that the case study mechanically invalidates the regression. It compels a causal re-specification that a regression coefficient cannot conduct for itself.

The empirical practice of democratic-backsliding research illustrates the division of labour. Haggard and Kaufman’s study defined a population of 16 backsliding cases through statistically significant decline in V-Dem liberal-democracy scores among states that had met a democracy threshold, triangulating V-Dem with three additional datasets.[71] It then compared polarization with regional benchmarks: 15 of 16 cases, or 94%, were relatively polarized, and within-case analysis found clear evidence that polarization caused backsliding in 14 cases.[71] The design did not treat the 94% regularity as conclusive proof.

It examined anomalies: Greece raised a case-classification question, while the Dominican Republic combined relatively low polarization with alternative processes and a milder, transient form of backsliding.[71]

This example demonstrates the payoff from nesting process evidence within an explicit population. The cross-case component supplies a transparent universe of cases and identifies an unusually strong empirical regularity. The within-case component determines whether the postulated mechanisms operated and whether anomalous cases instead reflect measurement, misclassification, scope conditions, or equifinality—different pathways to the same outcome. Haggard and Kaufman’s 2021 democratic-backsliding study is an example of Large-N Qualitative Analysis (LNQA): it treats polarization as a proposed necessary condition for backsliding and identifies three mechanisms linking polarization to democratic regress—loss of trust in institutions resulting from policy stalemates, increasing copartisan tolerance of autocratic behavior, and emergence of extreme parties or factions.[71] They also examine Brazil, Poland, and the United States as exemplary but diverse cases in which polarization contributes to the appeal of autocrats.[31] The design supports a qualified generalization about polarization and backsliding while preserving the possibility that mechanisms vary across the defined population.

This approach also clarifies why rare-event studies require special attention to the cases carrying the result. In Mansfield and Snyder’s dataset of 2,530 country-year observations, only six observations occupied the mechanism-relevant (1,1) cell; all occurred before the First World War and most involved the Ottoman Empire.[71] In Maat’s genocide analysis, 2,576 observations included 12 genocide cases and six (1,1) cases.[71] Large datasets do not eliminate the need for close case analysis when a causal claim hinges on a small, historically concentrated set of outcome cases.

The practical implication is clear: when key cells are sparse, intensive study of those cases is not supplementary storytelling but central to judging whether the statistical pattern travels.

Judgment — The recommended standard is therefore **assumption-sensitive complementarity**.[2] Researchers should pre-specify the causal question, causal estimand or explanatory target, population, plausible pathways, and rival explanations; use the method best suited to each inferential task; and allow findings from either component to revise the other. Cross-case analysis cannot by itself reveal the mechanism in a case, while process tracing cannot by itself estimate a population average effect.

This convergence has produced more explicit protocols for population scope and mechanism generalization. Beach and Pedersen recommend that mechanism-focused studies select typical cases in which X, Y, and the required contextual conditions are present, but trace mechanisms in more than one such case unless causal homogeneity is strongly demonstrated.[1] For deviant cases, they recommend tracing the point of breakdown and then comparing the deviant case systematically with a typical case to identify omitted causal or contextual conditions.[1] Comparative Process Tracing similarly begins by comparing two or more intensive case studies for similarity of process parts and surrounding conditions; if processes differ, analysis shifts to identifying the case difference that accounts for the variation.[92] These designs treat generalization as an empirical question about mechanisms and scope conditions, rather than an automatic inference from either one case or one coefficient.

The result is a more demanding account of external validity. **External validity** means whether a causal claim applies beyond the studied cases. Large-N models do not reliably reveal the causal-effect size in an individual case, even for statistically typical “onlier” cases.[90] For a pre-specified mechanism hypothesis, reinforcing outliers—cases with outcomes stronger than the statistical model predicts—can reduce false negatives; for inductive inquiry, selecting onliers rather than reinforcing outliers creates a trade-off between false negatives and false positives.[90] This means that case selection cannot be governed by one universal rule.

It must follow the inferential purpose: replication of an average effect, diagnosis of model failure, discovery of omitted conditions, or testing of a specified mechanism.

In the Mansfield–Snyder example, the six mechanism-relevant (1,1) observations constitute only 0.237% of the 2,530 country-year observations. This calculation shows why intensive scrutiny of the historically concentrated cases carrying a statistical interaction can be central to assessing the proposed mechanism.[71]

In Maat’s genocide analysis, the six mechanism-relevant (1,1) cases amount to 50.0% of the 12 genocide cases. Thus, close examination of that cell directly bears on half of the outcome cases in the analysis.[71]

The deciding criterion is whether a design both supports its stated cross-case causal claim under defensible identification assumptions and supplies observations that discriminate among proposed within-case mechanisms.[7][1][2][8][9] On this criterion, causal graphs coupled with cross-case design and estimation rank first for a population causal-effect claim when an adjustment set blocks every back-door path and contains no descendant of the treatment; process tracing ranks first for discriminating mechanisms in a specified case when it tests observable implications of rival mechanism hypotheses; and an integrative design ranks first when the study makes both claims.[7][8][9][2]

Multimethod LNQA (M-LNQA) makes the division of labour more explicit. Theory leads from an expected significant correlation or average treatment effect of X on Y, and within-case causal inference across a range of cases then strengthens the causal inference.[71] Its rare-event examples show why this is consequential.

In Maat’s M-LNQA analysis of mass violence and genocide, 2,576 observations contain 12 genocide cases, but the claims hinge on the six cases in the (1,1) cell; close examination of those cases would substantially increase confidence in the theory and correlational findings.[71]

Haggard and Kaufman’s analysis of democratic transitions and reversions during 1980–2000 used causal process observation and an original qualitative dataset. They found distributive conflict in just over half of transition cases, while less than one-third of reversions were driven by distributive conflicts between elites and masses.[58] They found alternative mechanisms of democratic reversion: authoritarian populist leaders promising more redistribution overthrew some incumbent democratic governments, while conflicts cutting across class lines and purely intra-elite conflicts—particularly military factions staging coups—were more common.[58] They found some support, often for only part rather than all of the target mechanism, usually in about 30 percent of the cases studied; alternative mechanisms were more important.[58] This is direct evidence of equifinality in democratic breakdown: the same outcome arose through multiple causal mechanisms.[58]

For mechanism-centered large-N qualitative testing, statistical analysis should be verified with case studies of the (1,1) cases: without convincing case studies showing the causal mechanism in action, statistical analyses are hard to find convincing.[58] Recent democratic-backsliding research also uses explicitly combined designs.

A study of federalism and democratic backsliding combines quantitative analysis of countries around the world with structured, focused comparative case studies of the United States, Brazil, Venezuela, and India.[93] The need for that connection is underscored by a review of Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán’s 2013 study, which found that mechanisms connecting its different causal arguments were often unclear and that its correlational, observational design supplied limited evidence on causation.[94]

Evidential Pluralism holds that establishing a causal claim normally requires evidence of both an appropriate conditional correlation and an appropriate mechanism complex, and it has been advanced as a philosophical rationale for mixed-method research in the social sciences.[95] (Shan and Williamson, 2021)[95]

Waldner’s 2015 “completeness standard” gives this division of labour a concrete sequence for combining case-specific explanations and average treatment effects: draw a causal graph of causal steps and relations; draw an event-history map for each case; assess their correspondence through construct validity, measurement reliability, and measurement validity; and identify the causal mechanisms for each graph step as instantiated by actual events.[40] A cross-case regression used to test whether a case-study finding generalizes must be credible, cover the same causal population relative to the causal effect of interest, and assess whether the rest of the causal pathway—not only the overall relationship—generalizes.[2]

Nesting theory-testing process tracing in a broader mixed-method design requires deterministic theory, or theory compatible with deterministic testing, and alignment between the within-case and cross-case causal targets: both must make inferences about roughly the same causal object.[96] Where those conditions are met, a mechanism developed through theory-building process tracing can be tested or retested with fsQCA.[96]

There is a documented gap between model-guided case-selection proposals and reported practice. One proposed procedure selects pathway cases by changing each case’s observed treatment to its minimum, holding its confounders at their observed values, and choosing cases with the largest predicted change in the outcome probability.[58] Yet the survey reports that scholars rarely use statistical analyses to choose cases, do not match on Z variables, overwhelmingly select extreme (1,1) cases, and rarely select representative, (0,0), or random cases; only two articles in a ten-year systematic survey used statistical models and results to choose cases.[58] Together, these findings show a gap between methodological proposals for model-guided case selection and reported practice, in which statistical results rarely guide selection and researchers often choose extreme cases.[58]

### Conditional verdict and unresolved disputes

**The current integration literature’s consensus is plural but not permissive: within-case and cross-case evidence should be integrated through a causal design in which each can challenge the other’s assumptions.** The deciding criterion is whether the design supports its stated cross-case causal claim under defensible identification assumptions and supplies observations that discriminate among proposed within-case mechanisms.[7][1][2][8][9] Conditional on that criterion, cross-case causal graphs, identification designs, and statistical estimation rank first for population average or conditional-effect claims; explicit, rival-focused process tracing ranks first for within-case mechanism claims; and integrated designs rank first where both claims are part of the stated inference.[7][8][9][2] Cross-case analysis maps patterns, estimates average or conditional effects under stated assumptions, and locates informative cases, while within-case analysis tests causal pathways, treatment assignment, source-level measurement, contextual conditions, and rival explanations.[28][2] Integration succeeds when these tasks bear on one stated causal inference rather than when separate methods merely reach rhetorically compatible conclusions.[2]

There is no consensus that all causal questions demand an average treatment effect. Mahoney and Goertz distinguish qualitative **causes-of-effects** inquiry—why did this outcome occur in this case?—from quantitative **effects-of-causes** inquiry—what is the average effect of X across a population?[57] They regard the approaches as complementary: explanations of individual cases can motivate broader analysis, while statistical findings should be checked against case histories.[57] Haggard and Goertz’s Large-N Qualitative Analysis (LNQA) goes further by making within-case causal inference the central basis for a causal generalization: it starts from a theory, hypothesized X–Y relation, and specified mechanism, then investigates cases to determine whether that mechanism is present, operates as expected, and has the predicted effect.[71] Their multimethod variant begins with a theoretically expected statistical relationship or average treatment effect and uses within-case inference across selected cases to strengthen causal assessment.[71]

LNQA’s contribution is especially clear on selection. It defines a scope of cases and seeks to examine all cases meeting the stipulated conditions, or a large share of them, thereby reducing the risk that inference depends on a hand-picked small set.[71] It also treats anomalous cases as analytically productive: close scrutiny can reveal coding errors, alternative mechanisms, and equifinality.[71] Yet the scope is itself a causal judgement about the enabling conditions of the mechanism, and it need not equal the full population in a statistical dataset.[75] The appropriate conclusion is bounded: comprehensive tracing can support strong claims about a specified historical population, but it does not automatically license prediction for future or radically different cases.[75]

The role of Bayesian reasoning remains contested. Explicit Bayesian process tracing treats causal inference as comparison among rival hypotheses. It requires researchers first to specify hypotheses and priors, second to identify relevant evidence, and third to assess likelihoods or likelihood ratios—that is, how much more expected the evidence would be if one hypothesis rather than another were true.[9] Its principal advantage is that it treats evidence as probative by degree and evaluates the total body of evidence according to how strongly it distinguishes rival explanations.[9] Fairfield and Charman (2022, 'Social inquiry and Bayesian inference: Rethinking qualitative research') (2018, 'The Bayesian foundations of iterative research in qualitative social science: A dialogue with the data') explicitly allow both within-case observations and cross-case evidence to be assessed in this common inferential framework.[6][9] They also require sequential assessment of evidence conditional on previously incorporated observations, guarding against double-counting related clues as independent confirmation.[9]

Fairfield and Charman’s explicit Bayesian procedure calls for sensitivity analysis when priors are contestable, separate treatment of distinct source types, and assessment of the log-likelihood ratio as the weight of evidence.[8][119]

This Bayesian position supports genuine integration because it asks the same question of archive documents, interviews, descriptive statistics, and cross-case patterns: how should each observation update confidence in concrete rival explanations? It also provides a principled way to handle testimonial evidence by assessing the likelihood that a particular source would make the observed statement under each hypothesis, given the source’s knowledge and incentives.[9] It follows that a mixed-method study need not assign a fixed qualitative or quantitative “weight” in advance. The evidence receives weight according to its discriminatory power in the causal model.

Checkel disputes that explicit numerical Bayesianism resolves the transparency problem. He argues that formal Bayesian analysis requires subjective estimates of priors and two evidentiary likelihoods, and that mathematical notation does not make those estimates transparent simply by displaying them.[16] He also argues that Bayesian process tracing fits deductive designs poorly when the research is inductive and therefore lacks deductively derived priors to which probabilities can be assigned.[16] Fairfield and Charman accept a related limitation: numerical probabilities in qualitative social research cannot be specified unambiguously, although they argue that explicit Bayesian reasoning can reveal the locus of disagreement and discipline inference.[9] The substantive dispute is not whether process tracers should compare rival explanations—both sides endorse that—but whether quantified priors and likelihoods improve on transparent qualitative reasoning enough to justify their cost and subjectivity.

QCA-based multimethod research displays a second unresolved disagreement. QCA is a cross-case method designed to identify necessary and sufficient conditions, conjunctions of conditions, asymmetric paths to outcomes, and equifinality.[97] Follow-up case studies are widely treated as valuable for assessing the causal quality of a QCA solution, its terms, underlying mechanisms, and omitted conditions.[62] Negative cases have particular value because they can explain outcome failure, test whether explanations of outcome presence and absence are theoretically coherent, and determine whether proposed success and failure mechanisms are too similar to generate different outcomes.[63] Schneider and Rohlfing (2013, Sociological Methods & Research, 'Combining QCA and Process Tracing in Set-Theoretic Multi-Method Research')’s integration proposals consequently make case selection and revision of the set-theoretic cross-case model in light of case evidence central to set-theoretic multimethod research.[61]

However, QCA–process-tracing integration is not mechanically coherent. A methodological-alignment critique argues that process tracing rests on a mechanism-based understanding of causation, whereas QCA makes counterfactual claims about configurations of conditions. These ontological foundations can produce conflicting methodological recommendations, forcing analysts to prioritize alignment with one method or the other.[26] This is not a minor technical disagreement.

A QCA conjunction shows that conditions are jointly associated with an outcome, but it does not reveal the interactions among those conditions inside a causal process.[28] Likewise, a condition that appears redundant in a QCA solution may still be causally important as a contextual support factor for a mechanism in a particular case.[28]

Mechanistic heterogeneity intensifies the problem. **Mechanistic heterogeneity** occurs when the same X and Y are connected by different mechanisms across cases; its equifinality form means that different pathways lead from the same cause to the same outcome.[28] Recent methodological work argues that it should be the default expectation rather than an exception, because causal homogeneity at the level of mechanisms is a demanding claim in social science.[28] Regression and QCA can establish patterns of covariation or difference-making, but they cannot reveal how a mechanism operates within a particular case.[28] The appropriate response is not to abandon cross-case analysis, but to use it to map relevant populations and then trace mechanisms across increasingly diverse cases, including deviant cases where pathways break down.[28]

This conclusion qualifies claims that nested designs solve the problem of generalization. Statistical selection of a case can systematically reduce researcher discretion, but it cannot make the selected case representative of unobserved within-case processes. Kuehn and Rohlfing argue that process tracing in a few cases does not yield confident inferences about the distribution of processes in the wider population, even when cases are selected from large-N results or theoretical expectations.[65] Model misspecification can compound the problem: residuals or effect sizes may direct researchers to the wrong cases, after which the case analysis can falsely reinforce the initial model.[28] Conflicting regression and case-study results therefore require diagnosis rather than a reflexive choice of winner; possible explanations include heterogeneous effects, omitted-variable bias, inconsistent measurement, or a flawed case inference.[2]

The conditional verdict is consequently clear. Cross-case statistical analysis contributes most when the question concerns population patterns, comparative prevalence, average or conditional effects, and disciplined case selection. Within-case evidence contributes most when the question concerns the operation of a mechanism, treatment assignment, causal timing, measurement, confounding, post-treatment controls, and scope conditions. The combination is strongest when researchers specify in advance which assumption each component tests, collect evidence capable of disconfirming it, and revise the design when either component fails.[3][2]

The ranking changes in favour of independent cross-case causal authority when treatment assignment is genuinely randomized or a causal graph and design block every back-door path without conditioning on a treatment descendant, followed by estimation of the identified estimand.[7][33] It changes in favour of broader authority for a within-case mechanism claim when the same disaggregated mechanism is traced across a clearly bounded and increasingly diverse population and contextual conditions demonstrate causal homogeneity.[1][28][92] Pearl’s framework makes the required causal assumptions explicit for deliberation and validation alongside statistical data.[33]

Judgment — The field agrees on a disciplined division of labour, not a single hierarchy of evidence. Cross-case analysis identifies patterns, estimates effects under explicit assumptions, maps populations, and guides case choice. Within-case evidence reconstructs mechanisms and probes the assumptions—selection into treatment, confounding, measurement, post-treatment conditioning, and contextual scope—that determine whether those patterns deserve a causal interpretation.[2]

The main disagreement concerns inferential authority. Bayesian advocates seek a unified likelihood-based evaluation of cross-case and within-case observations; Checkel challenges the transparency and inductive suitability of numerical priors and likelihoods.[9][16] QCA-oriented designs endorse follow-up tracing, but methodological-alignment critiques warn that configurational counterfactual claims and mechanism-centred explanation can pull case selection and inference in different directions.[62][26] The defensible conclusion is conditional integration: use each method to test the other’s vulnerabilities, generalize mechanisms only across demonstrated scope conditions, and treat disagreement between methods as evidence of a causal puzzle rather than a reason to privilege one method automatically.[28][2]

The empirical transportability of mechanisms across democratic breakdowns remains open: repeated tracing in comparable cases and cross-case estimates with explicit effect-heterogeneity tests would establish how far particular pathways generalize. The relative reliability of numerical Bayesian priors and likelihoods in inductive field-based process tracing also remains unresolved; transparent comparisons of competing codings and predictions would clarify whether formalization improves inference rather than only redescribes judgment.
