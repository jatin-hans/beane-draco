# Adaptive Input UX for Musicians with Hand Tremor in DAWs

## Executive summary

For musicians with essential tremor or Parkinson’s disease, the most defensible current approach is to separate tremor-tolerant DAW editing from expressive performance control, then calibrate filtering individually and no stronger than necessary.

Ableton Live’s Velocity effect is documented MIDI value remapping or gating, not temporal tremor smoothing, and it can reject low-velocity accidental notes but cannot preserve or identify vibrato. Logic Pro Smart Controls are parameter-mapping controls, not documented tremor compensation, so any tremor rejection must come from the controller, operating system, or external MIDI processing.

SteadyMouse can stabilise mouse use for DAW navigation and editing through adjustable cursor filtering and accidental-click protection, but it does not process MIDI or distinguish intentional musical vibrato. The strongest directly relevant conclusion is that fixed smoothing and threshold rules trade fewer unintended inputs for lost fast expression and added lag, while musician-specific measurements of false-positive rejection, creative flow, composition completion, satisfaction, and transfer back to standard interfaces remain unresolved.

## Why tremor-aware DAW input is an open UX problem

Evidence assessed in this section is current to 19 November 2026. It prioritises peer-reviewed research and preprints, followed by reviews and named research-group pages; vendor documentation is used to establish the documented functions of Ableton Live, Logic Pro, and SteadyMouse. Tremor-aware digital audio workstation (DAW) input concerns more than making a cursor steadier.[1][2][3][4][5][6] A DAW is software used to record, edit, arrange, and mix music; it receives performance data from keyboards, pads, faders, touchscreens, cameras, and other controllers.

For a musician with essential tremor (ET) or Parkinson’s disease, the same hand movement can be a barrier to operating the DAW, a source of unwanted MIDI events, or part of a chosen performance technique.

Judgment — The open design problem is therefore an intent-preservation problem. An adaptive system must reduce movement that the musician does not want represented in the music while retaining movement that they deliberately use for timing, dynamics, pitch, timbre, or vibrato. Vibrato is a controlled periodic change in pitch, loudness, or timbre used to shape a sustained sound. It can resemble the oscillatory patterns that tremor filters seek to attenuate.

### Which adaptive input systems and DAW practices are relevant to

Essential tremor is clinically defined as bilateral upper-limb postural or kinetic tremor, meaning shaking while holding a position or during voluntary movement, with at least a three-year history in the proposed syndrome definition. The definition allows head or other tremor but excludes isolated head and isolated voice tremor. This matters for DAW work because keyboard, mouse, fader, touchscreen, and controller actions are generally voluntary upper-limb movements.[7]

For Parkinson’s disease, tremor can cause extra keystrokes and reduced mouse control, while bradykinesia means slowed movement.[8][7] General computer-access practices therefore include Bounce Keys or Filter Keys for repeated keyboard events, macOS Slow Keys, larger-key keyboards, trackballs, Mouse Keys, on-screen keyboards, voice navigation, and dwell-click software. These techniques reduce the precision or speed required for ordinary desktop control rather than interpreting musical meaning in a MIDI performance.[8]

For musicians, these desktop adaptations matter because much composition work occurs outside live note entry: selecting clips, moving notes, adjusting plug-in parameters, drawing automation, setting loop boundaries, and navigating instrument libraries all depend on conventional graphical-user-interface control. A DAW also permits the separation of performance from editing. MIDI represents musical events as editable data—such as note pitch, time position, duration, and velocity—so a performer can record a rough take, correct unintended notes or timing afterward, and retain the intended musical material.[9][10]

Ableton Live documents MIDI clips as musical scores containing pitch, length, position, and dynamics, with dynamics termed velocity in MIDI. It allows clips to be created by recording on a MIDI track or through MIDI capture, and Live 12 allows timing quantization during recording as well as later editing. These are relevant post-entry practices for a musician who can generate an idea in real time but finds precision difficult under time pressure.[11][12]

Logic Pro similarly supports software-instrument recording, note-velocity editing, Piano Roll grid snapping, and fine movement in tick steps. Its Piano Roll can snap edits to bars, beats, divisions, ticks, frames, or note values; a tick is 1/3840 of a beat. Relative snapping can preserve a note’s offset from the grid rather than forcing absolute alignment, while directional dragging can constrain a note movement to horizontal or vertical movement and avoid accidental pitch or timing changes.[13][14][15]

These editing practices are not minor fallback options. A tremor-friendly gamepad-MIDI guide frames step sequencing, drawing automation, editing a take, and comping across several passes as standard professional production methods, because they shift precision from a difficult real-time motor action into revision of stored symbolic data.[16] The same guide recommends input quantization, in which incoming notes are snapped to a timing grid as they are recorded; coarser grids provide more timing tolerance.[16]

Judgment — In accessibility terms, this is task redistribution. Instead of requiring a single motor action to simultaneously create the correct note, velocity, timing, controller curve, and sound parameter, the DAW can divide the task across recording, quantization, automation, and editing. That improves access when live execution is the limiting factor, but it changes the immediacy of performance and is unsuitable when the continuous gesture itself is the musical material.

Accessible Digital Musical Instruments (ADMIs) are instruments designed or adapted to support inclusive music-making. Their research spans music therapy, education, engineering, disability studies, and participatory design. Grond, Shikako-Thomas, and Lewis’s 2020 Canadian Journal of Disability Studies article 《Adaptive Musical Instruments (AMIs): Past, Present, and Future Research Directions》 identified both intervention-oriented work with therapeutic or pedagogic aims and work focused on the social and creative value of collective improvisation.[23]

The MAMI Tech Toolkit is one example of participatory accessible-instrument development. Ward (2023, Frontiers in Computer Science, 'The development of a Modular Accessible Musical Instrument Technology Toolkit using action research')’s 2023 Frontiers in Computer Science study describes four tools developed across five years through action research involving stakeholders at four research sites.[24] The project used stakeholder involvement and gathered data to inform an iterative, participatory design process.[24]

AUMI, the Adaptive Use Musical Instrument, represents another broad-access approach. It was designed as an affordable alternative to instruments designed only for normative bodies and supports performance, listening, improvisation, and collaboration. Its relevance here is not tremor filtering; it is the principle that access can come from redesigning what counts as a playable musical action, rather than requiring a user to approximate conventional instrumental technique.[25]

The EyeHarp takes that principle further by replacing hand action with gaze. It was designed to enable people with severe motor disabilities to learn, perform, and compose music using gaze as the sole control mechanism. Its pilot evaluation involved 39 participants without disabilities: eight took part in a playing session, and 31 evaluated concert performance qualitatively. The reported result was a steep learning curve alongside expressive performance from performer and audience perspectives.[26]

Hands-free accessible-instrument research provides a wider taxonomy of alternative channels. It discusses design for performers who cannot use limbs and examines head, mouth, eye, and brain interaction channels, along with their respective possibilities and limitations for musical interaction. These channels reduce reliance on the tremor-affected hand, but they also entail new skills, mappings, and expressive constraints.[27]

Movement-based systems can preserve a player’s agency through physical layout and mapping rather than through signal suppression. The Peripersonal Modular Interface’s independent tiles allow the instrument to be placed where a performer can reach it, and its compliant sensing surface can be tuned without changing the robustness of the Hall-effect sensor. Its reported haptic–audio loop averaged approximately 7 ms and remained below 12 ms at maximum, alongside participant reports of immediate sonic response.[21]

A second class of systems adapts movement-to-sound mapping. AirSticks 1.0 used custom AirSticks MIDI Software and Ableton Live mapping templates; the software could transmit Note On and Note Off messages, including velocity. AirSticks 2.0 exposed orientation and linear acceleration through OSC and MIDI, used pointing direction for note selection and sharp movement changes for triggering, and offered sliders that changed how raw motion data became OSC and MIDI messages.[28]

In its documented accessible cases, AirSticks 2.0 used personalised mappings rather than a general tremor classifier. Its software could record an individual performer’s gestural data, play it back through the software, and use the data to design unique mappings around movement preferences. In the BoilOver inclusive theatre ensemble case study, AirSticks 2.0 hardware and software stayed unchanged while mappings were adjusted to performers’ differing movement personalities using recorded and analysed characteristic movement data; the resulting personalised mappings encouraged each performer to interact naturally without adapting to the technology.[28]

One AirSticks 1.0 account states that prepared sound mappings could be operated with large or small gestures, were not affected by physical tremors or shakes, and could be used with limited dexterity. This is an important design contrast: a system can make incidental movement musically harmless by choosing robust mappings, without estimating and subtracting tremor from a continuous control stream.[28]

Judgment — The relevant adaptive-input landscape has three distinct strategies: filter an existing hand-controlled channel; make the target or mapping more tolerant; or move musical control to a different body channel. A musician’s appropriate strategy depends on whether the musical role requires continuous hand nuance. Pointer and target adaptations are useful for arranging and editing. Mapping redesign or alternate input is more suitable when the task is to create sound, notes, or automation in real time.

MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) makes the need to preserve nuance especially clear. MPE is a MIDI Recommended Practice that uses individual MIDI channels, where available, to give each sounding note independent pitch bend, pressure, and timbre-related Control Change 74 control. It permits per-note control of pitch, pressure, and slide rather than applying a channel-wide value to every active note.[29][30]

Ableton Live supports MPE recording and editing. Its MPE workflow attaches parameter-control information to individual notes, supports real-time per-note control from compatible devices, and provides expression lanes for Slide, Pressure, Velocity, and Release Velocity, with Pitch displayed over the corresponding note. Live’s Note Expression tools allow the user to refine per-note pitch bend, slide, pressure, velocity, and release velocity after recording.[31]

MPE’s underlying data model also shows why tremor-aware processing cannot be treated as a simple “delete shaky data” command. The specification says that an MPE note must be treated as an entity with its own timeline of multidimensional control data. When DAWs change note starts, durations, or continuous data, they can unintentionally alter the control data attached to the note, leaving software designers with the task of determining musical intention.[29][30]

MPE-compatible instruments make intentional continuous movement central to performance. ROLI describes MPE as supporting independent bends and slides for every note of a chord, volume swells through upward sliding or increased pressure, and per-note variation in sound, effects, and filters. These motions may contain fast, small variations that a generic smoothing process could attenuate.[32]

The practical implication is that accessible DAW support cannot be defined only by whether a musician can trigger notes. It must also account for the route by which a performer enters expressive control and for whether the user wants the controller’s fine movement represented as sound. This makes possible a clearer division of labour: standard mouse filtering can support editing, while expressive controllers and adaptable musical mappings can support performance; neither should silently substitute for the other.[6][18][31][28]

In the reported EMG gesture-to-MIDI evaluation, the spread between the best-performing gesture classes (100%) and the two lowest-performing, similar muscle-pattern classes (60%) was 40 percentage points. The gap shows how strongly gesture separability can depend on the particular movement class even within one musical-input system.[22]

SteadyMouse filters mouse-cursor movement, whereas Ableton Live processes incoming MIDI data; these are different input paths.[6][4] Pointer-path filtering and target disambiguation support DAW navigation and editing; MIDI event processing changes recorded or incoming musical messages; parameter mapping changes the reach, range, or number of controls needed to shape sound; and alternative instruments replace or redesign the bodily channel used for performance. These are complementary adaptation loci rather than interchangeable forms of tremor compensation.[1][17][6][4][5][26][33]

The practical starting point is to map an action to its input path, because a tremor accommodation that helps one path does not necessarily help another.

Quantisation changes the timing assigned to notes; post-recording editing changes stored MIDI data; mapping changes the relationship between a control and a musical parameter; and alternative access changes the route by which the musician supplies a command. These interventions can be combined, but they address different actions.[16][9][5][33]

In addition to oscillation, Parkinson’s disease can affect DAW control through reduced movement amplitude, rigidity, impaired fine targeting, and medication-dependent variation. Relevant hardware adaptations include stable mounting, larger controls, alternate switches, foot controls, low-force keys, and configurable MIDI controllers.

Gesture-recognition research identifies an additional adaptation locus: continuous custom recognition. The Dollar General (TDG) filters, segments, and recognises custom gestures in continuous input using few training samples per gesture class; its design question is which command, if any, a motion signal was intended to communicate. Its pipeline separates incoming data into candidate gestures before a recogniser classifies or rejects each candidate.[34]

TDG identifies three requirements directly relevant to an adaptive musical controller: segment the continuous stream, set rejection criteria that distinguish gesture from non-gesture sequences, and keep the process interactive. Its account also identifies variability in speed, shape, scale, skew, position, duration, orientation, and noise as consequences of physical and emotional state, cognitive load, skill, environmental factors, sensing, and hardware or software error.[34]

Clinical tremor-control devices offer a distinct movement-level strategy. A systematic review classifies non-pharmacological essential-tremor devices as orthoses using electrical stimulation of opposing muscles, biomechanical loading devices, exoskeleton robots with tremor-suppression control, and external tremor-cancellation devices; all reviewed devices targeted upper-limb tremor. The review specifies a hierarchical policy: detect intended voluntary movement, estimate tremor amplitude, frequency, and phase, then determine the affected joints and muscles, using position, velocity, and acceleration sensing with minimum delay.[35]

Examples show that voluntary-motion preservation is an operational design goal. Loureiro and colleagues used kinematic sensing and controlled viscous damping to discriminate desired from undesired motion; their prototype drew on biomechanical data from 33 people with tremor and was applied to one person with essential tremor. Hernstadt and colleagues’ voluntary-driven, speed-controlled orthosis produced a 94% mean tremor-power reduction in computerized pursuit tracking by nine people with essential tremor or Parkinson’s disease, with no significant impact on voluntary movements.[35][36]

### Why is unintended input filtering versus intentional vibrato gestures an

The action-to-input-path map identifies two different error classes. Keyboard and pad input can produce repeated Note On events; pointer editing can produce unintended clicks or unwanted note movement; and continuous expressive controls can produce rapid oscillation in Control Change, pitch-bend, pressure, or timbre data. A tremor-aware workflow must select an adaptation for the affected path without treating all movement as unwanted.[16][6][15][29][31]

A discrete-event filter can prevent retriggering after an accidental activation. A re-trigger lockout is a window after a trigger during which the same input cannot fire again. The cited guide gives 150 ms as an example that stops many involuntary repeats, but at that duration the control can fire only about six times per second; a hi-hat part in sixteenth notes at 120 beats per minute needs eight triggers per second.[16]

This illustrates a basic musical cost. A lockout that prevents accidental doubles also removes intended fast repetitions. The guide’s proposed solution for that case is not more aggressive filtering; it is moving the fast part to sequencing or a more reliable input. That recommendation recognises musical structure as part of the accessibility decision: a part requiring dense, rapid repetition may be better represented as an editable sequence than as live button triggering.[16]

Hysteresis is another common method for reducing false state changes near a threshold. It uses one threshold to turn a control on and a lower threshold to turn it off. The gamepad-MIDI example turns a note on at 60% of travel and turns it off only below 40%, with a suggested 15–20% gap between thresholds.[16]

Hysteresis can prevent repeated on–off switching when tremor moves a control around one boundary, but it makes the transition less immediate and less precise near that boundary. In musical terms, the user must make a larger movement to cross the activation point and may experience the controller as less responsive. The technique is most suitable for discrete state changes, scene launches, or switches; it is less suitable when the crossing itself needs to be finely expressive.[16]

Output smoothing addresses continuous variation. The guide defines it as a low-pass filter: instead of transmitting the current controller value, the system sends an average of recent values. Rapid oscillation averages toward a stable line, whereas slow deliberate movement passes through more substantially; the unavoidable cost is latency approximately equal to the averaging window.[16]

Judgment — A low-pass filter is a signal-processing method that attenuates rapid changes while retaining slower changes. In tremor-aware musical control, it is not an intent detector. It makes a prediction about which temporal features should matter musically, then delays and attenuates the others. That is useful when unwanted movement is faster than the desired musical gesture, but the method fails when their time scales overlap.

The gamepad-MIDI guide states this limitation directly: filtering cannot distinguish intent when tremor and deliberate movement occupy the same speed range, because smoothing removes fast movement whether it is unwanted or intentional. It recommends the weakest smoothing that removes visible oscillation, applying it only to affected axes and testing a fast gesture for unacceptable lag.[16]

That trade-off appears in general tremor-assistance systems. SteadyMouse filters hand-tremor motion before it reaches the cursor and offers a strength control. Moving toward weaker filtering makes the mouse more responsive but lets more tremor pass; moving toward stronger filtering improves tremor removal but increases lag. The manual’s recommended calibration is the weakest setting that still removes the user’s tremor.[6]

SteadyMouse also offers multiple filter sets. Feather and Classic are described as snappy; Adagio as smoother but laggier; and Allegro as the default medium balance between tremor removal and cursor responsiveness. Its user interface therefore exposes the response-versus-stability trade-off as a user choice rather than assuming a single correct filter.[6]

Its accidental-click protection uses a different strategy. The software ignores a click unless the user holds the button until a visual indicator is green; releases while the indicator is yellow or red are ignored.

This duration rule is well matched to a mouse click because the intended action can reasonably be defined as a held press. It cannot be directly transferred to musical vibrato, pitch bend, aftertouch, modulation, or continuous fader movement. Those gestures are defined by their evolving trajectory, rate, direction, amplitude, timing relative to a note, and the musical context, rather than by a single held-button duration.[6][4][31]

Musical expression also changes the interpretation of the same MIDI message. In an Ardour user report, unwanted vibrato on a piano sound was traced to Channel Pressure, also called aftertouch. A respondent explained that aftertouch provides useful expression with a string sound but is typically not useful with a conventional piano-type sound; the user solved the immediate problem by filtering Channel Pressure.[37]

The solution removed an unwanted modulation in that particular piano setup. It also demonstrates why message-level blocking is a blunt instrument: Channel Pressure can be a desired expressive input for another sound. Gig Performer’s MIDI Filter similarly permits pitch bend, aftertouch, polyphonic key aftertouch, program change, and control-change messages to be passed, blocked, or remapped, while Note On and Note Off can be passed or blocked.[38]

Message blocking has operational consequences. Gig Performer warns that blocking a note before its Note Off is sent can leave a hanging note, requiring a Panic command to silence all notes. Thus, aggressive filtering can create a new form of performance disruption even when it prevents the original unwanted event.[38]

Ableton Live provides MIDI value processing rather than a tremor-recognition layer. Its Range and Lowest controls define accepted input velocities, while Out Hi and Out Low define outgoing values. In Clip mode, out-of-range values are constrained to the configured range; in Gate mode, they are removed; in Fixed mode, every note is output at Out Hi.[4]

Velocity is the dynamics of a MIDI note: Live defines it as the note’s loudness and records it when a key or pad is pressed. The Velocity effect can therefore reduce accidental soft hits by gating them, constrain dynamic range through clipping, or make dynamics uniform through fixed output. It acts on Note On velocity, Note Off release velocity, or both, depending on the selected Operation setting.[11][4]

These are potentially useful accommodations for note triggering. A performer who produces occasional low-velocity accidental pad strikes could set a lower velocity bound and gate below it. A performer whose force is inconsistent could use Clip or Fixed mode to make output more predictable. A performer can also edit recorded velocity later in Live’s Velocity Editor, draw ramps, or use Draw Mode to shape many note values together.[4][11]

The Velocity effect is not temporal smoothing. It compares velocity values to a configured input range; it does not estimate the frequency of a tremor, reconstruct a voluntary trajectory, or classify pitch-bend oscillation as intentional vibrato. Its Gate mode removes notes whose attack velocity lies outside the range, while vibrato is usually conveyed through pitch bend, pressure, timbre control, audio modulation, or an instrument-specific control route.[4][31][39]

Logic Pro’s Smart Controls address a different interaction layer: they provide configurable access to track and plug-in parameters, rather than tremor compensation or a tremor-versus-vibrato classifier.[40][41] A single Smart Control can change one or more mapped parameters, such as waveform, resonance, filter cutoff, articulation, EQ, reverb, or other effects.[5][14]

Logic supports automatic assignment of knobs, faders, sliders, buttons, drum pads, and pedals on supported USB MIDI controllers to Smart Controls and other functions. Users can accept Auto Assign on first connection or decline it and use the device simply as a MIDI controller. Expert view permits advanced controller assignments, controller zones, modes, direct editing of port and MIDI input message fields, and mapping-parameter values.[19][42]

Smart Controls can therefore consolidate a complex parameter set into a smaller number of larger, more legible, or more conveniently located controls. Parameter mapping graphs can scale values after a mapping is created, and multiple mappings can be reordered, with the top mapping determining the screen-control value. This is a strong accessibility mechanism for reducing interface navigation and matching control range to a musician’s comfortable movement range.[43][44]

Logic also offers MIDI plug-ins that generate continuous controller, aftertouch, and pitch-bend messages. Its Modulator includes a syncable LFO and a Delay/Attack/Hold/Release envelope; either source can output a continuous controller, aftertouch, a plug-in parameter, or pitch bend, including stepped modulation. These tools can create vibrato-like modulation synthetically or automate it, which can reduce the need for a tremor-affected hand to produce a steady continuous oscillation live.[13]

Judgment — Automation and generated modulation are accessibility-relevant because they separate musical result from motor production. A musician can decide that a vibrato curve belongs in the composition while choosing whether it should come from live gesture, an LFO, an edited MPE curve, or a recorded automation pass. This preserves authorship of the expressive decision even when the physical gesture is unreliable.

A musician with Parkinson’s can also consciously transform an oscillatory movement into expression. In an interview, Jean Martinez reported that tremors made it difficult to hold down guitar strings. She learned keyboard and described oscillating wrist motions as producing a jazzy flourish. This account shows that movement resembling tremor may have an intentional musical role in a particular instrument and technique.[45]

Other Parkinson’s musicians described changing their instrumental or environmental setup rather than suppressing every impaired movement. Paul Barr switched from three-finger banjo to bass guitar because the latter generally required one string at a time. Ron Wincek described tremor that sometimes stopped once he began playing saxophone, moved closer to drums and bass to support timing, used a foot pedal to turn pages on a tablet, and investigated electronic woodwinds with alternative key spacing because hand twisting made smaller-instrument spacing difficult.[45]

These examples support a broader interaction principle: the meaning of movement depends on instrument, sound, part, and performance context. A tremor-aware DAW should give musicians a way to choose when a channel is filtered, remapped, automated, sequenced, or left expressive. That preserves both error prevention and the possibility that non-normative movement becomes part of a musician’s technique.[45][16]

This trade-off produces false rejection when a filter discards an intended expressive gesture and false acceptance when unwanted movement passes through. The same calibration can work differently as posture, fatigue, and medication state change.

### How have digital audio workstations

DAWs and MIDI systems have developed substantial tools for recording, manipulating, and routing musical control data. MIDI is a protocol and interface standard for connecting electronic instruments, computers, and related devices. Every action on a key, button, knob, or slider can become a MIDI event carrying musical instructions such as pitch, timing, velocity, or control values; DAWs then sequence and edit those events.[10]

This architecture supports accessibility because it separates input source from musical destination. UniMIDI Hub, for example, accepts input from MIDI devices, typing keyboards, eye trackers, and touchscreens, then sends MIDI to DAWs and instruments. Other accessibility-oriented systems use switches, facial expression, voice control, eye tracking, or camera-based hand gestures to create notes, chords, sequences, samples, and sound controls.[33]

MIDI 2.0 infrastructure adds the possibility of dynamic configuration. MIDI Capability Inquiry and Profiles allow compliant devices and applications to discover supported capabilities, while Property Exchange can expose supported parameters, names, and value ranges. In principle, this reduces manual mapping between an adaptive controller and a DAW or instrument.[20][46]

That protocol flexibility is valuable for accessibility setup. It does not itself determine whether a fast pitch change is tremor, vibrato, a deliberate slide, or an accidental movement. The intent problem remains at the level of controller sensing, adaptive mapping, and user-configured performance policy.[46][30][16]

Tremor engineering provides methods that could inform that policy. An extended Kalman filter (EKF) can estimate tremor and voluntary motion simultaneously from measured movement data, while also estimating tremor parameters. The authors of *Filtering voluntary motion for pathological tremor compensation* describe the algorithm as suitable for direct application in real-time tremor-compensation systems.[3][47]

Frequency-adaptive approaches make a similar distinction. The online censoring weighted-frequency Fourier linear combiner (OC-WFLC) extracts informative frequency information and combines it with a Fourier linear combiner to decompose a pathological hand-tremor measurement into tremor and voluntary-movement components. Wavelet-based research has also proposed a multi-rate, auto-adjustable wavelet decomposition framework with hyper-parameter adjustment and real-time tremor prediction.[48][49]

Neural approaches have reported high performance in tremor-suppression settings. A task- and user-independent Parkinson’s model reported 99.2% average estimation accuracy; future voluntary-motion prediction declined from 97.0% at 10 steps ahead to 89.9% at 100 steps ahead, with a stated 1.5 ms prediction time at the 100-step horizon. In its real-time experimental setup, the model reported tremor reduction of 93.8% ± 1.5%, 25% greater than a weighted Fourier linear combiner comparator.[50]

A motorized elbow orthosis reported a mean tremor-power reduction of 94.4%, with p < 0.001. Its design goal was to suppress tremor while leaving voluntary movement available. Similarly, wearable-robot and neuroprosthesis research frames successful tremor compensation as applying biomechanical loads or stimulation that counteracts tremor while leaving concomitant voluntary movement unmodified.[51][52]

These are powerful demonstrations of voluntary-versus-pathological-motion separation. They concern measured motion in clinical, rehabilitation, orthosis, and assistive-device settings. The musical case adds an additional variable: deliberately oscillatory voluntary movement can be the target output rather than a disturbance. MPE pitch bend, aftertouch, slide, mod-wheel movement, and continuous fader movement may all be the material of expression.[3][48][52][29][4][31]

A frequency-only rule is therefore inadequate for music.Judgment — Pathological tremor is often discussed in frequency bands, including the 4–12 Hz range specified in the client question, but pitch vibrato and other musical oscillations can also fall into overlapping rates depending on genre, instrument, sound design, and performance technique. A classifier must use more than rate: it should consider note context, controller identity, directionality, amplitude, onset timing, musical meter, user-selected mode, and an explicit way for the performer to override the filter.

The AirSticks work shows one route toward that broader context. Its Custom AirSticks MIDI Software used Kalman-state-estimation predictive filtering to up-sample gestural analysis to 1 kHz, supporting a close causal association between action and sound. The system also calibrated virtual sliders to small or large gestures, and its Energy parameter used a configurable movement window: at 10 ms, movement had to be maintained to keep energy high; at 1,000 ms, energy built more slowly, was easier to sustain, and decayed more slowly.[28]

Those parameters change the relation between movement and sound. They can make a gesture easier to sustain or require stronger, more persistent movement; they are useful examples of sensitivity design in expressive interfaces. They do not turn the mapping into a diagnostic tremor classifier. The more general lesson is that an adaptive music controller can expose time-window and range decisions to a designer or performer rather than fixing them globally.[28]

Camera-, EMG-, and gesture-recognition systems offer further alternatives. One accessible DMI combines adaptive machine-learning gesture recognition with DAW integration and reported learning-related reductions in errors and mean time per note. An EMG-based musical instrument classifies forearm-muscle features using a decision tree and maps the recognised gesture to a MIDI note for DAW sound production; it reported 82.5% overall classification accuracy over 80 trials, with some gesture classes at 100% and two similar muscle-pattern classes at 60%.[2][22]

The EMG result demonstrates a relevant design difficulty. Similar bodily patterns can produce overlapping signal features, leading to confusion among intended gesture categories. Tremor-versus-vibrato recognition would face an analogous problem whenever intentional oscillation and involuntary motion share observable characteristics. The solution is likely to require personalised training, multimodal features, or performance context rather than a single fixed threshold.[22][53][54]

Fast adaptation is technically feasible in some gesture-recognition systems. The Dollar General’s C++ implementation trained in under 30 seconds with five templates on a 2.3 GHz Intel Core i5 MacBook Pro, and its authors identify fast online training as useful for iterative adjustment of user preferences. Such a workflow could let a musician teach an interface examples of wanted control gestures and examples of movement to ignore, provided the system also gives immediate, reversible feedback about its decision.[53]

Judgment — A musically appropriate tremor-aware capture system should be designed as a negotiated filter, not an invisible corrective layer. First, it should allow separate settings by controller axis and MIDI message type. Second, it should expose its effects audibly and visually while the musician calibrates. Third, it should provide an immediate bypass or “expression priority” mode for passages where vibrato, rapid modulation, or fast repetition is intentional. Fourth, it should preserve raw input for later recovery whenever recording storage permits.

DAWs already contain many elements of this architecture, but distributed across separate functions. Ableton permits velocity remapping and gating, MPE recording and editing, manual drawing of expression curves, and configurable CC controls. Logic Pro permits controller assignment, mapping graphs, advanced controller zones and modes, Smart Controls, input-message inspection, and MIDI plug-ins that generate modulation. SteadyMouse supplies a separately configurable mouse filter and a system-wide toggle.[4][31][42][43][13][6]

The unresolved UX task is integration. A musician should not need to infer how a pointer filter, controller map, MIDI gate, MPE editor, plug-in automation lane, and sound-design vibrato control interact during performance. A coherent tremor-aware DAW workflow would make the chosen policy explicit: which stream is filtered, which gestures are retained, what latency is introduced, and how the musician can revise or override the result.[16][29][30]

### Synthesis

**Tremor-aware DAW input is open because accessibility systems currently solve different parts of the problem at different layers.** Pointer tools such as APSS, Touch Guard, and SteadyMouse increase control by smoothing trajectories, enlarging or disambiguating targets, and rejecting accidental clicks; accessible instruments redesign the input channel, physical placement, or movement-to-sound mapping; and DAWs offer editable MIDI, mapping, automation, velocity conditioning, and per-note MPE expression.[1][17][6][2][21][4][31]

The conflict is musical rather than merely technical. Stronger filtering can remove unwanted oscillation and repeated triggers, but it adds lag or suppresses rapid intended movement. MPE, aftertouch, pitch bend, faders, and performers’ own adaptive techniques all make continuous variation part of expression. The supported direction is therefore personalised, reversible, context-sensitive adaptation: filter the control stream that needs help, preserve or automate the stream that carries musical intention, and let the musician decide which is which.[16][45][37][28]

## Established movement and musical-input characteristics

Judgment — Real-time MIDI capture turns bodily movement into musical control data. For a musician with tremor, the hard interaction problem is not simply removing rapid variation: the same movement channel can carry an unwanted oscillation, intentional vibrato—periodic pitch variation—intentional tremolo—periodic loudness variation—a pressure swell, or a timbral gesture.[55][29][32] The relevant distinction is therefore between movement signal characteristics and musical intent, which are related but not interchangeable.

### What characteristics of pathological tremor (4–12 Hz) and musical expression

Tremor is clinically defined as an involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part. The International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society distinguishes it from usually imperceptible physiological tremor: pathological tremor is normally visible and persistent, whereas physiological tremor commonly becomes noticeable only when enhanced by factors such as fatigue or anxiety. Motion transducers and electromyography (EMG)—recording of electrical activity from muscles—are the most accurate clinical means of measuring tremor frequency.[7]

The requested 4–12 Hz range describes approximately four to twelve oscillations per second and is not a unique signature of pathological movement.[7][56][57] The Movement Disorder Society notes that most pathological tremors occur at 4–8 Hz and that the central neurogenic component of physiological tremor is commonly 8–12 Hz; it explicitly cautions that frequency is generally of limited diagnostic value outside particular cases.[7] A real-time MIDI system that treats all 4–12 Hz movement as unwanted would consequently remove some non-pathological movement as well as some clinically relevant tremor.

A clinical movement-analysis study describes most Parkinsonian and essential tremors as occurring between 4 and 12 Hz, while placing the wider spectrum of tremor disorders at approximately 0.5–18 Hz.[57]

The principal clinical contrast between Parkinsonian and essential tremor matters directly to interface design:

- **Parkinsonian tremor:** Parkinson’s disease hand tremor is commonly described as rest tremor at 4–6 Hz. It often increases under mental stress or during contralateral movement, and usually diminishes or disappears, at least temporarily, during goal-directed voluntary action. It can later re-emerge when a new posture is held. [56][7]

- **Essential tremor (ET):** ET is an action tremor, meaning that it can appear during maintained posture against gravity or during voluntary movement. Its reported frequency range is 4–12 Hz, and the proposed syndrome definition includes bilateral upper-limb postural or kinetic tremor. [56][7][58]

In Gallego and colleagues’ real-time gyroscope study of five patients, two essential-tremor cases showed 7 Hz postural tremor and 4 Hz kinetic tremor; the participant with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease showed 3–4 Hz rest tremor and 6 Hz postural tremor.[113]

- **Task-specific musician tremor:** Tremor can also emerge only during a highly trained musical task. In a cohort of 23 musicians with task-specific tremor, tremor began while playing, remained unilateral, did not develop into rest tremor, and affected no posture other than the playing posture; mean measured frequency was 6.5 ± 0.9 Hz. [59] In four professional violinists with task-specific tremor, wrist-muscle coactivation associated with bowing tremor occurred at 3–8 Hz and was absent in four age-matched healthy controls. [60]

In that series, 11 of 23 musicians (48%) reported that tremor spread to other activities between one and six years after its onset during playing.[59]

These distinctions mean that movement onset can be informative for Parkinsonian rest tremor but cannot be used as a general declaration that voluntary musical input is tremor-free. ET can occur while the player is deliberately moving, and task-specific tremor can occur only in the musical activity the interface is intended to support. **For adaptive MIDI design, “action” is therefore not a reliable proxy for “intentional,” and “oscillation” is not a reliable proxy for “unwanted.”**[56][59]

Frequency content also varies substantially between people and body segments. A 2024 inertial-sensor study of 18 people with ET found that upper-limb involuntary activity most often had peak frequencies from 4 Hz to about 9 Hz, with lower side-band peaks at 1–6 Hz and upper side-band peaks at 7–15 Hz. Every participant showed an 8–12 Hz component, but its magnitude ranged from dominant to very small relative to the main peak.[58] The same study observed dominant peaks of roughly 3.5–5 Hz in some participants and 6–9 Hz in others, attributing the variation possibly to age or to a stronger physiological-tremor contribution during posture maintenance.[58]

This evidence creates an important apparent conflict in simple band-based filtering. ET is clinically described at 4–12 Hz, yet the 2024 spectral study treated minor 4–6 Hz peaks as a possible pathological component and 8–12 Hz peaks as a possible physiological component; its participants nevertheless all had an 8–12 Hz component.[56][58] The practical conclusion is not that either range is irrelevant. It is that a frequency band is a feature to weigh with other information, rather than a complete classifier of involuntary movement.

Amplitude, regularity, and spectral structure add useful information. In the ET sensor study, amplitude represented severity of involuntary movement, while regularity helped identify motor fluctuations or distinguish periods with similar amplitudes. The researchers extracted approximate entropy—regularity and predictability over time—mean absolute value for signal magnitude, power spectral density (PSD), peak power, and peak frequency.[58] They also found strong positive correlations between amplitude-sensitive sensor features and clinical severity scores, and strong negative correlations between peak frequency and clinical scores: more severe, higher-amplitude tremor tended to have lower dominant frequency.[58] Such features can characterize a movement pattern more richly than a single Hz cutoff; they still do not identify whether a musician meant a particular modulation as expression.

One clinical tremor algorithm treats a likely neurological tremor peak as narrow and symmetric: it rejects peaks wider than 2 Hz or asymmetrical peaks and interprets peaks above 10 Hz as tremor absence.[114]

Movement context is likewise informative. Clinical classification uses body distribution, activation condition, frequency, associated signs, and sometimes laboratory measurements, not frequency alone.[7] The activation conditions include rest, postural, kinetic, intention, task-specific, and position-specific tremor.

Kinetic tremor may remain roughly constant through a movement, whereas intention tremor increases as a body part approaches a visual target.[7] For MIDI interaction, comparable context includes the controller dimension being used, the current note state, whether the player is sustaining a posture or traversing a control surface, and the musical role assigned to that control.

Coordination across limbs and muscles offers another possible feature class. In the 1998 *Brain* study, muscles within a single extremity generally had the same instantaneous tremor frequency across Parkinson’s disease, ET, and psychogenic-tremor groups. Frequency dissociation—defined there as a contemporaneous difference greater than 0.1 Hz between extremities—occurred in 17 people with Parkinson’s disease and four with ET, but in none of the psychogenic-tremor group.[61] Tremor frequency also changed under voluntary activity: in Parkinson’s disease and ET it remained different from the tapping limb’s frequency but, in many cases, shifted by at least 0.3 Hz from the no-tapping condition.

In one Parkinson’s case, arm and leg tremors initially at 5.2 Hz and 3.8 Hz shifted to 4.6 Hz during opposite-arm tapping at a 2 Hz metronome.[61]

The implication is that voluntary activity can coexist with, suppress, or modulate tremor. It follows that an intent-sensitive system should model the player’s particular task and control channel instead of assuming that fast oscillation is independent noise. This matters especially because Parkinsonian rest tremor can subside at movement onset, whereas ET remains compatible with postural and kinetic action.[7][56][61]

Musical input supplies a second source of oscillation and continuous variation. MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) is a MIDI method that assigns a sounding note its own channel where possible, allowing Pitch Bend, Channel Pressure, and Control Change 74—normally mapped to timbre—to be addressed to that individual note.[29] The MPE specification leaves the physical gesture mapping to controller implementors, but identifies lateral movement on a continuous playing surface as an example of pitch control and pressure or finger movement toward and away from the player as examples of timbre control.[30] In MPE practice, left/right finger motion can be a performed vibrato gesture, while repeated pressure change can create tremolo.[62]

These controls are not marginal embellishments. They are the means by which an MPE controller can make each note of a chord bend, slide, swell in volume, vary timbre, or control effects separately.[32][63] A note’s pitch gesture may therefore be a rapid lateral oscillation producing vibrato; a pressure oscillation may produce tremolo, periodic loudness variation, or may modulate timbre.

The same temporal pattern that a tremor filter could classify as high-frequency jitter can thus be an intentional element of performance.[55][29][30][32]

Ableton Live’s Audio Effect Reference illustrates the numerical overlap. Its Auto Shift effect retains more natural vocal vibrato when pitch transitions are smoothed, and its vibrato-rate control spans 2–15 Hz.[39] That range includes the requested 4–12 Hz tremor range.

This is not evidence that a performer’s vibrato and tremor are physiologically identical; it demonstrates that frequency overlap is musically normal in a software environment that supports vibrato. A 4–12 Hz rejection rule would therefore be liable to suppress a musically plausible vibrato-rate signal merely because it falls inside a clinical tremor band.

MIDI representation also separates a key attack from continuous expression. A MIDI note-on includes pitch and velocity, where velocity represents the forcefulness of the key strike; note-off ends the note.[10] By contrast, MPE permits continuous, note-specific Pitch Bend, Channel Pressure, and CC 74 over a note’s lifetime.[29][63] This distinction matters for filtering: debouncing repeated discrete note-ons is a different problem from smoothing continuous pitch, pressure, or timbre data. A method that changes continuous controller values can alter expressive contour even where it leaves note timing and velocity untouched.

Some musical context is not readily visible in movement data at all. A musician’s intentional timing can include agogics—use of timing and stress to emphasize notes—and performers reporting flow described becoming more elastic in their timing.[64] In a separate study of music performance, college musicians identified improvisation, absence of anxiety and mistakes, emotional connection, and dynamic shaping among conditions conducive to flow.[64] The relevant point for tremor-aware capture is that expressive performance is not reducible to maximally smooth or mechanically regular output.

Judgment — The useful technical distinction is an *intent gap*: a sensor can measure frequency, amplitude, regularity, trajectory, and pressure, but those features do not by themselves state whether the player wanted the resulting musical change. An interface narrows that gap by combining signal features with task context—for example, whether pitch bend is armed for an active note, whether the player has selected an expressive mode, whether a gesture follows a controllable musical contour, and whether its parameters match that player’s calibrated tremor pattern.

Biomedical engineering provides several real-time separation methods, but they solve a narrower problem: estimating a voluntary component and a tremor component of recorded motion. The 2009 IROS paper *Filtering voluntary motion for pathological tremor compensation* uses an extended Kalman filter (EKF) to estimate voluntary motion, tremor, and tremor parameters from measured movement. Its evaluation compared tremor-patient data sets collected during spiral drawing.[47] The 2017 GlobalSIP paper *A multi-rate and auto-adjustable wavelet decomposition framework for pathological hand tremor extraction* adaptively decomposes measured movement into approximation levels and uses polynomial extrapolation to predict the desired next signal value.[49] The 2021 OC-WFLC method adaptively extracts informative frequency information and uses it with a Fourier linear combiner to decompose a hand-tremor signal into pathological-tremor and voluntary-movement components.[48]

Other approaches learn the relationship between voluntary and involuntary movement. The 2021 study *Real-Time Voluntary Motion Prediction and Parkinson’s Tremor Reduction Using Deep Neural Networks* characterizes Parkinsonian tremor as nonstationary, nonlinear, and stochastic, and proposes a neural model that learns correlations and nonlinearities in tremor and voluntary motion while making multistep predictions with minimal delay.[50] A gyroscope-based real-time system first estimates voluntary wrist movement, subtracts it from raw motion to estimate tremor, then estimates tremor amplitude and frequency; its authors assumed voluntary motion at 0–2 Hz and pathological tremor at higher frequencies.[52] That assumption fits tracking tasks but cannot be transferred directly to performed musical vibrato, whose deliberately produced rate may be well above 2 Hz.[52][39]

Clinical machine-learning research further demonstrates why simple thresholds are fragile. A 2023 study of kinematic and EMG signals isolated 4–10 Hz content for one heuristic classifier, then labeled one-second windows using the maximum PSD value and a global threshold. Its authors note that user-defined time- or frequency-feature thresholds require preliminary data and operator experience.[65] In the same study, physiological 8–12 Hz oscillations appeared in raw EMG from healthy people and people with ET; the tested models distinguished them from pathological patterns through differences in frequency and amplitude.[65] The study reported an LSTM classifier F1 score of 0.98 on its raw-EMG tremor classification task, but its classes were Tremor and No Tremor, not tremor and intentional musical vibrato.[65]

The clinically relevant feature set therefore extends beyond frequency: activation context, amplitude, regularity or predictability, PSD peak power, dominant frequency, body distribution, and relationships across muscles or limb segments all contribute useful evidence.[7][58][61] These measures characterize the movement. They do not independently label a MIDI pitch bend, aftertouch gesture, or pressure oscillation as musically intended.

Judgment — A defensible tremor-aware MIDI design should not apply a fixed 4–12 Hz notch filter to expressive controls. It should first separate discrete-event safeguards from continuous-control processing; second, calibrate a player- and controller-specific estimate of tremor features across relevant playing postures; and third, allow the musician to retain, override, or edit the expressive curve. This direction preserves the central trade-off: stronger rejection can reduce involuntary modulation, but it can also remove the fine pitch, pressure, and timbre variation that expressive MIDI exists to carry.

The requested 4–12 Hz working tremor range is entirely contained within Auto Shift’s documented 2–15 Hz Vibrato Rate setting, an 8-Hz overlap.[7][56][39] This overlap compares a working tremor range with an effect setting; it neither classifies a performer’s gesture nor makes every rate in the range pathological. Auto Shift documents pitch-transition smoothing that retains more natural vibrato, not tremor classification.[7][39][56]

A musician’s trained movement can itself contain activity in bands that overlap clinical tremor descriptions. In a 2014 finger flexion-extension study of 19 professional musicians and 24 age-matched non-musicians, musicians had significantly higher 8–12 Hz tremor amplitude during most of flexion and the second half of extension; their mean 4–8 Hz amplitude was also higher in both directions, although not significantly so. The authors related the 8–12 Hz result to motor dexterity through intensive training and did not determine whether the 4–8 Hz increase reflected motor-skill development or maladaptive plasticity.[66] This establishes that oscillatory content in the requested 4–12 Hz region can accompany expert musical motor control, reinforcing that this band cannot serve as an intent label in MIDI capture.[66][56]

Vibrato is a timbral-level musical parameter: a rapid, slight pitch oscillation that produces a richer tone. Digital-musical-instrument mappings may map a physical value continuously onto a musical parameter or map its rate of change through an excitation mapping; the mapping choice affects playability, expressiveness, and enjoyment.[67] Thus, an oscillation in position, pressure, or another physical channel may be the deliberately performed source of pitch vibrato or another continuous musical modulation rather than extraneous variation.[67][30]

A clinical review lists Parkinsonism at 3–6 Hz, including tremor that can re-emerge with posture, and ET at 4–12 Hz; it states that frequency alone is insufficient for diagnosis because the frequency ranges of conditions overlap.[68] The same review reports that tremor onset is unilateral in Parkinson disease and bilateral in ET, and that tremor latency is 8–9 seconds in Parkinson disease and 1–2 seconds in ET. Clinical EMG can document both tremor frequency and burst morphology—the shape and timing of muscle-activity bursts—and differentiate tremor from myoclonus and asterixis.[69][70][71]

Tremor measurement itself has no universally accepted clinical rating or measurement method, and computerized quantitative analysis provides objective, quantifiable data for research and certain clinical settings.[68] For biological motion up to 25 Hz, the clinical review specifies a sampling rate of at least 50 Hz, preferably several times higher, and reports that tremor frequency can be calculated to within 0.1 Hz and displacement amplitude to less than 0.1 mm under the stated recording circumstances.[68]

ET’s 4–12 Hz description is a population-level range. For an individual, ET frequency occupies a narrower range of approximately ±1 Hz and may vary across tasks; action tremor can begin and end abruptly with activity and, when sustained, changes frequency continuously.[72] Spectral-peak estimation typically requires at least 10 oscillations per segment—2.5 seconds for a 4-Hz tremor—and is not suited to brief transients such as intention tremor in a finger-to-nose task.[72] One ET inertial-sensor algorithm used a 4.0-second sliding spectrogram window updated every 0.1 seconds.[72]

That algorithm’s clinical validation recorded sensor data concurrently with clinician ratings from 13 people with classic ET, aged 63 ± 16 years, including nine women; the recordings were saved for offline processing.[72] Its estimates correlated positively with TETRAS performance scores: Spearman ρ = 0.683 for rotational sensor data and ρ = 0.641 for translational sensor data, both p < 0.001.[72]

The professional-musician comparison involved 19 musicians and 24 age-matched non-musicians. The musicians had begun their instruments at age 7.2 ± 2.9 years, had 19.3 ± 7.1 years of training, and practised 3.5 ± 2.0 hours per day.[66]

Observing more oscillation cycles can provide a classifier with a more stable estimate of rate, regularity, and amplitude, but it requires a longer observation window. That added window delays the system’s decision or the filtered control output, creating a discrimination–latency trade-off in real-time musical control.

Real-time MIDI capture can also carry modulation-wheel and pedal data, alongside note events, velocity, pitch bend, pressure/aftertouch, and spatial-controller positions. These input streams differ in sampling rate, value resolution, latency, and sound mapping, so tremor may alter note-attack timing or velocity as well as appearing as continuous controller jitter or rapid pitch variation.

Ableton Live uses timestamps from MIDI-interface drivers to position incoming events on its timeline and distinguishes consistent delay (latency) from inconsistent timing variation (jitter). During monitored recording, Live adds delay based on the audio buffer to align a recorded event with when the musician heard it, rather than when it was physically played; this timing correction does not filter pitch or controller movement.[115]

Debouncing or rejecting repeated discrete note events is consequently a different filtering problem from preserving or smoothing a continuous pitch, pressure, timbre, pedal, or modulation curve.[16]

A voluntary-driven, speed-controlled tremor-rejection method was evaluated with a robotic elbow orthosis in nine people with essential tremor or Parkinson’s disease tested off medication.[51] Participants performed computerized pursuit tracking of sinusoidal and random targets with and without the suppressive orthosis, and the evaluation measured tremor and voluntary movement through relative power change from power spectral density (PSD), velocity-tracking error, and position-tracking error.[51] The orthosis produced no statistically significant effect on relative power change at voluntary frequencies (p = 0.346) or velocity-tracking error (p = 0.283), while the effect on position-tracking error was marginal (p = 0.05).[51]

Dromey and Smith’s 2007 single-participant study directly compared vocal essential tremor and vocal vibrato in one person who exhibited both. Acoustic and laryngeal electromyographic recordings of spoken and sung vowels at three effort levels showed generally slower, more prominent, and more regular modulation in sung than spoken conditions; acoustic and muscle-activity measures both differentiated the modulations, although they did not always correspond.[73] This case identifies modulation rate, prominence, regularity, spectral structure, and muscle activity as candidate discriminators beyond frequency alone.[73]

In Dromey and Smith’s single-participant comparison, essential vocal tremor and intentional sung vibrato both produced frequency and amplitude modulation commonly in the 4–7 Hz range. Acoustic amplitude and frequency modulation were highly correlated in sung but not spoken vowels, and cycle-period variability was lower during singing.[116]

## Ableton Live: MIDI velocity smoothing

Judgment — Ableton Live’s documented Velocity MIDI effect remaps or gates discrete MIDI Note On and Note Off velocity values; it does not temporally smooth a movement trajectory.[4] MIDI velocity is the attack or release dynamic of a discrete note, whereas vibrato is a time-varying modulation, usually of pitch.[4][31] For a musician whose tremor produces accidental strikes or inconsistent force, a velocity-range rule can make notes more predictable; it cannot decide whether an oscillating performance gesture was involuntary or expressive.

### How does Ableton Live’s MIDI velocity smoothing handle unintended input

Live’s built-in **Velocity** MIDI effect changes incoming MIDI note velocities on the MIDI 1–127 scale. It can operate on Note On velocity, Note Off release velocity, or both. Its Velocity Curve maps a user-selected incoming range, set by Range and Lowest, onto an outgoing range set by Out Hi and Out Low. This is a configurable value-mapping system: it conditions the dynamics attached to note events rather than analysing the movement trajectory that produced them.[4]

Ableton’s Velocity transfer curve displays the incoming velocity range on its horizontal axis and the outgoing range on its vertical axis.[117]

The effect provides three handling rules for values outside the accepted input range:

- **Clip:** retains the note but limits its velocity to the range boundary. [4]
- **Gate:** removes the note entirely if its velocity is outside the selected range. [4]
- **Fixed:** outputs every note at the Out Hi value, irrespective of incoming velocity. [4]

Live also provides Drive and Compand controls to reshape the velocity curve. Negative Compand moves output values toward the middle of the dynamic range, while positive Compand and Drive push values toward the extremes; Random adds or subtracts a random amount. These are musical-dynamics transformations, not tremor classifiers.[74] **Accordingly, a Velocity Gate can suppress accidental notes only when their velocities fall outside a musician-chosen range; it rejects every intentional note in that range as well.**[74][4]

A Velocity Gate cannot distinguish an accidental strike from an intended ghost note, pianissimo attack, or accent whose velocity falls outside the accepted range.[117]

Ableton defines velocity as a note’s dynamics, recorded when a key or pad is pressed; its Velocity Editor adjusts Note On velocity and its separate Release Velocity Editor displays Note Off velocity.[11] Live also supports quantizing MIDI notes during recording as a timing-correction practice.[11] Those facilities can reduce the editing burden after performance, but they do not filter involuntary movement in the captured controller stream.[11] This makes Live’s practical adaptive workflow one of range setting, fixed-dynamic output where appropriate, and post-recording correction rather than intention-sensitive rejection.[11]

Intentional vibrato sits on different control dimensions. Ableton’s Live 12.1 release notes state that Live’s MPE MIDI Tools create curves for selected notes’ Pitch Bend, Pressure, or Slide, display the changes in the Clip Content Editor’s MPE View Mode, and include an LFO tool that sets an oscillating envelope with selectable shape, rate, and global amplitude envelope.[75] Live’s Auto Shift also supplies audio pitch tracking and correction, a dedicated vibrato control, and MPE modulation; it is an audio effect, not a MIDI-note input filter.[75] The distinction matters: reducing velocity variation does not inherently reduce pitch-bend tremor, pressure tremor, or mod-wheel fluctuation.[39]

Ableton’s Expression Control Max for Live device can use incoming velocity, pitch bend, pressure, expression, mod wheel, and other MIDI or MPE sources to modulate automatable parameters. It offers a response curve and separate rising and falling smoothing times from 0 to 1,000 milliseconds; MPE Control separately shapes per-note pressure, slide, and pitch bend.[118]

Auto Shift’s Smooth option specifically smooths pitch transitions between notes for 0–200 milliseconds, and Ableton says this retains more natural vibrato. Its vibrato-rate parameter spans 2–15 Hz, with a Natural Vibrato option that varies speed and depth.[39] Auto Shift’s 2–15 Hz vibrato-rate range contains the entire stated 4–12 Hz tremor band: the calculated overlap is 100%.[39] This frequency overlap is a design warning: an algorithm based on oscillation rate alone would risk treating a desired musical vibrato as tremor.[39] Live documents no mechanism that labels a captured 4–12 Hz MIDI oscillation as either pathological tremor or intentional vibrato.[56][39]

Judgment — The appropriate characterization is therefore velocity conditioning, not tremor-aware MIDI velocity smoothing. Live’s built-in effect can stabilize or reject discrete note dynamics through a user-selected curve and threshold, while preserving intentional vibrato requires leaving expressive MPE channels available or editing/replacing expression separately. A genuinely tremor-aware system would need real-time, time-series analysis of pitch, pressure, or controller trajectories and an explicit policy for false rejection of musical gestures.

1. Use Clip or Fixed mode when the priority is predictable dynamics without lost notes.
2. Use Gate only after testing the accepted velocity range against the musician’s deliberately soft and loud playing.
3. Keep pitch-bend, Pressure, and Slide expression separate from velocity processing when vibrato is musically important.

An Atlas report classifies Live’s mechanism as a static curve remap from Range and Lowest to Out Hi and Out Low, and its filter type as 《None (no frequency processing)》.[76]

Sound & Design’s Live 12 Suite article describes Velocity as changing the hard-or-soft dynamic of incoming MIDI notes, with minimum and maximum velocity settings, randomness, and compression or expansion of velocity values; it identifies these controls as useful for smoother dynamics and more consistent note intensities.[77]

Glitch Magic characterizes MIDI velocity as instruction data rather than audio data and gives its range as 0–127. It states that velocity can select different sampled-instrument samples at designated velocity breakpoints, so a velocity remap can change sound character as well as loudness.[78]

Glitch Magic describes changing one note’s velocity by dragging its marker in Live’s Velocity Editor and changing multiple notes together with Command-drag on Mac or Control-drag on Windows. It also describes the Velocity MIDI effect as permitting per-note adjustment or global velocity scaling, minimum-velocity setting, randomization, and compression-like curves.[78]

For MIDI clips, Glitch Magic describes setting a velocity range on a note or group of notes so that playback uses random values within that range. It identifies velocity as an expressive and nuanced but limited control, with a 0–127 dynamic range.[78]

Pitch vibrato may instead be carried by pitch bend, modulation or expression control, aftertouch, or MIDI 2.0 per-note expressive data, depending on the controller and instrument.

Ableton’s Live 12 release notes describe **Velocity Shaper**, a Max for Live MIDI Tool that transforms note velocities using an envelope. They also describe MIDI Tools as Transformations and Generators: with Auto Apply enabled, parameter changes apply to notes in real time; with Auto Apply disabled, they apply after the user presses Apply.[75]

Ableton Live’s Max for Live Expression Control can map velocity, pitch bend, pressure, modulation wheel and other MIDI/MPE sources to parameters. It offers linear or S-shaped response curves and independently adjustable rise and fall smoothing from 0 to 1,000 ms; this smooths mapped parameter changes rather than classifying tremor versus intentional vibrato.[118]

Ableton does not automatically smooth stepped 127-step MIDI CC controller data; the named workaround is Max for Live devices, including LFO Zero.[79]

Ableton’s Auto Shift can use notes from a separate MIDI track to pitch-correct incoming audio. In its Mod Routing section, Pitch, Formant, Volume, and Pan can each be modulated by Velocity, Pressure, Mod Wheel, Pitch Bend, Note PB, or Slide.[39] Auto Shift’s Latency Readout displays latency in milliseconds; enabling Live Mode can reduce latency for live performance, while producing slight glitches at note onsets or during rapid pitch changes.[39]

## Logic Pro: Smart Controls with tremor compensation

Smart Controls are Logic Pro’s compact interface for viewing and adjusting important parameters of the selected track without opening the Mixer or individual plug-in windows.[41] For a musician whose hand movement is variable, the useful distinction is between making a controller’s effect smaller or easier to reach and rejecting unwanted movement while retaining deliberate musical modulation.

### How do Logic Pro’s Smart Controls with tremor compensation handle

Apple documents Smart Controls as onscreen controls that can alter channel-strip, instrument, and effect plug-in parameters. One screen control may change several parameters, and the Smart Control inspector supports layout choice, parameter mapping, and assignment of external hardware controllers.[5][14] This makes it possible to consolidate frequently used sound controls onto a simpler interface.

A musician can map a screen control through MIDI Learn or a Parameter pop-up menu, edit the mapping, and use parameter-mapping graphs to scale its output values. Smart Control mappings also permit minimum and maximum values and inversion of the mapped range.[43][44] **These settings constrain the range and direction by which a controller affects a sound parameter; they are not a real-time classifier of hand movement.**

Logic Pro’s parameter-mapping graph plots input values horizontally against output values vertically and supports editable points, preset curves, and changes to minimum and maximum ranges.[119]

Smart Controls can therefore present a Vibrato parameter to a performer. In a documented Retro Synth example, one Contour control changes six parameters, including Vibrato; Smart Control movements can also be recorded as track automation.[44] A performer may map a physical fader, knob, button, drum pad, or pedal to such controls.[44] This supports intentional vibrato through a deliberately assigned control, but it does not separate vibrato gestures from involuntary oscillation at the input stage.

Logic Pro Smart Controls map onscreen or hardware controls to track, channel-strip, instrument, and effect plug-in parameters, and their mapping graphs plus minimum, maximum, and inverted-range settings constrain the output range and direction of those mappings.[5][14][43][44]

That distinction matters in reported accessible performance practice. In the *Inclusive Improvisation* BoilOver case study, mappings were made in Ableton Live directly from the custom software and in Logic Pro, with some Open Sound Control (OSC) messages filtered in Max.[28] The OSC-message filtering in that configuration was performed in Max, not by Logic Pro Smart Controls; Smart Controls remained the mapping and parameter-access layer.[28]

Logic Pro documents Smart Controls as a selectable-track interface: screen controls are labelled visual controls, optimized for the type of track or instrument, and can address EQ or tone, reverb and other effects, or instrument-specific settings such as synthesizer waveform, resonance, filter cutoff, and string articulation.[40][41] The Smart Control inspector provides layout selection, existing-mapping review, parameter mapping, and hardware-controller assignment.[41]

The Logic Pro guide separately identifies Input Filter settings, MIDI Inputs preferences, MIDI Controllers preferences, controller assignments, MIDI-processing functions, and the Velocity Processor.[41][80] The Velocity Processor provides Compress/Expand, Value/Range, and Add/Scale modes; these are separate MIDI-velocity-processing facilities rather than Smart Controls.[41][80] Logic Pro also provides Scripter MIDI-processing functions, including HandleMIDI and ProcessMIDI, as a route for custom MIDI processing.[41]

For performance-oriented control, the Alchemy documentation names a Transform pad, performance-control knobs, XY pad controls, and performance-control envelopes.[41] Logic Pro also documents pitch-bend, aftertouch, poly-aftertouch, live-automation recording, and automation writing with external controllers.[40] These facilities support performance capture and parameter control; Smart Controls remain the control-to-parameter mapping layer.[41]

Logic Pro contains vibrato-capable sound and modulation features, including Retro Synth LFO and vibrato modulation, Sculpture vibrato parameters, and Scanner Vibrato controls.[40][41] It also supplies an Arpeggiator MIDI plug-in that can be used with Smart Controls on a software-instrument track; it turns chord input into notes played one at a time and offers preset, note-order, note-range, and octave-range settings.[81] These are sound-generation and note-sequencing functions, not Smart Controls tremor compensation.[81]

**Stability–expressivity trade-off.** Narrowing a Smart Control’s minimum-to-maximum mapping range or reshaping its parameter-mapping graph can reduce unwanted parameter excursions from variable hand movement, but it also reduces the range or responsiveness available for intentional expressive control, including hand-performed vibrato.[43][44]

Logic Pro has no built-in Smart Controls feature called tremor compensation. Smart Controls do not perform real-time pathological-tremor detection, gesture rejection, or protection of intentional MIDI vibrato.

## SteadyMouse: assistive input

### How do specialized assistive tools like SteadyMouse handle unintended input

SteadyMouse is an adaptive pointer-access system: it makes ordinary computer pointing easier when tremor makes a cursor jump or a button press unreliable. Its UX problem is different from real-time musical performance: it stabilizes selection and clicking in the graphical user interface, whereas vibrato is an intentionally varying performance-control gesture.

SteadyMouse is designed for people with essential tremor, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis. It runs in Windows user space, intercepting the mouse-data stream through Windows mouse APIs between the physical pointing device and the on-screen cursor. It supports standard mice, trackpads, and trackballs; the manual also describes experimental pen-tablet support, including Wacom Intuos.[18][6]

Its **anti-tremor filtering** removes shaking motion from cursor movement. A strength slider and named filter sets alter the feel: Feather and Classic are presented as responsive, Adagio as smoother with more lag, and Allegro as the default middle balance. The Atlas evaluation report instead characterizes Feather as the lighter default filter set, favoring intentional movement at the cost of some jitter; this conflicts with the manual’s Allegro-default description.[6][76] The central trade-off is explicit: moving toward stronger filtering improves tremor removal but increases cursor lag, while weaker filtering makes the pointer more immediate while allowing more tremor through.[6]

SteadyMouse separately uses a duration-based **accidental-click gate**. A brief press-and-release is ignored; the user holds the button until a nearby visual indicator turns green, then releases for the click to pass. After that threshold, continued holding permits dragging, and two deliberate qualifying clicks permit double-clicking. The manual recommends the smallest effective hold period and says that 0.1–0.2 seconds works well for many users; a demonstration notes that longer hold windows, adjustable up to two seconds, can make double-clicking difficult.[6][82][83]

Calibration is individualized. The vendor’s procedure starts with slow cursor speed and overly strong filtering; the user first tries filter sets, then reduces strength until tremor returns and raises it slightly, before increasing pointer speed and retuning if necessary. It also recommends staying below 50% mouse speed because Windows upscaling reduces accuracy above that setting.[6] Icon Targeting supplies a second, discrete aid: it finds clickable screen content nearest the cursor and, after a configurable trigger-key double-tap, can snap the cursor to that target and click it. A system-wide toggle can turn all SteadyMouse features on or off.[6]

SteadyMouse’s calibration guidance recommends starting at around 95% filter strength, lowering it until shaking reappears, and then increasing it slightly.[6]

Customer accounts describe reduced cursor jumps and misclicks for people with Parkinson’s disease and essential tremor, including use in photography software, but they are reports of desktop work rather than controlled music-production outcomes.[84]

**SteadyMouse removes shaking motion from cursor movement and passes a mouse click only after the configured hold period: releases while the visual indicator is yellow or red are ignored, and a release when it is green counts as a click. MIDI note, pitch-bend, and continuous-controller data are separate performance streams, so pointer stabilization is not a mechanism for recognizing intentional MIDI vibrato.**[6][18]

**Pointer stability can compete with pointer-mediated expression: stronger SteadyMouse filtering can make DAW controls easier to target, but can also smooth an intentional oscillating pointer gesture. The manual directs users to the weakest setting that still removes tremor, preserving the snappiest cursor response. It should therefore be calibrated for editing and navigation, not treated as a vibrato-preservation or real-time MIDI tremor-classification tool.**[6][18]

SteadyMouse’s vendor identifies it as Windows assistive software for Essential Tremor and tremor variants accompanying Parkinson’s disease and multiple sclerosis, and says it has been helping users since 2005. The vendor lists anti-tremor filtering, automatic blocking of unintentional mouse clicks, and the Icon Targeting System, which snaps the cursor toward the intended target after a button action; it also states that the software works with mice, trackballs, and touchpads and has a Num-Lock global on/off toggle.[85]

The Atlas evaluation report characterizes Feather, rather than Allegro, as SteadyMouse’s lighter default filter set, selected to preserve intentional movement while allowing some jitter through. This conflicts with the Allegro-default description above and identifies two reported default-characterizations.[76][6]

SteadyMouse v1 is described as filtering high-frequency motion characteristic of tremor, but SteadyMouse publishes no filter-frequency cutoff in Hz: its adjustment is a tunable pointer feel rather than a stated frequency boundary that could separate 4–12 Hz tremor from vibrato. It filters cursor jitter rather than MIDI note, velocity, or pitch-bend data, so it does not process or preserve intentional MIDI vibrato.[86][76]

The January 2026 SteadyMouse manual says macOS support is not yet available. SteadyMouse’s FAQ says that porting the commercial version to Mac is underway and slated for SteadyMouse 3 or 4, and directs users to its SteadyMouse for Mac page for ways to use SteadyMouse on a Mac today.[6][87]

The documented pointer-level scope also excludes other raw MIDI performance channels, including modulation and aftertouch, in addition to note velocity, pitch bend, and MIDI continuous-controller data.

SteadyMouse says that a physical mouse cannot separate shaking from movement the user intended, whereas its software can make that distinction; it does not describe the method used to do so.[88]

The available filter choices also include Artistry, Laggy, and an experimental filter set; the demonstration recommends starting with Classic and trying the sets to find the one that works for the user.[83] The cursor-position crosshair preview and its adjustment can be turned off once the user is familiar with the system, to avoid distraction.[83]

## HCI approaches to real-time MIDI capture

### What HCI research exists on distinguishing pathological tremor (4–12 Hz)

Judgment — Real-time MIDI capture turns a performer’s movement into note, velocity, pitch-bend, modulation, or other control messages. A tremor-aware musical interface must therefore make an intent decision: suppress movement that the musician did not mean to send while retaining rapid, repeated movement that the musician did mean to use expressively. That requirement is more demanding than conventional tremor compensation, which usually defines success as recovering a smooth voluntary trajectory.

Pathological tremor is an involuntary rhythmic oscillation of a body part. Essential tremor (ET) commonly affects the hands, can occur during voluntary movement, and varies from 4 to 12 Hz according to body segment; Parkinsonian tremor is commonly reported at 4–7 Hz, while ET can span much of 3–12 Hz.[58][52] The clinical categories also depend on when tremor appears—at rest, while holding posture, or during movement—not frequency alone.[52] **The immediate design consequence is that a DAW cannot safely equate fast oscillation with unintentional input.**

Frequency overlap is substantial. In an ET inertial-sensor study of 18 people, most upper-limb involuntary-motion peaks lay between 4 Hz and about 9 Hz, but lower sidebands occurred at 1–6 Hz and upper sidebands at 7–15 Hz.[58] Every participant had an 8–12 Hz component, yet its magnitude ranged from dominant to very small relative to the principal peak.[58] The same study found individual dominant peaks around 3.5–5 Hz for some participants and around 6–9 Hz for others; more severe, higher-amplitude tremor tended to have lower dominant frequency.[58] Tremor spectra were often bimodal or multimodal—multiple superimposed oscillations rather than one stable signature.[58]

Physiological tremor further complicates a simple frequency rule. Physiological tremor is described as 8–12 Hz and often barely visible without instruments, while a 2023 ET classification study found 8–12 Hz physiological components in raw electromyography (EMG), the electrical measurement of muscle activity, from both healthy people and people with ET. That study isolated a 3–10 Hz tremor component for some analyses, but its models distinguished pathological patterns by characteristics including frequency and amplitude rather than by the presence of an oscillation in one band.[52][65] Amplitude, regularity, peak power, peak frequency, and entropy—an index of signal predictability—are all measurable tremor features.[58] **A useful Tremor–Intent model must therefore treat frequency as one feature among several, rather than as a rejection threshold.**

Clinical signal-processing research provides several technical precedents for separating tremor from voluntary motion. Bó, Poignet, and Gény’s 2009 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems paper, *Filtering voluntary motion for pathological tremor compensation*, used an extended Kalman filter (EKF)—a recursive estimator that updates a state estimate as new motion data arrive—to estimate voluntary and tremor components while also estimating tremor parameters.[47] The authors compared the method with published techniques on two datasets in which tremor patients drew spirals and stated that the algorithm could be applied directly in real-time pathological-tremor compensation.[47] This establishes real-time voluntary-motion separation for measured limb trajectories; the evaluated voluntary task was spiral drawing.[47]

A gyroscope-based approach used the same basic separation principle with explicit frequency assumptions. *Real-Time Estimation of Pathological Tremor Parameters from Gyroscope Data* measured wrist flexion–extension with one gyroscope on the forearm and one on the hand, transmitting data to a host computer at 1 kHz.[52] Its five clinically diagnosed participants, aged 48–74 years, continued their regular medication; the authors first estimated voluntary movement, subtracted that estimate from raw motion to obtain tremor, then estimated tremor frequency and amplitude.[52] The selected architecture used a critically dampened filter with θ = 0.990 for voluntary motion, a Weighted Frequency Fourier Linear Combiner (WFLC) for tremor frequency, and a Kalman filter for tremor amplitude.[52] The reported average amplitude-estimation error was 0.001 ± 0.002 rad/s, and the authors assumed voluntary motion occurred between 0 and 2 Hz.[52] That 0–2 Hz assumption suits the cited tracking tasks, but it is not an intent model for deliberately rapid musical control.

Gallego and colleagues reported an average delay-corrected tremor-estimation error of 0.001 ± 0.002 rad/s across four clinical movement tasks using hand- and forearm-gyroscope data sampled at 1 kHz; these were not MIDI-performance tasks.[113]

More recent systems replace fixed-band separation with adaptive estimation or learned temporal models.

- **MAWD wavelet decomposition:** Shahtalebi and colleagues’ 2017 MAWD framework adaptively decomposes a measured signal containing tremor and voluntary motion into approximation levels, then uses polynomial extrapolation to predict the desired next value. It combines hyper-parameter adjustment with real-time tremor prediction and was evaluated on real pathological-tremor data, where its authors reported improved accuracy over a prior tremor-estimation method. [49]

- **OC-WFLC adaptive filtering:** Mengüç and colleagues’ 2021 online-censoring weighted-frequency Fourier linear combiner extracts informative frequency information and combines it with a Fourier linear combiner to separate pathological hand tremor from voluntary movement. Simulations on real-world pathological-hand-tremor data reported reduced processing costs without adverse performance effects, positioning lower computation as important for real-time rehabilitative, wearable, and assistive systems. [48]

- **Deep voluntary-motion prediction:** Ibrahim and colleagues’ 2021 neural model learned nonlinear relations between Parkinson’s tremor and voluntary motion, reporting 99.2% average estimation accuracy, 97.0%, 94.0%, 91.6%, and 89.9% voluntary-motion prediction accuracy at 10, 20, 50, and 100 steps ahead, respectively, and 1.5 ms prediction time for 100 steps ahead. In its real-time experimental setup, it reported 93.8% ± 1.5% tremor reduction, 25% greater than the WFLC comparator. [50]

These systems show that a filter can estimate and subtract an involuntary component with low computational delay. Their target is voluntary movement recovery, however; MIDI vibrato, pitch inflection, and modulation are not represented as their own intentional gesture class.

The strongest classifier results are likewise clinical classifications rather than musical-intent classifications. A real-time diagnostic system using acceleration and surface EMG from 561 people in different hand positions classified Parkinson’s disease, ET, and physiological tremor with relative diagnostic accuracy of 83.14% for a decision tree, 84.24% for a neural network, and 85.76% for their combination.[89] Its purpose was differentiation between tremor types, which is distinct from deciding whether a musician currently intends an oscillatory gesture.

Xing and colleagues reported Parkinson’s-versus-essential-tremor diagnostic false-positive rates of 0.21 for random forest and 0.36 for XGBoost, with respective validation accuracies of 0.84 and 0.85. Those false positives concerned disease classification from accelerometer and EMG features, not rejection of intentional MIDI gestures.[120]

Xing and colleagues collected acceleration and surface electromyography during four 30-second postures from 398 clinically diagnosed patients: 257 with Parkinson’s disease and 141 with essential tremor. For distinguishing those diagnoses, random forest had a validation false-positive rate of 0.21 and XGBoost a rate of 0.36; neither rate measures rejection of intended MIDI gestures.[120]

Pascual-Valdunciel and colleagues’ 2023 *Classification of Kinematic and Electromyographic Signals Associated with Pathological Tremor Using Machine and Deep Learning* recruited 12 patients with ET and 11 healthy subjects.[65] The study acquired inertial-measurement-unit orientation data at 50 Hz and surface EMG at 2,042 Hz from bipolar electrodes on the flexor carpi radialis and extensor carpi radialis during 60-second postures selected to facilitate tremor, and labelled sequences only as Tremor or No Tremor.[65] All tested models achieved F1 scores above 0.80; the detailed results report long short-term memory (LSTM) scores of 0.94 for kinematics, 0.96 for EMG envelopes, and 0.90 for raw EMG. Elsewhere, the same article reports an LSTM raw-EMG F1 score of 0.98 and average F1 scores across models of 0.95 ± 0.02 for kinematics, 0.91 ± 0.10 for EMG envelopes, and 0.89 ± 0.08 for raw EMG. The separate 0.90 and 0.98 raw-EMG LSTM values should remain distinct because the article gives both.[65]

The 2023 classifiers also reveal why threshold calibration cannot be reduced to a universal musical setting. Their rule-based kinematic labelling used a second-order, zero-lag Butterworth band-pass filter from 4–10 Hz, power spectral density in one-second windows, and a heuristic global threshold on maximum band power.[65] The authors identify user-defined time- or frequency-feature thresholds as requiring preliminary data and operator experience.[65] Pathological tremor also changes over short periods, while the authors call for recordings from motor tasks beyond postural or isometric contractions to capture patterns arising from different causes.[65] **For MIDI interaction, calibration must consequently be person-, limb-, controller-, and gesture-specific rather than a fixed 4–12 Hz gate.**

Musicians add a further source of overlap because tremor can itself be task-specific. The International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society’s consensus statement says task-specific tremors occur in people who frequently repeat the affected task and may occur in musicians.[7] Lee and colleagues’ work includes task-specific tremor in string-instrument players and a violinist study reporting coactivation in the 3–8 Hz range.[90][91][60] Healthy-musician research also measured movement-induced tremor using a fingernail-mounted 3D accelerometer and surface EMG during paced finger flexion–extension; it used empirical mode decomposition and a Hilbert transform to derive instantaneous frequency and amplitude.[90] These findings make performance context part of the signal: an oscillation during an instrumental action may be pathological, physiological, task-specific, or an intentional shaping gesture.

Musical-interface research supplies an intent-sensitive design direction but not a tremor classifier. Ko (2020, no venue recorded, 'The GLOBE: Evaluating Sensors for Interactive Computer Music Performance')’s 2020 thesis, *The GLOBE: Evaluating Sensors for Interactive Computer Music Performance*, proposes evaluating sensor affordances and limitations when designing digital musical instruments.[92] The 2025 ImproVision system uses live pose estimation to translate body gestures into real-time musical control signals; its six predefined gestures use relative wrist, head, and shoulder distances and are sent through Open Sound Control (OSC) to mapped improvisation parameters.[93] Its authors propose a future system that jointly observes visual gesture and audio output to learn the relation between gesture and musical intention.[93] That multimodal direction directly addresses a limitation of motion-only filtering: musical intention can be expressed by the sound a performer is producing as well as the movement trace.

Wekinator lets performers train continuous, classification, or dynamic-time-warping models in real time from examples they provide to sensors. In a Myo-armband music-gesture study, models trained on static gesture examples were more accurate than models that included gesture onset, although the latter retained timing information discarded by static training.[121]

In a piano-practice recognition study using one Myo armband, participants supplied three gestures: raising an arm overhead, spreading fingers at rest, and sequential five-digit fingering. Training on stable gesture examples improved recognition accuracy by reducing variation, while including gesture onset preserved transition timing but reduced accuracy; the study did not classify tremor or MIDI vibrato.[121]

Wekinator accepts real-time sensor data and lets users train continuous models, classifiers, or dynamic-time-warping models by demonstrating examples. In a musical-gesture study involving piano-practice gestures, a performer evaluated the models directly and adjusted parameters until satisfied; the study did not test pathological tremor against MIDI vibrato.[121]

General HCI gesture-rejection research formalizes the acceptance problem. Taranta II, Maslych, Ghamandi, and LaViola’s CHI 2022 Voight-Kampf Machine (VKM) addresses malformed gestures and non-gesture patterns in continuous custom gesture recognition.[53] Its pipeline first segments continuous input into candidates with Machete, second scores candidates with Jackknife, and third accepts or rejects them using a learned threshold.[53] In the SHREC 2019 continuous-gesture benchmark, Dollar General reported 0.7% false positives, 0.7% mislabeled gestures, and 8.1% false negatives.[53] VKM’s simulations found that holding a threshold constant as training examples increase raises false positives, whereas reducing the threshold as training size grows can improve accuracy.[53] This is a transferable interaction principle: the rejection boundary must follow the variability of the intended gesture class.

Judgment — A MIDI tremor filter should therefore use a two-stage Tremor–Intent model: first estimate tremor likelihood from kinematics, EMG where wearable sensing is acceptable, amplitude, regularity, and time-varying spectral structure; second decide whether to pass, attenuate, or request confirmation using musical context such as controller assignment, note state, tempo, repetition pattern, and the contemporaneous audio result. The second stage should be trained with labelled intentional vibrato and other expressive controls from the actual performer, not inferred from clinical tremor labels alone.

The HCI and rehabilitation literature supports real-time separation of tremor from broad voluntary motion through EKF, WFLC, Kalman, wavelet, and learned prediction methods.[47][52][49][48][50] It also shows that tremor frequency varies across people and tasks, overlaps physiological 8–12 Hz activity, and changes over time.[58][65] **What follows is clear: a real-time MIDI system can borrow these estimators, but only an evaluation that labels both pathological tremor and intentional musical oscillation can measure whether it preserves vibrato while rejecting unwanted movement.**

A systematic review of essential-tremor control devices specifies a three-stage sensing hierarchy: detect the user’s intended voluntary movement; estimate tremor amplitude, frequency, and phase; and identify the affected joints and muscles. It specifies position, velocity, and acceleration sensing with minimum delay.[35] This hierarchy gives real-time MIDI capture a concrete architecture: intent detection precedes tremor estimation rather than treating every oscillation in a frequency band as unwanted input.[35]

Tremor-suppression orthoses demonstrate that retaining broad voluntary movement while suppressing tremor is technically achievable in non-musical tasks. Loureiro and colleagues used kinematic sensors and controlled viscous damping to discriminate desired from undesired motion; biomechanical data from 33 people with tremor informed a prototype applied to one person with ET. WOTAS combined joint gyroscopes, impedance control, and notch filtering that attenuates frequencies coinciding with tremor; active control reduced mean tremor power by 81.2% in 10 people with tremor disorders, versus 70% with passive suppression, and a further six-person ET study reported reductions up to 98%.[35] Hernstadt and colleagues’ voluntary-driven, speed-controlled orthosis produced a 94% mean tremor-power reduction in computerized sinusoidal and random pursuit tracking by nine people with ET or Parkinson’s disease, with no significant effect on voluntary movements.[35][36]

The sensing features extend beyond motion frequency. The TREMOR neuro-robot detected voluntary movement and tremor, then electrically stimulated antagonist muscle pairs against the tremor oscillation; reported suppression reached 60% of peak-to-peak tremor amplitude and power-spectral-density frequency in one person with Parkinson’s disease, and averaged 16.77 ± 8.33% of power spectral density in one person with ET. Ando and colleagues reported 89.5% average recognition of voluntary elbow movement in people with ET and, in one person with ET, a 50–80% reduction in action tremor while eating.[35] EMG phase is also informative: alternating antagonist-muscle bursts are phase-shifted and usually have higher peak amplitude and lower average frequency than synchronous, in-phase bursts; wearable-EMG work reported alternating activation in Parkinson’s disease and synchronous activation in ET and drug-induced Parkinsonism.[94]

In a separate study of people with essential tremor who had implanted deep-brain-stimulation electrodes, real-time voluntary-movement detection achieved mean sensitivity of 76.8–88.6% and false-positive rates of 16.0–23.1%; a false positive meant movement detected while the participant was at rest, not rejection of musical expression.[122]

In the same deep-brain-stimulation research context, posture-and-tremor decoding was characterized by sensitivity of roughly 0.8 and a false-positive rate of 0.2–0.3; these were stimulation-control measures, not MIDI-gesture measures.[122]

A clinical upper-limb system detected voluntary movement with mean sensitivity of 76.8–88.6% and a 16.0–23.1% false-positive rate, where a false positive meant detecting movement while the participant was at rest. For posture-associated tremor, it reported 90.5% tremor suppression and 20.3% false detection during rest; these are not rates of rejected MIDI gestures.[122]

Musician-specific clinical work strengthens the case for performance- and posture-aware calibration. The IMMM Hannover project identifies task-specific tremor in musicians as occurring with or independently of dystonia, particularly in string players’ bow arms; it investigates dystonic tremor and task-specific bowing tremor with accelerometry, EMG, and questionnaires.[95] The project describes tremor during slow finger flexion–extension in affected and non-affected fingers of some musicians with focal dystonia, with greater amplitude in extended and flexed postures, and defines frequency, amplitude, and position dependence as properties to characterize.[95] A 25-year clinical series excluded instrumental-performance-linked task-induced tremor because whether it is ET, dystonia, or a separate condition remains unsettled.[96]

A direct MIDI-capture evaluation must record musicians’ diagnoses and tremor characteristics, capture controller and MIDI data during musical tasks, and label both unwanted tremor and intentional vibrato or other expressive gestures. It must compare a defined real-time discriminator with baseline input handling and report tremor rejection, erroneous rejection of intended expression, latency, and musical usability.

No peer-reviewed, DAW-specific study has tested separation of pathological tremor from intentional MIDI vibrato or other expressive oscillatory gestures during real-time MIDI capture.[76]

Two adjacent real-time human–computer-input approaches add implementation precedents. Riviere and Thakor’s 1996 Weighted-Frequency Fourier Linear Combiner (WFLC) models tremor as an approximately sinusoidal signal, tracks its changing frequency, and was designed for zero phase lag.[76] Rocon, Miranda, and Pons’s 2006 TechFilter places a microcontroller adapter between mouse and PC; its learning error-cancellation algorithm estimates tremor with zero-phase estimation, removes that component, and treats the residual movement as intentional.[76] These approaches operationalize tremor-versus-voluntary-motion separation at the input layer, before application-specific commands are generated.

TechFilter, a 2006 mouse-cursor filtering device, uses a learning algorithm for real-time discrimination between voluntary and tremorous movement and includes evaluation and validation with prototype users.[97] A later clinical tremor-detection study curated 2,272 separate kinematic recordings contemporaneously annotated as tremor present or absent by a movement physician and compared six tremor-identification algorithms.[57] It captured behavioural testing with 60 reflective markers during a one-hour clinical assessment using 14 optical-motion-capture cameras at 120 Hz; recordings averaged 27 ± 9 seconds and ranged from 3 to 92 seconds.[57]

The clinical motion-capture study’s 2,272 recordings came from 52 movement-disorder clinic patients, 43 of whom had Parkinson’s disease or essential tremor as their primary diagnosis. It classified three-second windows as Tremor or No Tremor; its rule-based A2r method rejected dominant peaks below 3.5 Hz or above 10 Hz and labelled an in-range peak as tremor when it exceeded a trial-and-error threshold of 0.1 mm.[114]

For the upper-limb tasks in that clinical motion-capture study, frequencies from 4.3 to 7 Hz were particularly informative for tremor detection, whereas frequencies below 3 Hz were uninformative.[114]

## Where the approaches conflict: gesture rejection and creative flow

### How do Ableton Live’s MIDI velocity smoothing

Unintended-input filtering and intentional vibrato operate on different kinds of musical data. A filter can reject a discrete accidental note or stabilise a continuous pointer movement, while vibrato is usually a purposeful oscillation in pitch, pressure, or timbre. The central UX conflict is that an intervention strong enough to suppress unwanted variation can also remove a performer’s controllable nuance.

**Ableton Live’s documented Velocity MIDI effect is a note-dynamics processor, not a temporal tremor smoother.** It transforms incoming MIDI Note On velocity, Note Off release velocity, or both; MIDI velocity ranges from 1 to 127 and represents a note’s dynamics rather than its continuous pitch movement. The effect maps an input velocity range to an output range, can clip out-of-range values, gate and remove notes outside the accepted range, or output one fixed velocity for every note.[4][74]

This makes Live’s Velocity effect useful where tremor produces accidental low- or high-velocity note attacks—for example, an unintended pad strike below a chosen minimum velocity can be rejected in Gate mode. It does not inspect the time pattern of a hand movement, and it does not classify a cyclic pitch gesture as either pathological tremor or intentional vibrato. The user configures the accepted input range and output curve; Live supplies the value transformation rather than an adaptive, condition-specific calibration.[4][74]

Live separately supports continuous expression. Its CC Control device can output modulation, pitch bend, and channel pressure/aftertouch, while MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) attaches control information to individual notes rather than applying it globally to an entire MIDI channel. In Live 12, the Note Expression editor can view and edit per-note Pitch, Slide, Pressure, Velocity, and Release Velocity; recorded expression can therefore be refined after capture.[4][31]

MPE’s per-note design matters because a player can bend or vibrate one note without changing others. The MPE specification assigns a sounding note its own channel where possible, allowing individual Pitch Bend, Channel Pressure, and CC #74 messages; however, notes that must share a channel lose unique control.[29][63] Live’s MPE workflow therefore preserves a richer record of the gesture, but preservation is not rejection: it represents pitch and pressure movement without deciding whether that movement was intended.[31]

Live also exposes choices that can stabilise audible output by suppressing expression. Operator’s per-note pitch-bend support can be switched off, which Ableton says can help preserve stable pitch for MPE-controller input. That is a direct stability–expression trade-off: disabling pitch bend may stop unwanted pitch wobble, but it also removes deliberately played pitch bends and finger vibrato on that path.[75]

Ableton’s Auto Shift provides a related but distinct audio-processing example. It pitch-corrects monophonic audio, offers transition smoothing from 0 to 200 ms, and says smoothing retains more natural vibrato; its vibrato-rate control ranges from 2 to 15 Hz. This overlap with the 4–12 Hz range often associated with tremor illustrates why a frequency range alone cannot supply an intent decision, especially when musical vibrato may occupy the same region.[39]

The practical conclusion for Live is narrow but important: Velocity can reject or normalise accidental note attacks, while MPE can retain per-note expression; neither documented mechanism selectively removes tremor from intentional vibrato during real-time MIDI capture.[4][31][75]

### What documented case studies report false positive rates for gesture

For this review, the **expression-loss rate** is the number of rejected intended gestures divided by all eligible intended gestures. A **false rejection** is one rejected intended gesture; an **accepted unintended activation** is an unintended input that the system accepts. A gesture recognizer’s **false-positive rate** measures its own defined false-positive class, not expression loss.[98] Threshold setting creates a precision–recall trade-off: a tighter criterion can reduce accepted unintended activations while increasing false rejections, whereas a looser criterion can preserve intended gestures while permitting more unintended activations.[98] Threshold setting creates the familiar precision–recall trade-off: tightening a criterion reduces false positives but can reject more intended gestures, whereas loosening it increases recall but also permits more unintended activations.[98]

The published figures come from general gesture-recognition settings rather than DAW performance. They are useful for understanding rejection design, but their denominators, devices, movements, and tasks differ substantially.

| Study and interaction context | Reported false-positive result | What the result measures |
|---|---:|---|
| **Taranta et al., 2022, continuous-gesture benchmark** | The Dollar General recognizer recorded **0.7% false positives**, alongside **0.7% mislabeled gestures** and **8.1% false negatives**, in the SHREC 2019 competition table. [53] | A percentage result in a continuous-gesture benchmark, not MIDI performance or tremor filtering. |
| **Bi-Level Thresholding smartphone pilot** | The tight model was tuned to **80% precision**; researchers observed **no more than two false activations** during **20 minutes** of everyday phone movement from two researchers. [98] | A bounded count over a fixed observation period, not a percentage rate. |
| **TCD smartphone accelerometer study** | At threshold **θ = 1.02**, TCD produced **no false positive** in its walking-noise data. Nine people used an iPhone 8 with a 100 Hz three-axis accelerometer. [99] | A zero-event result for the tested walking-noise samples. |
| **Low-false-positive wearable gesture design** | The authors reported a designed gesture with **no false detection**, without giving a numerical rate in the reported result. [100] | A qualitative false-detection outcome for a wearable gesture-design method. |

The 0.7% SHREC result is the only explicit false-positive percentage in the cases listed in the table. A result of no more than two activations in 20 minutes and a zero-event statement for walking-noise samples should not be converted into or treated as equivalent percentage rates.[53][98][99][100]

Taranta et al.’s continuous-recognition work explains why this distinction matters in real interaction. Its pipeline first segments continuous movement into candidate gestures with Machete, then scores them with Jackknife, and finally accepts or rejects candidates through a learned threshold. The authors report that loose decision boundaries in high-activity data can generate many false positives and degrade user experience; their method tunes rejection using training-set size and expected gesture-production variability.[53]

Bi-level thresholding takes another approach. Its first threshold is tight to limit false positives; later attempts can receive a looser threshold so repeated false negatives do not force a user through the same failed action repeatedly. In an eight-participant pilot using flick-left, flick-right, and double-flip smartphone gestures, the approach achieved 93% recall and 95.3% precision; 65% of correctly recognised gestures used the double-relaxed model, whereas a single tight threshold would have captured 35% within the 15-second attempt period.[98]

In a later 36-person study of smartphone gestures while walking, 22 participants (61.1%) preferred the bi-level condition, 7 (19.4%) preferred the fixed-threshold condition, and 7 (19.4%) were indifferent. Participants rated bi-level recognition higher for application stability, 6.3 versus 5.6, and for the statement I performed well, 7.2 versus 6.5; the experiment held the overall number of errors constant.[98] The same research reports that repeated errors changed behaviour: after a second or third failure, participants slowed, sometimes stopped, and varied how they performed the gesture.[98]

**These cases show that rejection thresholds shape not only error totals but also the sequence in which errors occur, which changes perceived stability, effort, and how users move.**[98]

In the SHREC 2019 result for Dollar General, the 8.1% false-negative rate was 11.6 times the 0.7% false-positive rate (8.1 ÷ 0.7). Thus, in that continuous-gesture benchmark, missed intended gestures were much more frequent than accepted non-gestures.[53]

The same study’s participant P2 completed 70% of gestures, skipped 30%, and had 94% of successful recognitions made by the double-loose model. This case shows that the fallback mechanism can carry most successful recognition for a user who has difficulty completing the gesture.[101]

No identified case reports the requested intentional-MIDI-vibrato **expression-loss rate**: rejected intended vibrato gestures divided by all eligible intended vibrato gestures. The reported cases concern general continuous gestures, smartphone gestures, walking noise, wearable gestures, or injected false-positive clicks rather than real-time MIDI vibrato.[53][98][99][102]

Additional continuous-gesture results show that reported false-positive rates depend on both recognizer and calibration. In the SHREC 2019 competition, The Dollar General reported 0.7% false positives; uDeepGRU2 and uDeepGRU1 each reported 3.0%; uDeepGRU3 and SW 3-cent each 2.2%; DeA 25.2%; AJ-RN 23.0%; PI-RN 48.9%; Seg. LSTM1 60.0%; and Seg. LSTM2 68.1%. The competition reported mislabeled gestures separately from false positives.[34]

Taranta’s continuous Kinect test reported its highest F1 score, 96.9%, at rejection-threshold parameter λ = 2.0, with precision 97.3%, recall 96.5%, and false-positive rate 0.2%; the automatically selected λ = 1.98 produced F1 96.4%, precision 96.8%, recall 96.1%, and false-positive rate 0.2%. In the Leap Motion test, the highest F1 was 94.4% at λ = 1.75, with precision 95.0%, recall 94.4%, and false-positive rate 0.6%; automatic λ = 1.95 produced F1 93.8%, precision 95.7%, recall 92.5%, and false-positive rate 0.5%. Across the reported Leap Motion settings, false-positive rates ranged from 0.2% to 0.8%.[34]

These threshold results came from 40 University of Central Florida students aged 18–28 performing 14 Kinect or eight Leap Motion gestures, not from MIDI capture or musicians with movement disorders.[34] Taranta also reports that retaining a constant threshold as training-set size grows increases false positives, whereas reducing the threshold can improve accuracy.[34]

Lee and Kim define an insertion error as reporting a nonexistent gesture, a deletion error as failing to detect an input gesture, and a substitution error as falsely classifying a gesture. In their continuous-motion test of 60 200-frame sequences from one person, lowering the threshold-model transition probability from 1.0 to 0.1 sharply reduced deletion errors; reducing it below 0.1 increased deletion errors because insertion errors increased.[103]

The HMM system reported 93.14% reliability and 218 ms/frame. Its endpoint detector postponed the decision until detection of the next gesture to avoid a premature decision; the authors state that the delay can leave a user wondering whether recognition succeeded and that the intention-inference heuristic is not very natural.[103]

Taranta identifies several rejection mechanisms applicable to continuous input: a class-specific accept–reject threshold; a waiting period between recognition events; requiring a pattern to score well over multiple consecutive frames; purging or retaining prior data and internal state after detection; low sampling rates; and interpolation across frames. The work also describes Machete segmentation followed by Jackknife evaluation, and VKM selection of a rejection threshold from synthetic positive and non-gesture score distributions to maximize accuracy.[34]

### What impact on creative flow states do documented case studies

Creative flow is total absorption in an activity: attention is held by the task, action feels fluent, and self-monitoring recedes. In music-performance research, it can be measured retrospectively with the 10-item Flow State Scale Short Form and with participants marking moments when they recall losing themselves in performance.[64]

The cited gesture-rejection experiments measured workload, preferences and ratings, repeated-error movement changes, or recovery from false-positive clicks; they did not measure a creative-flow score during composition by professional musicians with movement disorders.[98][102] In the Bi-Level Thresholding study, the authors found significantly lower NASA-TLX workload and lower accelerometer variance for bi-level than fixed thresholding while holding recognition rates constant.

Participants made the same number of gesture attempts under both conditions at each recognition rate, so this was a change in interaction experience rather than a reduction in required task attempts.[98]

Alghofaili et al.’s 2024 error-aware gesture-input study examined a different part of the problem: recovery after false-positive inputs. The researchers injected false-positive clicks while participants selected 25 coloured objects in a simulated virtual-reality environment, because the controlled schedule made errors measurable. All four mediation designs reduced mean recovery time from the no-mediation baseline of 29.14 ± 10.14 seconds to 10.72 ± 6.41 seconds for Spatial + Recovery Facilitation, 9.16 ± 7.23 seconds for Spatial + Automatic Recovery, 9.52 ± 5.48 seconds for HUD + Recovery Facilitation, and 4.48 ± 1.99 seconds for HUD + Automatic Recovery.[102]

The same study found that error mediation increased immediate recovery. The baseline proportion was 0.63 ± 0.06, compared with 0.75 ± 0.11 for Spatial + Recovery Facilitation, 0.78 ± 0.12 for Spatial + Automatic Recovery, 0.79 ± 0.12 for HUD + Recovery Facilitation, and 0.84 ± 0.09 for HUD + Automatic Recovery. The four mediation techniques each improved recovery against baseline, though recovery times did not differ significantly from one another.[102]

Automatic recovery produced a clear agency conflict. Nine of 16 participants said they liked automatic correction, citing time savings, while six said they did not; participants described occasions in which the system acted against their intentions. For the most accurate simulated error-detection model, 10 of 16 blocks, or 62.5%, indicated willingness to continue using mediation; the lower-accuracy models produced willingness counts of 7/16, 8/16, and 8/16.[102]

This distinction is important: an **input false positive** is an unintended action accepted by the interface, whereas an **error-detection false positive** occurs when the system incorrectly labels an intended action as an error and intervenes unnecessarily. An aggressive tremor filter can generate the second kind of failure when it suppresses an intended vibrato, pitch bend, or parameter movement.[102]

Music-flow research gives the interaction consequences a specific creative meaning. College musicians report that mistakes, anxiety about physical or technical aspects of performance, sudden musical changes, intonation abnormalities, and being out of sync with others disrupt flow. They identify a lack of anxiety and mistakes, improvisation, expressive dynamics, agogics—intentional timing and stress variation—and emotional connection as conditions that support flow.[64]

A musician with essential tremor who described missed notes also reported distraction and uncertainty about whether errors reflected ordinary performance variation, tremor, or personal inadequacy.[110]

A study of 20 undergraduate classical musicians found mean total Flow State Scale scores of 4.74 ± 0.99 on a seven-point scale. The share of performance time participants identified as flow correlated with self-reported flow, r(18) = 0.562, p < .01, primarily through the Fluency subscale, r(18) = 0.569, p = .009.[64] These measures show how flow can be assessed in a tremor-aware DAW study: record the session, collect post-performance flow marking and scale scores, and align those reports with filter activations, rejected gestures, corrections, latency, and editing interruptions.[64]

Accessible digital musical instrument research provides qualitative evidence of engagement without turning it into a false-positive result. AirSticks case studies used observation and audio-video analysis; the authors report that participants with differing abilities found their own sense of flow and explored sound creatively. AirSticks used custom CAMS software, customised Ableton Live mappings, Kalman-state-estimation filtering for 1 kHz gestural analysis, and mappings that could be operated with large or small gestures and were described as unaffected by tremors or shakes.[28]

**The measured UX consequence of gesture error is interruption and recovery cost; the musical consequence most plausibly at risk is fluency, because mistakes and technical anxiety are established flow disruptors for performers.**[98][102][64]

In the VR false-positive recovery task, HUD plus Automatic Recovery reduced mean recovery time from 29.14 s without mediation to 4.48 s, an 84.6% reduction.[102]

### Synthesis

The documented approaches operate at different layers. Live’s Velocity effect conditions or rejects discrete note-velocity values; Logic Smart Controls map hardware controls to parameters and can record their movement as automation; and MPE preserves per-note pitch, pressure, slide, velocity, and release-velocity expression. Representation or mapping of a gesture is not an intent classifier.[4][31][104]

The strongest general HCI result is not a tremor-versus-vibrato classifier but a design pattern: thresholds must manage false positives, false negatives, latency, and recovery. Tight first-attempt rejection plus more permissive later attempts can reduce repeated failure, while error mediation can shorten recovery; automatic correction gains speed at the cost of sometimes overriding intention.[98][102]

For tremor-aware music production, the design direction is therefore to preserve continuous expressive data by default, reject clearly accidental discrete events conservatively, and make any adaptive suppression visible, reversible, and quickly bypassable. That protects the performer’s agency while keeping recovery from inevitable errors short.[4][75][6][102]

## Calibration and professional-musician outcomes

### How do different sensitivity threshold calibrations affect composition completion times

A sensitivity threshold is the rule that decides how much movement, or how long it persists, before an input becomes active. In music systems it sets a direct trade-off: filtering brief movement can suppress unintended activation, while demanding sustained movement can also delay or weaken deliberate control.

AirSticks 2.0 parameterises sensitivity as an acceleration-energy integration window. At 10 ms, users must sustain substantial movement to keep energy high; at 1,000 ms, energy builds more slowly but remains high and decays more slowly. This makes the window a musically consequential control over attack and release behaviour, rather than a documented tremor-specific rejection threshold.[28]

BoilOver performers also worked with researchers who made small changes to AirStick sensitivity during a later session and added sounds or instruments on request. The account describes collaborative calibration in performance practice, but gives neither a numeric setting nor composition-completion time.[28]

Gesture-recognition research provides adjacent threshold methods rather than composition outcomes. VKM adjusts its threshold according to training-set size and application-dependent variability; it reduces the threshold as more training examples cover the gesture space, with the stated aim of reducing false-positive errors. Its evaluation spans Kinect, Vive Position, Vive Quaternion and mouse datasets, not DAW composition.[53] VKM was reported as the only tested technique to achieve near-optimal performance consistently across those four device types.[53] In the fixed-versus-bi-level threshold study, participants made the same number of gesture attempts at a given recognition rate and showed no significant walking-speed difference.[98]

In an adjacent electrical-stimulation study, researchers tested the more tremorous arm of 20 neurologist-diagnosed, right-handed participants with essential tremor, measuring sensory and motor thresholds at 10, 50, 100, and 150 Hz; this was stimulation calibration, not MIDI-input calibration.[123]

**No evaluated calibration in these studies provides composition-completion times for professional musicians with movement disorders.** The AirSticks control establishes a usable timing trade-off—short windows require continuously sustained movement, whereas long windows build and decay slowly—but it does not identify the setting that speeds professional composition.[28]

AirSticks’ documented sensitivity-window endpoints span a 100-fold range, from 10 ms to 1,000 ms, making the calibration consequential for how quickly movement energy accumulates and decays.[28]

AirSticks sensitivity could be tuned through a time window that changed how movement energy built and decayed, and the researchers also made small changes to AirStick sensitivity while working with BoilOver performers.[28]

Composition completion includes ideation, listening, editing, arrangement decisions and error recovery as well as motor entry, so target-acquisition speed in a cursor task is not a composition-time measure. A threshold-calibration study should define the disorder and tremor characteristics, professional-musician status, DAW task, input modality, the exact filter settings and affected MIDI or pointer channels, and completion as a specified finished arrangement. It should counterbalance calibration order within participants and record unintended-input errors, re-performance of intended nuances and expressive losses alongside completion time.

The filtering synthesis infers two completion-time costs: over-filtering can reject intended expression, creating frustration and repeated takes, whereas under-filtering can leave unintended notes that require manual cleanup. These inferred costs of rejecting intended input or retaining unintended input were not measured in a DAW.[76]

### How do different sensitivity threshold calibrations affect user satisfaction scores

The bi-level thresholding experiment compared fixed and bi-level recognizers at simulated recognition rates of 50%, 60% and 70%. It enrolled 36 general-student participants aged 20–39, who performed smartphone motion gestures while walking; it was not a musician or movement-disorder study.[98]

| Outcome measure | Fixed threshold | Bi-level threshold |
|---|---:|---:|
| Perceived stability rating | 5.6 | 6.3 [98] |
| Reported perceived-stability rating value, smartphone-gesture participants | 5.6 | 6.3 [98] |
| Self-rated “I performed well” | 6.5 | 7.2 [98] |
| Reported self-rated performance value, smartphone-gesture participants | 6.5 | 7.2 [98] |
| Session preference, participants | 7 of 36 (19.4%) | 22 of 36 (61.1%); 7 of 36 (19.4%) indifferent [98] |

Bi-level thresholding also produced significantly lower NASA-TLX workload scores and lower accelerometer variance than fixed thresholding when overall recognition rates were held constant.[98] The reported higher ratings concern perceived stability and performance; ratings for likability, ease of use, fun, comfort, relaxation and perceived application performance were not statistically significant.[98]

For people with Parkinson’s disease or involuntary tremor, adaptive path smoothing participants described APSS as easy to use and useful and indicated intention to use it. This is qualitative acceptance rather than a satisfaction score or a comparison of threshold settings.[1]

In the 36-person fixed-versus-bi-level smartphone study, the bi-level condition’s 61.1% session preference exceeded fixed-threshold preference (19.4%) by 41.7 percentage points.[98]

The bi-level recognizer’s perceived-stability rating was 12.5% higher than the fixed-threshold rating, calculated from 6.3 versus 5.6.[98]

The reviewed material supplies no threshold-specific user-satisfaction scores for professional musicians with movement disorders.[98][28]

A satisfaction evaluation should report the named, validated scale and its score range for every calibration condition, together with the composition task and participant-level or summary results. It should separately assess latency, predictability, filter override, preservation of intentional microtiming and dynamics, and control of expressive gestures such as vibrato, because an overall usability score can conceal dissatisfaction with flattened expression.

In Study Two, five 7-point Likert questions assessed acceptability, usefulness, effort, frustration, and overall user experience for each trial and condition; the effort and frustration questions incorporated aspects of NASA-TLX.[105]

In Study Two, lower-precision recognition scenarios produced worse acceptability, usefulness, frustration, and overall-user-experience results than the P95/R95 scenario, while the measured effects of lower recall were less severe.[105]

### Synthesis

Threshold calibration changes the feel of control: AirSticks makes this explicit through a 10–1,000 ms temporal window, while bi-level gesture recognition improves perceived stability, self-rated performance, workload and preference in a non-musician smartphone task.[28][98] The latter result favours adaptive thresholding over a fixed threshold for repeated gesture input, but it does not transfer directly to professional DAW musicians with essential tremor or Parkinson’s disease.[98]

1. Calibrate temporal sensitivity with the musician, preserving a separately controllable expressive path for sustained gestures.
2. Measure composition-completion time and satisfaction by threshold setting in professional musicians with movement disorders rather than using general gesture-task ratings as substitutes.

## Transition to standard interfaces and open questions

### What findings exist on motor skill retention when transitioning between

No cited study measures delayed standard-DAW MIDI performance after musicians with essential tremor or Parkinson’s disease use a tremor-adapted interface. Motor-skill retention after an adaptive-to-standard interface transition is performance maintained on the standard interface at a later test after an explicit interval. Immediate transfer is performance tested as assistance is removed, rather than evidence that the underlying motor skill has persisted.[106][107][108]

In motor-learning terminology, retention tests later performance under the same conditions, whereas transfer tests performance after the task or interface changes.[124]

The first indirect transition is Parkinson’s piano training rather than an interface switch. In *Quantifying Changes in Dexterity as a Result of Piano Training in People with Parkinson’s Disease*, three participants completed six weeks of piano training; pre- and post-intervention tests found some improvement in nine-hole peg-test and finger-force-synergy measures, with significant overall nine-hole peg-test improvement for participants 1 and 3. The trained activity was piano playing and the tested outcomes were component dexterity measures, not standard-DAW MIDI performance or delayed retention.[106]

A meta-analysis of Parkinson’s motor learning found a small retention disadvantage relative to neurologically intact controls after a single practice session (standardized mean difference −0.17), but no significant overall difference after extended practice of at least two sessions (standardized mean difference −0.04). After a single session, sensory-motor and visuomotor-adaptation tasks showed poorer retention, while sequential fine-motor and gross-motor tasks showed no statistically significant deficit.[125]

In Pendt, Reuter, and Müller’s 2011 semi-virtual throwing study, 19 medicated, non-demented people with mild-to-moderate Parkinson’s disease and 19 matched controls trained over five days. At a test seven to nine months later, the Parkinson’s group’s average performance decline was numerically greater but not significantly different from controls’; four of the six Parkinson’s participants tested retained performance and two declined. This was retention of a throwing task, not a switch from an adaptive to a standard DAW interface.[126]

In a musician’s-dystonia study, participants had intact basic auditory–motor timing on off-instrument tapping-synchronization and auditory timing-perception tasks. They reported mean accumulated practice of 32.7 (23.0) × 10³ hours, compared with controls’ 26.6 (22.4) × 10³ hours; the authors linked greater practice to more accurate stable tapping synchronization and proposed off-instrument sensorimotor practice and instrument adaptation. The tested tasks concerned discrete movements, not continuous expressive control or a transition from adaptive to standard MIDI input.[109]

One musician with essential tremor said that becoming suddenly tremor-free might require relearning her artistry. This account identifies relearning of embodied musical control after a major change in movement conditions.[110]

A pianist with essential tremor reported missed notes and distraction during high-pressure performances; she said tremor became apparent when her muscles were tense, although good piano technique required relaxation.[110]

A proposed NIME study asks how quickly musical skills fade without practice, but excludes professionally trained musicians and reports no retention result. Accessible music-interface work also remains commonly prototype-based, with few longitudinal evaluations.[111][112]

**The findings separate improved dexterity and preserved off-instrument timing from retained performance after an adaptive-to-standard switch; they therefore support testing transfer, persistence, and relearning as distinct outcomes.**[106][109]

A concrete transition study should record a standard-interface baseline, then counterbalance adaptive sensitivity settings and standard-interface use on the same composition and expressive-gesture tasks. It should label intended gestures and unwanted movements during capture, and test standard-interface performance immediately after adaptive use and again at a pre-specified delayed follow-up. The study should measure composition-completion time, satisfaction, flow, input-to-output latency, error recovery, and retained skill as separate outcomes; flow can be measured with the 10-item Flow State Scale Short Form and performance-video recall of moments of losing oneself in the music.[106][109][64]

The second indirect transition is removal of visual guidance in Hsieh et al.’s VR piano-learning study. Thirty learners practiced short, right-hand monophonic melodies with either performance-adaptive Dynamic ghost-hand guidance or fixed-transparency Static guidance, then performed the melodies without the ghost hand immediately and after a 10-minute retention interval. Dynamic pitch accuracy was 99.1% immediately and 99.6% at retention, compared with 96.8% and 87.8% under Static guidance; Dynamic finger accuracy was 95.8–96.5%, compared with 88.2% immediately and 76.7% under Static. The condition effects were significant for pitch accuracy (*F*(1,29) = 5.04, *p* = 0.03) and finger accuracy (*F*(1,29) = 9.68, *p* < 0.01). Timing accuracy showed no significant condition or retention-test effect and ranged from 46.8% to 55.0%. This is immediate and 10-minute unguided piano performance, not a delayed transition to an unfiltered DAW controller.[107] Dynamic guidance also received higher 5-point learning-experience ratings than Static guidance during practice (mean difference 0.529) and retention (0.452); 47 of 53 decisive preference responses (88.7%) favoured Dynamic. These are short-term outcomes after removing visual guidance, rather than outcomes from a change between adaptive and standard input interfaces.[107]

Hsieh et al. also found immediate order-dependent transfer within the experiment: participants trained with Dynamic guidance first began the new melody and new guidance condition with a higher adjusted initial performance score than Static-first participants (β = +0.073, *p* = .050), and the advantage remained at the end of that block.[107] The third indirect transition is a delayed retest of a browser-based mirror-reversal reaching task.

The study distinguishes motor adaptation, modifying an existing control policy to regain performance, from de novo learning, establishing a new control policy. Forty-eight healthy adults returned after at least three days to the same mouse- or trackpad-controlled cursor task: first-block completion time and path length in session 2 were approximately 50% shorter than at initial training, but longer than at the end of session 1; subsequent trials returned to approximately end-of-training levels. The authors characterize this as partial retention after three or more days.[108]

In the indirect VR piano comparison, Dynamic guidance’s pitch-accuracy advantage over Static guidance was 11.8 percentage points at the 10-minute unguided retention test (99.6% minus 87.8%).[107]

The Dynamic-versus-Static pitch-accuracy gap increased from 2.3 percentage points immediately after guidance removal to 11.8 percentage points at 10-minute retention, a 9.5-percentage-point increase.[107]

At the 10-minute unguided retention test, Dynamic guidance also had a 19.8-percentage-point finger-accuracy advantage over Static guidance (96.5% minus 76.7%).[107]

Removing smoothing changes the movement-to-output mapping, so the same tremulous movement can become more visible in the output without demonstrating that the musician’s motor ability has improved or deteriorated. The specificity-of-learning principle predicts imperfect transfer when an adaptive interface changes the mapping between movement and output.[76]

### Open questions

No documented result establishes a real-time MIDI classifier that reliably separates 4–12 Hz pathological tremor from intentional vibrato across instruments, sounds, controllers, and individual musicians. No case-study measure establishes gesture-rejection false-positive rates together with creative-flow effects for professional musicians with ET or Parkinson’s in DAWs. No evaluated threshold calibration reports both composition completion time and satisfaction scores for that population, and no direct finding establishes motor-skill retention after transition from adaptive to standard DAW interfaces. These questions would be settled by longitudinal, musician-specific DAW studies that log MIDI-level false positives and false negatives, controller latency and threshold settings, completed composition tasks, validated flow and satisfaction measures, and post-adaptation performance on standard interfaces.
