Neither DSM-5 nor ICD-11 has demonstrated superior autism diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or clinical utility in a direct head-to-head autism study. Both require social-communication differences and restricted, repetitive or inflexible patterns, and both allow sensory hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, or unusual sensory interests to evidence the latter domain.

DSM-5 operationalizes sensory differences as one of four restricted/repetitive-behaviour options within a two-of-four threshold, whereas ICD-11 treats them as illustrative manifestations without a fixed item count. Sensory differences alone do not establish autism under either manual, and claimed differences in sensory-focused false-positive or false-negative diagnosis remain hypotheses rather than demonstrated outcomes.

In the United States, DSM-based clinical diagnosis and ICD-10-CM billing codes remain the practical reimbursement framework; ICD-11 adoption is nationally variable in Europe and does not itself establish a uniform coverage change.

**Interpretive note on sensory weighting.** Both systems make the restricted/repetitive domain essential and allow sensory hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity or unusual sensory interest to demonstrate that domain. DSM-5 explicitly counts sensory differences as one of four RRB options toward a two-of-four threshold. ICD-11 gives sensory phenomena as illustrative manifestations of its essential restricted, repetitive and inflexible-pattern domain and leaves the required number and combination of individual manifestations to clinical judgment.[18][14][10][15][17]

Interpretation of the operational difference. DSM-5 requires all three specified social-communication components and two of four restricted/repetitive-behaviour components, whereas ICD-11 presents manifestations as examples rather than applying corresponding item counts.[9] Kamp-Becker (2024) argues that this permits ICD-11 a greater variety of symptom combinations than DSM-5 and may do so at the expense of diagnostic precision, with effects on clinical practice, care and research.[32]

The greatest DSM-5/ICD-11 difference is in the restrictive, repetitive and inflexible-behaviour pattern, rather than social communication. Both systems describe ASD as heterogeneous and as symptoms on a continuum that blends into normal variation.[33] Sensory differences occur in multiple clinical and nonclinical contexts; their diagnostic significance depends on how they fit the complete developmental pattern.[34]

## Evidence synthesis

Direct comparison means that the same autism-relevant population is assessed against both DSM-5 and ICD-11 requirements, ideally against an independent clinical reference standard. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with autism correctly identified; specificity is the proportion without autism correctly excluded. Neither should be inferred from how the written manuals appear to differ.

Judgment — The published literature supports comparison of the manuals’ wording and of ICD-11’s general clinical utility, but it does not support a numerical conclusion that either DSM-5 or ICD-11 is more accurate, sensitive, specific, or useful for autism diagnosis.

### GRADE quality rating

**GRADE conclusion —** GRADE is not applicable to the requested comparative DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 autism outcomes because no identified source measures diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or autism-specific clinical utility for both manuals.[35][36][32] Assigning “high,” “moderate,” “low,” or “very low” certainty would falsely imply that a direct comparative autism effect estimate exists.

If a future evidence body contains paired classifications, GRADE for diagnostic-test accuracy should assess risk of bias in participant selection and the reference standard, indirectness from setting or population, inconsistency among studies, imprecision of sensitivity/specificity confidence intervals, and selective reporting. For clinical utility, the outcome must be specified separately—for example diagnostic time, clinician usability, access to services, or diagnostic stability. This distinction prevents a favorable ICD-11-versus-ICD-10 vignette result from being relabelled as DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 autism evidence.

WHO field studies reported ICD-11 CDDR performance as incrementally superior or equivalent to ICD-10 CDDG for clinicians’ vignette diagnoses, with improved clinical-utility ratings.[16] That supports ICD-11 development and implementation research relative to ICD-10, but does not change the GRADE applicability conclusion for the requested DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 autism comparison.

NICE evidence profiles extract evidence for individual studies, quantitatively synthesise it where possible, and present it in GRADE evidence profiles.[47]

### direct comparator evidence

**Diagnostic agreement or yield —** No identified publication assigns both DSM-5 and ICD-11 autism classifications to the same autism-relevant population and reports diagnostic agreement, concordance, prevalence, or diagnostic yield.[35][36][32]

Reference-standard accuracy — No identified publication compares DSM-5 and ICD-11 autism classifications against an independent clinical reference standard.[35][36][32]

Sensitivity — No direct DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 autism sensitivity estimate for either manual is reported.[35][36][32]

Specificity — No direct DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 autism specificity estimate for either manual is reported.[35][36][32]

The available quantitative studies compare DSM-5 with DSM-IV-TR, ICD-10, clinical diagnosis, or a study-specific classification. Their figures are older-manual or local-reference comparators, not independently established diagnostic correctness and not DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 estimates.[38][41][42][43]

Clinician-rated clinical utility — No measured head-to-head clinician-rated clinical-utility outcome compares DSM-5 with ICD-11 autism criteria.[35][36][32] Kamp-Becker advances concerns that broader ICD-11 symptom combinations may produce false-positive diagnoses, increased prevalence, reduced access to ASD-specific services, and less specific treatment.[32][26] First predicted that the manuals would not produce significant differences in who receives an autism diagnosis.[44]

NICE’s 2021 surveillance review identified no new evidence relevant to ICD-11.[46]

**The evidence supports a cautious, non-ranked conclusion: DSM-5 and ICD-11 autism criteria have been compared conceptually and at the level of written requirements, but neither manual has demonstrated superior autism diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or clinical utility in a direct head-to-head study.** First et al. identify autism as a minor definitional difference within a structured comparison, while emphasizing that their ratings were not empirical.[9] Hualparuca-Olivera et al.

report generally positive ICD-11 CDDR utility across disorders, but not against DSM-5 or specifically for autism. Kamp-Becker advances an important precision concern, whereas another expert predicts little change in diagnostic assignment; both positions remain untested in paired autism samples.[9][35][37][44]

1. Do not use existing DSM-5 or ICD-11 figures from older-manual comparisons as head-to-head estimates.
2. Treat claims of differential false-positive or false-negative autism diagnosis as hypotheses until tested in paired, reference-standard studies.
3. For clinical decisions, use the criteria requirements described in the criteria table rather than a claimed evidence-based ranking of the two manuals.

## Clinical guideline positions

Clinical guidance serves a different function from a diagnostic manual. It tells clinicians how to apply criteria, gather developmental history, assess needs and adapt care; it does not necessarily endorse one classification system over another. Across the four bodies, sensory processing differences are recognised as clinically important, but none treats sensory differences alone as sufficient to diagnose autism.[16][48][49][50][51]

### WHO

The World Health Organization (WHO) is the classification authority for the *International Classification of Diseases*, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11). The World Health Assembly approved ICD-11 in 2019; it came into effect on 1 January 2022, and WHO reports that health-statistics reporting based on ICD-11 began on 1 January 2022.[52][36][2] WHO encourages Member States to move promptly to ICD-11 but permits continued use of ICD-10 for as long as necessary during the transition.

The adoption resolution provided transitional arrangements from 1 January 2022 for at least five years, and longer where needed for reporting under prior ICD revisions.[52][6][3]

WHO’s adoption position concerns implementation of an international health classification, especially morbidity and mortality reporting, rather than a command that every country immediately replace its clinical or payment systems. WHO states that national transition decisions remain country-specific. Its decision-maker roadmap sets out Year 1: Governance & System Preparation; Year 2: Pilot Implementation & Evaluation; and Year 3: Full Implementation & Optimization.[6][53][54]

WHO’s *Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements* (CDDR) are the clinical version of ICD-11 for mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders. The CDDR are integral to ICD-11 but distinct from the Mortality and Morbidity Statistics (MMS) linearization, which supplies shorter descriptions for health-information reporting. WHO describes the CDDR as a clinically useful guide to implementation, designed to support accurate and reliable identification in care settings worldwide.[16]

WHO’s ICD-11 browser identifies its 2025-01 release as *ICD-11 for Mortality and Morbidity Statistics* (ICD-11 MMS), the version used for health reporting and classification.[136]

WHO published the ICD-11 *Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements* (CDDR), a comprehensive diagnostic manual for mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders, on 8 March 2024. WHO says it is designed to support qualified mental-health professionals and qualified non-specialist health professionals in identifying and diagnosing disorders in clinical settings; other health professionals may use it to understand disorders without personally assigning diagnoses.[4]

The CDDR are not a stand-alone assessment protocol. WHO directs clinicians to apply them with broader disorder knowledge and professional competencies acquired through education, training and experience. Its general CDDR approach avoids rigid symptom counts unless globally established evidence supports them, leaving room for clinical judgment about the person, culture and context. WHO states that the CDDR were developed and field-tested through a multidisciplinary, participatory process involving hundreds of experts and thousands of clinicians worldwide.[16][4]

For autism spectrum disorder, ICD-11 category 6A02, WHO makes persistent restricted, repetitive and inflexible patterns of behaviour, interests or activities an essential diagnostic component. WHO lists lifelong excessive or persistent hypersensitivity or hyposensitivity to sensory stimuli, and unusual interest in sensory stimuli, as possible manifestations within that required component. The examples include sounds, light, textures, odours, tastes, heat, cold and pain.[2]

Sensory differences therefore have diagnostic relevance in ICD-11, but they are one possible presentation of the restricted, repetitive and inflexible-pattern domain rather than a separate required domain or an independently scored criterion. The CDDR’s flexible format does not assign numerical weights to individual sensory features.[16]

WHO also addresses sensory experiences in differential diagnosis. For example, the CDDR recognises that both avoidant-restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) and autism may involve food avoidance related to sensory characteristics, but autism additionally requires persistent social-communication and reciprocal-interaction difficulties plus restricted, repetitive and inflexible patterns extending beyond food.[2]

**WHO’s position is implementation of ICD-11 as the current international classification, with national transition flexibility; within its autism requirements, sensory differences are clinically relevant examples in an essential domain, not a separately mandatory or numerically weighted criterion.**[6][3][16]

WHO characterises the ICD as the global standard classification for mortality and morbidity statistics.[55]

As of May 2024, WHO reported that 132 Member States and areas were at various phases of ICD-11 implementation: 72 countries had commenced implementation, including translation efforts; 50 were conducting or expanding implementation pilots; and 14 countries and areas had begun collecting or reporting ICD-11-coded data.[56]

WHO states that its 194 Member States agree by international treaty to use ICD for reporting health information to WHO. It also says Member States use ICD for access to care, clinical guidelines and research, and governments use it in frameworks defining obligations to provide free or subsidized health care; diagnosis of a condition typically confers eligibility for a specified range of health services.[16]

Of the 132 Member States and areas that WHO reported as being at some phase of ICD-11 implementation in May 2024, 72 (54.5%) had commenced implementation, 50 (37.9%) were piloting or expanding pilots, and 14 (10.6%) had begun collecting or reporting ICD-11-coded data.[56]

ICD-11 autism diagnosis also requires developmental onset and clinically significant functional impairment, in addition to the social-communication and restricted, repetitive and inflexible-pattern requirements.

### APA

The American Psychiatric Association (APA) presents DSM-5-TR and the ICD as companion publications. APA describes DSM-5-TR as providing current diagnostic criteria and descriptive text, while the ICD supplies the diagnostic code numbers used throughout medicine, including for insurance reimbursement and morbidity and mortality monitoring. APA says it works with WHO, the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Center for Health Statistics (CDC-NCHS) to maximise compatibility between the systems.[5]

APA participated in ICD–DSM harmonisation during the development of ICD-11. The DSM-5 Task Force Chair and Co-Chair attended ICD-11 Advisory Group meetings, and an ICD–DSM Harmonization Group met repeatedly; WHO’s account says the greatest success concerned the organization and ordering of diagnostic groupings, termed the metastructure.[16][36]

APA presents DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria and ICD codes as companions and does not expressly recommend replacing DSM autism criteria with ICD-11 autism criteria.[5] APA’s stated model is complementary use: DSM-5-TR supplies the clinical diagnostic criteria, while ICD codes support administrative, statistical and reimbursement functions.[5]

DSM-5-TR revised DSM-5 text, references, diagnostic criteria and ICD-10-CM codes.[137]

APA’s public description of autism includes sensory hypersensitivity, such as aversion to loud noises, among restricted and repetitive features. In DSM-based practice, sensory hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity and unusual sensory interests belong within the restricted/repetitive behaviour domain, not a third core diagnostic domain.[48][49]

**APA’s documented position is compatibility rather than replacement: it retains DSM-5-TR as its diagnostic-criteria manual and treats the ICD as the coding companion, while recognising sensory hypersensitivity as one restricted/repetitive autism feature.**[5][48]

In DSM-5, sensory hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity or unusual sensory interests are one of four restricted/repetitive-behaviour subcriteria; at least two of the four subcriteria are required. Sensory differences can therefore contribute to, but cannot alone establish, the restricted/repetitive-behaviour requirement or an autism diagnosis.

APA’s 2022 clarification changed DSM-5 Criterion A from “as manifested by the following” to “all of the following,” making explicit that all three social-communication manifestations are required; it did not change sensory weighting.[138]

### American Academy of Pediatrics

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) directs paediatric clinicians to diagnose autism spectrum disorder through a clinical evaluation that supports DSM-5 criteria. Its 2020 clinical report describes diagnosis as based on developmental history and observation of characteristic behaviours, preferably using standardised approaches.[57][49]

The AAP report states that it reflects diagnostic-criteria changes after DSM-5 was published in 2013. DSM-5 established one ASD category in place of the DSM-IV-TR subtypes autistic disorder, Asperger syndrome, and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified.[49] Its autism resources recommend general developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months, autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months, referral for diagnostic evaluation when surveillance or screening indicates increased risk, and intervention for identified developmental delays without waiting for a completed autism diagnosis.[58][59]

AAP guidance describes the two DSM-5 autism domains as social communication/social interaction and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour. The report states that recognition of sensory-processing symptoms led DSM-5 to include hyperreactivity or hyporeactivity to sensory input and unusual sensory interests.[60][49]

Sensory processing differences thus inform the clinical assessment of autism under the AAP’s DSM-5 framework, but they are not described as an independent required diagnostic domain. The AAP also recommends standardised assessment of psychoeducational, adaptive and language abilities, including pragmatic or social language, for every child evaluated for autism, regardless of age.[49][57]

The AAP’s service guidance extends beyond diagnosis. It directs clinicians to individualise supports and services, use person- and family-centred shared decision-making, avoid aversive or traumatic practices, and provide strength-based support for an autistic way of being.[61]

**AAP guidance uses DSM-5 clinical diagnosis and treats sensory symptoms as part of the restricted/repetitive-behaviour domain; it also directs clinicians to begin support for identified developmental delays before an autism evaluation is complete.**[57][49][58]

The AAP recommendation reported in the Medicaid ABA Toolkit names DSM-5-TR criteria for diagnosing autism spectrum disorder. The AAP clinical report was written in 2020 and reaffirmed in 2025.[62][63]

AAP guidance recommends structured evaluation of behaviour, cognition, language, adaptive function, motor function, hearing, vision and sensory processing.[64]

The 2020 AAP clinical report is Hyman, Levy and Myers’s Identification, Evaluation, and Management of Children With Autism Spectrum Disorder. It recommends comprehensive developmental and diagnostic assessment in the US paediatric context using DSM-5 criteria.

It describes the supportive evidence for sensory therapies as still low. Assessment of sensory needs and endorsement of a particular sensory therapy are therefore distinct positions.[64]

The AAP guideline describes the supportive evidence for sensory therapies as still low.

### NICE

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) takes an explicitly either/or approach in its under-19 autism diagnostic guideline, CG128. Its operative recommendation states: 《Use information from all sources, together with clinical judgment, to diagnose autism based on ICD-11 or DSM-5 criteria.》 NICE published CG128 in 2011, amended it in 2017 to reflect DSM-5, changed its terminology in June 2021 from children and young people with autism to autistic children and young people and from symptoms to features, and updated mentions of ICD-10 to ICD-11 in March 2022.[65][51]

NICE therefore permits either ICD-11 or DSM-5 as the diagnostic basis; it does not require adoption of ICD-11 alone or express a preference between the two manuals. NICE also instructs clinicians not to rely on an autism-specific diagnostic instrument alone and to combine information from all sources with clinical judgment.[51]

The National Autistic Society’s account of NICE guidance says that the DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria selected for diagnosis should be recorded in the assessment notes.[83]

Some older NICE material refers to ICD-10 and DSM-5, including quality-standard text describing developmental history and observation in relation to those systems. That wording predates or does not reflect the CG128 diagnostic recommendation amended in 2017, which names ICD-11 or DSM-5.[66][51]

NICE gives sensory processing differences a prominent assessment and care role. For adults receiving a comprehensive autism assessment, NICE directs clinicians to enquire about and assess hyper- and/or hypo-sensory sensitivities and attention to detail. Its stated core autism features are social interaction and communication difficulties plus stereotypic behaviour, resistance to change or restricted interests; sensory sensitivities appear separately in the assessment list.[50]

For children and young people, NICE lists over- or under-reaction to sensory stimuli—such as textures, sounds and smells—and strong reactions to food taste, smell, texture or appearance as features suggesting possible autism. These signs are pattern-recognition aids: NICE says they should not be used alone but should help identify a pattern of reciprocal social-communication difficulties together with unusual restricted and repetitive behaviours.[51][67]

NICE also places sensory issues in the person’s individual profile, alongside skills, needs and behaviours that affect daily functioning and social participation. This means sensory differences can shape referral, assessment, support and accommodation even when they do not determine whether diagnostic criteria are met.[51]

NICE’s sensory guidance directly affects care delivery. When assessing behaviour that challenges, it recommends a functional analysis—an examination of triggers and factors maintaining the behaviour—that includes sensory factors in the physical environment. When deciding on care or interventions, NICE says clinicians should consider hyper- and hypo-sensory sensitivities, whether the environment is suitably adapted, and how those sensitivities could affect intervention delivery.[50]

NICE gives practical examples of sensory-responsive care. For autistic adults, it recommends considering noise reduction, earplugs or ear defenders, and altering the duration or format of assessment or intervention, including regular breaks, where the environment cannot be adapted. For autistic children and young people, it recommends taking account of individual sensory sensitivities to lighting, noise and the colour of walls and furnishings.[50][68]

NICE also frames sensory sensitivity as clinically meaningful in its own terms: hyper- and hypo-sensitivity means being over-sensitive or under-sensitive to sound, light, colour, smell or taste, potentially causing anxiety or pain. The guideline requires clinicians to address environmental and other identified factors that trigger or maintain challenging behaviour before moving to other interventions.[50]

**NICE treats ICD-11 and DSM-5 as alternative accepted diagnostic bases, while giving sensory differences substantial assessment, profiling and care-planning importance without making them standalone diagnostic thresholds.**[51][50][67]

NICE’s update history records that two stakeholders sought closer alignment with ICD-11. NICE said it would assess ICD-11’s impact after full implementation, planned editorial use of the ICD-11-consistent phrase features suggesting possible autism, and in 2021 decided that no substantive guideline update was necessary at that time.[69]

NICE’s assessment safeguards reinforce that sensory and other listed features are not diagnostic checklists. For children and young people, autism-specific tools may structure information gathering but must not make or rule out diagnosis; a negative screening score does not rule out autism. NICE also says not to rule out autism because of good eye contact, affection towards family, reported pretend play or normal language milestones, apparent improvement after needs-based intervention, or an earlier negative assessment when new information is available.[46][70]

For adults, NICE says the AQ-10 may be considered alongside clinical judgement: a score above 6, or clinical suspicion informed by history, should lead to an offer of comprehensive assessment. It states that clinicians should not rely solely on the AQ-10 or any autism-specific tool to decide referral.[46][71]

NICE directs teams to construct an individual profile of strengths, needs, skills and impairments, with sensory sensitivities given as an example of an assessment that may be needed. It also directs clinicians to consider differential diagnoses and coexisting neurodevelopmental conditions.[70][71]

NICE identifies recognition inequities relevant to clinical application: clinicians should bear in mind that autism may be underdiagnosed in girls; it also identifies lower recognition by standard case-identification tools among people with coexisting conditions, women, older people, people from black and minority ethnic groups, and transgender people. NICE directs that signs and features be considered in developmental and cultural context and that parental or carer concerns be taken seriously even when others do not share them.[70][71]

NICE CG142 states that some autistic people are sensitive to changes in their physical or social environment and that problems, including risk-related problems, can escalate rapidly.[71] Its person-centred-care approach requires care to take account of individual needs, preferences and wishes and provides autistic people the opportunity to make informed decisions with healthcare professionals.[71][70]

NICE’s 2021 surveillance found that 74% of respondents reported difficulty accessing mental-health services after an autism diagnosis. It also recorded that the median waiting time for children’s autism diagnosis was 30 weeks, compared with 16 weeks in 2016; the reported increase was attributed largely to a 40% increase in the population-based diagnosis rate.[46]

NICE’s October 2017 consultation draft instead stated that diagnosis should be based on ICD-10 or DSM-5 criteria. In 2021, NICE said it would assess ICD-11’s impact on recommendations when ICD-11 was fully implemented in January 2022, and had previously said that it would consider updating ICD-11 references and recommendation wording. These historical NICE records differ from the current CG128 operative recommendation, which names ICD-11 or DSM-5.[72][73][74][51]

The reported median waiting time for children’s autism diagnosis increased by 14 weeks, from 16 weeks in 2016 to 30 weeks in the later reported period—an 87.5% increase.[46]

NICE produces clinical guidance for England and Wales rather than international classification policy.

For a suspected autistic adult, NICE recommends that a comprehensive assessment cover core autism signs and symptoms, early developmental history where possible, functioning, and hyper- and/or hypo-sensory sensitivities and attention to detail.[75]

NICE includes hyper- and/or hypo-sensory sensitivities among the possible differential diagnoses and coexisting disorders or conditions to assess during a comprehensive assessment.[75]

NICE treats sensory impairment separately from sensory sensitivities that can suggest autism. It identifies sensory impairment, including severe hearing or visual impairment, as a context in which diagnosis may be uncertain; clinicians may need specialist expertise to distinguish signs attributable to the impairment from signs outside that explanation, and should consider a second opinion or referral to a specialised tertiary autism team where severe sensory impairment or insufficient local expertise complicates assessment.[47][72][67][51][76]

NICE advises considering the child’s or young person’s overall development when interpreting signs and symptoms, including that signs may not have been recognised by the child, family or professionals.[76]

NICE guidelines cover health and care in England. Decisions on their application in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland are made by the Welsh Government, Scottish Government and Northern Ireland Executive respectively. NICE states that its recommendations are not mandatory and do not override healthcare professionals’ responsibility to make decisions appropriate to the individual, in consultation with the patient and/or carer or guardian.[77]

NICE says autism may be missed in children and young people with intellectual disability, verbally able children and young people, and girls.[76]

WHO supports ICD-11 implementation internationally while allowing national transition periods; its CDDR places sensory hyper- or hyposensitivity within the essential restricted, repetitive and inflexible-pattern domain of autism. APA and AAP instead centre DSM-5 or DSM-5-TR for clinical diagnosis in their US-facing materials, with the ICD functioning as a complementary coding system for APA and with no AAP instruction to substitute ICD-11 criteria. NICE differs by expressly authorising diagnosis under either ICD-11 or DSM-5.[6][16][5][57][51]

All four positions support the same practical distinction: sensory experiences merit active inquiry and may be diagnostically relevant, but autism diagnosis depends on the full required pattern rather than sensory differences in isolation. WHO and APA/AAP locate sensory phenomena within restricted/repetitive patterns; NICE additionally requires sensory-informed assessment and environmental adaptation in clinical care.[16][48][49][50]

## Autism advocacy organization positions

Judgment — A neurodiversity-affirming approach treats neurological differences as part of human variation, seeks support and access rather than removal of autistic traits, and includes autistic people in decisions that affect them. This is not the same question as which diagnostic manual clinicians should use: a manual can be clinically useful while retaining medical-model language such as disorder, impairment, or deficit.

### Autistic Self Advocacy Network

The Autistic Self Advocacy Network (ASAN) took a direct, participatory and reform-oriented role in the development of DSM-5 autism criteria. It produced two policy briefs on proposed revisions, stating that its purpose was to promote greater autistic-community involvement and representation in development of the fifth *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*.[78] The first brief considered how the draft criteria could affect services, supports and accommodations.[78] The second identified concerns, set out recommendations for addressing them, and said that each recommendation had extensive research support.[79][78]

ASAN’s DSM-5 work also considered variation across age, racial, ethnic and cultural background, and gender.[79][78] That focus matters because diagnostic criteria can affect who is recognized as autistic and who can access support, particularly where presentations differ from historically narrow diagnostic expectations.

ASAN’s wider public-facing approach is explicitly anti-stigma and autistic-perspective based. Its 5 April 2021 parent guide says that many resources present autism as something to be “overcome,” rather than as part of an autistic child, and describes itself as a resource explaining autism from an autistic perspective.[80] The guide includes self-advocacy, communication, presuming competence—assuming a person can understand and participate unless there is sound reason to think otherwise—and good services among its subjects.[80]

**ASAN’s documented position is that autism criteria should be developed with autistic-community representation, preserve diagnostic access and individualized support, and avoid service goals directed at making autistic people appear less autistic.** Its published material in this set makes recommendations concerning DSM-5 development, but it does not explicitly rank DSM-5 or ICD-11 as the more neurodiversity-affirming manual.[78][81][11][79] Therefore, ASAN’s statements support using participation, non-stigma, and equitable access to support as standards for assessing either manual, rather than attributing a DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 preference to ASAN.

ASAN’s neurodiversity-based critique was also specific about the proposed DSM-5 severity scale. It urged the DSM-5 Workgroup to eliminate the scale; if retained, ASAN asked that it not be used to measure treatment outcomes or service eligibility, and that its restricted/repetitive-behaviour component address flexibility rather than redirecting people from focused interests or repetitive behaviours.[81] ASAN was concerned that severity ratings could produce service goals designed to make autistic people appear less autistic and could pathologize special interests and repetitive motor movements that autistic people may value or use for self-regulation.[11] The final DSM-5 accompanying text states that descriptive severity categories should not determine eligibility for or provision of services, which must be developed individually through discussion of personal priorities and targets.[11]

ASAN supports a non-pathological identification system that includes evidence-based neutral differences and strengths, recognizing that autistic traits may be strengths, challenges, or neutral according to context.[11] A 31 May 2023 account in *The Transmitter* reported that ASAN supported folding Asperger syndrome, PDD-NOS and autism into one diagnosis because it believed this would eliminate the stigma attached to an autism diagnosis versus an Asperger diagnosis; ASAN continued to support the unified diagnosis because it had eliminated diagnostic confusion and clinician-by-clician variation.[24] This is a reported account of ASAN’s position; ASAN’s own primary statements in this section are its DSM-5 policy briefs and proposals.[24][79][78] ASAN’s director also said in May 2023 that future DSM criteria should reflect autistic people’s experiences rather than only outside observation, including experiences of movement and motor planning such as getting stuck or catatonia.[24]

From 2009 to 2012, ASAN lobbied the American Psychiatric Association during the DSM-5 revision because the preceding DSM diagnostic categories did not encompass all autism-related diagnoses and symptoms. ASAN did not secure all of its requested changes, but DSM-5 incorporated some of them to make diagnosis more inclusive.[82]

ASAN described the principal potential benefit of an ASD diagnosis as access to services and its principal risk as stigma. It sought to preserve service access and reduce stigma, and stated that DSM-5 had the potential to advance both aims.[81] ASAN distinguished clinical diagnosis, which it said prioritizes sensitivity to avoid under-diagnosis, from research, which it said emphasizes specificity to avoid diagnosing non-autistic people with ASD. ASAN also cited independent studies reporting significant drops in sensitivity from DSM-IV to DSM-5, particularly among people without intellectual disabilities, and stressed the importance of how criterion examples are interpreted.[81]

ASAN’s proposed DSM-5 wording placed “Hyper-or hypo-reactivity to sensory input or unusual interest in sensory aspects of environment” in the restricted, repetitive behaviour, interests or activities domain. It proposed that this domain require at least two manifestations and illustrated the sensory manifestation with apparent indifference to pain, heat or cold; adverse responses to particular sounds or textures; excessive smelling or touching of objects; and fascination with lights or spinning objects.[81]

ASAN stated that DSM-IV and ICD-10 had similar autism-spectrum, or pervasive-developmental-disorder, classifications, which assisted comparison of research findings across countries. During the DSM-5 revision, ASAN stated that the ICD-11 proposal under development included multiple diagnoses within the ASD category and would further distance DSM and ICD classification; it contrasted this with ICD’s more relaxed pervasive-developmental-disorder set, including Atypical Autism, Pervasive Developmental Disorder—Unspecified, and Other Pervasive Developmental Disorders.[81]

ASAN’s participation in DSM-5 revision was substantial but incomplete. A 2020 account states that ASAN was “only partially successful at achieving our advocacy objectives” because autistic input was not formally recognized early in the criteria-development process; the Neurodevelopmental Disorders Workgroup recognized Ari as a formal adviser only after the criteria were finalized, and most Workgroup meetings occurred without autistic input.[11] ASAN’s perspective accepts autism diagnosis and assigns scientific research a key role in defining diagnosis, while challenging a medical model in which treatment seeks to restore autistic people to normalcy or make them indistinguishable from peers.[11]

ASAN asked the Workgroup to reduce the social-communication threshold from three of three subcriteria to two of three, or, alternatively, add a fourth language-and-speech item and require three of four; the Workgroup did not accept this recommendation.[11] ASAN feared that proposed recovery criteria could lead autistic adults to lose services and legal protection, and sought the lower social-communication threshold to keep diagnosis, support and services available.[24] ASAN instead advocated diagnosis on the basis of present or past autistic traits so that learned behaviour or other mitigating measures would not count against a person seeking or retaining diagnosis. The final diagnosis included no recovery criteria, and the severity scale contained no subclinical-autism category.[11]

Following ASAN recommendations, DSM-5 changed the social-communication wording from deficits “across contexts” to deficits “in multiple contexts,” limited relationship-deficit emphasis to peers, and clarified that examples in the text “are illustrative, not exhaustive.” ASAN also sought explicit material on adults; women and girls; racial and cultural minorities; socioeconomic status; and other factors influencing disparities in access to diagnosis.[11] The final accompanying text addressed adults, women and girls, and racial and ethnic minority groups, though less extensively than ASAN sought.[11] The final onset wording stated that symptoms must be present in early childhood but may not become fully manifest until social demands exceed limited capacities or may be masked by learned strategies in later life.[11]

ASAN sought elimination of the DSM-5 severity scale. After that request was rejected, the Workgroup adopted ASAN’s fallback recommendation that the scale not be used for services; the account also attributes removal of fixated interests and repetitive motor movements from the scale to ASAN’s involvement.[11] ASAN considered special interests potentially valuable and advocated service goals focused on adaptive functioning, other person-centred goals, happiness and quality of life rather than correcting or curing autistic traits or enforcing imitation of typical behaviour and appearance.[11] DSM-5 accompanying text states that “Special interests may be a source of pleasure and motivation and provide avenues for education and employment.”[11]

ASAN is an autistic-led organization: it is “operated by and for autistic individuals” and was founded in 2006 by autistic self-advocates Ari Ne’eman and Scott Michael Robertson.[82]

ASAN supported replacing the separate DSM-IV autism diagnoses with a unified autism diagnosis, including Asperger syndrome, pervasive developmental disorder-not otherwise specified, and autism. It simultaneously sought safeguards so that masking or other learned behaviour, apparent recovery, and narrow thresholds would not exclude people from diagnosis and support: it advocated recognition of present or past autistic traits, opposed proposed recovery criteria, and sought a lower required count of social-communication sub-items.[11][24]

ASAN’s DSM-5 influence produced concrete wording and severity-scale changes: DSM-5 described examples as “illustrative, not exhaustive,” changed social-communication deficits from “across contexts” to “in multiple contexts,” limited the relationship-deficit emphasis to peers, and—after ASAN’s unsuccessful request to eliminate the scale—adopted its recommendation that the scale not be used for services; ASAN’s involvement also led to removal of fixated interests and repetitive motor movements from the scale.

ASAN sought recognition of sensory differences within the restricted, repetitive behaviour, interests, or activities domain, proposing hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input or unusual sensory interests as one of the listed manifestations. It also resisted severity measures that could devalue sensory self-regulation or valued interests: ASAN sought to eliminate the severity scale or prevent its use for services, and DSM-5 accompanying text stated that special interests may be sources of pleasure and motivation and avenues for education and employment.[81][11]

ASAN framed diagnostic access as a balance: clinical diagnosis should prioritize sensitivity to avoid under-diagnosing autistic people, researchers emphasize specificity to guard against diagnosing non-autistic people with autism spectrum disorder, and diagnosis carries both access to services and a threat of stigma.[81]

### National Autistic Society

The National Autistic Society (NAS) presents DSM-5 and ICD-11 as alternative accepted systems for autism diagnosis. It says that an autism diagnosis should be based on criteria in DSM-5 or ICD-11 in line with NICE guidance, and calls both manuals standard classification systems used by healthcare professionals.[83] This is an operational diagnostic position: clinicians may use either system rather than treating one as the Society’s preferred neurodiversity-affirming manual.

NAS also articulates a clear neurodiversity position. NAS states that, “In alignment with the principles of the neurodiversity paradigm,” neuro-affirmative support “acknowledges and does not try to ‘remove’ the differences that make someone neurodivergent.” It describes neurodiversity as natural human diversity that can bring benefits and should be valued.[84] NAS states: “Although many people (including most autistic people) do not consider autism to be a disorder, and autism is not a disease,” DSM-5 and ICD-11 are standard classification systems used by healthcare professionals to assess and diagnose autism.[83]

The National Autistic Society describes repetitive behaviours as possible “self-soothing or self-regulating behaviours” and focused interests as “focused and dedicated.”[83]

At the same time, NAS explains that autism diagnostic criteria are framed through a medical model, using terms such as disorder, impairment and deficit.[84] It says that language drawn from medical manuals may differ from how it ordinarily writes about autism, but uses it to represent professional assessment criteria and tools accurately.[30] This separates the Society’s preferred language and support philosophy from the terminology required to describe clinical classification systems.

In a report dated 6 September 2019, NAS chief executive Mark Lever stated that it was unlikely that the Department of Health would regard adjudicating whether clinicians use ICD-10 or DSM-5 criteria as its role; he said this was the role of UK lead clinicians and that civil servants had set up bodies such as NICE to make those decisions.[85] Lever added that, if DSM V is followed fully, a DSM V diagnosis should lead to a clearer picture of the person’s autism and needs.[85] That observation concerns clinical description of needs, not comparative neurodiversity affirmation.[85]

**NAS treats DSM-5 and ICD-11 as accepted diagnostic alternatives while applying its neuro-affirmative principles to support, language and social inclusion rather than to a ranking between the manuals.** This permits a clinician or service to use either manual while avoiding the mistake of treating medical diagnostic terminology as NAS’s preferred way of characterizing autistic people.[83][84][30]

In its 2020 submission to NICE’s surveillance consultation, NAS asked NICE “to better align with ICD-11 and DSM-V in the language it uses around neuro-developmental conditions, rather than to conflate them with mental health disorders,” stating that this “would help clinical practice and tackle persisting confusion among medical professionals.” In the 2021 surveillance consultation, NAS repeated that the guideline should “better align with ICD-11 and DSM-V in the language it uses around neuro-developmental conditions, rather than to conflate them with mental health disorders.” This is a preference for language aligned with both manuals, not a preference for either manual as more neurodiversity-affirming.[70][71]

### Autism Speaks

Autism Speaks discusses DSM-5 principally in clinical and service terms. It says that the goals of updating autism criteria included more accurate diagnosis, identification of symptoms that may warrant treatment or support services, and assessment of severity level.[86] These are goals of diagnostic classification: identifying a clinical category, determining support-relevant symptoms, and describing severity.

Autism Speaks describes DSM-5 Level 1 as involving noticeable social impairment without support and inflexibility affecting one or more settings; Level 2 as marked differences despite support, with behaviours apparent to a casual observer across contexts; and Level 3 as severe social impairment and behaviours interfering “in all spheres.”[21]

Autism Speaks has also shared material discussing DSM-5 criteria, changes in understanding autism, and the benefits of a formal diagnosis. A 2 September 2026 post linked an expert discussion by Peter J. Chung on these topics.[87] The supplied Autism Speaks material does not frame DSM-5 using neurodiversity-affirming concepts or compare it with ICD-11.[86][87]

**Autism Speaks’ documented DSM-5 framing is clinical and service-oriented—diagnostic accuracy, potential treatment or support needs, and severity—not an explicit judgment that DSM-5 or ICD-11 is more neurodiversity-affirming.** Accordingly, its statements can inform discussion of why diagnostic criteria are used, but not an organizational ranking of the two manuals on neurodiversity grounds.[86]

The evaluative criterion here is autistic participation, non-stigmatizing language, and access to individualized support. On that criterion, the cited positions do not decide a winner between DSM-5 and ICD-11: ASAN promoted autistic-community representation and considered consequences for services, supports and accommodations; NAS supports neuro-affirmative support while identifying medical-model diagnostic language; and Autism Speaks frames DSM-5 in terms of diagnostic accuracy, treatment or support needs, and severity.[78][84][86] The three organizations do not share or state a DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 ranking on neurodiversity affirmation. An express organizational comparison of DSM-5 and ICD-11 against this criterion would change that verdict.[78][84][86]

A broader concern about classification language should not be converted into an organizational preference. Some self-advocate organizations have argued that merging Asperger disorder into autism spectrum disorder without distinction could undermine identity, but that report neither identifies ASAN, NAS or Autism Speaks nor ranks DSM-5 against ICD-11.[18] Similarly, a scholarly critique characterizes ICD-11 as a medical-model disease classification while describing growing stakeholder interest in a biopsychosocial account that considers the fit between individual characteristics and environmental demands; this is an academic analysis, not a position of the three advocacy organizations.[88][33]

1. Treat ASAN, NAS and Autism Speaks as holding different priorities, not as a bloc endorsing either DSM-5 or ICD-11.
2. Assess neurodiversity affirmation by participation, language, access to support, presumption of competence and responsiveness to environmental barriers—not by assuming that use of either manual settles those questions.

## Diagnostic safety risks

Autism diagnostic safety concerns arise when a person is given the wrong label, misses a needed label, or receives care that does not match their actual needs. The central safety issue for sensory-focused presentations is that sensory hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, or sensory-seeking can be highly disabling but is neither unique to autism nor sufficient for an autism diagnosis under either manual. ICD-11 includes hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity to sensory stimuli in its restricted, repetitive, and inflexible behaviour component; these were not diagnostic features in ICD-10.[89][90]

**Conclusion:** the supplied comparative material identifies mechanisms by which DSM-5 and ICD-11 could be applied unsafely, but it reports no measured misdiagnosis, over-diagnosis, under-diagnosis, or intervention-harm rate caused by choosing DSM-5 rather than ICD-11. The relevant direct findings instead concern sensory-feature discrimination, diagnostic boundaries, and clinical assessment safeguards.[33][32][26]

### misdiagnosis

Misdiagnosis means that an autism diagnosis is clinically incorrect, or that autism is incorrectly excluded, after adequate assessment against an appropriate reference standard. Classification disagreement means that two manuals or two algorithms classify the same person differently; it identifies a potential safety issue but does not alone determine which classification is correct.

DSM-5 places hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input, or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment, within the restricted and repetitive behaviour domain. It is one of four examples in that domain, rather than an independent route to an autism diagnosis.[89] A clinical sample of 776 people aged 7–61 years was assessed and diagnosed under DSM-IV-TR: 244 had autism, 285 had another pervasive developmental disorder on the autism spectrum, and the remainder had non-ASD diagnoses.

Ghanizadeh (2015) studied sensory features in the multidisciplinary, DSM-IV-TR-era clinical sample of 776 people described here; the study did not test final ICD-11 criteria.[89]

In that DSM-IV-TR-era sample and comparator, none of the sensory features examined predicted autism or broader autism-spectrum status versus non-ASD diagnoses; participants were aged 7–61 years, with a mean age of 19.06 years (SD 6.32).[89] The only sensory item with sensitivity above 0.75—failure to respond to noises that others notice—had specificity of 0.34, meaning it incorrectly classified many people without autism as having autism-spectrum features.[89]

That finding matters because sensory differences are common in other developmental and learning disorders and discriminate autism less strongly than core social dysfunction.[89] **A sensory presentation alone is therefore an unsafe basis for inferring autism under either DSM-5 or ICD-11.** Sensory needs may warrant support, but their diagnostic value is limited unless they occur in the required broader pattern of persistent social-communication differences and restricted, repetitive, or inflexible behaviour.[89][16]

ICD-11 supplies several differential-diagnosis safeguards for sensory presentations. In preschool children, sensitivity to everyday sounds or foods may overshadow underlying social-communication differences.[16] For sensory-related restrictive eating, ICD-11 distinguishes autism from avoidant-restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID): autism requires persistent social-communication differences and restricted, repetitive, inflexible patterns extending beyond food, while ARFID may be separately diagnosed when eating restriction causes significant weight loss, other health consequences, or significant functional impairment.[16] ICD-11 also states that explosive outbursts may follow aversive sensory stimulation, anxiety, a routine change, or interruption of a behaviour sequence rather than intentional defiance.[2]

These boundaries address two opposite clinical errors: mistaking sensory-related food avoidance or sensory-triggered distress for autism, and failing to recognise autism because the sensory concern dominates the referral or consultation. **The practical implication is to assess sensory phenomena alongside developmental social communication, reciprocal interaction, restrictive patterns, physical health, and co-occurring conditions—not in isolation.**[16][2]

Misdiagnosis also occurs when autism is confused with another psychiatric condition. Autism may be confused with personality disorder, particularly among female adolescents, and prior personality-disorder diagnoses were among the frequent diagnoses reported in adults subsequently diagnosed with autism.[90] Sensory overload can contribute to social withdrawal, and sensory hypersensitivity can contribute to emotional turmoil that resembles personality-disorder-related emotional over-reactivity.[90] Misdiagnosing autism as personality disorder may adversely affect prognosis and self-concept.[90]

Judgment — The diagnostic safeguard is a longitudinal developmental formulation: first establish whether there is evidence of early-emerging social-communication differences and restricted/repetitive patterns; second, determine whether sensory distress, withdrawal, emotional dysregulation, eating restriction, or repetitive behaviour is better explained by autism, another condition, or both. This approach prevents a visible sensory or psychiatric presentation from substituting for the required autism pattern.

NICE’s 2021 surveillance guidance identifies assessment-process safety risks separate from either manual’s criteria. NICE says autism-specific diagnostic tools must not be used alone to diagnose autism, and that tools used to identify an increased likelihood of autism must not be used either to make or to rule out a diagnosis; a positive result can have causes other than autism and a negative result does not rule it out.[46] NICE states that relying on assessment-tool scores can cause harm through an incorrect autism diagnosis or false reassurance.[46]

In a UK referral-service sample of 476 adults, 64% of people below the AQ-10 threshold had autism; NICE also reported that the AQ-10 had very low specificity and poor negative predictive value in that population.[46] NICE retained the recommendation to consider AQ-10 for adults with possible autism, but specifies that a score above six **or** clinical suspicion informed by an informant’s history should lead to a comprehensive assessment.[71] This supports multi-source clinical assessment rather than treating a screening result, including a sensory-related referral concern, as a diagnostic conclusion.[46][71]

A sensory or auditory referral can be an entry point to assessment rather than evidence of autism by itself. The Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health reported that some children first attend audiology because they appear not to hear well but have no hearing difficulty, and that behavioural difficulties may become clearer during hearing testing.[70]

NICE directs clinicians to diagnose autism using information from all sources together with clinical judgment rather than relying on an autism-specific diagnostic tool alone. NICE also identifies severe hearing or visual impairment as a context of diagnostic uncertainty and advises consideration of a second opinion where local expertise is lacking for a complex coexisting condition such as severe sensory impairment.[72][76][67][51]

DSM-5 application also depends on assessment quality, not criteria wording alone. In a prospective multicentre study of 349 young children referred for suspected autism, routine developmental-behavioural-paediatrician diagnoses agreed 90% with diagnoses after ADOS-2 results were added; clinician diagnostic certainty was the strongest predictor of diagnostic stability.[91] A 2022 DSM-5 reliability paper also reports earlier evidence that one quarter of community autism diagnoses disagreed with an independent best-estimate assessment battery and that only 60% of differential autism diagnoses had high clinician certainty.[13]

For adults with current social difficulties plus sensory or repetitive behaviours, developmental verification distinguishes autism from complex post-traumatic stress disorder, borderline or avoidant personality disorders, social anxiety, and other conditions that can resemble autistic traits. The recommended safeguard is substantive childhood history; where records are absent, assessment is provisional or multidisciplinary and concurrently assesses trauma, personality pathology, and anxiety disorders.[92] The 2021 diagnostic review likewise recommends detailed developmental histories alongside standardized observations and interpretation of behaviour in cultural context.[25]

NICE identifies sensory impairment, including severe hearing or visual impairment, as a circumstance in which autism diagnosis may be uncertain. When reported signs and clinical observation disagree, NICE recommends obtaining information from other sources; when uncertainty, disagreement, or a complex coexisting sensory, motor, or mental-health condition persists, it recommends considering a second opinion or specialised tertiary autism-team referral.[47]

**Direct evidence versus inference:** No supplied study measures misdiagnosis caused by applying DSM-5 rather than ICD-11. The observed sensory-specific finding is limited discrimination of the sensory items studied in a DSM-IV-TR-era clinical sample; inferring autism from sensory differences alone is therefore a plausible diagnostic-error pathway, not a measured DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 misdiagnosis outcome.[89]

A conceptual comparison describes DSM-5 as presenting selective mutism and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) as differential diagnoses, whereas ICD-11 places conditions that can frequently co-occur with autism in Boundaries with Other Disorders and Conditions and provides greater detail for distinguishing conditions with an autism-like phenotype.[33]

In the 776-person sensory-feature clinical sample, 529 participants (68.2%) had an autism-spectrum classification under DSM-IV-TR and 247 (31.8%) had a non-ASD diagnosis. This was the clinical comparison population for the reported poor discrimination of the sensory features examined.[89]

DSM-5 requires all three social-communication criteria and at least two of its four restricted/repetitive-behaviour manifestations; a sensory feature can contribute to only one of those manifestations. Thus, sensory differences cannot by themselves meet DSM-5 autism criteria.

ICD-11 identifies obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) as a differential diagnosis because its repetitive thoughts or behaviours can be difficult to distinguish from autism’s restricted, repetitive, and inflexible patterns.[14]

In a CDC report of 185 previously diagnosed children aged 6–17 years, parents reported that 15.5% (95% CI 8.4–26.7) had lost the diagnosis because the child never had ASD, ASD was a misdiagnosis or misinterpretation of symptoms, or the child had not been properly tested.[93] Among 83 children whose parents reported that new information led clinicians to say the child did not have ASD but had another condition, a sensory, auditory or processing disorder was the reported replacement diagnosis for 22.6% (n=9).[93]

In people with intellectual disability or global developmental delay, autism should be diagnosed only when social-communication challenges and restricted or repetitive behaviours exceed what is expected for developmental age.[29]

### over-diagnosis

Over-diagnosis is the assignment of autism to someone who does not have the condition under an appropriate clinical reference standard. A 2023 critique of ICD-11 predicts that its broad constellation of symptoms could be difficult to distinguish from other mental disorders and autism-like traits, increasing false-positive diagnoses, prevalence, limits on autism-specific services, and treatment non-specificity.[26] Those claims are forecasts about ICD-11’s likely consequences, rather than reported DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 false-positive rates.[37][26]

The APA later clarified DSM-5’s social-communication requirement with the phrase “all of the following,” intending to preserve the requirement that all three manifestations be present.[131][31][138]

DSM-5 requires clinically significant current impairment but specifies no numerical impairment threshold.[23][128][129]

A contrary forecast came from Michael B. First, an ICD chapter drafting committee member, who described DSM-5 and ICD-11 as very close and predicted no significant difference in who would receive an autism diagnosis.[44] These positions disagree about the expected effect of ICD-11’s wording, but neither supplies an outcome comparison of diagnostic accuracy or false-positive diagnosis.[44][26]

DSM-5 has one more specific false-positive finding, although it concerns a proposed toddler symptom-mapping algorithm rather than ordinary clinical use of the final manual. In a toddler study, a very liberal threshold—one point on each of three social-communication symptoms and two of four restricted/repetitive-behaviour symptoms—produced high sensitivity but classified about half of children without autism as having autism.[94] The authors also reported poor agreement among experts about mapping ADOS and ADI-R behaviours onto DSM-5 symptoms, creating scope for idiosyncratic interpretation in toddlers.[94]

In a registry sample of 726 participants, severe classifications across autism symptoms, cognition and adaptive impairment were concordant for 179 participants (24.7%); moderate classifications were concordant for 25 (3.4%), and mild classifications for 2 (0.3%). Quantitative distinctions between DSM-5 support levels were not initially established, and concordance across these measures was limited.[23][8][139]

Sensory features illustrate why specificity matters. In the 776-person clinical sample, endorsement of at least one sensory feature—the researchers’ closest approximation to the current DSM-V sensory criterion—had poor power to distinguish autism or autism-spectrum diagnoses from non-pervasive-developmental-disorder diagnoses.[89] Sensory differences can be clinically important and appropriate intervention targets even when they do not help discriminate autism from another developmental condition.[89]

**The measured findings are that the sensory features examined had poor discrimination between autism-spectrum and non-autism diagnoses, and that a specified liberal toddler symptom-mapping threshold classified about half of children without autism as having autism.** Clinician overweighting of non-specific sensory signs is a proposed pathway to false-positive diagnosis, rather than an observed clinician behaviour in these studies.[89][94] ICD-11’s explicit instruction that repetitive, routine, or restricted behaviour alone is not indicative of autism without impaired reciprocal social interaction and social communication is an important countermeasure.[14]

In its 2021 surveillance response for CG128, NICE noted that CG128 recommendation 1.2.7 says not to rule out autism because of pretend play, and that the signs-and-symptoms tables are not intended to be used alone to make a diagnosis but to help professionals recognise a pattern.[70] NICE also says signs and symptoms must be considered in the context of overall development and cultural variation.[70] These safeguards address false-positive risk from treating a checklist, a screening score, emotional reactivity, or a sensory concern as sufficient diagnostic evidence.[46][70]

Stakeholders raised concerns that subjective descriptions of emotional reactivity could lead either to dismissal of autism or to over-medication, particularly through gender bias. NICE replied that its tables should be applied to boys and girls equally where appropriate and were not intended for standalone use.[70]

A further system-level concern is that broad use of the autism label to obtain limited educational resources can produce overdiagnosis or inadequate differentiation from other neurodevelopmental or psychiatric conditions.[91] This concern concerns resource-allocation practices rather than a DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 diagnostic effect.

When services require an autism label before providing accommodations, the requirement may encourage inappropriate labelling or leave non-autistic people with sensory disabilities without needed support.[128][139]

DSM-5’s Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder (SPCD) was intended for pervasive social-communication difficulties without the restricted and repetitive behaviours required for autism. The boundary requires clinical judgment about whether restricted or repetitive behaviour is sufficiently present, creating a route for similar presentations to receive different labels.[92]

Direct evidence versus inference: No supplied finding measures an autism false-positive rate attributable to DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 criteria differences. The ICD-11 false-positive concern is an authors’ forecast; sensory findings from the 776-person DSM-IV-TR-era sample support a plausible false-positive pathway if non-specific sensory differences are overweighted, not a measured manual-specific over-diagnosis outcome.[26][89]

The ICD-11 critique calculates 304 (19 × 16) possible combinations of social-domain and restricted/repetitive-domain manifestations. It gives as an example a person with limitations in making and sustaining typical peer relationships plus a persistent preoccupation with special interest in media, and argues that this combination fulfils ICD-11 autism criteria.[15]

### under-diagnosis

Under-diagnosis means that a person who meets autism diagnostic requirements is missed or diagnosed too late. Historical findings show that DSM-5 classifications sometimes differed materially from earlier DSM-IV and ICD-10-era classifications. In one adult comparison, 44% of adults diagnosed under ICD-10R did not meet DSM-5 criteria, and 22% meeting DSM-IV-TR Asperger’s or autistic-disorder criteria did not meet DSM-5 criteria.[1] A review of DSM-IV-TR comparisons reported reductions in autism diagnoses ranging from 25% to 68%, while one study reported a 9% reduction, or sensitivity of 0.91.[28]

Several studies compared DSM-5 criteria with DSM-IV-TR criteria in clinical samples by retrospectively applying DSM-5 criteria to people with an established DSM-IV-TR diagnosis; interpretation is challenging because DSM-IV-TR did not include some behavioural criteria specified in DSM-5.[28] A separate preschool study of 1,061 children—773 classified as autism spectrum disorder and 288 as another developmental disorder with autism symptoms—reported DSM-5 sensitivity of 0.95 and specificity of 0.78 against the study’s SEED2 classification.[42] The differing results reflect different ages, clinical populations, methods, and reference classifications.[1][28][42]

For sensory-focused presentations, ICD-11 identifies a specific missed-recognition mechanism: sensory sensitivities to ordinary sounds or foods can overshadow the social-communication differences that are also required for autism in preschool children.[16] This is especially relevant when the referral question begins with feeding, distress, school refusal, avoidance, aggression, or apparent behavioural dysregulation rather than social communication.

Early autistic traits may initially be interpreted as temperament, giftedness, shyness, language delay, behaviour, or sensory sensitivity; symptom onset, recognition, and diagnosis can therefore occur at different times.[10]

Sensory-related food avoidance can occur in both ARFID and autism, so assessment must determine whether autism’s broader social-communication and non-food restricted/repetitive pattern is present.[16][2]

Under-recognition also affects groups whose autism characteristics may be less visible. ICD-11 notes that females may show fewer restricted, repetitive interests and behaviours and that less severe presentations may go undetected.[16] DSM-5-TR similarly notes that compensation and support can mask developmental difficulties, particularly among people without cognitive or language impairment and perhaps especially adult women.[95] NICE warns that autism may be missed in verbally able children and young people, those with intellectual disability, and girls; it also identifies children with sensory impairments as a group in which autism is under-diagnosed.[51][47]

**The under-diagnosis concern supported here is not that DSM-5 misses more people than ICD-11; it is that both frameworks can be applied too narrowly when assessors fail to elicit developmental social-communication history beyond a prominent sensory, psychiatric, intellectual-disability, or compensated presentation.** A developmental history is particularly important in adolescent and adult assessments because early social-communication signs may only become apparent retrospectively.[16][95][51][88]

Limited childhood information, including in adoption or care histories, can make retrospective assessment of early traits particularly difficult.[10]

NICE’s 2021 surveillance found evidence of underdiagnosis in girls and women.[69][70] The systematic review cited by NICE included 13,784,284 participants and found 4.2 boys diagnosed for each girl diagnosed (95% CI 3.84 to 4.60); the ratio was 3.32 (95% CI 2.88 to 3.84) in studies rated high quality and 3.25 (95% CI 2.93 to 3.62) in population-screening studies.[46] In one observational study, girls with autism (n=24) were less likely than boys with autism (n=106) to show restricted and repetitive behaviour (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.92; p=0.03) and more likely to have parent-reported emotional and behavioural problems (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.13 to 5.29; p=0.02).[46] NICE says high-quality diagnostic assessment may reduce this inequality and recommends that clinicians be aware that autism may be underdiagnosed in girls; it also recommends local autism-strategy pathways specifically for women.[70][71]

NICE reported adult underdiagnosis and diagnostic-access concerns: Scottish population-based estimates were 1.6% in children and 0.6% in adults, compared with the 1.1% adult prevalence cited in the full NICE adult guideline.[46] NICE also reported that service capacity made timely assessment difficult, that delays to full assessment exceeded the recommended three months, and that access to diagnostic services was better for people with learning disabilities than for autistic people without learning disabilities.[69][73][46]

NICE says not to rule out autism because of good eye contact, smiling or affection to family members, reported pretend play or normal language milestones, apparent improvement after a needs-based intervention, or an earlier assessment that found no autism when new information becomes available.[70] NICE also says that the exact features in its signs-and-symptoms tables need not be evident because the tables do not include every possible manifestation of autism.[70]

For very young children, NICE reports diagnostic uncertainty rather than a manual-specific under-diagnosis effect. In toddlers aged 12–36 months, diagnostic stability was 84% (95% CI 80% to 87%); 23.8% did not receive an autism diagnosis at their first visit but did at a later visit, and stability was 50% (95% CI 32% to 69%) at 12–13 months, 79% at 14 months, and 83% at 16 months.[46]

A child-and-adolescent study reported that 83.3% of participants previously diagnosed with autistic disorder, 91.5% with Asperger's disorder, and 34.8% with PDD-NOS met DSM-5 ASD criteria; across these prior DSM-IV-TR groups, 75% retained an ASD diagnosis and 25% were classified differently. The authors reported DSM-5 sensitivity from .69 to 1.00, apart from .57 in an ASD column with 7 participants, and specificity from .64 to .73; sensitivity was lower in the study's low-functioning intellectual-quotient group and higher for females.[20]

Direct evidence versus inference: No supplied finding demonstrates that DSM-5 criteria under-diagnose autism more than ICD-11 criteria, including in sensory-focused presentations. DSM-IV/ICD-10-era to DSM-5 classification losses are historical comparisons, while ICD-11’s warning that sensory sensitivities can overshadow social-communication differences identifies a plausible missed-recognition mechanism rather than a measured DSM-5-versus-ICD-11 under-diagnosis rate.[1][28][16]

A person with marked sensory differences but without sufficiently documented social-communication differences or an additional restricted/repetitive-behaviour manifestation may not meet DSM-5 criteria. That result can represent appropriate diagnostic differentiation rather than under-diagnosis; the safety concern is failure to detect a full autism pattern when developmental history is incomplete or social differences are subtle or camouflaged.

### inappropriate interventions

No supplied finding measures inappropriate intervention caused by choosing DSM-5 rather than ICD-11 autism criteria.[26] Neither diagnostic manual prescribes a treatment.[96][16] The American Psychiatric Association states that DSM does not specify recommended treatments or services, and WHO states that ICD-11 Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements are a classification resource rather than a guide to patient care.[96][16] Therefore, a diagnostic label alone cannot justify a particular sensory therapy, behavioural programme, medication, or restrictive intervention.

The proposed intervention risk differs by manual. The ICD-11 critique predicts that greater diagnostic heterogeneity could reduce diagnostic precision and the fit of both pharmacological and behavioural treatments, increasing treatment non-specificity.[26] DSM-5’s explicit sensory example could, conversely, encourage clinicians or families to treat a non-specific sensory sign as proof of autism and pursue autism-specific treatment without adequate differential diagnosis. The latter is an inference from the poor discrimination of sensory features, not a reported treatment-outcome finding.[89]

Sensory needs still require active clinical response. NICE advises clinicians assessing behaviour that challenges to identify sensory factors in the physical environment and, when planning care, to consider the nature of hyper- or hypo-sensory sensitivities and how they affect intervention delivery.[50] For autistic children and young people, NICE includes lighting and noise levels among environmental factors that may increase challenging behaviour.[68] For autistic adults, NICE recommends environmental adaptations and spaces where a person can be alone if overstimulated.[50]

Some harmful care pathways are documented as general autism-service problems rather than consequences of either manual. The UK national autism strategy reports that chaotic, noisy, unfamiliar inpatient settings can cause sensory distress and that staff misunderstanding of autistic behaviour can contribute to restraint, seclusion, or segregation.[97] NICE advises against medication for core autism features because drug treatments are ineffective for that purpose and carry significant risks; it also advises against antipsychotics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, and exclusion diets for core autism features in children and young people.[66][98]

**The appropriate response to sensory distress is an individualised, trigger-informed plan—often including environmental change, communication support, occupational or medical assessment where indicated, and treatment of co-occurring conditions—rather than treating sensory behaviour as diagnostic proof or as a target for normalisation.** Sensory integration therapy may be considered medically necessary when incorporated into a licensed occupational-therapy plan addressing documented functional impairments, whereas standalone sensory integration therapy and weighted or compression clothing may be classified as investigational by individual payers.[2][50][62][99]

NICE’s 2021 surveillance recorded concerns from topic experts and patient groups that drug treatments continued to be used inappropriately for autistic people despite existing guidance; NHS England’s STOMP and STAMP programmes aim to stop overmedication of people with a learning disability, autism, or both.[46][71] NICE states that antipsychotics, antidepressants and anticonvulsants must not be used to manage core autism features in children and young people because of limited evidence of effectiveness and robust evidence of potential harms.[46] For adults, NICE advises against anticonvulsants, cognitive-function drugs, oxytocin, secretin, antipsychotics and antidepressants for core autism features because of lack of effectiveness evidence and known side effects.[46]

When antipsychotic medication is considered for behaviour that challenges, NICE requires specialist prescribing and careful quality-of-life monitoring, review after 3–4 weeks, and discontinuation at 6 weeks if there is no clinically important response.[71] In a randomized controlled trial of 25 children aged 2.5–12 years with autism and disruptive behaviour, inappropriate drug selection, medication non-adherence and subtherapeutic dosage were the most common drug-related problems; a specialist psychiatric pharmacist increased the number of patients who resolved at least one drug-related problem at eight weeks compared with a hospital pharmacist.[46]

NICE also directs care away from interpreting distress as a behaviour to fix. It defines behaviour that challenges as behaviour arising from interaction between individual and environmental factors, including stereotypic behaviour, anger, aggression, self-injury, and disruptive or destructive behaviour.[71] NICE says that some autistic people are sensitive to changes in their physical or social environment and that problems, including risk-related problems, may escalate very rapidly.[71] Support and care must take account of individual needs and preferences and enable informed decisions in partnership with healthcare professionals.[70][71][74]

NICE states that children and young people who show autism-spectrum behaviours without reaching ICD-10 or DSM-5 criteria for definitive diagnosis should be considered for referral to appropriate services based on their profile. It also advises a second opinion when therapeutic interventions do not produce the expected response.[72][67][76]

Direct evidence versus inference: No supplied finding measures inappropriate intervention caused by choosing DSM-5 rather than ICD-11 criteria. The proposed ICD-11 treatment-non-specificity effect is a forecast, while sensory-responsive assessment and environmental adaptation are clinical safeguards rather than comparative manual effects.[26][50]

DSM-5 severity levels create a separate implementation risk when treated as a service or treatment prescription. ASAN objected during DSM-5 development that a severity scale could produce goals of making autistic people appear less autistic and could target special interests or repetitive motor movements that autistic people value or use for self-regulation.[11] DSM-5 accompanying text states that descriptive severity categories should not determine eligibility for or provision of services and that services require individual discussion of priorities and targets.[11] Nevertheless, ASAN reported that Australia’s National Insurance Disability Scheme usually treated the lowest level, Requiring support, as disqualifying autistic people from services.[11]

A support level is not a comprehensive care plan or a permanent prediction of adult functioning. Individualised functional assessment should determine support, because support needs change across time and environments; successful participation while using an accommodation can demonstrate that the accommodation works rather than that it should be withdrawn.[100] Self-report screeners cannot establish a formal autism diagnosis, assign a validated DSM support level, or determine service eligibility.[100]

For children and young people whose behaviour challenges, NICE requires assessment of physical-environment factors, including lighting and noise, treatment of coexisting physical, mental-health, and behavioural problems, family support, and necessary environmental changes. NICE recommends psychosocial intervention informed by functional behavioural analysis as first-line only when no coexisting mental-health or behavioural problem, physical disorder, or environmental problem has been identified as triggering or maintaining the behaviour.[101] For autistic adults, NICE advises adapting lighting and noise and varying the duration or nature of assessment or intervention, including regular breaks, when the environment cannot be adapted.[102]

The American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2020 guideline characterises the supportive evidence for sensory therapies as still low.[64] Physical and occupational therapists use strategies addressing motor function, motor planning, and sensory processing.[63]

DSM-5 and ICD-11 both make sensory differences diagnostically relevant within the restricted/repetitive behaviour domain, but neither permits sensory differences to establish autism without the required social-communication pattern. The strongest sensory-specific result is that the sensory features studied in a 776-person clinical sample had poor discrimination between autism-spectrum and non-autism diagnoses, including specificity of 0.34 for the only item with sensitivity above 0.75.[89] ICD-11 adds useful clinical boundaries for sensory-related eating, sensory-triggered distress, and preschool presentations in which sensory concerns can obscure social-communication differences.[16][2] Predictions that ICD-11 will increase false positives and treatment non-specificity conflict with an ICD drafter’s expectation of little practical difference in who is diagnosed; neither prediction is a measured comparative outcome.[44][26]

1. **Do not diagnose autism from sensory features alone.** Confirm the full developmental social-communication and restricted/repetitive pattern under the applicable manual. [89][14]
2. **Treat sensory presentations as differential-diagnosis prompts.** Assess ARFID, oppositional defiant disorder, personality disorder, medical contributors, and co-occurring conditions where clinically relevant. [16][2][90]
3. **Separate diagnosis from support allocation.** Provide needs-based sensory and environmental supports even where autism criteria are not met or remain under assessment; do not use DSM-5 severity categories or a self-report screener as a substitute for an individual functional assessment or as a service-eligibility rule. [89][51][50][11][100]
4. **Use developmental history, all available sources, and clinical judgment; do not rely on an autism-specific diagnostic tool alone.** This is particularly important for preschool children with prominent sensory sensitivities and for compensated, verbally able, female, adolescent, and adult presentations. [72][76][16][88][95][51]

No comparative finding demonstrates that selecting DSM-5 rather than ICD-11 directly increases inappropriate sensory therapies, medication, behavioural programmes, or other inappropriate interventions.[26][50]

## Brief reimbursement/coding summary

A clinical diagnostic manual tells the clinician whether a person meets diagnostic requirements. A coding classification supplies the alphanumeric label recorded in health records and claims. A payer then makes a separate coverage decision under its benefit terms, medical-necessity rules, authorization requirements, and procedure-code rules.

**Adopting ICD-11 clinical criteria does not itself change autism coverage or payment.** Coverage changes only when a jurisdiction or payer changes its coding, benefit, medical-necessity, authorization, or payment rules. WHO identifies reimbursement and service eligibility as uses of ICD, and its implementation guidance requires national amendment of payer instruments and country-specific workflow decisions.[56][103]

WHO also identifies clinical decision-making, quality measurement, and patient safety among the health-system uses of ICD-10.[55]

### US

**Clinical criteria:** Aetna states that qualified clinicians for an autism diagnostic evaluation have training and competence in autism spectrum disorder assessment and DSM-5-TR criteria.[104] **Accepted claim codes:** US claims use ICD-10-CM, not ICD-11; F84.0 Autistic disorder includes autism spectrum disorder, while F84.3, F84.5, F84.8, and F84.9 remain related F84-category codes and Z13.41 denotes an autism-screening encounter.

**Payer decision:** diagnosis coding does not itself establish coverage; plan benefits, medical necessity, documentation, and prior authorization govern payment. **ICD-11 adoption:** ICD-11 autism code 6A02 is not the US claims code set, so use of ICD-11 clinical reasoning does not authorize ICD-11 claim submission.[104][105][106][5][2] The current CMS FY 2027 ICD-10-CM materials apply to encounters from 1 October 2026 through 30 September 2027.

On 1 October 2015, the US health-care industry transitioned from ICD-9 to ICD-10 for diagnoses and inpatient procedures; CMS directs use of ICD-10 for services provided on or after that date. ICD-10 applies to all HIPAA-covered parties, not only providers billing Medicare or Medicaid.[107][105]

The American Psychiatric Association (APA) describes DSM-5-TR and ICD as companion publications: DSM-5-TR supplies clinical diagnostic criteria and descriptive information, while ICD supplies the code numbers used for insurance reimbursement and health statistics. The APA states that there are no DSM codes; the codes printed in DSM-5 in addition to ICD-10-CM codes were ICD-9 codes, which ceased to be valid in the United States when ICD-10-CM replaced them on 1 October 2015. US clinicians should submit claims with ICD-10-CM codes.[5]

US autism payment policy commonly combines a DSM-5-TR clinical diagnosis with an ICD-10-CM claim diagnosis. For example, Blue Cross Blue Shield of North Carolina requires a confirmed autism spectrum disorder diagnosis based on DSM-5-TR criteria and identifies it for its adaptive behavioral treatment policy as ICD-10-CM F84.0. Evernorth/Cigna similarly defines a confirmed autism spectrum disorder diagnosis as one made using DSM-5-TR criteria and requires specified ICD-10-CM F84.0–F84.9 codes, excluding F84.2 Rett syndrome, for intensive behavioral interventions. It distinguishes this medical diagnosis from educational identification or autism-service eligibility under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), which is not necessarily a formal autism spectrum disorder diagnosis.[108][109]

Payers do not pay merely because an autism diagnosis code appears on a claim. Aetna covers its listed ICD-10 codes only if its selection criteria are met and states that screening, diagnostic, and treatment coverage remains subject to the member’s particular benefit plan; Capital Blue Cross likewise states that a listed code does not itself establish coverage or payment. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advises clinicians to document the diagnosis or code that supports the specific service requested, noting that an insurer may deny a feeding-related claim when autism spectrum disorder and speech/language disorder are the only diagnoses listed.[104][99][61]

Medicaid makes the distinction especially clear. For children eligible for Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment (EPSDT), Medicaid must cover section 1905(a) services that are medically necessary to correct or ameliorate identified conditions, whether or not the service is otherwise in the state plan. EPSDT does not require a particular diagnosis for coverage; the operative threshold is a qualified provider’s documentation that the requested service is medically necessary for the child’s identified needs. States nevertheless set their own medical-necessity definitions, prior-authorization requirements, documentation standards, and utilization-review processes.[62]

State Medicaid programs may deliver applied behavior analysis (ABA) through fee-for-service, managed care, or both, and payment can be based on a fee schedule, bundled payment, or value-based arrangement. The Medicaid and CHIP ABA Toolkit analyzed Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 97151–97158 in fee-for-service claims and managed-care encounters from 2021–2025; CPT codes describe the service provided, whereas ICD-10-CM codes describe the diagnosis. States may differentiate CPT codes by site of service and by the billing provider’s credential level, including Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA), Board Certified Assistant Behavior Analyst (BCaBA), and Registered Behavior Technician (RBT); BCBA and BCaBA providers may receive a higher rate than an implementing technician. State-specific rules also determine provider qualifications, authorization, service limits, and payment rates.[62]

This structure means that a change in clinical diagnostic criteria could matter to access only where a payer expressly makes those criteria an eligibility condition. North Carolina Medicaid’s autism behavioral-treatment policy, for example, requires a current ASD diagnosis recognized by the current APA DSM and separately requires claims to use ICD-10-CM/PCS codes at the highest supported specificity. That is a payer policy choice, not a consequence automatically produced by DSM-5-TR or ICD-11.[110]

The practical consequence is that US clinicians should document the clinical basis for diagnosis and functional treatment need, while billing staff must select the current ICD-10-CM diagnosis code and applicable CPT service code required by the payer. A clinical decision to use ICD-11-style reasoning does not authorize submitting ICD-11 codes to a US HIPAA claim.[105][5][62]

For autism, ICD-10-CM category F84 is Pervasive developmental disorders. F84.0 is Autistic disorder and includes Autism spectrum disorder, Infantile autism, Infantile psychosis, and Kanner’s syndrome; Asperger’s syndrome is excluded from F84.0 and coded F84.5. Other F84-category codes used in payer policies and public-program analyses include F84.3 other childhood disintegrative disorder, F84.8 other pervasive developmental disorders, and F84.9 pervasive developmental disorder, unspecified; Z13.41 denotes an encounter for autism screening. The F84 tabular instruction is to code associated medical conditions and intellectual disabilities as well.[106][104][62]

Thus, F84.0 is the principal ICD-10-CM claim and documentation label for autism spectrum disorder, while complete clinical documentation supports selection of the applicable autism code and additional codes for associated conditions. DSM-5-TR diagnostic formulation and ICD-10-CM claim coding are related but distinct functions.[106][105][5]

ICD-11 supplies autism spectrum disorder code 6A02, with subcategories based on co-occurring disorder of intellectual development and functional-language impairment, but it is not the diagnosis-code set currently used for US HIPAA claims. A US ICD-11 transition would require revisions to HIPAA standards, billing and payment methodologies, quality measures, data-processing algorithms, statistical programs, and classification software. In a 2021 study, only 23.5% of ICD-10-CM codes could be fully represented by a single ICD-11 stem code. These are coding-system implementation requirements, not a demonstrated change in autism coverage, eligibility, or payment rates.[2][107][105][111][112]

US coverage also varies by funding arrangement. State autism-insurance mandates generally govern fully insured commercial plans, whereas self-funded employer plans governed by ERISA are generally not subject to those state mandates; Medicaid fee-for-service operates through EPSDT, the state plan and provider manual, Medicaid managed care through the state contract and managed-care organization policy, and CHIP coverage under each state’s CHIP plan.[113] A Pennsylvania Insurance Department notice published in the Pennsylvania Bulletin on 4 November 2023 required state-regulated commercial insurers, from 1 January 2024, to meet state-law obligations to cover autism benefits, treat autism spectrum disorder as a mental health condition subject to the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) of 2008 and Pennsylvania Act 14, and comply with mental-health-parity requirements.[114]

A quasi-experimental interrupted-time-series study of electronic medical records and insurance claims from ten US health-care systems, serving more than 8 million members annually, examined 30 monthly observations from July 2014 through December 2016 around the October 2015 ICD-9-to-ICD-10 transition. It found no meaningful overall change in recorded autism diagnosis rates associated with that coding transition. For ages 0–5 years, rates were 4.302 and 6.082 per 1,000 enrolled members before and after transition, respectively, but the change in monthly slope was not significant (ΔB=0.005, SE 0.004; p=0.251); for ages 6–11 years, rates were 6.189 and 6.075 per 1,000; for ages 12–17 years, 3.990 and 3.388 per 1,000, with a post-transition trend change of ΔB=−0.031 (SE 0.007; p=0.0003); and for ages 18 years and older, 0.327 and 0.338 per 1,000.[115]

CDC’s 2020 Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) Network surveillance in 11 US sites treated a qualified professional’s written ASD diagnosis, an autism special-education classification, or an ASD ICD code as alternative record indicators for a child aged 8 years. Of 6,245 children meeting that surveillance case definition, 74.7% had a documented diagnostic statement, 65.2% an autism special-education classification, 71.6% an ASD ICD code, and 37.4% all three indicators. The percentage with an ASD ICD code varied from 51.9% in Minnesota to 82.7% in California.[116]

CDC reported that 89.5% of the 4,472 children with an ASD ICD code also had a documented ASD diagnostic statement or autism special-education classification; 7.5% of all 6,245 children met the surveillance case definition through an ICD code alone.[116]

### EU

**Clinical criteria:** clinical diagnosis and reimbursement coding are separate national acts. **Accepted claim codes:** ICD-11 classifies autism spectrum disorder as 6A02, but each country determines its transition and claim-coding system; Germany currently requires ICD-10-GM for inpatient and outpatient diagnosis coding. **Payer decision:** national benefit and needs-assessment rules determine eligibility and payment. **ICD-11 adoption:** no EU-wide rule makes adoption of ICD-11 clinical criteria an autism coverage or reimbursement change.[2][54][117][118] ICD-11 became effective internationally on 1 January 2022, but WHO Member States determine their own transition arrangements and may continue using ICD-10 as necessary. WHO’s implementation timetable is expressly a template rather than a prescribed national schedule.[6][54][103]

WHO says countries may retain ICD-10 as needed without a five-year implementation penalty for ICD-11.[6]

ICD-11 identifies autism spectrum disorder as 6A02. Its listed subcategories record co-occurring disorder of intellectual development and functional-language impairment: 6A02.0, 6A02.1, 6A02.2, 6A02.3, and 6A02.5.[2] It also includes 6A02.Y, other specified autism spectrum disorder, and 6A02.Z, autism spectrum disorder, unspecified.[2][27] A provisional autism diagnosis can be represented by postcoordination—linking an additional extension code—with the example 6A02&XY7Z.[2][16]

These ICD-11 categories provide more structured clinical coding detail than a single broad autism label, but they do not specify a benefit entitlement, a therapy authorization rule, or a reimbursement amount. WHO identifies reimbursement, resource allocation, and clinical recording as potential ICD-11 uses, while leaving implementation decisions to national health systems.[119][56][103]

Germany illustrates why an EU-wide answer would be misleading. Germany’s current official classification for inpatient and outpatient diagnostic coding is ICD-10-GM, the German Modification of ICD-10; BfArM identifies ICD-10-GM 2026 as the current valid version. ICD-10-GM remains the official German classification until ICD-11 is introduced in the relevant application area.[117][54]

German payment architecture is directly connected to ICD-10-GM coding. The classification is an important basis for the G-DRG and PEPP inpatient case-payment systems; outpatient reimbursement under the Einheitlicher Bewertungsmaßstab (EBM, Uniform Assessment Standard) and the morbidity-based risk-adjustment fund also require diagnoses encoded under ICD-10-GM. Its use in inpatient and outpatient care is set out in sections 301 and 295, respectively, of Book V of the German Social Code.[117]

Germany has not yet made ICD-11 the statutory coding classification. BfArM states that ICD-11 in German is available as a test version, not released for statutory use, and that introduction for morbidity coding will take several years because ICD is deeply integrated into the health-care system. The German ICD-11 translation is undergoing quality assurance.[54][120]

A transition cannot safely be treated as a simple one-code conversion. WHO says ICD-10-to-ICD-11 mapping tables are designed to compare data, not automatically generate ICD-11 codes. Germany’s transition analysis found that WHO mappings were often insufficiently specific for German applications and identified erroneous mappings requiring quality assurance; the one-to-one relationships examined were insufficient to analyse the change in diagnosis assignments across ICD-10-GM use cases.[6][121]

EU autism reimbursement therefore follows the national ICD version and the national payment architecture, not an EU-wide rule attached to ICD-11 code 6A02. Germany shows that ICD-11 may be clinically available while ICD-10-GM remains the mandatory reimbursement coding framework.[2][54][117][120]

ICD-11 uses postcoordination: additional codes can be linked to a stem code to record clinically significant features. For example, 6A02&XY7Z records a provisional diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. ICD-11’s Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR) provide detailed guidance for clinicians applying mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental diagnoses, while the Mortality and Morbidity Statistics (MMS) linearization provides briefer definitions for statistical reporting and health-information purposes.[2][14][16]

ICD-11 is designed for direct clinician use as well as coder-mediated, statistical, and institutional workflows; each country determines the applicable workflow in its implementation plan. Its autism coding detail and postcoordination mechanism record clinical information, but do not themselves create a benefit entitlement, therapy-authorization rule, or reimbursement amount.[103][2][119]

The Netherlands provides a second national example of why neither diagnosis nor coding creates a uniform European entitlement. Support falls under the Long-term Care Act (Wlz), Youth Act (Jeugdwet), Social Support Act (Wmo), or Health Insurance Act (Zvw), with different insurers or payers according to the applicable law.[118] Speech and occupational therapy are primarily financed through statutory health insurance, but coverage varies by contracted-provider status, therapy, insurer and plan; speech therapy is typically uncapped, while occupational therapy is often limited to around ten sessions per year.[118] A medical autism diagnosis alone does not entitle a family to a Personal Budget or Double Child Benefit: Personal Budget eligibility depends on care and support needs, and Double Child Benefit requires extensive care needs confirmed through a positive recommendation from the Care Needs Assessment Centre.[118] Under municipal Youth Care, a formal medical diagnosis is not a strict requirement for a Personal Budget, and municipal assessment procedures, budget ceilings and caregiver hourly rates differ.[118]

The Netherlands’ clinical guidance for children and young people recommends DSM-5-TR criteria, or the Dutch equivalent DC:0-5, for assessment of autism spectrum disorder and related psychiatric issues. Its use of DSM-5-TR for assessment alongside multiple needs-based funding routes demonstrates that a clinical diagnostic framework and a reimbursement decision are separate acts.[118]

European coding can connect to payment through national funding groupings and tariff rules. Germany uses ICD-10-GM; France applies ICD-10 in the PMSI funding framework, whose grouping logic affects reimbursement; Nordic countries use ICD-10 national extensions that affect how F-codes map to reimbursement tariffs; and the United Kingdom uses SNOMED CT as the primary clinical terminology in primary care and ICD-10 for secondary-care reporting. Because Germany’s ICD-10-GM is integrated into payment systems, reimbursement effects of an ICD-11 transition depend on each country’s coding and payment arrangements.[122][117]

### ICD-10-CM

The US entry above states the current ICD-10-CM autism claim mapping: F84.0 is the central claim and documentation label for autism spectrum disorder, with F84.3, F84.5, F84.8, and F84.9 used in related coding contexts.[106][104][62] In US coding practice, F84.0 is Autistic disorder and includes autism spectrum disorder as an inclusion term.

Related F84-category codes used by payer policies and public-program analyses include F84.3 other childhood disintegrative disorder, F84.5 Asperger’s syndrome, F84.8 other pervasive developmental disorders, and F84.9 pervasive developmental disorder, unspecified; Z13.41 denotes an encounter for autism screening.[106][104][62]

This coding structure does not exactly mirror DSM-5’s single clinical autism spectrum disorder diagnosis. The difference is administrative as well as historical: a payer may accept a DSM-5-TR diagnosis but require a particular ICD-10-CM code for claims. The CMS Medicaid and CHIP ABA analysis, for example, operationalized ASD using F84.0, F84.5, F84.8, and F84.9 claims codes for 2021–2025.[5][62]

ICD-10-CM also permits associated conditions to be coded in addition to autism. The F84 tabular instruction is to code associated medical conditions and intellectual disabilities, and complete clinical documentation is necessary for accurate code selection. This supports recording co-occurring needs separately rather than allowing an autism label to stand in for every medical, developmental, or behavioral presentation.[106][105][62]

Coding specificity has operational importance but does not establish medical necessity. North Carolina Medicaid requires ICD-10-CM/PCS reporting at the highest level of specificity that supports medical necessity, while its behavioral-treatment policy separately requires prior approval and adequate diagnostic documentation. Thus, accurate coding is necessary for compliant claims, but authorization still depends on the payer’s substantive coverage criteria.[110]

**ICD-10-CM is the current US claims language for autism, while DSM-5-TR or another accepted clinical standard supplies the diagnostic formulation required by the clinician or payer.** Conflating the two can lead to incorrect assumptions that a diagnosis code alone guarantees either diagnostic validity or service payment.[105][5][99]

F84.0 does not encode DSM-5-TR Level 1, Level 2 or Level 3 support needs. Those support levels are recorded in the narrative clinical record, separately for social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviours, rather than on the F84.0 claim line; payer review can use that documentation in judging medical necessity.[113]

The US clinical modification differs from international ICD-10 in claim-valid codes. F84.1 atypical autism and F84.4, although present in WHO ICD-10, were not adopted in ICD-10-CM and are invalid on HIPAA-covered claims.[113]

The American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) states that complete and accurate diagnostic and procedure coding requires joint work by the health-care provider and coder, and that the accountable physician or qualified practitioner establishes the diagnosis. For Medicare, APTA directs providers to the local Medicare Administrative Contractor to determine whether specified ICD-10 codes must accompany specified CPT codes for coverage.[123]

### ICD-11

The US and EU entries above state the current ICD-11 position: ICD-11 classifies autism spectrum disorder as 6A02, with subcategories for co-occurring disorder of intellectual development and functional-language impairment, but national implementation determines whether 6A02 is a reportable or billable code.[2][54][103] Its coding system can use postcoordination, meaning that a stem code may be linked to additional codes to express clinically significant features. WHO describes 6A02 as the autism spectrum disorder stem category and its intellectual-development and functional-language subcategories as the main autism coding structure.[103][2]

ICD-11’s clinical diagnostic requirements and its Mortality and Morbidity Statistics (MMS) coding linearization must be distinguished. The Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR) provide detailed guidance to clinicians applying mental, behavioural, and neurodevelopmental diagnoses, whereas MMS offers briefer definitions for health-information and statistical purposes. This distinction matters because a clinician’s formulation and a national reporting or reimbursement code may be related but are not the same administrative act.[16][2]

In the United States, ICD-11 is not the diagnosis-code set currently specified for HIPAA claims. A future US transition would require revisions to standards, billing and payment methodologies, software, quality measures, and crosswalk tools; one analysis notes that only 23.5% of ICD-10-CM codes could be fully represented by a single ICD-11 stem code in a 2021 study. These are implementation requirements, not evidence that autism coverage, service eligibility, or reimbursement rates would change.[107][105][111][112]

Germany likewise anticipates systems work rather than an automatic benefit change. BfArM expects that payment systems, quality-assurance systems, and the morbidity-based risk-adjustment system (Morbi-RSA) will need adaptation to a new diagnostic key. That prediction concerns the infrastructure required to process new codes; it does not set a new autism entitlement or payment amount.[124][121]

Judgment — If a US payer or European national payer formally changed its policy to require ICD-11 diagnostic criteria or ICD-11 codes, the effect on access would depend on the exact transition rule: whether existing diagnoses are grandfathered, which 6A02 subcategories are accepted, whether prior-authorizations are re-reviewed, and whether payment groupers are recalibrated. The manual alone does not answer those policy questions.

The 6A02 subcategories include 6A02.4, autism spectrum disorder with disorder of intellectual development and with impaired functional language; this completes the sequence 6A02.0 through 6A02.5.

The United States has not announced an ICD-11 adoption timeline.[125] Workers’ compensation and auto insurers are not subject to HIPAA and were not required to switch to ICD-10-CM, although most likely transitioned.[123]

US autism claims currently use ICD-10-CM, often alongside DSM-5-TR-based diagnostic documentation; diagnosis codes, CPT service codes, medical necessity, benefit design, and authorization are distinct claim elements.[5][62] ICD-11 provides the international autism category 6A02 and clinically useful subcategories, but national adoption determines whether it becomes a reportable or billable code.[2][54][103] Germany demonstrates that European implementation remains national: ICD-10-GM currently underpins diagnosis coding and major payment systems despite ICD-11’s international availability.[117][120] An ICD-11 transition can require extensive payer and information-system redesign, but neither US nor EU rules cited here establish an automatic autism coverage or reimbursement change.[112][124][103]

1. Use the diagnostic criteria accepted by the relevant clinician, payer, and jurisdiction, but submit the diagnosis code required by the current claims system.
2. Verify coverage against the individual plan or state/national policy, including medical necessity and prior authorization; do not rely on an autism code alone.
3. For cross-border, research, or transition work, retain the clinical diagnosis and coding version used, rather than assuming ICD-10 and ICD-11 autism codes are interchangeable.

Whether DSM-5 and ICD-11 identify different autistic people, particularly people with sensory-prominent presentations, remains open. It would be settled by paired application of both manuals in the same clinically representative participants, using an independent diagnostic reference standard and reporting sensitivity, specificity, agreement, service access, and intervention consequences. Whether either manual produces different reimbursement or coverage outcomes in individual European jurisdictions also remains open and depends on the applicable national implementation and payer rules.
