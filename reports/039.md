# Independent Directors Under NASDAQ Listing Standards

## Executive summary

Under Nasdaq Rule 5605(a)(2), an independent director is a non-executive, nonemployee director whom the board affirmatively determines has no relationship that would interfere with independent judgment. A person who falls within Nasdaq’s specified bright-line disqualifications cannot be found independent, and clearing those tests alone does not qualify the person because the board must still make its own contextual determination.

Most Nasdaq-listed companies must have a majority-independent board under Rule 5605(b)(1), rather than an entirely independent board. Controlled Companies are exempt from that majority requirement and certain independent-director functions, while Foreign Private Issuers may generally follow qualifying home-country governance practices; specified audit-committee requirements remain applicable.[1][2][3][4]

## What an independent director is

The current Nasdaq Rule 5600 Series contains Nasdaq’s corporate-governance listing standards, including its independent-director requirements.[1][5][6] Rule 5605(a)(2) defines an **Independent Director**; Rule 5605(b)(1) sets the general requirement that most Nasdaq-listed companies have a majority-independent board; Rules 5605(c) through (e) impose separate independence duties for audit committees, compensation committees, and director nominations; and Rule 5615 modifies the applicability or timing of specified governance requirements through exemptions and phase-in schedules.[1][5][6]

The central point is that independence is not self-certified by the director. It is a formal determination made by the company’s board of directors under Nasdaq’s listing standards. The board must apply Nasdaq’s specified rules, but must also assess the actual relationships that could affect the director’s ability to act independently.[2][7]

### Define an independent director under the NASDAQ listing standards.

Nasdaq Listing Rule 5605(a)(2) defines an **Independent Director** as 《a person other than an Executive Officer or employee of the Company or any other individual having a relationship which, in the opinion of the Company’s board of directors, would interfere with the exercise of independent judgment in carrying out the responsibilities of a director.》 In ordinary terms, the director must not be part of management or the workforce and must be sufficiently free from company ties to make board decisions on their merits.[8][1][6]

An **Executive Officer** is a defined securities-law term. Nasdaq incorporates the officers covered by SEC Rule 16a-1(f) under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, regardless of whether the entity concerned is itself public. A **non-executive employee** means an employee who is not a Section 16 officer. This category generally includes senior officers such as the president, principal financial officer, principal accounting officer, certain vice presidents responsible for a principal business unit or function, and other people who perform policy-making functions; it can include qualifying officers of a parent or subsidiary.[5][9]

The word **Company** is also broader than the listed legal entity alone. For the Rule 5000 Series, it means an issuer with a security listed or applying to list on Nasdaq and includes an unincorporated issuer, such as a limited partnership. For the independence rules, Nasdaq’s interpretive material treats a parent or subsidiary as included where the listed company controls the entity and consolidates it into SEC-filed financial statements. An entity shown solely as an investment in those financial statements is not included on that basis. Together, these definitions mean that the independence inquiry can reach relevant officer, employment, and relationship ties involving controlled and consolidated parent or subsidiary entities, not just the listed company.[10][5]

**Nasdaq therefore uses a two-part independence standard: the director must be outside management and employment, and the board must affirmatively conclude that no other relationship would interfere with independent judgment.**[1][2][7]

That second part is the **affirmative board determination**. “Affirmative” means the board must make a positive finding of independence after considering the relevant facts; it is not enough that the director has not disclosed an obvious problem. The board’s task is to decide whether a relationship—directly or through another organization—would interfere with the director’s ability to exercise independent judgment when performing director duties.[2][11][12]

Nasdaq’s standard is therefore both **principles-based** and **rule-based**. It is principles-based because the board must evaluate relationships in their actual context. It is rule-based because Rule 5605(a)(2) also contains categorical, or “bright-line,” disqualifications: relationships that prevent a board from treating a director as independent even if the board otherwise believes the director could act objectively. The SEC described the relationships addressed by Nasdaq’s revised definition as employment, business, financial, and family relationships, and stated that the board must identify any additional material relationship that could interfere with independent judgment.[7][12][13]

The distinction is important. In 2017, Nasdaq staff stated that, where an enumerated bright-line circumstance applies, the director cannot be deemed independent and the board may not reach the opposite conclusion. Where none applies, the board must still affirmatively determine under the Rule and IM-5605 that no relationship would interfere with independent judgment.[14]

The later section, **Who qualifies**, addresses the practical positive conditions for qualification. The following section, **Who is disqualified**, addresses the full list of categorical employment, business, financial, and family relationships, look-back periods, thresholds, and exceptions. For present purposes, the key definitional point is that those categories are minimum boundaries around, not substitutes for, the board’s independence judgment, which must also identify other material relationships that could interfere with independent judgment.[7][13][2]

A useful example is share ownership. Nasdaq does not treat ownership of Company stock by itself as a reason that the board may not find a director independent. Ownership is deliberately absent from Nasdaq’s objective factors. But a board must still consider whether a particular ownership stake, together with other facts, creates a relationship that could interfere with the director’s independent judgment.[15][16][1]

A second example is interim service as an Executive Officer. Under IM-5605, interim employment by a director as an Executive Officer does not automatically bar a later independence finding under Listing Rule 5605(a)(2)(A), provided the interim employment lasted no longer than one year. The board must nonetheless make the affirmative overall relationship determination; the interim-service provision does not itself confer independence.[2]

The term **Family Member**, which is important to several categorical tests discussed in **Who is disqualified**, includes a person’s spouse, parents, children, siblings, mothers- and fathers-in-law, sons- and daughters-in-law, brothers- and sisters-in-law, and anyone other than a domestic employee who shares that person’s home.[17][8] A domestic employee who shares the director’s home is not a Family Member for the bright-line tests, but the board must still review that relationship on a facts-and-circumstances basis and make the affirmative independence determination.[8] For the bright-line tests, Nasdaq interprets children as excluding stepchildren; the board instead decides, based on the facts and circumstances, whether a stepchild relationship would interfere with the director’s independent judgment.[6][8] A stepchild who was the director’s dependent or lived in the director’s household as a minor may have a relationship similar to that of a biological child, while an adult stepchild whom the director never parented and who never shared the director’s household may have an attenuated relationship.[18][19] For the rule’s three-year look-back provisions, a person who ceased to be a Family Member because of legal separation, divorce, death, or incapacitation need not be considered.[5] Nasdaq’s 2020 rule change also excludes a domestic employee residing in the director’s home from the Family Member definition; the board must nevertheless assess that relationship under the general facts-and-circumstances independence test.[1][5] An adult stepchild who never shared the director’s household and for whom the director never acted as a parent likewise remains subject to that contextual board assessment rather than an automatic Family Member bright-line test.[8]

For registered investment companies, Nasdaq provides a separate measurement. Rather than applying the ordinary Rule 5605(a)(2)(A)–(F) bright-line tests, Rule 5605(a)(2)(G) treats a director as non-independent if the person is an **interested person** of the Company under Section 2(a)(19) of the Investment Company Act of 1940, other than solely in the capacity of a board or committee member. This is a tailored statutory alignment, not a relaxation of the underlying concern about conflicted oversight.[1]

The general independence definition applies throughout Nasdaq’s listing standards, although audit-committee and compensation-committee service bring additional requirements or considerations. In particular, general board independence should not be conflated with audit-committee independence: audit-committee members must meet Rule 5605(a)(2) and separate federal independence standards under SEC Rule 10A-3.[15][6][20]

For compensation-committee service, the board must make the Listing Rule 5605(a)(2) determination and must additionally consider all factors specifically relevant to whether a relationship is material to the director’s ability to be independent from management in performing compensation-committee duties. Nasdaq specifically identifies the source of the director’s compensation—including any consulting, advisory, or other compensatory fee paid by the Company to the director—and whether the director is affiliated with the Company, a subsidiary of the Company, or an affiliate of a subsidiary of the Company as factors for that committee-specific inquiry. These are required considerations, not automatic disqualifications merely because such a relationship exists.[4][20][21]

What follows is that Nasdaq independence is not equivalent to being a nonemployee director in the everyday sense. A nonemployee may still fail the rule, while a director who avoids every automatic bar still needs an affirmative, contextual board finding of independent judgment.[1][22][2]

Nasdaq’s fact-specific inquiry also applies to relationships outside the defined Family Member category. Nasdaq explained that a stepchild who was the director’s dependent or lived in the director’s household as a minor may have a relationship similar to that of a biological child, whereas an adult stepchild whom the director never parented and who never shared the director’s household may have an attenuated relationship.

A director serving currently as the Company’s interim chief executive officer is not independent while holding that executive role. This is distinct from Nasdaq’s rule for a director who previously served as an interim Executive Officer for no more than one year and is being considered for a later independence determination.[23][2]

The definition excludes a current employee. Separately, subject to specified exceptions, the three-year look-back prohibits an independence finding for a director who was a Company employee during the preceding three years.[24][16]

The Company must disclose director-independence information in its annual-meeting proxy statement or annual report on Form 10-K.[25][15]

A Nasdaq-listed domestic issuer generally reports director independence under Item 407(a) of Regulation S-K. A Foreign Private Issuer identifies the directors its board has determined to be independent under Rule 5605(a)(2) in its annual report on Form 20-F or Form 40-F.[4]

A person who does not qualify as independent may still serve on the board; the consequence under Nasdaq’s listing standards is that the person cannot be counted toward requirements for independent directors, such as the majority-independent-board rule or independent committee membership.

### Where do these requirements sit within the NASDAQ listing-standards framework?

Nasdaq’s independent-director rules are **listing standards**: exchange rules that a company must satisfy to obtain and maintain a Nasdaq listing. They sit within the Nasdaq Listing Rule 5000 Series, which governs qualification, listing, and delisting on The Nasdaq Stock Market. The Rule 5200 Series addresses listing procedures, prerequisites, and disclosure obligations; the Rules 5300, 5400, and 5500 Series contain quantitative listing requirements for the Global Select Market, Global Market, and Capital Market.[10]

The independent-director rules are not quantitative financial tests. They are in the Nasdaq Listing Rule 5600 Series, Nasdaq’s **corporate-governance requirements**. Corporate governance means the legal and institutional arrangements through which a company is directed and overseen: who makes key decisions, how management is monitored, and what safeguards address conflicts of interest. Nasdaq identifies the Listing Rule 5600 Series as relating to corporate governance and requires prompt notice after a Company executive officer becomes aware of noncompliance with that Series.[10][4]

Rule 5605 is titled **Board of Directors and Committees**. It contains the core rules on director independence, board composition, independent-only meetings, and committee oversight. Rule 5615 is titled **Exemptions from Certain Corporate Governance Requirements** and contains exemptions and phase-in schedules for specified company types and transition circumstances.[1][24]

The structure matters because it separates three questions that are often blurred together. First, Rule 5605(a)(2) asks whether a particular person is an Independent Director. Second, Rule 5605(b)(1) asks whether enough directors on the full board are independent. Third, Rules 5605(c) through (e) allocate particular oversight functions to independent directors or independent committees.[1][3][6]

The general full-board rule does not mean that every director must be independent. It requires a **majority** of the board to be independent for the companies to which Rule 5605(b)(1) applies. Thus, a Nasdaq-listed company can generally have executive directors, including a chief executive officer who serves on the board, while still satisfying the rule if independent directors comprise more than half of the board.[3][20][7]

The board requirement is paired with the executive-session rule. Independent directors must meet regularly without other directors present. Nasdaq calls these independent-only meetings executive sessions, and its interpretive material contemplates at least two meetings each year. This requirement gives the independent directors a formal opportunity to discuss oversight issues without management or other non-independent directors in the room. When director nominations are decided by a vote of independent directors, an executive session can provide the required forum for those directors to meet alone and make that decision.[4][5]

Rules 5605(c), (d), and (e) go further for sensitive functions. Audit-committee members must be independent and meet additional federal-law and Nasdaq requirements; compensation-committee members must be independent and be assessed for independence from management; and director nominations must be handled by independent directors acting alone or through an all-independent committee.[20][4]

These committee provisions are separate from the majority-independent-board rule. A board can be majority independent but still fail a committee rule if, for example, a non-independent director sits on a committee that must be composed of independent directors. Conversely, a company’s reliance on a limited committee exception does not transform the affected director into an Independent Director generally.[4][26]

The audit committee illustrates the additional layer particularly clearly. The committee must have at least three members. Each member must meet Nasdaq’s Rule 5605(a)(2) definition, meet SEC Rule 10A-3(b)(1), not have participated in preparing the Company’s or a current subsidiary’s financial statements at any time during the previous three years, and be able to read and understand fundamental financial statements. The separate federal-law independence test adds limits on compensation and affiliation: the member may accept no Company compensation other than for board service and may not be an affiliated person of the Company or a subsidiary—meaning a person that controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with the issuer.[20][33]

Rule 5615 acts as the qualifications and transition layer to Rule 5605. It does not rewrite the definition of an Independent Director. Instead, it identifies companies and circumstances in which Nasdaq permits an exemption, a home-country alternative, or a limited period to come into compliance with the underlying governance requirement.[1][30][34]

For example, a **Controlled Company**—a category addressed in more detail in **Which companies must have independent directors**—is a company in which an individual, a group, or another company holds more than 50% of the voting power for electing directors. It is exempt from the majority-independent-board requirement in Listing Rule 5605(b) and from independent-director oversight requirements for executive compensation and nominations in Listing Rules 5605(d) and 5605(e). It is not exempt from Listing Rule 5605(b)(2)’s executive-session requirement.[4][5][21][27]

A **Foreign Private Issuer** is generally subject to Nasdaq corporate-governance requirements, but Rule 5615(a)(3) permits it to follow home-country governance practices instead of certain Nasdaq requirements if it provides the required notice and disclosure. The issuer remains subject to specified requirements, including the applicable audit-committee requirements. Foreign Issuers—non-U.S. companies that are not Foreign Private Issuers—are generally subject to all Nasdaq corporate-governance requirements set out in the Listing Rule 5600 Series.[3][35][29]

Rule 5615 also contains phase-in schedules for companies entering or changing their relationship to the Nasdaq governance regime. The listed categories include initial public offerings, companies emerging from bankruptcy, transfers from other markets, companies ceasing to be smaller reporting companies or controlled companies, former Section 12(g)-registered companies that list on Nasdaq, carve-outs or spin-offs, and companies ceasing to qualify as Foreign Private Issuers.[30]

For an IPO company, Nasdaq Rule 5615(b)(1)(A) permits the majority-independent-board requirement to be phased in over 12 months from the date its securities first trade on Nasdaq. The phase-in schedule requires one independent director on each committee upon initial listing, a majority of independent directors on each committee 90 days after initial listing, and full committee-independence compliance and a majority-independent full board one year after initial listing. Nasdaq also has cure periods for a failure to maintain a majority-independent board, audit-committee composition, or compensation-committee composition where the problem arises from one vacancy or one member ceasing to be independent because of circumstances beyond that member’s reasonable control.[30][27]

These transition rules do not erase the underlying governance obligation. Nasdaq’s 2024 amendments provide that a company is ineligible for the applicable cure period immediately after a Rule 5615(b) phase-in expires unless it complied during the phase-in and then fell out of compliance before the phase-in ended. If the company relied on the phase-in but allowed it to expire without demonstrating compliance, Rule 5810(c)(E) requires Nasdaq’s Listing Qualifications Department to issue a Staff Delisting Determination letter to delist the Company’s securities. This prevents a phase-in from becoming an open-ended extension of noncompliance.[30][34]

What follows is that Rule 5605 supplies the substantive architecture—who is independent, how many independent directors are needed, and which functions they must perform—while Rule 5615 identifies when Nasdaq adjusts the timing or applicability of those duties for particular issuer categories.[1][3][6]

Nasdaq Rule 5101 supplies a broader enforcement backdrop. In addition to applying the enumerated Rule 5000 Series listing criteria, Nasdaq has discretion over initial and continued listing to maintain market quality and public confidence, prevent fraudulent and manipulative conduct, promote just and equitable trading principles, and protect investors and the public interest. Nasdaq may review a Company’s past corporate-governance activities and, case by case, impose restrictions or additional listing requirements, or deny listing of a security, when corporate-governance standards have been violated or evaded, as necessary to protect investors and the public interest.[36]

Rule 5615 also differentiates among investment-company issuers. **Management investment companies** receive exemptions from Rule 5605(b), the independent-director board requirements, and from Rules 5605(d) and (e), which address compensation committees and independent-director oversight of nominations. By contrast, **business development companies**, as defined in IM-5615-4, remain subject to all Rule 5600 Series requirements. For a business development company, a director is independent if the director is not an **interested person** of the Company under Section 2(a)(19) of the Investment Company Act.[37][38][39]

The audit-committee requirements include a qualification beyond director independence: every listed Company’s audit committee must have at least one member with past accounting or finance experience, or comparable experience or background producing financial sophistication. Nasdaq provides no exemption or phase-in period for that requirement for a newly listed company.[40]

A company that ceases to be a Controlled Company may phase in compliance with the nominating- and compensation-committee independence requirements: it must have one independent member when it ceases to be controlled, a majority of independent members within 90 days, and all independent members within one year. It has 12 months to meet the majority-independent-board requirement.[4][27]

Rule 5615 also contains corporate-governance exemptions for issuers of only non-voting preferred securities and for issuers of only debt securities.[41]

A Foreign Private Issuer that ceases to qualify for that status has six months from the date it is determined no longer to qualify to comply with the majority-independent-board requirement.[29]

The audit-committee exceptional-and-limited-circumstances provision permits a non-independent director to serve without Nasdaq’s prior approval, but that director may not serve for more than two years and may not chair the committee.[4]

To rely on the Foreign Private Issuer home-country-practice alternative, the issuer must disclose significant differences between its home-country corporate-governance practices and the Nasdaq requirements applicable to U.S. domestic companies.[42]

### Why do Nasdaq’s corporate-governance listing standards distinguish independent board oversight

Nasdaq’s distinction between independent directors and management reflects a basic corporate-governance problem: directors oversee management, but management may influence directors through employment, compensation, family connections, commercial dealings, affiliations, or other relationships. Nasdaq’s rule asks whether those connections could interfere with a director’s ability to exercise independent judgment.[1][13][7]

The SEC has described exchange corporate-governance standards as supporting good governance, including fair and impartial boards and key committees. In approving Nasdaq’s 2024 governance-rule changes, the SEC stated that the standards promote transparency, accountability, and objectivity in the oversight of listed companies.[29][43]

Nasdaq has given a more specific investor-confidence rationale for the objective parts of its standard. It has said that the bright-line relationship measures provide transparency to investors and companies, facilitate uniform application of the rules, and ease administration. Nasdaq also stated that investor confidence depends on independent directors not having a relationship with the listed company that impairs their independence.[24][22]

The bright-line tests therefore serve a practical legal purpose: they give companies, investors, and Nasdaq clear boundaries. Nasdaq describes them as objective measures that provide transparency, facilitate uniform application, ease administration, and identify relationships likely to interfere with independent judgment. A compensation threshold, a prior-employment period, or a defined Family Member relationship can be identified and applied more consistently than an entirely open-ended inquiry. But the board-determination requirement deals with the other side of the problem: not every potentially compromising relationship can be reduced to a single numerical threshold or category.[18][2]

**The rule’s design is deliberately cumulative: bright-line disqualifications create non-negotiable minimum protections, while the board’s affirmative determination catches other relationships that could impair judgment in the particular facts and circumstances.**[13][2]

That structure explains why a person who passes the objective tests is not automatically independent. Nasdaq has expressly recognized that even when an individual passes a bright-line compensation test, the board could still conclude that the director should not be considered independent in light of the amount of compensation and the surrounding circumstances.[22]

It also explains why stock ownership alone is not automatically disqualifying. Nasdaq does not assume that every shareholder-director lacks independence. Instead, it reserves the board’s ability to determine that a particular ownership position, combined with other relationships or influence, would interfere with independent judgment.[15][16][1]

The board-level independence rule has operational consequences. The majority-independent-board requirement gives independent directors a governing presence on the full board. Independent-only executive sessions provide a recurring setting in which those directors can confer without management or non-independent directors present.[21][4][20]

The SEC has concluded that requiring a majority of the board to be independent should increase the likelihood that boards will make decisions in shareholders’ best interests. It also said that the requirement helps assure that key decisions are considered by directors without relationships to the issuer that could impair their judgment.[13]

Nasdaq directs independent directors to functions where management conflicts are particularly obvious. Executive-officer compensation is one: the people whose pay is being decided should not control the decision-making body. Nasdaq’s interpretive material states that independent-director oversight of executive compensation helps assure appropriate incentives consistent with the board’s responsibility to maximize shareholder value, while preserving flexibility in board structure.[1]

Director nominations are another. Rule 5605(e) requires that director nominees be selected or recommended by independent directors voting alone or by a committee composed solely of independent directors. In ordinary terms, Nasdaq separates board succession from management’s unilateral control and from a process in which non-independent directors can determine the candidates without independent-director participation.[4][5]

Related-party transactions show the same governance logic. Rule 5630 requires ongoing review and oversight of transactions that may involve a conflict of interest by the audit committee or another independent body of the board. Nasdaq regards a committee composed solely of independent directors as an independent board body for that purpose.[3][16]

The controlled-company exemption shows that the general rule is not absolute. A Controlled Company may be exempt from the majority-independent-board requirement in Listing Rule 5605(b) and from the independent-director compensation and nomination requirements in Listing Rules 5605(d) and 5605(e). Yet Nasdaq retains the Listing Rule 5605(b)(2) executive-session requirement: even a Controlled Company’s independent directors must have regularly scheduled meetings without other directors present.[4][5][21]

This retained requirement is significant. The Controlled Company exemption recognizes that majority shareholders, including parent companies, have ownership-based rights to select directors and control certain key decisions, such as executive-officer compensation, while preserving independent directors’ right to meet in executive session.[44][45]

The same distinction explains why committee standards can be more demanding than the general board standard. An audit committee supervises financial reporting and the external auditor, so its members must satisfy both Nasdaq’s general independence definition and SEC Rule 10A-3’s additional restrictions, including limits on compensatory fees and affiliation. A compensation committee likewise requires a focused assessment of ties that could make a director dependent on management when deciding executive pay.[6][35][20]

Judgment — In plain financial terms, independent-director rules seek to reduce the risk that management, a controller, or a financially important relationship can shape the people charged with monitoring the company. The rules do not guarantee better decisions, but they require a board structure intended to make independent challenge and objective oversight more likely.

What follows is that Nasdaq’s independence standard is not a ceremonial label. It is a governance mechanism that separates oversight from management influence where the board reviews financial reporting, executive compensation, related-party conflicts, and its own membership.[29][1][4][3]

The independence rules also operate within the Exchange Act’s requirements for national securities exchanges. The SEC stated in its February 2020 approval order that exchange rules must be designed to prevent fraudulent and manipulative acts and practices, promote just and equitable principles of trade, support a free and open market and national market system, and protect investors and the public interest.[8]

The SEC has also characterized the independent-board and independent-committee requirements as important to adequate oversight. It approved Nasdaq’s limits on additional cure time after an unsuccessful phase-in period because those limits restrict the time a company can remain listed without satisfying the independent-board or independent-committee requirements.[45][40]

In December 2013, Nasdaq removed the bright-line prohibition on compensatory fees for compensation-committee members and instead required the board to consider those fees and other relevant factors in determining compensation-committee independence. Nasdaq made the change after companies and others reported that the prohibition burdened director recruitment because board, audit-committee, and compensation-committee composition requirements differed, and that the burden could affect a Company’s listing choice.[46]

### Synthesis

Nasdaq’s independent-director standard combines a legal status rule with a board judgment rule. A director must be outside management and employment, must not fall within Nasdaq’s categorical disqualifications, and must receive an affirmative board finding that no other relationship would interfere with independent judgment. Passing a bright-line test is therefore necessary but not sufficient, while stock ownership alone does not prevent an independence finding.[1][22][2][15]

These standards sit in the Rule 5600 Series corporate-governance framework. Rule 5605(a)(2) defines independence; Rule 5605(b) addresses board composition and independent-only executive sessions; Rules 5605(c)–(e) allocate specified oversight roles to independent directors; and Rule 5615 supplies applicable exemptions and transition schedules. The framework generally requires independent directors to have a governing role, while recognizing defined exceptions such as the Controlled Company exemption and Foreign Private Issuer home-country-practice alternative.[1][3][6][4]

## Who qualifies

An Independent Director is not simply a director who is outside management in everyday terms. Under Nasdaq Listing Rule 5605(a)(2) and IM-5605, a person must clear the specified categorical restrictions, and the board must make an affirmative determination—an express board finding—that no relationship would interfere with the person’s independent judgment as a director.[24][7][2][14] This matters because a director can pass the numerical and status tests yet still fail the board’s broader relationship assessment.[24][7][2]

### List the eligibility criteria (who qualifies).

**A director qualifies under the general Nasdaq standard only when all three conditions are met: the director is not an Executive Officer or employee of the Company; no categorical disqualification in Rule 5605(a)(2) applies; and the board affirmatively determines that the director has no relationship that would interfere with independent judgment.** Rule 5605(a)(2) defines an Independent Director as a person other than an Executive Officer or employee of the Company, or another individual whose relationship, in the board’s opinion, would interfere with independent judgment in carrying out director responsibilities.[1][24][6]

The first condition is status-based. An **Executive Officer** is an officer covered by Exchange Act Rule 16a-1(f) under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, a federal securities-law definition Nasdaq applies even when the entity at issue is not public. A non-executive employee is an employee who is not a Section 16 officer.[4][5] In ordinary terms, a current management officer or employee is not eligible to be independent, and recent employment can trigger one of Nasdaq’s categorical restrictions addressed in the next section.[4][5]

The second condition is compliance with Nasdaq’s categorical or **bright-line** tests. These are objective relationship categories that the board cannot override: Nasdaq staff states that, if an enumerated circumstance is present, the director cannot be deemed independent. The detailed disqualifications, their thresholds, exceptions, and look-back periods appear in **Who is disqualified**. A candidate therefore qualifies only if none of those applicable prohibitions applies.[14]

The third condition is the **affirmative independence determination**. Passing the bright-line tests is necessary but not sufficient. Rule 5605(a)(2) and IM-5605 require the board, on an ongoing basis, to assess the director’s actual relationships and affirmatively determine that none would interfere with the exercise of independent judgment.[14] The board’s analysis can reach relationships not expressly listed in the categorical tests, including a relationship that is material because of its practical economic or personal effect.[14]

This is a relationship-based test, not a self-certification by the nominee. The board must form the opinion; a director cannot become independent merely by representing that he or she has no conflict. Under Nasdaq’s Rule 4200 framework, the company identifies in its annual proxy statement—or, if it does not file a proxy, in Form 10-K or Form 20-F—the directors the board has determined to be independent.[13][9][47]

#### The Company scope

For this purpose, **Company** is broader than the Nasdaq-listed legal entity standing alone. It includes the listed issuer and relevant consolidated Parent and Subsidiary relationships: IM-5605 treats references to a Parent or Subsidiary as covering entities controlled and consolidated in the Company’s financial statements filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).[5][15] It does not include an entity that the company reflects solely as an investment in those financial statements.

Thus, a candidate’s relevant employment, compensation, or other relationship may be with a consolidated parent or subsidiary, not only with the listed issuer itself.[4][5][15][21]

That distinction prevents a board from treating a relationship with an operating subsidiary or controlling parent as irrelevant simply because the listed company is a different corporation. In contrast, an investment that is not consolidated does not fall within this defined Parent-or-Subsidiary scope.[5][15]

#### Family Member relationships

Several categorical tests extend to a director’s **Family Member**, rather than stopping with the director’s own relationships. Under Nasdaq Rule 5605(a)(2), Family Member means the person’s spouse, parents, children, siblings, mothers- and fathers-in-law, sons- and daughters-in-law, brothers- and sisters-in-law, and anyone other than a domestic employee who shares the person’s home. For the rule’s three-year look-back provisions, a person who ceased to be a Family Member because of legal separation, divorce, death, or incapacitation need not be considered.[19][15][4][5]

Later secondary summaries describe a broader definition that adds mothers- and fathers-in-law, sons- and daughters-in-law, brothers- and sisters-in-law, and excludes domestic employees from persons sharing the director’s home.[16][15] Accordingly, a Family Member connection can make a director ineligible even where the director personally has no disqualifying employment, payment, auditor, or business relationship.[16][17][6] The precise Family Member definition should be applied from the current Rule 5605(a)(2) and IM-5605 text in effect for the determination.[5][16][15]

#### Ownership is not automatically disqualifying

Owning Company stock, even a substantial investment, does not by itself prevent a director from qualifying as independent under Nasdaq’s general board-independence definition. Nasdaq’s interpretive approach treats stock ownership alone as insufficient to bar an independence finding.[48][25][17][9]

That does not make ownership irrelevant. The board must still evaluate whether ownership, combined with another relationship, could interfere with the director’s independent judgment. For example, a shareholding may coexist with a commercial, employment, family, or management relationship that warrants a negative board determination even if the stockholding alone would not.[48][7][6]

#### Limited pathways for former insiders

Nasdaq’s rules do not permanently disqualify every person with a historical connection to an acquired business or an interim executive role. A former officer of an entity acquired by a Nasdaq-listed company may qualify as independent for the combined company if the person was not employed by the listed company after completion of the acquisition.[4][3][21]

Similarly, under IM-5605 and Listing Rule 5605(a)(2)(A), a director who served as an interim Executive Officer may be considered independent after that interim employment if it lasted no longer than one year. Nasdaq has applied that principle to interim Executive Chairman service.[2] The exception removes the automatic employment bar; it does not produce independence automatically, because the board must still make the affirmative relationship-based determination.[2]

These pathways recognize that a short, transitional service period or a pre-acquisition role need not necessarily compromise future independent judgment. They do not permit a board to disregard continuing employment, financial, family, or other material connections.[4][2]

#### Investment companies use a different eligibility screen

For an **investment company**—such as a registered fund—Rule 5605(a)(2)(G) substitutes a different independence measurement for the ordinary Rule 5605(a)(2)(A)–(F) categorical screen. Instead of applying those ordinary paragraphs, the director must not be an **interested person** of the Company under Section 2(a)(19) of the Investment Company Act of 1940, except solely through serving as a member of the board or a board committee.[1]

In ordinary terms, Nasdaq harmonizes its independence test for investment companies with the federal investment-company statute rather than layering the ordinary corporate issuer tests on top of that statutory framework. This is a substitution, not an additional optional route for an ordinary operating company.[1]

#### Board independence versus committee independence

Qualifying as an Independent Director under Rule 5605(a)(2) is the general board-level determination. The **Audit Committee** and **Compensation Committee** have more restrictive independence requirements than the general board definition.[16] An audit committee member must satisfy Rule 5605(a)(2) and the separate SEC Rule 10A-3 independence criteria, among other audit-committee qualifications.

A Compensation Committee member must qualify under Listing Rule 5605(a)(2), satisfy Exchange Act Rule 10C-1, and undergo the board’s additional assessment of relationships material to that person’s ability to be independent from management in compensation-committee work, including the source of compensation and affiliations with the Company or related entities.[20][40][6]

Those additional committee standards should not be mistaken for extra qualifications for every independent director serving on the full board. They determine eligibility for particular oversight roles once the general board-independence analysis has been applied.[16][20]

The organizational-payments test illustrates why eligibility cannot be reduced to a simple three-year checklist. Under Nasdaq Listing Rule 5605(a)(2)(D), a director may be ineligible when the director or a Family Member is a partner, controlling shareholder, or Executive Officer of an organization making or receiving qualifying payments with the Company in the current or any of the past three fiscal years.[5][21] Nasdaq’s FAQ says that if the director currently ceases the relevant association with that organization, this particular paragraph’s three-year look-back no longer precludes independence.[4][5][21]

A secondary comparison guide instead states broadly that every listed independence criterion has a three-year look-back and that a precluded relationship during that period prevents an independence finding even after it ends.[16] Read together, the more specific Nasdaq FAQ controls the narrow point: the organizational-payment rule has a stated cessation-of-association treatment, while other categorical tests must be read according to their own text and timing rules.[5][16][21]

The eligibility analysis therefore has a clear sequence. First, determine whether the candidate is outside management and outside every applicable categorical prohibition. Second, apply the defined Company and Family Member scope to all relevant relationships. Third, have the board evaluate the remaining facts and affirmatively find that none interferes with independent judgment. That sequence makes it possible to distinguish a director who is legally eligible to be found independent from one whom the board should, in the particular circumstances, decline to classify as independent.[24][5][7][2][6]

1. **Map the candidate’s relationships:** Include relationships with the listed issuer, its consolidated parents and subsidiaries, and relevant Family Members. [5][15][21]
2. **Apply the categorical tests:** Check the employment, compensation, family-officer, business-payment, compensation-interlock, and auditor restrictions described in **Who is disqualified**. [7][6]
3. **Obtain the board’s affirmative determination:** Document why no remaining relationship would interfere with the candidate’s independent judgment, rather than treating passage of the bright-line tests as conclusive. [7][2][6]

Application. A share-owning candidate who is not an employee, clears the categorical tests, and has no other disqualifying relationship is not independent merely because of those facts. The board must still affirmatively determine that no relationship, including the ownership when considered with all other facts, would interfere with the candidate’s independent judgment. If the board makes that finding, the candidate qualifies as an Independent Director.[48][7][2]

Under Listing Rule 5605(c)(2)(A)(iii), a director who used the interim-Executive-Officer pathway may nevertheless be barred from the Audit Committee for three years if the director participated in preparing the Company’s financial statements while serving in that interim role.[14] This is an audit-committee restriction, not a further condition of general board independence.[14]

Practical applications of the three conditions. A candidate who has never been employed by the Company may qualify if no categorical disqualification applies and the board affirmatively determines that no relationship would interfere with independent judgment. A former employee may be considered once the three-year employment bar has expired, subject to that same affirmative board determination.[16][7][2]

A Family Member’s employment as a non-Executive Officer does not itself trigger the separate bar for a director who is a Family Member of a current or former Company Executive Officer. Further, compensation for board or committee service; compensation to a Family Member who is a non-Executive Officer employee; benefits under a tax-qualified retirement plan; and non-discretionary compensation are excluded only from the $120,000 compensation test. Each example still requires the board’s affirmative independence determination.[16][7][2]

For a company listing securities previously registered under Exchange Act Section 12(g), only directors who are independent under Listing Rule 5605(a)(2) and meet the Exchange Act Rule 10A-3(b)(1) independence criteria may serve on the Audit Committee during the transition period, unless a Rule 10A-3 exemption applies.[40] Exchange Act Rule 10A-3 also requires at least one Audit Committee member to be independent on the listing date, even where the company may phase in the independence of its other Audit Committee members.[40]

Nasdaq permits a limited Audit Committee exception for a non-independent member without Nasdaq approval. A member appointed under it may serve for no longer than two years and may not chair the Audit Committee. A company other than a Foreign Private Issuer relying on the exception must meet Item 407(d)(2) of Regulation S-K disclosure requirements; a Foreign Private Issuer must disclose in its next annual report the relationship making the member non-independent and the board’s reasons.[28] The individual must meet the statutory audit-committee service criteria, must not be a current Executive Officer or employee or a Family Member of an Executive Officer, and the board must determine that the appointment is required by the best interests of the company and its shareholders.[28]

Nasdaq does not impose a separate affirmative credential for general director independence: no particular profession, public-company experience, ownership level, or lack of prior management experience by itself establishes independence. The operative inquiry remains the board’s affirmative judgment and the absence of an applicable categorical disqualification.

Nasdaq interprets children in the Family Member definition to exclude stepchildren.[8]

## Who is disqualified

### List the disqualification criteria (who cannot serve).

Judgment — These are disqualifications from being *classified as an Independent Director* under Nasdaq Listing Rule 5605(a)(2), not usually bans on holding a board seat at all. Rule 5605(a)(2) defines an Independent Director as a person other than an Executive Officer or employee of the company and other than a person with a relationship that, in the board’s opinion, would interfere with independent judgment.[49] A company may have non-independent directors; the consequence is that they cannot fill board-majority or committee positions that Nasdaq reserves for independent directors.

The rules work as a set of bright-line tests—specific relationships that automatically prevent an independence finding—plus the board’s broader duty to decide whether any other relationship compromises independent judgment.

Nasdaq’s bright-line disqualifications cover six principal categories for ordinary operating companies: recent employment, significant compensation, family ties to company executive officers, material business payments, compensation-committee interlocks, and outside-auditor relationships. They reach both the director’s own relationships and defined *Family Member* relationships, so independence is not assessed solely by asking whether the director personally works for or is paid by the listed company.[7][6]

For these rules, a *Family Member* is a relationship that Nasdaq treats as close enough to affect the independence assessment. The current formulation described in the 2020 SEC approval materials includes a person’s spouse, parents, children, siblings, mothers- and fathers-in-law, sons- and daughters-in-law, brothers- and sisters-in-law, and anyone, other than a domestic employee, who shares the person’s home. Nasdaq’s approved interpretation also treats children as excluding stepchildren for these bright-line tests.[17][6]

That definition matters because several bars arise from a Family Member’s executive job, compensation, business role, or audit-firm connection rather than from the director’s own conduct. For the three-year look-back provisions, Nasdaq says a person need not be treated as a Family Member after legal separation, divorce, death, or incapacitation.[21]

An *Executive Officer* is not simply any manager. Nasdaq incorporates the officers covered by Rule 16a-1(f) under the Securities and Exchange Act of 1934, and applies that meaning even when the relevant entity is not itself publicly traded.[3] A non-executive employee, in contrast, is an employee who is not a Section 16 officer.[3]

Under IM-5605, a *Parent or Subsidiary* for these purposes includes an entity that the company controls and consolidates into its financial statements filed with the SEC, but not an entity shown solely as an investment.[3] Thus, the employment and compensation screens can cover a consolidated corporate group, rather than only the legal entity whose securities trade on Nasdaq.[3]

The principal disqualifications are as follows:

- **Recent company employment — Rule 5605(a)(2)(A):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director is currently employed by the company, or was employed by the company, a Parent, or a Subsidiary at any time during the preceding three years. This is a categorical bar: former employment remains disqualifying throughout the applicable period rather than becoming irrelevant merely because it ended. [16][17][6]

- **Significant company compensation — Rule 5605(a)(2)(B):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director, or a Family Member, accepted compensation from the company, a Parent, or a Subsidiary exceeding **$120,000** in any period of **12 consecutive months** during the **three years preceding the independence determination**. [17][9][50] The rule is concerned with compensation that directly benefits the director or Family Member, such as consulting or personal-service arrangements; Nasdaq’s earlier interpretive material also identifies political contributions to a director’s or Family Member’s campaign as within this type of direct-benefit payment. **Exceptions:** compensation for board or board-committee service; compensation to a Family Member who is a company employee other than an Executive Officer; benefits under a tax-qualified retirement plan; non-discretionary compensation; and compensation for former interim Executive Officer service that lasted no longer than one year and has ended do not count under this test. [16][15][6][13]

- **Family tie to a company Executive Officer — Rule 5605(a)(2)(C):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director is a Family Member of an individual who is currently, or was at any time during the preceding three years, employed by the company, a Parent, or a Subsidiary as an Executive Officer. This is narrower than a bar based on every Family Member employee: the Family Member must be a covered Executive Officer. [16][17][6]

- **Material business-payment relationship — Rule 5605(a)(2)(D):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director, or a Family Member, is a partner, other than a limited partner, controlling shareholder, or Executive Officer of an organization that made payments to, or received payments from, the company for property or services in the current or any of the preceding three fiscal years. The specified threshold is payments exceeding the greater of **5% of the payment recipient’s consolidated gross revenues for that year** or **$200,000**. **Exceptions:** payments arising solely from investments in the company’s securities and payments under non-discretionary charitable-contribution matching programs do not count. Nasdaq also provides that, if the director ceases the specified association with the payment-counterparty organization, this paragraph’s three-year look-back no longer precludes independence. [16][17][13][6][9][5] [21]

- **Compensation-committee interlock — Rule 5605(a)(2)(E):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director, or a Family Member, is employed as an Executive Officer of another entity and, at any time in the preceding three years, an Executive Officer of the Nasdaq-listed company served on that other entity’s compensation committee. The rule addresses reciprocal compensation-governance influence: one company’s executive should not sit on the compensation committee overseeing an executive from the other company while that relationship exists within the look-back period. [16][17][6]

- **Outside-auditor relationship — Rule 5605(a)(2)(F):** A director cannot be considered independent if the director, or a Family Member, is a current partner of the company’s outside auditor. The bar also applies if the director or Family Member was a partner or employee of the outside auditor and worked on the company’s audit at any time during the preceding three years. [16][15][6]

**The key result is that a person who falls within any of these bright-line categories may remain a director, but cannot be counted as an independent director for Rule 5605(a)(2) purposes.** The board has no discretion to override a listed categorical bar by concluding that the individual happens to exercise sound judgment in practice.[7][6]

#### 1. Recent employment and the limited interim-officer exception

For Rule 5605(a)(2)(A), the employment screen covers any director employment, not only service as chief executive officer or another Executive Officer. The three-year period begins when the employment relationship ends.[16]

There is a limited treatment for a director who served as an *interim Executive Officer*—a temporary executive appointment. Interim service that did not last longer than one year and that has ended does not automatically bar an independence finding merely because it constituted employment or involved compensation above the ordinary compensation threshold.[16][9] But the director is not independent while actually serving as interim officer, and, after service ends, the board must affirmatively decide that both the service and its compensation do not interfere with independent judgment.[15][9]

This exception is narrow. It does not mean every short executive engagement is harmless, and it does not eliminate other restrictions. Separately, every audit-committee member must not have participated in preparing the company’s financial statements, or those of a current Subsidiary, at any time during the preceding three years. This audit-committee restriction applies to every member, including a former interim Executive Officer who participated in that preparation, even if the board may otherwise consider the person independent for general-board purposes.[51][4][15] That is an audit-committee restriction, not a general prohibition on board service.[4][15]

The employment bar therefore prevents a company from treating management, recently departed employees, or recently ended interim executives as independent without both satisfying the narrow interim exception and making the required board judgment.[16][15]

#### 2. The $120,000 compensation test

Rule 5605(a)(2)(B) is a direct-compensation test, distinct from the organizational-payment test in Rule 5605(a)(2)(D): it measures compensation against the $120,000 threshold rather than against the recipient organization’s revenue.[16][21]

Several payment categories are excluded from the $120,000 screen:

- **Board and board-committee compensation:** Fees for serving as a director or committee member do not count toward the threshold. [16]

- **Compensation to a non-executive Family Member employee:** Pay to a Family Member who works for the company but is not an Executive Officer does not count toward the threshold. This exception does not protect compensation paid to a Family Member who is a company Executive Officer. [16]

- **Tax-qualified retirement-plan benefits and non-discretionary compensation:** Benefits under a tax-qualified retirement plan, and compensation that is non-discretionary, are excluded. [16]

- **Qualifying former interim-officer compensation:** Compensation for a director’s interim Executive Officer service may be excluded where the interim employment lasted less than one year and has ended, subject to the board’s independent-judgment determination. [16][15]

Nasdaq treats compensation in substance, not just cash payroll. For the **$120,000** limit in Listing Rule 5605(a)(2), an option awarded for consulting services counts toward the limit at its grant-date value, calculated using a commonly accepted option-pricing method such as Black-Scholes or a binomial model.[3][28] It is treated as a payment when granted even if it is unvested or subject to vesting or exercise conditions; subsequent cancellation or forfeiture of an unvested portion reduces the valuation proportionately.[3]

The rule’s application to professional-service firms illustrates why the two payment tests must remain distinct. A payment to a law firm generally falls under Listing Rule 5605(a)(2)(D), which asks whether payments exceed the greater of **5% of the recipient’s gross revenues** or **$200,000**. If the firm is a sole proprietorship, Listing Rule 5605(a)(2)(B), which asks whether compensation exceeds **$120,000**, applies instead.[21]

Accordingly, the compensation rule is not a general ban on every payment between a company and a director or relative; it is a direct-compensation screen with a stated monetary threshold, defined period, and specific exclusions.[16][21]

#### 3. Family ties to company executives

The Family Member executive-officer test applies across the covered consolidated Parent and Subsidiary group.[3][17]

This test reflects the concern that a director’s judgment could be affected by a close relative’s senior management position even if the director has no personal employment contract, consulting agreement, or commercial relationship with the company. It also demonstrates the difference between the employment bar and the family-executive bar: the director’s own recent employment of any kind is disqualifying, whereas a Family Member’s employment is disqualifying under this test when it is Executive Officer employment.[16]

The scope of Family Member should be applied with care. The definition described as current in the SEC’s September 2019 order included a spouse, parents, children, and siblings, whether by blood, marriage, or adoption, and anyone residing in the person’s home; it therefore included stepchildren as children by marriage.[49] The 2019 SEC notice stated that Nasdaq proposed a revised formulation including specified in-laws, excluding domestic employees from household residents, and excluding stepchildren; the proposal would make Nasdaq’s definition identical to the NYSE definition of an “immediate family member.” Nasdaq’s rule-filings index lists that proposal, SR-NASDAQ-2019-049, as approved.[24][52][53] The SEC’s February 2020 approval materials describe the revised definition and Nasdaq’s interpretation that children excludes stepchildren.[17][6][3][24]

The practical consequence is that companies must assess the particular family relationship under Nasdaq’s operative definition when performing the independence determination, rather than treating all relatives or all household connections alike.[17][6]

#### 4. Material business relationships and organizational payments

Rule 5605(a)(2)(D) is aimed at commercial or service-provider relationships that are large enough to raise independence concerns. It applies where the director or Family Member is a partner, controlling shareholder, or Executive Officer of an organization involved in qualifying payments with the company. The payments must be for property or services and must exceed the greater of 5% of the recipient’s consolidated gross revenues for the relevant fiscal year or $200,000; charitable contributions are payments for this test.[16][17][15]

The calculation is based on the *recipient’s* consolidated gross revenues, not the Nasdaq-listed company’s revenue. For example, a $300,000 payment to a vendor with $4 million of consolidated gross revenue exceeds both $200,000 and 5% of the vendor’s revenue; the relevant threshold is the greater amount, $200,000, so the payment exceeds the rule’s threshold.Derived — This example applies the Rule 5605(a)(2)(D) formula stated in: 5% multiplied by $4,000,000 equals $200,000; $300,000 is greater than $200,000.[16]

Charitable contributions are payments for purposes of the organizational-payment test. Two categories are excluded: payments arising solely from investments in the company’s securities, and payments made under non-discretionary charitable-contribution matching programs. Those exclusions do not convert another commercial payment into a permitted one; they identify transaction types that the rule does not treat as disqualifying payments under this paragraph.[15]

A partner for this purpose ordinarily means a partner other than a limited partner. The rule focuses on affiliations likely to involve control, management authority, or a meaningful economic connection to the payment recipient or payer.[13][9]

Nasdaq’s specific cessation treatment: a director who currently holds a specified role at an organization that made or received qualifying payments is ineligible under Rule 5605(a)(2)(D).[3]

The business-payment rule therefore bars directors with a current, specified senior or controlling affiliation to an organization in a sufficiently material property-or-services relationship with the company, while preserving stated exceptions for investment transactions and non-discretionary charitable matching.[16][5]

#### 5. Compensation-committee interlocks

A *compensation-committee interlock* is a reciprocal governance relationship involving executive pay oversight. The bright-line bar applies if the director or Family Member is an Executive Officer of another entity where one of the Nasdaq-listed company’s Executive Officers served on that entity’s compensation committee at any time during the preceding three years.[16][17]

The rule does not require proof that the company executive influenced the director’s or Family Member’s compensation. The specified cross-board relationship itself is the bright-line condition. It is designed to prevent an executive-pay oversight arrangement from becoming reciprocal or creating divided loyalties among those responsible for corporate governance.[16][6]

This test means that companies must investigate external executive roles held by both the director and covered Family Members, not merely their jobs within the listed company’s group.[16][17]

#### 6. Outside-auditor relationships

The outside-auditor bar separates board oversight from the company’s independent audit firm.[16][6]

The rule is not framed as a permanent prohibition on all former audit-firm employees. The look-back condition for former personnel requires that the person worked on the company’s audit, while current partnership in the company’s outside auditor is separately disqualifying.[16][15]

A further rule applies when considering service on the audit committee. SEC Rule 10A-3 generally makes a partner in a law firm that receives payments from the issuer ineligible for that issuer’s audit committee. That is an audit-committee-specific restriction and should not be confused with the general Nasdaq board-independence test.[21]

The auditor rule thus blocks independence where an ongoing or recent audit-engagement connection could compromise the board’s ability to oversee financial reporting and the outside auditor objectively.[16][6]

#### 7. Investment companies: a different statutory test

For an *investment company*, such as a registered fund, Rule 5605(a)(2)(G) substitutes a different test for the ordinary paragraphs (A) through (F). A director is not independent if the director is an *interested person* of the company within the meaning of Section 2(a)(19) of the Investment Company Act of 1940, other than solely in the director’s capacity as a board or board-committee member.[16][17]

This is a replacement test, not an additional seventh bar layered on top of every ordinary-company bright-line test. Nasdaq uses the Investment Company Act concept to align its listing-standard assessment with the statutory governance framework that already governs investment companies.[1][16]

For an investment company, therefore, the initial question is whether the director is an interested person under the Investment Company Act definition, rather than whether each of Rules 5605(a)(2)(A) through (F) independently applies.[16][17]

Passing all of the bright-line screens does not itself establish independence. Nasdaq requires the board to make an affirmative determination that no other relationship exists that would interfere with the director’s exercise of independent judgment, including relationships that fall outside the categorical list.[6][48][17] Stock ownership alone does not disqualify a director under the general Nasdaq independence test.[9] The separate audit-committee affiliated-person test has a 10% safe harbor: a non-Executive Officer who beneficially owns, directly or indirectly, less than 10% of a class of voting equity securities is deemed not to control the issuer; ownership above 10% does not itself create a presumption of control.[13]

1. First, test the director and each relevant Family Member against the applicable Rule 5605(a)(2) bright-line category, using the correct three-year, 12-month, fiscal-year, percentage, or dollar measure. [6]

2. Second, identify whether a stated exclusion applies, particularly for board fees, non-executive Family Member employment compensation, retirement-plan benefits, non-discretionary compensation, securities investments, charitable matching, or qualifying interim-officer service. [16][15]

3. Third, for an investment company, apply the Investment Company Act interested-person standard in place of the ordinary paragraphs (A) through (F). [16]

4. Finally, make and document the board’s separate affirmative judgment about any other relationship that could interfere with independent judgment; clearing the bright-line rules is necessary, but it is not sufficient. [6][24]

For the **$120,000** compensation test, certain non-compensatory payments also do not preclude an independence finding: non-preferential payments made in the ordinary course of a financial institution’s business services, such as interest or loan proceeds, insurance-policy claim payments, payments arising solely from investments in company securities, and loans permitted under Sarbanes-Oxley.[50]

For example, Nasdaq staff concluded that merger consideration paid pro rata to all shareholders of an acquired target was acquisition consideration, not compensation under Rule 5605(a)(2)(B), and was not a payment for property or services under Rule 5605(a)(2)(D).[14]

Nasdaq interprets the company’s outside auditor consistently with the SEC concept of an accounting firm. The term can therefore include the audit firm’s departments, divisions, parents, subsidiaries, and associated entities, including associated entities outside the United States; an audit-firm connection through such a foreign affiliate can disqualify the director.[14]

There is a narrow audit-committee exception under which a company may appoint a non-independent director without Nasdaq approval, even when the committee already has three or more independent directors. The appointee must satisfy the Exchange Act Section 10A(m)(3) audit-committee criteria, must not be a current Executive Officer or employee or a Family Member of an Executive Officer, and must be determined by the board to be required by the best interests of the company and its shareholders. The appointee may serve for no more than two years, may not chair the audit committee, and the company must make the required disclosure.[28]

Interim Executive Officer service lasting more than one year invokes the ordinary employment rule and precludes an independence finding for three years.[23]

The general $120,000 screen does not settle compensation-committee eligibility. Nasdaq Rule 5605 contains additional independence criteria for audit-committee and compensation-committee members.[49] Nasdaq’s December 2013 notice stated that, although board and board-committee compensation is excluded from the general $120,000 cap, the board must consider that compensation when determining independence for compensation-committee service.[54] The notice proposed that the board assess the director’s sources of compensation, including consulting, advisory, and other compensatory fees, and affiliate relationships that could place the director under the company’s or senior management’s direct or indirect control or create a direct relationship with senior management.[54] Stock ownership alone, including a controlling interest, does not necessarily preclude compensation-committee service.[54]

Prior employment at an entity later acquired by the company does not disqualify a director if the former officer was not employed by the company after the acquisition.[9]

A stepchild who shares the director’s home remains a Family Member through the shared-home category, even though stepchildren are otherwise excluded from the definition of children. Nasdaq explained that a stepchild who was the director’s dependent or part of the director’s household since being a minor is likely to have a relationship similar to that of a biological child, whereas an adult stepchild whom the director never parented and who never shared the director’s household is likely to have an attenuated relationship.[19]

A domestic employee who shares the director’s home is excluded from Family Member, but the board must still scrutinize that relationship under the general requirement to determine that no relationship interferes with the director’s independent judgment.[19]

Under Exchange Act Section 10A(m) and SEC Rule 10A-3, an audit-committee member is not independent if the member is an affiliated person of the issuer or a Subsidiary, including a person who controls the issuer. Rule 10A-3(e)(1)(ii) provides a safe harbor: a person who is not an Executive Officer and does not beneficially own, directly or indirectly, 10% or more of any class of the issuer’s voting equity securities is deemed not to control the issuer for this purpose. Beneficial ownership of 10% or more does not itself create a presumption of control; independence may still be determined from the particular facts and circumstances.[13]

Although stepchildren are excluded from the defined category of children, a stepchild who shares the director’s home is a Family Member under the separate household-member part of the definition.[19]

Because Nasdaq applies its Executive Officer definition whether or not the relevant entity is public, this interlock test can apply when the director or Family Member is an Executive Officer of a private outside entity.[28][49]

The two limbs of the organizational-payment threshold meet at **$4.0 million** of recipient consolidated gross revenues: at that revenue level, 5% equals $200,000. Above $4.0 million, the 5%-of-revenue limb sets the higher threshold; at or below it, the $200,000 limb is the higher threshold.[16]

It separately applies where a Family Member is a current employee of the outside auditor and participates in the company’s audit.

## Which companies must have independent directors

### Which types of companies are required to have independent directors

Judgment — The practical question is not whether a Nasdaq-listed board must be entirely independent. It normally must have an independent *majority*—more independent directors than non-independent directors—and separate rules assign sensitive oversight functions, especially audit, executive pay, and director nominations, to independent directors. The exceptions are rule-specific: an entity may be excused from the board-majority rule yet still need independent directors for audit-committee or executive-session purposes.

Nasdaq places these requirements in its Rule 5600 Series, the corporate-governance portion of the listing standards. Rule 5605(b)(1) is the core board-composition provision; Rules 5605(c), (d), and (e) address the audit committee, compensation oversight, and director nominations respectively. Rule 5615 contains several issuer-type exemptions and phase-in arrangements, while Rule 5625 requires prompt notice to Nasdaq when an executive officer becomes aware of noncompliance with the Rule 5600 Series.[5][21]

**Most Nasdaq-listed companies must have a board made up of a majority of Independent Directors under Nasdaq Marketplace Rule 5605(b)(1).** This is a majority test, not a requirement that every director qualify as independent.[55][5][20][28] Thus, a standard operating company may have executive directors, a founder-director, a controlling shareholder’s representative, or other non-independent directors, provided independent directors still outnumber them and no specific exception applies.[5][20][21]

A five-director board needs at least three Independent Directors to meet the majority requirement; a six-director board needs at least four.[68][4]

The rule is an exchange listing condition, meaning it applies both when a company first lists and on an ongoing basis. Nasdaq’s corporate-governance certification form describes compliance with board composition, executive sessions, audit committees, director nominations, officer compensation, quorum, and a code of conduct as requirements that apply upon listing, subject to an available exemption.[20]

The majority-independent-board rule works alongside, rather than substitutes for, committee requirements. Rule 5605(c)(2) generally requires an audit committee of at least three members, each of whom must be an Independent Director under Rule 5605(a)(2), satisfy the separate SEC audit-committee independence standard in Exchange Act Rule 10A-3, subject to that rule’s exemptions, and be able to read and understand fundamental financial statements, including a balance sheet, income statement, and cash-flow statement.[1][56][8] Under NASDAQ Rule 5605(c)(2)(A)(iii), an audit-committee member also may not have participated in preparing the company’s or a current subsidiary’s financial statements at any time during the preceding three years.[50] Rule 5605(d)(2) requires a compensation committee of at least two members, each an Independent Director under Rule 5605(a)(2), subject to rule-specific exceptions.

A member may not accept, directly or indirectly, consulting, advisory, or other compensatory fees from the company or a subsidiary, except fees for service on the compensation committee, board, or another board committee and fixed retirement-plan compensation for prior service that is not contingent on continued service. The board must also consider whether a member’s affiliation with the company, a subsidiary, or a subsidiary’s affiliate would impair the member’s judgment on the compensation committee.[57][21][1] Rule 5605(e) requires director-nomination decisions to be made either by a nominations committee composed solely of independent directors or, if there is no nominations committee, by independent directors constituting a majority of the board’s independent directors in a vote in which only independent directors participate.[4][20][49]

This structure matters because a company can satisfy the board-majority rule while failing a committee rule, or fall within an exception to the board-majority rule while retaining an audit-committee obligation. For example, a majority-independent board does not by itself establish that the audit committee meets Rule 10A-3, and the presence of independent directors on an audit committee does not itself establish that the full board has an independent majority.[1][29][15]

Nasdaq also requires the independent directors to have regularly scheduled meetings attended only by independent directors, called **executive sessions**. Interpretive Material IM-5605-2 contemplates that these sessions will occur at least twice annually, and possibly more often in connection with regular board meetings. The requirement creates a forum in which directors without management relationships can deliberate without executive or other non-independent directors present.[4][5][20]

The SEC has explained the governance purpose of an independent-board majority in shareholder terms: it should increase the likelihood that boards make decisions in shareholders’ interests and ensure that key matters are considered by people whose relationships with the issuer do not impair judgment. That is the policy behind the Rule 5605 framework, although the operative compliance question remains whether the company satisfies the specific board and committee rules applicable to its issuer type.[13][43]

For this purpose, the majority is measured against the directors then serving on the board, not merely the directors attending a particular meeting. Emerging growth company status, by itself, does not excuse a company from Rule 5605(b)(1)’s majority-independent-board requirement.

### What are the relevant exceptions?

The exceptions fall into three different legal categories that should not be conflated. First, some issuer types are **exempt** from the majority-independent-board requirement. Second, a Foreign Private Issuer may choose an **alternative compliance route**—home-country practice—subject to conditions. Third, newly listed or status-changing companies may receive a **phase-in**, which postpones full compliance but does not remove the underlying obligation.[21][34][43]

#### Controlled Companies

A **Controlled Company** is a company in which more than 50% of the voting power for the election of directors is held by one individual, a group, or another company. For a group, the voting-power calculation can include shares subject to voting agreements about director elections, but Nasdaq says the shareholders must have filed notice that they are acting as a group, such as a Schedule 13D.[21]

A Controlled Company may rely on an exemption from the Listing Rule 5605(b) majority-independent-board requirement.[3] It is also exempt from the independent-director oversight requirements for executive-officer compensation and director nominations under Listing Rules 5605(d) and 5605(e).[3][43] This means a controlled issuer may have a board that does not contain a majority of Independent Directors and may avoid the usual independent compensation and nominations structures.

The exemption is optional. A company eligible for Controlled Company status may instead comply with the ordinary Rule 5605 standards, and Nasdaq says it has no special controlled-company disclosure obligation if it does not rely on the exemption.[4][21] Eligibility and reliance therefore are distinct: a concentrated ownership structure alone does not establish that the company is using the governance exemption.

A Controlled Company relying on the exemptions must make the specified disclosure. A Controlled Company that is not a Foreign Private Issuer must comply with Instruction 1 to Item 407(a) of Regulation S-K; a Foreign Private Issuer must disclose in its annual report on Form 20-F or Form 40-F that it is a Controlled Company and the basis for that determination.[21][4][3] In ordinary terms, investors must be told that the company’s controlling ownership permits it to operate with a governance structure that differs from Nasdaq’s standard independent-board model.

The exemption has important limits. Controlled Companies are not exempt from Rule 5605(b)(2), which requires executive sessions of independent directors. They are also not relieved of the applicable audit-committee requirements.[4][5][21] A Controlled Company can therefore be exempt from needing an independent *majority* but still need some independent directors to conduct executive sessions and, ordinarily, to satisfy audit-committee rules.

If a company ceases to be controlled, it must meet the majority-independent-board requirement within 12 months after that date. Its compensation and nominations committees may phase in independence on a separate schedule: one independent member immediately when controlled status ends, a majority within 90 days, and fully independent committees within one year. Audit-committee requirements apply as of the date controlled-company status ends, while the executive-session rule applied from the original listing date and continues afterward.[4][21]

The Controlled Company exception therefore shifts the governance balance toward the controller while preserving selected independent oversight protections; it does not authorize a complete departure from Nasdaq’s governance framework.[3][21]

#### Foreign Private Issuers

A **Foreign Private Issuer** is an issuer that qualifies as such under SEC Rule 3b-4. An FPI is generally within Nasdaq’s corporate-governance framework, but Rule 5615(a)(3) permits it to follow home-country governance practice instead of specified Nasdaq Rule 5600 Series requirements if it provides Nasdaq the required notice and disclosures.[3][43][34]

For the majority-independent-board requirement, this means an FPI may elect home-country practice in place of Rule 5605(b)(1). It is an election, not an automatic conclusion that every FPI is exempt. An FPI that chooses the alternative must publicly identify each Nasdaq requirement it does not follow and describe the home-country practice used instead.[4][35][17]

The FPI accommodation is broad but not unlimited. Under Rule 5615(a)(3)(A), an FPI using home-country practice instead of Rule 5600 Series requirements must still comply with Rule 5625’s Notification of Noncompliance requirement and Rule 5640’s Voting Rights requirement, have an audit committee satisfying Rule 5605(c)(3), and ensure that audit-committee members meet the Rule 10A-3-based independence condition in Rule 5605(c)(2)(A)(ii).[1][34]

On December 11, 2024, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit vacated the SEC order approving Nasdaq Rules 5605(f) and 5606, Nasdaq’s board-diversity rules. Those rules concerned diversity objectives and diversity disclosures, rather than independent-director requirements; after the vacatur, Nasdaq-listed companies were no longer required to comply with those diversity rules, although they could disclose diversity information voluntarily.[64][55]

The practical distinction is critical. An FPI may replace the ordinary Nasdaq board-majority, compensation, or nominations requirements with valid home-country practice, but it cannot use home-country practice to dispense with the specified federal-style audit-committee independence safeguards.[1][29][3] A qualifying FPI may also have a board of auditors, similar body, or statutory auditors that can satisfy all or part of the audit-committee regime if the applicable conditions are met.[35]

If an FPI later ceases to qualify for that status, it has six months from its Foreign Private Issuer Determination Date to comply with the Rule 5605(b) majority-independent-board and executive-session requirements, along with specified compensation, nominations, and audit-committee requirements. During that transition, it must continue to maintain the Rule 5605(c)(3) audit committee and the Rule 10A-3-based audit-member independence condition.[29][43]

The FPI exception is consequently an alternative governance route, not a general release from independent-director obligations: it can replace the domestic board-majority model, but it cannot eliminate the stated audit-committee protections.[43][34]

#### Entities excluded from the majority-independent-board rule

Nasdaq identifies several entity categories as not subject to Rule 5605(b)’s majority-independent-board requirement: management investment companies registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940, asset-backed issuers, unit investment trusts, and cooperative entities such as agricultural cooperatives.[21] The Rule 5605(b) exclusion is categorical for those listed forms, but it should always be read alongside the separate rule governing the particular function at issue.[21][26]

A registered management investment company is also exempt from Rule 5605(d)’s compensation-committee requirement, Rule 5605(e)’s independent-director oversight of director nominations, and Rule 5610’s code-of-conduct requirement. Nasdaq’s rule text distinguishes these registered management investment companies from business development companies, which are subject to all Rule 5600 Series requirements.[1][21] The difference reflects the fact that the Investment Company Act already imposes a specialized governance framework on registered funds.[13]

Asset-backed issuers and qualifying other passive issuers, including unit investment trusts, receive special treatment because their activities may be limited to holding or administering designated assets rather than operating a conventional business through a board and management structure. Nasdaq’s earlier SEC filing proposed exempting asset-backed and other passive issuers, such as unit investment trusts, from NASD Rule 4350(c) and (n), concerning board and key-committee independence and codes of conduct, and from the Rule 4350(d) audit-committee requirements.[13] That filing described passive issuers as trusts or similar unincorporated associations without a board or equivalent governing persons whose activities are limited to passive ownership, holding, administration, and distribution of assets for listed-security holders.[13] Their exclusion from the conventional board-independence rule follows that organizational reality.

Qualifying cooperative entities, including agricultural cooperatives structured to comply with relevant state and federal tax law and without a publicly traded class of common stock, are exempt from Rules 5605(b), (d), and (e), including the majority-independent-board, compensation-committee, and independent-director nomination-oversight requirements.[44] The SEC’s description of the cooperative exception refers to cooperatives structured to comply with relevant state and federal tax law and without a publicly traded class of common stock; applicable federal securities laws, including Exchange Act Section 10A(m) and Rule 10A-3, continue to apply.[21][13]

Limited partnerships are exempt from the majority-independent-board requirement.[31][20]

Nasdaq’s certification form also identifies an exemption for issuers whose only Nasdaq-listed securities are non-voting preferred securities, debt securities, or specified Derivative Securities under Rule 5615(a)(6)(B); if such an issuer also lists common stock, voting preferred stock, or their equivalent on Nasdaq, it is subject to all Rule 5600 Series requirements.[20][38] This exception recognizes that the holder of those securities may not possess ordinary voting rights to elect the issuer’s board.[20]

These entity-based exclusions mean that the phrase most Nasdaq-listed companies is deliberate. A reader should identify the issuer’s legal form and the securities it has listed before treating the board-majority rule as applicable.[21][20]

#### Phase-ins: delayed compliance, not permanent exceptions

An IPO company may phase in compliance with the Rule 5605(b) majority-independent-board requirement, but it must achieve compliance within 12 months from the Listing Date—the date its securities first trade on Nasdaq.[4][16][34] The IPO rule recognizes that a newly public company may need time to recruit suitable independent directors, but it does not permanently exempt the company from the board-majority requirement.

The IPO phase-in also has separate committee schedules. For the audit committee, eligible newly listed companies may have one qualifying independent member by the Listing Date, at least two within 90 days, and at least three within one year; Rule 10A-3 requires at least one independent audit-committee member on the Listing Date even where the other members may phase in.[29][34] For compensation and nominations committees, the schedule described for IPO companies is one independent member by the earlier of the IPO closing date or five business days after the Listing Date, a majority within 90 days of the Listing Date, and all members within one year.[58][30]

A company listing in a carve-out or spin-off transaction receives the same 12-month period to satisfy the majority-independent-board requirement. It must have one independent member on its compensation and nominations committees by the transaction closing date, a committee majority within 90 days of the Listing Date, and fully independent committees within one year.[29][34][58][40]

A company emerging from bankruptcy may phase in the independent-board-majority and independent nominations and compensation requirements. It generally must satisfy Rule 5605(c)(2) audit-committee requirements by the Listing Date unless a Rule 10A-3 exemption applies.[29] The distinction again matters: bankruptcy changes the timing for certain governance reforms, but it does not automatically suspend audit-committee independence.

A company transferring from another national securities exchange may receive the balance of a comparable grace period offered by its former exchange. If the former exchange lacks a substantially similar requirement, Nasdaq affords one year from Nasdaq listing.[29] This avoids forcing a transferor into immediate governance changes solely because it switches markets, while still establishing a deadline for Nasdaq compliance.

Nasdaq’s 2024 approved amendments also provide phase-in schedules for companies that list securities previously registered under Exchange Act Section 12(g), companies listing through a carve-out or spin-off, and companies that cease to be FPIs.[43][30] A company that ceases to be an FPI has the six-month schedule described above; a company that ceases to be a Controlled Company has the 12-month board-majority schedule and the separate committee timetable.[43][21]

A company that ceases to be a **Smaller Reporting Company** may also fall within Rule 5615(b)’s phase-in schedules. However, smaller reporting company status is principally relevant here because Rule 5605(d)(5) provides modified compensation-committee requirements: an SRC must have a compensation committee of at least two members, each an Independent Director, even though it is not subject to all Rule 5605(d) requirements.[1][65][30] Smaller Reporting Company status should therefore not be read as a general exemption from the Rule 5605(b) independent-board-majority standard.

Phase-ins make a company temporarily nonconforming by design, but only until the stated deadline. Once the period ends, the company must satisfy the applicable rule; it cannot treat the transition as a substitute for compliance.[29][34]

#### Limited committee exceptions do not alter the board-majority rule

Nasdaq provides narrow exceptional-and-limited-circumstances provisions for committee membership. These do not make a director independent and do not waive the requirement that the full board be majority independent where Rule 5605(b)(1) applies.[1][65]

For the audit committee, the board may appoint one non-independent director if the person satisfies the specified Exchange Act criteria, is not a current executive officer or employee, is not a Family Member of an executive officer, and the board determines that the appointment serves the best interests of the company and shareholders. The person may not chair the committee and may serve no more than two years.[1][4]

For the compensation committee or nominations committee, a company may appoint one non-independent director only if the relevant committee has at least three members, the director is not a current executive officer, employee, or Family Member of an executive officer, and the board makes the required best-interests determination. The appointee may serve no more than two years, and the company must make the required public disclosure.[1][65][4]

These committee exceptions are deliberately narrow. They permit a specified non-independent director to fill a limited committee role in exceptional circumstances; they do not convert that person into an Independent Director or reduce the number of independent directors needed for a Rule 5605(b)(1) board majority.[1][65][3]

The exemption analysis therefore proceeds in order: first identify whether Rule 5605(b)(1) applies to the issuer; second determine whether a category exemption, home-country election, or phase-in changes its timing or application; and third apply the separate committee rules, which can impose additional independent-director requirements even when the board-majority rule is unavailable or modified.[5][29][15][43]

Rule 5101 does not give Nasdaq a free-standing power to waive these enumerated requirements: Nasdaq may impose additional or more stringent criteria under that rule, but an exemption or exception must be authorized by a rule that expressly provides it.[36]

A company may use the audit-committee exceptional-and-limited-circumstances provision without Nasdaq approval, including where the committee already has three or more independent directors, if the stated statutory, employment, family-relationship, board-determination, term, chairing, and disclosure conditions are met.[28]

A company with a binding obligation that requires director selection contrary to Rule 5605(e) need not have a nominations committee if that obligation predates November 3, 2003.[28]

A company whose securities were registered under Exchange Act Section 12(g) immediately before it lists those securities on Nasdaq receives a phase-in similar to the IPO-company schedule, except that the audit-committee composition requirement does not receive that phase-in. Section 12(g) is an Exchange Act registration provision for securities that were not transferred from another national securities exchange.[66][45]

Independent directors serving on a compensation committee must also satisfy Exchange Act Rule 10C-1. This federal-law condition is additional to Nasdaq independent-director status.[45]

A company that lists securities that were registered under Exchange Act Section 12(g) immediately before the Nasdaq listing has 12 months from its Listing Date to meet the majority-independent-board requirement. It must meet the Rule 5605(c) audit-committee requirements by the Listing Date, except for the three-member minimum and subject to any Rule 10A-3 exemption; during the transition, audit-committee members must satisfy both Rule 5605(a)(2) and the Rule 10A-3(b)(1) independence criteria, subject to Rule 10A-3 exemptions.

For IPO companies, the audit-committee independence phase-in requires one qualifying member by the Listing Date, a majority within 90 days of the registration statement’s effective date, and all members within one year of that effective date. Rule 10A-3 nevertheless requires at least one independent audit-committee member on the Listing Date, and Rule 5605(c) provides no phase-in for the requirement that the audit committee include a financially sophisticated member.[58][40]

A Smaller Reporting Company has no general exemption from the majority-independent-board rule. Its limited accommodation concerns specified compensation-committee provisions. When it ceases to be a Smaller Reporting Company, it cannot use the phase-in for the compensation committee’s minimum size or for the requirement that the committee consist only of Independent Directors; it may phase in only the additional compensatory-fee and affiliation eligibility requirements, with one qualifying member at six months from the Start Date, a majority at nine months, and all members at one year.[67][44]

A company listing securities previously registered under Exchange Act Section 12(g) may phase in audit-committee size: at least one independent director member is required on the Listing Date, two independent director members within 90 days, and three independent director members within one year. During that audit-committee phase-in, the committee must still include at least one financially sophisticated member—someone with past finance or accounting employment, an accounting certification, or comparable experience or background.[58][43]

Compensation-committee members may not accept, directly or indirectly, consulting, advisory, or other compensation from the issuer or any subsidiary.[27]

Before full independence is required, non-independent directors may fill seats needed to meet that temporary minimum-size schedule.[58]

Independent-director oversight of director nominations does not apply when a third party legally holds the right to nominate a director. That exception does not relieve the company of its audit-committee and compensation-committee composition obligations.[14][13]

A company may rely on the exceptional-and-limited-circumstances exception for its compensation or nominations committee without Nasdaq approval. It must disclose the relationship and the reasons for its determination, and the appointed member may not serve longer than two years.[3]

The former-Controlled-Company board phase-in is **twice as long** as the former-Foreign-Private-Issuer phase-in: 12 months versus six months, a **six-month gap**.[45][21]

### Synthesis

**The governing baseline is a majority-independent board for most Nasdaq-listed companies, not an all-independent board.** Rule 5605(b)(1) applies to ordinary Nasdaq-listed issuers, while Rule 5615 and related provisions create three different departures from that baseline: permanent or issuer-form exemptions, a home-country-practice alternative for Foreign Private Issuers, and time-limited phase-ins for newly listed or status-changing companies.[5][3][20][43]

Controlled Companies and FPIs are the most important exceptions for operating companies, but neither is a blanket waiver. A Controlled Company remains subject to Listing Rule 5605(b)(2)’s independent-director executive-session requirement.[3][21] Controlled Companies retain the independent-director executive-session requirement and face audit-committee obligations; FPIs cannot replace specified audit-committee independence requirements with home-country practice.[3][21] Registered management investment companies, asset-backed issuers, unit investment trusts, cooperatives, limited partnerships, and certain issuers with only non-voting preferred, debt, or derivative securities receive more category-specific treatment.[20][21]

1. **Classify the issuer first:** determine whether it is an ordinary operating company, Controlled Company, FPI, registered management investment company, passive issuer, cooperative, limited partnership, or other specified exempt issuer.
2. **Apply Rule 5605(b)(1) next:** unless an exemption, home-country election, or phase-in applies, ensure independent directors are a board majority.
3. **Check the separate committee rules:** audit, compensation, nominations, and executive sessions can still require independent directors even where the board-majority rule does not apply.

An FPI that elects home-country practice instead of Rules 5600, 5250(b)(3), or 5250(d) must submit Nasdaq a written statement from independent counsel in its home country certifying that the issuer’s practices are not prohibited by home-country law; it must nevertheless comply with Rules 5625 and 5640 and the specified audit-committee requirements.[55]

The home-country-practice election has specific procedural conditions. The FPI must submit a written statement from independent counsel in its home country that the alternative practice is not prohibited by home-country law; this statement is required once, at initial listing or before the company first adopts a non-conforming practice. The FPI must disclose each Nasdaq requirement it does not follow and briefly state the home-country practice it follows instead, generally in its annual SEC filings and original U.S.-listing registration statement.[32]

A company that fails to make disclosure required for reliance on an exemption or Rule 5615(a)(3)’s non-conforming-practices provision is not in compliance with Nasdaq listing requirements and may be subject to delisting unless it promptly cures the failure.[32]

Nasdaq defines a qualifying passive issuer in IM-5615-1 as a trust or other unincorporated association without a board of directors or comparable governing persons, whose activities are limited to passively owning or holding, administering, and distributing amounts relating to securities, rights, collateral, or other assets for listed-security holders.[38]

Nasdaq requires that letter once, at initial listing or before the company first adopts a non-conforming practice. The issuer must publicly identify each Nasdaq requirement it does not follow and briefly describe the home-country practice it follows instead; failure to make that disclosure is noncompliance with Nasdaq listing requirements unless promptly cured.[62]

For asset-backed issuers and qualifying passive issuers such as unit investment trusts that lack a board or similar governing persons and are limited to passively holding, administering, and distributing assets, Nasdaq’s exemption extends beyond Rule 5605(b): it also covers the cited audit, compensation, nominations, director-election, Controlled Company, and code-of-conduct requirements.[44]

Qualifying cooperative entities, including agricultural cooperatives structured to comply with relevant state and federal tax law and without a publicly traded class of common stock, are exempt from Rules 5605(b), 5605(d), 5605(e), 5606, and 5615(c)(2). They must comply with federal securities laws, including Exchange Act Section 10A(m) and Rule 10A-3.[44]

Rule 5615(a)(2) and Rule 5615(a)(4) describe exemptions from the Nasdaq Rule 5600 Series for cooperatives and limited partnerships, respectively; Nasdaq states that those entities are not listed under the Nasdaq Rule 5700 Series.[38]

An FPI that elects home-country practice may replace the majority-independent-board requirement and may use the exemptive process to have fewer than three audit-committee members. Every audit-committee member must nevertheless meet the applicable Rule 10A-3 independence requirements.[28][14]

If an FPI makes its home-country-practice disclosure only on its website, its annual report and registration statement must state that fact and provide the website address where the disclosure is available.[62]

Rule 5615(a)(5) separately exempts management investment companies issuing Index Fund Shares, Managed Fund Shares, or NextShares from Rule 5605(c)’s audit-committee requirements, except for applicable SEC Rule 10A-3 requirements.[38]

Management investment companies that are Derivative Securities also receive the separate Rule 5615(a)(6)(A) exemption from additional Rule 5600 requirements, including Rule 5605(c)’s audit-committee requirements, except for applicable SEC Rule 10A-3 requirements. Index Fund Shares, Managed Fund Shares, and Exchange Traded Fund Shares are exempt from Rule 5605(c) because they are subject to the Investment Company Act of 1940’s accounting and auditing requirements, including Section 32(a).[37]

The SEC considered the special provisions for limited partnerships, companies in bankruptcy, and cooperative entities reasonable in light of those entities’ specific characteristics and their design for consistency with Rule 10A-3.[13]
