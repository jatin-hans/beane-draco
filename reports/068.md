# The Cambrian Explosion: Causes, Timing, and Fossil-Record Bias

## Executive summary

The evidence supports a genuinely rapid but extended Ediacaran–Cambrian radiation, most concentrated around 539–520 million years ago, rather than either an instantaneous creation of animal phyla or a mere preservation illusion.[1] Preservation bias greatly magnified the apparent burst because skeletons and particular soft tissues fossilize selectively, yet the coordinated rise of trace-fossil behaviours, skeletonized faunas, ecological complexity, and exceptional soft-bodied biotas requires real biological diversification.[2][3] Rising oxygen, developmental-regulatory capacity, and predator–prey ecological feedback are best treated as interacting conditions and mechanisms, not mutually exclusive single causes.[3] Chengjiang and Burgess Shale fossils reveal far more soft-bodied and extinct early animal diversity than shelly records alone, while Bayesian relaxed molecular clocks commonly place lineage divergences before their first identifiable fossils and therefore separate evolutionary origin from fossil appearance.[4][5]

## The question and why it remains open

The Cambrian Explosion is the name for a striking fossil pattern: many major animal body plans become visible over a geologically short interval near the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition. It matters because the pattern can reflect evolutionary diversification and selective preservation, and fossils alone do not assign a share of the apparent abruptness to either process.[6] The question is not whether the fossil record changes dramatically, but what that change dates: animal lineages’ origins, their acquisition of recognizable anatomy, their ecological expansion, or their preservation.[3][7][8]

### Why is it open whether the Cambrian Explosion was a

The traditional “explosion” was a contrast between an apparent pre-Cambrian absence of bilaterian fossils and a rich Cambrian record. A bilaterian is an animal in the branch that includes most familiar animals, typically with left–right symmetry and directional movement. Yet the observed contrast does not directly date the origin of Bilateria, because a lineage must first diverge, then evolve diagnostic anatomy, become sufficiently abundant and widespread, be buried in a preservable setting, survive geological alteration, and be recovered and recognized by researchers before it enters the fossil record. A first fossil occurrence is therefore a minimum age for a recognizable form, not its evolutionary birth date.[9][6][10]

This is the **origin–appearance gap**: molecular divergence dates estimate when lineages became genetically isolated, whereas fossils record when a population with identifiable morphology became preservable and sampled. The gap can be substantial because new body plans may evolve tens of millions of years after lineage splitting. Older members of a lineage may also lack the diagnostic characters by which their later crown group—its living members and their last common ancestor—is recognized.[5][11][10]

The fossil record is also structurally uneven. Most ordinary Cambrian fossils are shells, sclerites, or other hard parts because biomineralized tissues preserve much more readily than soft tissues. The resulting difference is not a small taxonomic correction: a shelly record can make the acquisition of skeletons look like the origin of animal diversity.[4][3]

Even the exceptional deposits that correct this bias are selective. Butterfield defines Burgess Shale-type preservation as exceptional preservation of non-mineralizing organisms as carbonaceous compressions in marine shale. It preferentially retains decay-resistant extracellular structures such as cuticle, chaetae, and jaws, while animals lacking those structures—such as acuticular flatworms, nemerteans, and unshelled molluscs—may leave no record under the same conditions.[3] Preservation also varies among animal groups within one Lagerstätte, meaning an exceptionally preserved assemblage is not a literal census of its original community.[3][12]

Environmental and geological filters further affect apparent timing. Fossil occurrence depends on where organisms lived and on whether those environments remain represented in rock; reworking can also concentrate fossils and inflate local apparent diversity. At the Selinde River section, for example, high generic diversity in transgressive lags may result partly from reworked skeletal fossils concentrated during low sedimentation and stratigraphic condensation.[6] These processes can sharpen a gradual biological change into a more abrupt stratigraphic appearance.[13]

**The key implication is that first appearances cannot by themselves distinguish a sudden evolutionary origin from a delayed fossil record.** They do, however, establish when recognizable forms became widespread enough, preservable enough, and ecologically important enough to enter the record. Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') argues that skeletonized animals and substrate-invading burrowers near the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary likely mark the ecological rise of such forms even if their unskeletonized ancestors were older.[14][15]

The preservation-centered interpretation has substantial support. The Ediacaran has relatively fewer sedimentary strata than the succeeding Cambrian, ancestral animals had unequal preservation potential, and fossil occurrences follow environmental tolerances and changing preservation of those environments. Molecular-clock estimates that place animal and bilaterian origins tens to hundreds of millions of years before their first fossils have therefore encouraged the proposal that the Cambrian event was partly an “explosion of fossils.”[16][8]

But preservation alone does not erase the biological signal. Briggs concludes that the sudden Cambrian fossil appearance is real and coincided with rapid animal diversification and biomineralization; earlier fossils are rare because both diversity and preservation potential were lower. Quantitative work reviewed by Marshall found that correcting incompleteness produces range extensions small relative to observed stratigraphic ranges and still leaves the suddenness of the adaptive radiation intact.[17][15]

Butterfield’s taphonomic work supplies a particularly important test. Exceptional preservation occurs in terminal Proterozoic as well as Cambrian strata, but the identifiable metazoans in terminal Proterozoic preservation windows appear fundamentally more primitive than those observed through comparable windows at the transition.[3] Thus, the availability of a preservation pathway did not simply reveal a fully developed Cambrian fauna already present in the Ediacaran.[3]

This conclusion is reinforced, but not settled, by evidence on Ediacaran preservation. Daley and colleagues argued that Ediacaran taphonomic conditions were no worse than Cambrian ones, and soft-bodied organisms of different sizes occur in several Ediacaran preservation windows. Nevertheless, unequivocal metazoan fossils have not been recovered from rocks older than the approximately 560 Ma trace-fossil evidence, leaving open the possibility that earlier animals were rare, small, ecologically negligible, or morphologically unlike later crown groups.[8]

Preservation bias is taxonomically directional as well as environmental. Ecdysozoans, the moulting bilaterians that include arthropods and nematodes, possess a tough cuticle that experimental taphonomy shows is decay-resistant; moulting produces multiple potentially fossilizable cuticles per individual. Their dominance in many soft-tissue Cambrian assemblages can consequently overstate their living-community representation.[18]

This selectivity is measurable within Chengjiang. The proportion of genera with preserved internal organs was 0.66 in Annelida, 0.71 in Vetulicolia, and 0.75 in Lobopodia, compared with 0 in Hyolitha, 0.37 in Radiodonta, 0.44 in Euarthropoda, 0.87 in Brachiopoda, 0.91 in Priapulida, and 1 in Chordata.[12]

The Cambrian Explosion is best captured by early- to middle-Cambrian Konservat-Lagerstätten—exceptional fossil deposits with soft-tissue preservation—which are often dominated by ecdysozoan worms and arthropods.[18]

Marshall (2010) formalized why an origin–appearance gap is expected: a phylum passes from an undiagnosable stem phase after lineage splitting, to a diagnosable stem phase with recognizable phylum characters, and then to its crown group, comprising living members and their last common ancestor. Fossils can document diagnosable stem and crown phases, whereas molecular clocks can estimate total-group and crown divergences; the two records therefore observe different parts of the same history.[19]

Cunningham et al. (2017) likewise distinguish divergence—a genomic event—from fossil appearance, which requires descendants to acquire recognizable fossilizable characters and then encounter suitable preservation, discovery, and interpretation conditions. The earliest members of a clade may also be species-poor, geographically restricted, and sparsely populated, further reducing their chance of entering the record.[18]

The geological record itself can accentuate the apparent discontinuity. Cunningham et al. (2017) identify the Great Unconformity as late Neoproterozoic continental erosion followed by marine transgression: in many regions it removed shallow-marine latest Ediacaran strata, environments expected to contain the greatest animal diversity. Plate-tectonic reconstructions further indicate that much remaining latest Precambrian and Early Cambrian sediment was subducted or accreted and destroyed at the Delamerian–Ross subduction zone.[20]

### The Cambrian Explosion concerns the relationship between late Ediacaran animal

The Cambrian Explosion is best treated as a progressive late Ediacaran-to-Cambrian diversification, rather than as a single instantaneous fossil horizon.[21] The Cambrian Explosion is a rise in animal diversity and abundance recorded in fossils between about 540 and 520 Ma, within a macrofossil record extending to at least about 571 Ma in the late Ediacaran.[22] A broader term, the Ediacaran–Cambrian radiation, covers the longer progressive diversification from the late Ediacaran into the Cambrian.[5][21][1]

Animal history clearly begins before the conspicuous early Cambrian burst. Possible stem-eumetazoan rangeomorphs occur by about 574 Ma, and the cnidarian *Auroralumina attenboroughii* attests crown Eumetazoa by 561 Ma. The first unequivocal evidence of bilaterians is late Ediacaran trace fossils, with the earliest uncontroversial bilaterian activity dated to approximately 560–555 Ma. These traces show that large worm-like animals capable of creeping or shallow burrowing existed before the Cambrian boundary, even though the identity of their makers is difficult to determine.[16][8]

The body-fossil record also shows a build-up rather than a wholly instantaneous beginning. During the last approximately 25 million years of the Ediacaran, about 565–541 Ma, trace fossils record increasingly complex behaviours, sensory-muscular activity, grazing, deposit feeding, and a growing motile component of benthic ecosystems.[23] The terminal Ediacaran includes tubular forms, small mineralized structures, increasingly complex traces, and evidence of grazing, deposit feeding, and greater animal mobility.[5] Some terminal Ediacaran tubes overlap stratigraphically with the earliest small shelly fossils, although simple tubes may have evolved in several unrelated multicellular groups and cannot all be assumed to be animals.[23][8]

The Cambrian base itself is not a biological starting gun. It is formally defined at 538.8 Ma by the first appearance of the trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum*, which records complex sediment penetration and three-dimensional substrate use. At Fortune Head, Newfoundland, the Cambrian Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point (GSSP)—the formally selected reference point for the boundary—is 2.3 m above the base of Member 2A of the Chapel Island Formation. Although intended to coincide with the first appearance datum (FAD), the lowest recorded occurrence, of *T. pedum*, the GSSP lies above the trace’s first appearance in the stratotype section.[24] *T. pedum* is also lithofacies controlled, so the boundary point is not interchangeable with the first appearance of either that behaviour or all Cambrian animals.[25]

The Fortune Head boundary is tied to the lowest occurrence of the *Treptichnus pedum* Assemblage Zone; occurrences of the trace elsewhere must be correlated with that reference level rather than assumed to be synchronous worldwide.[27]

Stratigraphic correlation adds another reason not to treat boundaries as instantaneous evolutionary breaks. Early small shelly fossils can appear in inconsistent orders between regions, trilobite first appearances are provincial and diachronous, and carbon-isotope excursions are not necessarily perfectly synchronous because of local conditions and diagenetic alteration. Formal stage boundaries can also shift which strata are assigned to a given interval.[26] The relevant evolutionary question is therefore the duration and sequence of diversification across correlated sections, not merely whether a fossil lies on one side of a named boundary.[27]

The fossil pattern nonetheless includes a pronounced early animal radiation. Erwin and colleagues’ compilation found first occurrences of animal phyla, classes, and stem-classes by 555 Ma, followed by a dramatic rise over about 25 million years in the first Cambrian stages and continuing into the Ordovician. The pattern occurs in skeletal fossils, trace fossils, and soft-bodied faunas from China, Greenland, Australia, Canada, and elsewhere.[2] This is rapid in geological terms, but it is neither a single moment nor necessarily a uniquely high evolutionary rate in every lineage.

Trace fossils are crucial because they record behavior by both hard- and soft-bodied organisms and extend continuously across the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition. The record progresses from simple late Ediacaran trails and shallow burrows, through more penetrative burrows and drilling predation, to an unparalleled increase in trace-fossil morphologies during the Fortunian, about 538.8–529.0 Ma.[28] This independent behavioral record makes a preservation-only explanation inadequate, while its staged sequence rejects an instantaneous-radiation caricature.[8][1]

Morphological disparity—the range of anatomical designs—also expanded early. Quantitative studies find that most clades reached their greatest initial morphological range near their first fossil appearances, and broader mapping of animal morphospace finds that most phylum-level clades attained maximal initial disparity in the Cambrian. Arthropods and chordates are important exceptions, continuing to explore additional morphology later in the Phanerozoic.[1] The “explosive” aspect of the event is therefore most defensible for the early appearance of high-level body-plan variety, rather than for a simultaneous peak in species richness across every animal lineage.[21][29]

The causal debate follows directly from this chronology. Oxygenation, developmental genetic capacity, and ecological interactions each address a different portion of the observed pattern. Oxygen could make larger bodies, active metabolisms, and new habitats viable; developmental systems could make novel anatomies constructible; and ecological interactions could make those anatomies selectively advantageous and ecologically widespread. None follows automatically from the others, and no single hypothesis explains both the timing and the increase in diversity and disparity.[30] The following sections evaluate these mechanisms separately.[31]

Formal stage assignments also alter the apparent placement of diversification. The base of the Miaolingian Series and Wuliuan Stage is defined in South China by the first appearance of *Oryctocephalus indicus* at the highest boundary level considered; it approximately coincides with traditional lower–middle Cambrian boundaries in South China and Laurentia but nowhere else. Consequently, traditional middle Cambrian strata in most regions, including *Paradoxides*-bearing strata of Siberia, Avalonia, and West Gondwana, are assigned to Stage 4, and the boundary can be identified with confidence only in South China, Laurentia, and Siberia if *O. indicus* is synonymous with *O. sibiricus*.[27]

The Chengjiang biota of Yunnan, China, about 518 Ma, preserves an early Cambrian marine community containing arthropods and their relatives, scalidophoran worms, sponges, cnidarians, possible early deuterostomes, and problematic forms. The Burgess Shale of British Columbia, Canada, about 508 Ma, records a later exceptionally preserved community rich in soft-bodied arthropods and extinct stem-group branches close to living animal clades; together these deposits show that shell-based records greatly understate Cambrian anatomical and ecological diversity.

The Kuanchuanpu biota, dated to 537–532 Ma, preserves scalidophorans, molluscs, chaetognaths, cnidarians and a debated ctenophore, adding an earlier exceptional-preservation window to the Chengjiang and Burgess Shale record.[6]

### Synthesis —

The evidence supports a real but extended Ediacaran–Cambrian radiation: animal activity predates the Cambrian, body-plan and ecological diversification accelerated across the boundary, and first occurrences rose sharply over roughly 25 million years rather than at a single instant.[8] Butterfield states that 《the fact of the Cambrian explosion is most clearly and consistently recorded in the conventional fossil record》, whereas exceptionally preserved biotas add detail but cannot independently define large-scale patterns.[2][8][3] Trace fossils, body fossils, and time-calibrated molecular phylogenies together indicate a real diversification event—the radiation of extant animal phyla—rather than merely an increase in fossil abundance.[7] Preservation nevertheless amplified the radiation’s visibility because skeletons fossilize readily and soft-bodied preservation depends on restricted taphonomic pathways.[3] Butterfield’s comparison of terminal Proterozoic and Cambrian preservation windows particularly weighs against a preservation-only account, while molecular divergence estimates and pre-Cambrian traces weigh against treating Cambrian first appearances as lineage origins.[3] The appropriate question for the rest of the review is therefore not which single trigger caused an instantaneous event, but how environmental opportunity, developmental capacity, ecological feedback, and preservation jointly shaped a geologically rapid transition.[3][5]

## The late Ediacaran–Cambrian record

The Cambrian Explosion names a striking change in the fossil record: animals with many different body organizations, behaviours and ecological roles become common over a geologically short interval. It matters because the visible fossil pattern combines two histories that must be kept distinct: when animal lineages actually diverged and when their bodies, traces, or skeletons first became preservable and recognizable in rocks.

Judgment — Here, *diversity* means the number of species or other taxa, whereas *disparity* means the range of fundamentally different body forms. The Cambrian Explosion is chiefly “explosive” in disparity—the early appearance of very different animal architectures—not necessarily in a simultaneous origin of all animal lineages.

### When did the Cambrian Explosion's rapid diversification occur?

The answer depends on the definition used. Erwin’s 2020 review defines the broad Ediacaran–Cambrian radiation as the fossil appearance and initial diversification of major animal groups from about 570 to 520 million years ago (Ma); a stricter Cambrian Explosion runs from about 539 to 520 Ma. The broad definition captures the late Ediacaran roots of animal evolution, while the strict one identifies the most concentrated early Cambrian expansion of recognizable body plans and ecosystems.[1]

Marshall measures the interval from heavily skeletonized animals to the first trilobite body fossils at about 20 million years, but the interval from abundant trace fossils to the end of the Cambrian at about 65 million years; these durations track different milestones in the radiation.[140][15][141]

One analysis frames the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition more broadly, at roughly 575–500 million years ago, and places the sharpest expansion of taxonomic and morphological diversity in Cambrian Epoch 2.[142]

The formal beginning of the Cambrian Period is a stratigraphic convention, not a date for the origin of animals. The current numerical age for the base of the Cambrian is 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma.[32] The 541.0 ± 1.0 Ma value is an earlier numerical estimate for the boundary, whereas 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma is the current adopted age.[33][32] These estimates date the formal rock-boundary marker, not the earlier late Ediacaran traces or skeletal occurrences, and not the evolutionary origin of Bilateria, animals with a left–right body axis.[24][8][11]

On the current standard timescale, the Ediacaran Period begins at approximately 635 Ma, and Cambrian Series 2 spans approximately 521–506.5 Ma.[105]

Late Ediacaran evidence shows that animal history was already underway before that boundary. The oldest uncontroversial traces of bilaterian activity date to about 560–555 Ma and record large worm-like animals capable of crawling or shallow burrowing.[8] Ediacaran macrofossils appeared after 570 Ma, larger-bodied and skeletonized animals appeared late in the Ediacaran, and Chengjiang later records that almost all major metazoan clades—including vertebrates—were present by 518 Ma.[1] Possible paired-appendage trackways in South China occur in terminal Ediacaran strata dated to about 551–541 Ma, although the trace makers cannot be identified to phylum.[34]

Bilaterian sediment-disturbing traces occur at about 540–539 Ma in the Nama Group of southern Africa, before the currently adopted 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma Cambrian boundary age.[143]

Regular horizontal traces occur by roughly 555 Ma, and unequivocally bilaterian sediment-bulldozing traces occur by about 540–539 Ma.[15][143]

One synthesis identifies a first phase of animal diversification beginning around 560 million years ago, dominated by non-bilaterians and stem bilaterians; another places crown animals in the fossil record by 574 million years ago.[144][6]

The fossil record nonetheless shows a pronounced early Cambrian concentration. Mángano and Buatois’s trace-fossil synthesis identifies the Fortunian, about 538.8–529.0 Ma, as a phase of unparalleled increase in trace-fossil morphologies—different kinds of tracks, burrows, and feeding structures—which they treat as strong evidence for rapid body-plan diversification.[8] Between 538 and 520 Ma, all major groups of durably skeletonized marine animals entered the fossil record, together with soft-bodied forms preserved in exceptional deposits.[1] A review of the body- and trace-fossil record places the major burst of bilateral body plans in the first 20 million years of the Cambrian.[8]

The timing was staggered, not synchronous. Ecdysozoan and lophotrochozoan protostomes are well represented in the Terreneuvian, approximately 538.8–521.0 Ma, whereas deuterostome lineages do not appear in the fossil record until Cambrian Stage 3, approximately 521.0–514.5 Ma.[8] Biomineralization also unfolded in phases: calcareous tubes and modular structures from about 550.0–538.8 Ma; broader lophotrochozoan and non-bilaterian mineralization in two pulses from about 538.8–521.0 Ma; and widespread ecdysozoan mineralization with echinoderms from about 521.0–514.5 Ma.[8]

Erwin et al. (2011, *Science*) compiled first occurrences of animal phyla, classes, and comparably disparate extinct stem-classes—branches outside the living *crown group*, the clade descended from the last common ancestor of living members. Their compilation places first occurrences by 555 Ma, followed by a dramatic rise over roughly 25 million years through the first Cambrian stages, continuing into the Ordovician.[2] By about 520 Ma, the especially intense burst of evolutionary novelty had shifted toward more ordinary patterns of speciation and extinction, with fewer new morphological innovations.[1]

A regional Siberian analysis by Zhuravlev and colleagues complicates the familiar picture of one discrete event. Their dataset contains 1,188 described skeletal species from approximately 545–505 Ma, including 430 lophotrochozoan skeletal species, divided into sixteen approximately 2.5-million-year time bins.[11] In this record, stem-group lophophorates, brachiopods, and molluscs appeared around 542.5 Ma, diversified through the Terreneuvian, and underwent another diversification from the end of Stage 3 into the beginning of Stage 4.

Crown-group gastropods first occur around 537 Ma, crown-group brachiopods and bivalves around 535 Ma, and their principal radiation began around 522.5 Ma.[11] The study therefore proposes an earlier, stem-group-dominated phase from about 542 to 513 Ma and a later crown-group phase beginning around 513 Ma and continuing into the Ordovician.[35]

The two-phase Siberian model also identifies the Sinsk extinction as separating its approximately 542–513-million-year-old, stem-group-dominated interval from the later crown-group radiation extending toward the Ordovician Radiation.[144]

**The temporal conclusion is clear: the strongest concentration of morphological and ecological innovation lies roughly between 539 and 520 Ma, but it emerged from late Ediacaran animal history and continued in lineage-specific pulses after the early Cambrian.** The Cambrian boundary remains useful for correlating rocks, but it is not a biological starting gun.[1][8][35]

Using the approximately 560–555 Ma range for the earliest uncontroversial bilaterian traces and the ~518 Ma age of Chengjiang, the interval is approximately 37–42 million years. This places the Chengjiang ecosystem substantially after the first secure evidence of bilaterian activity, rather than at bilaterian origins.[8][36]

Marshall (2010) places the conspicuous peak in small shelly fossil abundance and diversity, and the first fossil representation of a larger invertebrate fauna including many soft-bodied forms, at 530–520 Ma. He describes this abrupt appearance of disparate body plans as occurring roughly 100 million years after the first indications of metazoans.[19] Cunningham et al.

(2017) identify trace-fossil evidence for animals by about 565 Ma and for bilaterians by about 555 Ma; they likewise identify a marked Fortunian increase in trace-fossil diversity, size, and complexity.[20]

A three-phase fossil pattern further resolves the apparent single event: basal metazoan phyla occur in the latest Ediacaran, many lophotrochozoan lineages occur in the Terreneuvian, and the largest phase occurs in Cambrian Stage 3, when all three major eubilaterian groupings appear in the fossil record.[37] Lophotrochozoan appearances were gradual from the latest Ediacaran or earliest Cambrian, whereas many ecdysozoan and all deuterostome lineages in this compilation first appear in Stage 3.[37] At genus level, Terreneuvian first appearances form three pulses: a small early-Fortunian pulse, a large late-Fortunian pulse, and a moderate Stage 2 pulse.[37]

### What fossil appearances and evolutionary diversification define the Cambrian Explosion?

The Cambrian Explosion is defined by more than the arrival of shells. It includes a rapid-looking rise in animal diversity and disparity, new ways of moving and feeding, repeated acquisition of mineralized skeletons, increasingly complex ecosystems, and the expansion of animals into sediment and the water column. A synthesis describes it as a three-phased event involving body plans, episodic biomineralization, pulses of generic diversity, body-size variation, and increasing ecosystem complexity.[29]

First, trace fossils record an expansion in animal activity. Ediacaran traces are generally simple, shallow, and associated with animals feeding on microbial biofilms at or immediately beneath the sediment surface.[28] At Fortune Head, late Ediacaran burrows reached up to 0.8 cm deep and 0.3 cm wide; Fortunian burrows reached up to 3.5 cm deep and 1.0 cm wide, with a modest increase in vertical bioturbation—sediment mixing by organisms.[38] Higher in the same succession, increasing trace-fossil intensity is associated with larger sediment bulldozers and development of a sediment mixed layer by Cambrian Age 2.[38][39]

This trace evidence is important because it does not depend on the preservation of an animal’s body. Butterfield’s taphonomic analysis identifies the early Cambrian increase in the diversity and intensity of infaunal activity—animals living within sediment—as independent documentation of the arrival of Phanerozoic-style ecosystems.[3] Across the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition, the trace-fossil record is continuous for both hard- and soft-bodied organisms and records behaviour, ecosystem engineering, and environmental context.[28] The full suite of active vertical-burrowing behaviours, however, did not become widespread across many marine environments until after 529 Ma.[1]

Second, early Cambrian rocks record an expansion of skeletonized animals. The earliest skeletal fossils occur in the latest Ediacaran. During the Fortunian, approximately 541–530 Ma in Erwin et al.’s chronology, bilaterian-affinity plates, spines, shells, and other skeletal elements became abundant; these are collectively termed the *small shelly fauna* (SSF).[2] The earliest SSF are mainly interpreted as lophotrochozoans, whereas biomineralized ecdysozoans and deuterostomes appear later, in Cambrian Stage 3.[2] This sequence demonstrates evolutionary and ecological change across successive intervals, rather than a simultaneous first appearance of every animal group.

Small shelly fossils are a polyphyletic collection of early biomineralized body fossils, not a single evolutionary group; terminal Ediacaran–Fortunian examples include *Protohertzina*, *Anabarites* and *Cambrotubulus*.[27][143]

A synthesis describes the Terreneuvian as approximately 20 million years long and finds that small shelly fossils reached their highest diversity and morphological disparity after archaeocyathid sponges arose in the middle of that series.[145]

Skeletal diversification crossed the traditional boundary. Siberian sections contain diverse Cambrian-type skeletal assemblages—including halkieriids, chancelloriids, hyoliths, anabaritids, and protoconodonts—between dated levels of 543.9 ± 0.24 and 529.7 ± 0.3 Ma; in some places they predate the basal Terreneuvian *Trichophycus pedum* assemblage.[11] In southeastern Siberia, Ediacaran cloudinids occur with early Cambrian anabaritids, demonstrating continuity of the skeletal record around the boundary.[35] Thus, first appearances used to label the Cambrian do not mark a clean replacement of a non-animal Ediacaran world by a fully formed Cambrian one.

Third, the record documents a substantial expansion of ecological roles. A comparison of Ediacaran macrofossil communities with Chengjiang and Burgess Shale biotas recognized 12 Ediacaran life modes and 30 Cambrian ones, a gain of 24 and loss of 6 through the transition.[8] By Cambrian Stage 2, approximately 529–521 Ma, the substrate or agronomic revolution had reorganized benthic habitats: suspension-feeding infauna became established, food webs became more complex, and benthic and planktonic ecosystems became coupled.[40] In Stage 3, approximately 521.0–514.5 Ma, further diversification of deposit-feeding strategies followed; the fundamental organization of metazoan marine food webs appears established in the Chengjiang biota at about 518 Ma.[8] The ecological-feedback model is addressed in the later section on ecological feedback; here the relevant point is chronological—complex ecological structure is itself part of what diversified.

Fourth, the Cambrian record includes both living-lineage relatives and extinct experiments. Many unusual Cambrian organisms that once seemed to require separate phyla are now interpreted as stem members of high-ranking living clades.[8] The early Cambrian therefore did not merely produce the modern phyla in familiar form; it also produced numerous extinct branches that reveal intermediate stages in the assembly of modern animal anatomies.

The overall envelope of metazoan morphospace—occupied range of body-form variation—was already broad in the early Cambrian, although it did not then reach its maximum and did not subsequently contract greatly through extinction.[29]

The pattern is especially pronounced near the first fossil appearances of many clades. Quantitative studies find that most clades attain their greatest morphological range close to their first recorded fossil occurrence, although arthropods and chordates continued to explore new forms later in the Phanerozoic.[29][1] Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') and Valentine distinguish this exceptional accumulation of disparity from a simple count of species: the difference between a sea anemone, a whale, and a sea urchin is greater than the difference among many beetle species, even if the beetles are more numerous.[14] They describe the early Cambrian rate of disparity accumulation as unprecedented whether measured by phyla or their constituent classes.[15]

First fossil appearance is not evolutionary origin. Probable animal-related rangeomorphs occur by about 570–574 Ma, and late Ediacaran trace fossils provide the first unequivocal evidence of bilaterians with bodies capable of peristalsis, or wave-like muscular movement.[35][6] A recent fossil-based reconstruction infers late Ediacaran origins for Chordata, Cnidaria, and Mollusca, while placing Euarthropoda, Echinodermata, Ctenophora, Hemichordata, Rotifera, and Chaetognatha in the Cambrian.[16] This differs from the traditional shorthand that essentially all animal phyla first appear in the Cambrian fossil record.[14]

The distinction explains why the fossil pattern admits two readings. One is a genuine, sudden diversification of bilaterian animals and body plans in the latest Ediacaran or early Cambrian. The alternative is that animals became much more visible because skeletonization, burrowing, and other preservable features became widespread.[16] The body of evidence supports both a biological radiation and a visibility change: mineralized skeletons are more likely to fossilize, but Butterfield identifies their rapid early Cambrian expansion as a straightforward record of major radiation; trace fossils, phytoplankton cysts, and exceptional soft-bodied faunas independently correlate with the same broad transition.[3]

The formal boundary itself reinforces the need for caution. The Fortune Head GSSP was intended to coincide with the first appearance of *Treptichnus pedum*, but reported specimens occur 3.11 m and 4.41 m below the designated point.[26] A bed-by-bed reassessment of the *T. pedum* interval recorded 20 ichnospecies in 13 ichnogenera and found a more gradual appearance of trace-fossil diversity and a more protracted ecosystem transition than earlier interpretations suggested.[41] Yet the same interval records new substrate interactions, complex vertical burrows, arthropod scratch marks, and persistence of Ediacaran matground feeding.[41]

**The fossil appearances therefore define a real but composite radiation: new body architectures, skeletons, behaviours, and ecological roles accumulated rapidly, while different lineages and traits appeared in successive pulses across the late Ediacaran and early Cambrian.** This rules out treating either the formal boundary or the first fossil of a lineage as the single date of its evolutionary origin.[2][8][41]

The reported three biomineralization phases, from ~550.0 to 514.5 Ma, span 35.5 million years in total. That summed duration quantifies why skeleton acquisition is better understood as a phased transition than as a single Cambrian-boundary event.[8]

Recognized life modes rise from 12 in the selected Ediacaran assemblages to 30 in the Chengjiang and Burgess Shale biotas, a 150% increase. This numerical change captures the scale of the documented expansion in ecological roles.[8]

The early Cambrian signal is concentrated but not complete. Of 38 living phyla, 20 had fossil first appearances by Cambrian Stage 3, nine were added in later periods, and nine have not been identified in the fossil record.[37] The later first appearances of most of those phyla, except Bryozoa, are attributed to low preservation potential, particularly soft-bodied anatomy.[37] Conversely, the recorded Stage 3 burst is linked in part to intensive excavation of exceptionally preserved Lagerstätten, including Chengjiang and Sirius Passet.[37]

Middle Cambrian assemblages, including the Burgess Shale of British Columbia at approximately 508 Ma, show that the diversification visible in Cambrian fossils extended beyond the earliest Cambrian.

Cambrian Series 2 also records archaeocyath reefs and widespread trilobites, adding reef construction and mobile, mineralized arthropods to the expanding range of marine ecosystems.

Trilobites appear abruptly and diversely at about 521 Ma, although traces indicate that their relatives walked and burrowed before mineralized exoskeletons made their bodies readily fossilizable.[90]

Trilobite mineralized fossils appear abruptly and diversely near 521 million years ago, but earlier traces indicate that trilobite-grade animals existed before their readily fossilized exoskeletons.[90]

Wood and colleagues (2019) place the oldest Ediacaran macrofossils in Newfoundland at about 571 Ma and report a significant increase in the diversity and disparity of macroscopic soft-bodied organisms after about 560 Ma.[42] Their chronology records widespread surface-locomotion traces from at least 560 Ma, the globally distributed skeletal macrofossil *Cloudina* from about 550 Ma, and possible predatory borings in *Cloudina*.[42] The Avalon, White Sea, and Nama assemblages span approximately 571–557 Ma, 560–551 Ma, and 555–541 Ma, respectively, demonstrating successive late Ediacaran intervals of biological change before the formal Cambrian boundary.[42]

### Synthesis

The late Ediacaran–Cambrian record supports neither a literal instantaneous explosion nor a wholly illusory fossil pattern. Animal activity, probable animal-related body fossils, skeletonized forms, and ecological change begin before the formal Cambrian boundary; the sharpest fossil concentration of new body forms and behaviours occurs in the early Cambrian, especially about 539–520 Ma.[1][8] The changes were staggered among protostomes, deuterostomes, stem groups, crown groups, and skeleton-forming lineages, and regional records preserve multiple pulses rather than one uninterrupted rise.[8][35]

Read together, the evidence supports a geologically rapid radiation superimposed on a longer evolutionary buildup and amplified by changing fossilization opportunities. The next sections test why that combination occurred: whether environmental oxygenation, developmental regulatory capacity, and ecological interaction explain different parts of the same extended transition.[16][3]

## Environmental trigger: rising oxygen

Rising oxygen is a plausible environmental trigger because animals need oxygen for aerobic respiration, the high-energy metabolism that powers movement, feeding, growth and other demanding activities. The hypothesis matters not because oxygen automatically creates new animal forms, but because it could have removed physiological and ecological limits that had previously kept many animals small, simple, or restricted to particular habitats.

### How could rising atmospheric oxygen levels trigger the Cambrian Explosion's

**Rising atmospheric oxygen could expand oxygenated marine habitat, but its biological effect would be mediated through seawater.** Ocean oxygenation depends on oxygen supply from the atmosphere, ocean mixing, and oxygen consumption by the biosphere, including nutrient-driven organic-carbon export. Consequently, atmospheric oxygen and the dissolved oxygen experienced by an animal on a shelf or seafloor were not identical.[43] Higher dissolved oxygen could permit larger, more three-dimensional animals and energetically expensive behaviours, including active locomotion, sediment mixing by animals (bioturbation), and muscular carnivory.[44][45]

The proposed mechanism is an environmental release from constraint. Low oxygen could support small animals with low-energy feeding modes, whereas a rise could permit active predators and more complex food webs. Sperling and colleagues’ modern oxygen-minimum-zone comparison found that carnivores were absent only below 0.34 mL/L oxygen (about 15 μM); both the proportion of carnivorous individuals and the number of carnivorous taxa increased above suboxic conditions.[46] **Thus, oxygenation could help explain the timing of ecological escalation by enabling carnivory, while predator–prey feedback could then amplify diversity and disparity.**[30]

This route also explains how oxygen may affect habitat area rather than merely individual metabolism. A molybdenum-isotope reconstruction from South China inferred expansion of strongly and moderately oxygenated seafloor to more than 97% at approximately 521 Ma, at least episodically; its authors proposed that declining anoxia enlarged habitable continental-margin space and reduced disruptions by incursions of anoxic water.[45] Their model further proposes feedback: early animals, plankton and burrowers could themselves alter carbon, nutrient and oxygen cycling, helping stabilize wider marine oxygenation.[44][45] This makes oxygenation and ecological change potentially reciprocal rather than rival explanations.

The environmental account does not, however, supply a mechanism for originating new species, new clades, bilaterality, segmentation, biomineralization or fundamentally new body plans. Sperling and colleagues explicitly distinguish oxygenation, which can remove a barrier and potentially explain onset, from ecological processes that generate the observed morphological pattern.[47][46] The environmental trigger therefore makes diversification more feasible; it does not replace the developmental and ecological mechanisms considered in the following sections.

Cole and colleagues (2016) identified a baseline transition in oxidative chromium cycling at approximately 0.9–0.8 billion years ago and inferred surface oxygen below 1% of present atmospheric level through much of the Proterozoic before that transition. This supplies a long pre-Cambrian history in which oxygen could constrain animal expansion, rather than evidence for a single oxygen step at the Cambrian boundary.[48]

Local oxygen availability was also shaped by circulation, productivity, oxygen consumption during organic-matter decay, and sulfide and iron chemistry, so an oxygenated atmosphere did not produce uniformly oxygenated seafloors.

### What do Lyons and Reinhard's geochemical data support about rising

Lyons, Reinhard and Planavsky’s 2014 *Nature* review, *The rise of oxygen in Earth’s early ocean and atmosphere*, addresses the history of oxygen in Earth’s early ocean and atmosphere.[49] Reinhard and Planavsky’s 2020 *Elements* paper, *Biogeochemical Controls on the Redox Evolution of Earth’s Oceans and Atmosphere*, frames oxygenation as a coupled ocean–atmosphere–biosphere history, not a single atmospheric switch.[50] Redox-sensitive geochemical systems and biogeochemical models reconstruct the long transition from a reducing Earth surface to a strongly oxidizing one, coupled to changes in ocean structure and biosphere activity.[49][50] The relevant tools are **paleo-oxybarometers**: chemical and isotopic signatures in rocks that constrain past oxygen conditions. They are indirect proxies, so combining several indicators is important because oxygen varied across environments and through time.[51]

Lyons and colleagues describe roughly 1.5 billion years of generally low, though above-Archean, oxygen levels before the later rise in ocean–atmosphere oxygen; they leave unresolved whether oxygenation drove major developments in complex life or followed them.[55]

The distinct 2014 *Science* study by Planavsky, Reinhard, Lyons and colleagues, *Low Mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen levels and the delayed rise of animals*, used chromium isotopes from Proterozoic sediments in China, Australia and North America.[52] It inferred that mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen, between 1.8 and 0.8 billion years ago, was at most 0.1% of the present atmospheric level; the authors argued that this was probably below the minimum needed by the earliest animals and could have delayed their emergence and diversification.[52] This result supports oxygen as a long-term constraint on animal evolution, not a measurement of oxygen during the Cambrian radiation itself.

Lyons and colleagues note that estimates of mid-Proterozoic atmospheric pO2 below 1% of the present atmospheric level, perhaps as low as 0.1%, depend on translating soil-derived chromium-isotope signals into a global concentration, a step they regard as intrinsically uncertain.[55]

Closer to the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition, the geochemical picture supports dynamic, geographically heterogeneous oxygenation. Krause and colleagues’ isotope-mass-balance reconstruction predicts Neoproterozoic atmospheric oxygen oscillations from roughly 1% to 50% of present atmospheric level, rejecting a simple unidirectional rise.[53] Regional Ediacaran records likewise show continuing redox instability: early skeletal animals appeared in redox-stratified waters, while sustained reef construction appears to have required more persistent oxygenation.[54]

A 2019 model of tectonically driven Ediacaran oxygenation estimated atmospheric pO2 below 0.25 of the present atmospheric level (PAL) at the start of the Ediacaran and above 0.25 PAL, perhaps above 0.3 PAL, by its end. Oxygen increased in 97% of 10,000 Monte Carlo runs; the model’s trajectory from about 0.2 to 0.3 PAL was an informed estimate, not a direct measurement.[146]

Compilations of carbonate carbon, carbonate-associated sulfur and carbonate uranium isotopes infer repeated ocean-oxygenation events lasting 1–10 million years between about 580 and 515 Ma. The uranium-isotope baseline becomes more positive across that interval but is repeatedly interrupted by evidence of anoxia.[13]

A separate uranium-isotope compilation identifies at least ten possible oxic–anoxic intervals between 580 and 510 Ma, including negative carbonate δ238U excursions as large as about −0.6‰.[147]

An inferred oxygenation event at about 575–569 Ma coincided with the appearance and rise of the Avalon assemblage; a further shallow-marine event is inferred around 557 Ma, followed by maximum oxygenated seafloor area at about 551–550 Ma.[147]

Early Cambrian evidence strengthens the case for pulses rather than a single threshold crossing. On the Siberian Platform, Alexander and colleagues combined fossil distributions with iron speciation, redox-sensitive trace metals and carbonate iodine ratios for approximately 527–519 Ma. They found mainly dysoxic, meaning oxygen-limited, shallow waters inhabited by motile skeletal benthos; transient, modest oxygenation intervals coincided with regional species-richness increases and habitat expansion.[13] Their conclusion is direct: high oxygen was not required for Cambrian diversification, although oxygen pulses could facilitate diversification by allowing existing clades to expand their habitat ranges.[13]

Early Cambrian models suggest atmospheric oxygen of about 5–10% by volume, equivalent to 24–48% of the present atmospheric level; these are modeled atmospheric values, not measurements of oxygen at every seafloor.[13]

Nitrogen isotopes from nine Yangtze Basin sections spanning shallow to deep settings from about 551 to 515 Ma show repeated shifts: late Ediacaran δ15N averaged +3.9‰, fell toward −4‰ to 0‰ across the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition, returned to a +3.6‰ mean during the Fortunian to early Stage 2, and shifted again through Stages 2 and 3.[148]

The proxy framework includes iron-speciation measurements, sulfur-isotope systematics, redox-sensitive trace-metal enrichments, and isotope records. These signatures characterize the redox conditions of particular depositional settings rather than directly measuring one universal atmospheric oxygen percentage.

Carbonate uranium-isotope and nitrogen-isotope records also contribute to reconstructions of repeated, spatially variable oxygenation and anoxia across the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition.[149][147][148][150]

There is no direct proxy for ancient atmospheric oxygen partial pressure (pO2); atmospheric estimates rely chiefly on long-term carbon-cycle models. Oceanic indicators include molybdenum, vanadium and rhenium inventories, selenium-isotope values and cerium anomalies, with cerium data indicating well-oxygenated shallow water after 551 Ma.[146]

Iron speciation, nitrogen isotopes, molybdenum and uranium from Yangtze Sea intra-shelf basin, slope and slope-basin settings indicate a predominantly ferruginous—anoxic and iron-rich—water column; in slope settings, local bottom-water anoxia reached the photic zone.[149]

In the Yangtze Sea sections, molybdenum concentrations ranged from 1.8 to 735 ppm at Zhongnan and reached 808 ppm at Longbizui; uranium reached about 300 ppm in basal phosphate-rich beds and 331 ppm near the Longbizui sulfide layer.[149]

Deep-water South China sections record locally euxinic, or anoxic and sulfide-rich, conditions throughout the Ediacaran, alongside repeated oxygenation events in a predominantly anoxic Ediacaran–early Cambrian ocean.[151]

Early Cambrian platform carbonates from three sites have mean iodine/(calcium + magnesium) ratios of 1.47, 1.02 and 0.65 μmol/mol, respectively, below the study’s approximately 2.6 μmol/mol oxic threshold and consistent with dysoxic conditions.[13]

Lyons, Reinhard and Planavsky (2021) date a renewed oxygen increase to around 800 Ma, following a long interval of mostly low oxygen, and identify major developments in complex life as coincident with it.[55] They describe the Neoproterozoic Oxidation Event as a proposed second major oxygenation step in the roughly 800–600 Ma interval.[55]

Liu and colleagues (2021) used the cerium anomaly (Ce/Ce*) in carbonate rocks as a proxy for redox conditions in the local marine environment.[56]

Their thermodynamics-based modelling estimated atmospheric oxygen at about 1% of the present atmospheric level through most of the Proterozoic, from 2.4 to 0.65 Ga.[56]

### Synthesis

**The evidence supports oxygen as a context-dependent facilitator of Cambrian diversification, not as a demonstrated sole trigger.** Very low mid-Proterozoic oxygen plausibly delayed animal emergence, while later oxygenation could enable larger bodies, active carnivory, expanded habitat and more complex food webs.[52][46] Yet the relevant transition was variable in time and place, and biodiverse Cambrian communities could occur under dysoxic conditions.[53][13] Read together, these findings favor a coupled model: oxygenation could make ecological escalation possible, ecological interactions could amplify diversification, and evolving animals could feed back on marine chemistry.

The unresolved empirical question is whether oxygen increases consistently preceded specific biological innovations at the same places and times, rather than merely coinciding with them.[29][30][13]

## Genetic toolkit: Hox genes and regulatory complexity

### How could Hox gene evolution and regulatory complexity enable the

Judgment — The genetic-toolkit hypothesis concerns developmental capacity: how inherited genes and their regulatory controls could generate many different animal forms. It is not chiefly a claim that one newly evolved gene suddenly made the Cambrian radiation happen.

Hox genes are transcription-factor genes that assign regional identities along the main body axis; related ParaHox genes participate in gut-region patterning. Comparative work associates Hox genes with positional coding along the central nervous system, ParaHox genes with mouth, midgut and anus specification, and NK genes with mesoderm patterning. Duplications of the ANTP-class homeobox genes produced these three clusters, supplying patterning components that could be deployed in new combinations. Holland (2015, Zoological Letters, 'Did homeobox gene duplications contribute to the Cambrian explosion?') therefore proposes that ANTP diversification contributed to bodies capable of high-energy directed locomotion and active burrowing.[57]

Hox genes can activate or repress local patterning networks rather than directly specify a finished organ; changes in their expression domains or downstream connections can therefore alter morphology while retaining much of the machinery that builds local structures.[152]

Hox, ParaHox and NK genes are part of a broader developmental architecture that also includes Pax, Fox and T-box regulatory-gene families. Modern *Drosophila* has more than 100 homeobox genes but only eight typical Hox genes; humans have more than 200 homeobox loci, of which 39 are Hox genes.[140]

A developmental gene regulatory network (GRN) is the connected system of regulatory genes, transcription factors, signals and cis-regulatory modules—DNA elements that control when and where genes are expressed—that specifies cell fates and patterns a developing body. Altering GRN architecture, particularly cis-regulatory modules, can change the spatial and temporal deployment of existing genes without requiring an entirely new set of protein-coding genes. Increasing numbers of enhancer binding sites and enhancers per gene expand the possible expression patterns, cell types, patterning systems and morphological outcomes. **Thus, duplication, co-option—the reuse of a regulator or subcircuit in a new developmental context—and regulatory redeployment provide a credible mechanism for rapid morphological innovation.**[1][58]

This mechanism is constrained as well as productive. Hox and ParaHox genes emerged before the cnidarian–bilaterian split, while one reconstruction estimates three Hox and two ParaHox genes in that ancestor and seven Hox and three ParaHox genes in the crown-bilaterian ancestor. Yet the early evolutionary relations of these genes remain unresolved, and an older reconstruction allows that the last common metazoan ancestor lacked a complete Hox complex. Hox history therefore supports an ancient patterning toolkit, not a demonstrated Cambrian-specific Hox trigger.[59][60][2]

Coral genomic evidence supports a more extensive Hox cluster in the cnidarian–bilaterian ancestor than earlier reconstructions inferred.[140][153]

Surveys of Xenacoelomorpha infer that all 11 animal homeobox classes were already present in the first bilaterians.[1]

Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') and Valentine (2010) place regulatory-gene accumulation before the Cambrian radiation: 10 transcription-factor classes are known in sponges, compared with about 88 in the cnidarian–bilaterian last common ancestor and about 108 in the protostome–deuterostome last common ancestor.[14] They report that the sea anemone Nematostella shares Hox genes and genes used in bilaterian mesoderm development with bilaterians despite not being triploblastic, and that 1,584 gene families, about 20% of the eumetazoan gene families in their comparison, are first found in Nematostella.[19]

Marshall and Valentine (2010) identify peak additions of protein-coding regulatory genes deep in metazoan and eumetazoan stem groups, when the body plans of early-branching clades remained relatively simple. They characterize this earlier accumulation as genomic preadaptation: developmental components initially used for other functions supplied capacity for later morphological differentiation.[19]

Marshall and Valentine (2010) also identify microRNA regulation as a component of developmental regulatory complexity that grew with the rise of the explosion fauna and fine-tunes and compartmentalizes gene expression; they report about 40 microRNAs in a sea anemone and 677 in humans.[19]

Regulatory changes can modify expression in one body region or developmental stage without disrupting every function of a pleiotropic gene, a gene with multiple effects. If a revised developmental programme produces a viable, heritable form, natural selection can preserve and elaborate it.

In most animals, Hox genes occur on the same chromosome in the same order as the order in which they are expressed along the body axis, a relationship called spatial colinearity.[61]

### What evidence and argument do Davidson (2010, Nature, 'Emerging properties of animal gene regulatory networks') [62] and Erwin advance for

Davidson and Erwin’s *Science* 2006 article, *Gene Regulatory Networks and the Evolution of Animal Body Plans*, argues that because GRNs build animal body plans, body-plan evolution depends on changes in their architecture. Their comparative-developmental evidence links GRN reorganizations to morphological and body-plan differences; experimentally mapped sea-urchin networks illustrate how perturbing transcription factors, signals and receptors can identify causal regulatory connections.[63][64][65][66]

Davidson and Erwin proposed that developmental systems became more hierarchical as spatial and temporal regulators were inserted into older cell-type-specification pathways: upstream networks establish broad embryonic territories, while downstream networks direct local differentiation.[1]

Their central model is a hierarchy. Deeply interconnected upstream subcircuits, called kernels, underlie major body parts and are unusually resistant to change; more terminal modules and cis-regulatory linkages can evolve more readily. Davidson and Erwin therefore explain how inherited phylum-level organization can remain conserved while changes at other network positions produce new traits and major morphological differences. A subsequent analysis reported slower evolution in genes determining phylum- and superphylum-level characters than in genes associated with classes, families and speciation.[67][63][64][68]

In Davidson and Erwin’s hierarchy, changes to downstream differentiation genes may redeploy one cell-type function, whereas changes higher in the network can redeploy an entire regulatory state or multiple structures. They located the developmental basis of phylum-scale differences principally in upstream patterning and body-part specification networks, rather than isolated changes to terminal structural genes.[152]

This is a claim about regulatory-network evolution, not a claim that Hox genes alone caused the event. Indeed, much of the protein-coding developmental repertoire is shared across animals and even occurs in single-celled opisthokonts; proposed toolkit diversification begins before the sponge–eumetazoan split and expands in some families near the base of Eumetazoa. Regulatory innovation—including added microRNA families and character-specific GRNs—offers a better account of changing form than gene presence alone.[2][31]

Marshall and Valentine (2010) specify the relevant GRN architecture: kernels are gene associations with recursive expression patterns dedicated to basic functions; subcircuits produce functional units or modules; signalling cassettes serve multiple pathways; and largely non-regulatory structural-gene batteries occupy network peripheries and differentiate cells, tissues and organs. They propose that selection for novel gene-expression patterns, including regulatory RNAs, chiefly underlay Cambrian diversification of bilaterian body plans.[19]

Marshall and Valentine (2010) further report that a gene-origin analysis of Drosophila melanogaster found remarkably few novel protein-coding genes introduced during the origins of Bilateria, Protostomia, Ecdysozoa and crown Arthropoda. They infer that radiation into lineages ancestral to living bilaterian phyla involved evolution of network architectures rather than the invention of many protein-coding genes.[19]

This regulatory explanation has a longer history: Valentine and Campbell’s 1975 paper proposed that evolution of the regulatory genome may underlie the rapid development of major animal groups, and Marshall identifies it as the first paleontological paper to connect gene regulation to the rapid fossil appearance of animal phyla.[69]

### Synthesis

Read together, the evidence favors the genetic toolkit as an enabling and form-generating mechanism: shared Hox-related genes, signalling systems and GRNs could be duplicated, co-opted and rewired into new spatial and temporal developmental combinations. Davidson and Erwin’s hierarchy also explains the coexistence of innovation with conserved core body-plan organization. But the toolkit’s deep, pre-Cambrian origins create a timing problem for any stand-alone genetic trigger: it supplied capacity well before the conspicuous diversification. The most coherent interpretation is therefore that developmental regulation made new forms possible, while the conditions that promoted and selected those forms belong with the environmental and ecological explanations. Coyne (2006, Science, 'Comment on "Gene Regulatory Networks and the Evolution of Animal Body Plans"')’s contemporaneous criticism—that ordinary natural selection can explain phylum origins—disputes Davidson and Erwin’s stronger theoretical claim, not the demonstrated importance of GRNs in development.[70][2][47][63][58]

A distinct evo-devo hypothesis holds that unusually high genetic and developmental flexibility early in animal evolution produced a burst of body-plan experimentation, then became partly constrained as genetic control systems grew more elaborate. In this formulation, the locus of evolutionary change shifts from conserved developmental kernels to less constrained genomic regions.[71]

The broad conservation of important developmental genes across phyla shifted attention away from treating the establishment of major developmental structures as itself the cause of the Cambrian Explosion. Comparative developmental studies instead describe the earliest animals as having relatively few cell types and lacking complex developmental patterning, with complex development and morphology arising convergently in different Ediacaran lineages.[71][55]

A different reconstruction describes Urbilateria—the last common ancestor of protostomes and deuterostomes—as a complex animal with a Hox gene complex, eyes, a contractile circulatory system and perhaps appendages. In that account, selection acting on a conserved developmental patterning system, channelled by deep homologies, generated diverse functions and morphologies.[72]

The timing of developmental capacity determines what the genetic account explains. Sperling and Stockey (2018) argue that an early Proterozoic origin of crown animals, followed by convergent and simultaneous appearance of large size, skeletonization and Cambrian morphological innovations, points to external ecological or environmental forcing; a mid-to-late Ediacaran origin immediately before the Cambrian instead makes the radiation an adaptive radiation.[73]

## Ecological feedback: predator–prey arms races

### How could predator–prey arms races drive the Cambrian Explosion's rapid

A predator–prey arms race is reciprocal natural selection: a predator’s new way of finding, capturing, or processing prey favors defensive prey traits, and those defenses in turn favor improved predator traits. In the Cambrian case, the relevant ecological innovation was **carnivory**, mobile animal-on-animal feeding, rather than predation in the broad sense of any organism consuming another. Carnivory creates strong selection for armour, spines, burrowing, escape movement, sensory systems, and specialized feeding structures.[47][46]

Sperling et al. (2013, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 《Oxygen, ecology, and the Cambrian radiation of animals》) argue that selection within evolving food webs supplies a direct mechanism for morphological innovation. Different ecological tasks create trade-offs: a form well suited to resist attack may be poorly suited to moving rapidly or feeding efficiently. This produces multiple successful body-form solutions rather than one optimum, increasing both diversity and disparity, the range of fundamentally different anatomical designs.[47][46]

Fossils place carnivory and defensive responses across the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition. Circular perforations in upper-Ediacaran *Cloudina* tubes have been interpreted as drill holes, and some populations contain drilled individuals at about 20%.[47][74] Early Cambrian evidence includes *Protohertzina*, interpreted as chaetognath grasping spines, chaetognath body fossils, preserved gut contents such as hyolith shells in priapulid guts, and defensive morphologies including brachiopod punctae and wiwaxiid spines.[47][75] Thus, predator–prey selection could have multiplied ecological roles and morphological solutions once active carnivores became established.

An integrated model connects this feedback to oxygen availability. Sperling and colleagues propose that low oxygen permitted small animals and low-energy feeding but constrained active carnivory because prey capture and digestion create high episodic oxygen demand. Rising or stabilizing oxygen could therefore permit larger, muscular carnivores; their interactions with prey could then accelerate the expansion of animal diversity and disparity.[47] Modern oxygen-minimum-zone data fit that physiological link: carnivores were absent only below 0.34 mL/L oxygen, and both carnivore abundance and carnivorous-taxon richness rose at higher oxygen levels.[46]

**Predator–prey escalation therefore offers a mechanism for rapid diversification, while the environmental conditions that enabled energetically costly carnivory help account for when that mechanism could operate.**[30][47]

Increasingly complex late Ediacaran and early Cambrian trace fossils, including vertical and branching burrows, record infaunalization: the expansion of animal life into the seafloor sediment. This shift both supplied burrowers with refuge from surface predators and altered seafloor habitats and food resources through bioturbation, the mixing of sediment by organisms.

### What evidence and argument do Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') [14] and Valentine advance for

Peterson, McPeek, and Evans’s 2005 synthesis, *Tempo and mode of early animal evolution: inferences from rocks, Hox, and molecular clocks*, centers on **macrophagy**, feeding on large food items. They argue that macrophagy near the end of the Marinoan glacial interval increased benthic predation pressure, favored larger body sizes, and eventually favored skeletons. They further propose that those pressures produced mesozooplankton, connecting the pelagic water column with the benthos, the seafloor community, and thereby establishing the Phanerozoic-style ocean.[76]

Marshall’s 2006 *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences* synthesis adds a formal ecological model. He treats the bilaterian developmental system as already capable of producing many forms, then argues that macroscopic interactions—especially predation—increased the number of functional needs organisms had to meet. Predators favored many prey-avoidance strategies; those strategies favored specialized predation. Marshall calls the resulting increase in selective trade-offs a roughening of the morphogenetic fitness landscape: more locally successful anatomical designs become available even without a new developmental system.[15][77]

Cambrian Lagerstätten support the existence of the complex food webs required by this model. Dunne et al. (2008, *PLoS Biology*, 《Compilation and Network Analyses of Cambrian Food Webs》) reconstructed detailed Chengjiang and Burgess Shale food webs whose overall network topology resembles modern food webs, although Chengjiang had longer paths between species and more feeding loops.[78] Direct Burgess Shale evidence includes *Sidneyia inexpectans* specimens with fragments of the trilobite *Ehmaniella burgessensis* preserved in their guts.[79] These communities contained both predators and diverse prey defenses, so the ecological-feedback process was available early in Cambrian animal ecosystems.

The proposed causal strength of predation remains contested. A genus-level test of the Cambrian–Ordovician radiation found a negative correlation between predator ratio and origination rate, r = −0.346, with permutation-test p = 0.773; the authors concluded that predation did not show the predicted positive association with origination across their seven time-series points.[80] The same study nevertheless proposes that predation may have helped initiate diversification indirectly by favoring exoskeletons and other defenses, because skeletons can serve as versatile anatomical scaffolds for later evolutionary divergence.[80]

James W. Valentine’s 2004 book *On the Origin of Phyla* likewise treats predation and the occupation of new modes of life as ecological amplifiers operating alongside developmental evolution and environmental permissiveness, rather than as a single clock that dates the origin of all animal phyla. His account therefore complements Marshall’s: interacting communities could make diversification self-reinforcing after animals had acquired sufficient size, mobility, sensory capacity, and protection to affect one another strongly.

Chengjiang and Burgess Shale animals make the ecological roles behind this argument visible: radiodonts such as *Anomalocaris* were nektonic, water-column predators with frontal appendages adapted for prey capture, alongside scavengers, suspension feeders, deposit feeders, and mobile prey. Such coexisting roles show that Cambrian communities had the trophic structure through which ecological feedback could operate.

Marshall and Valentine’s claim is stronger than a claim that predation merely contributed: their 2005 paper calls macrophagy the most likely cause of the Cambrian Explosion and characterizes the event as the inevitable outcome of macrophagy evolving near the end of the Marinoan glacial interval.[81] Their later formulation makes the mechanism compatible with developmental explanations: ecological interaction supplied the complex selective regime that expressed, rather than created, preadapted genomic developmental capacity.[81][19]

Marshall and Valentine (2010) specify the feedback at the level of whole body plans: improvements in locomotion, sensory equipment, respiration, circulation, and other organs of interaction both recorded and intensified increasingly complex biotic interactions.[19] They argue that these interactions extracted phenotypic richness already latent in animal genomes.[19] In this account, genomic capacity and an environment permissive of complex animals were necessary but insufficient; the selective regime itself had to become complex as larger bilaterian body plans proliferated after latest Neoproterozoic and Early Cambrian environmental amelioration.[19]

They argue that genomic capacity and an environment permissive of complex animals were necessary but insufficient because the selective regime had to become complex as larger bilaterian body plans proliferated after latest Neoproterozoic and Early Cambrian environmental amelioration.[19]

### Synthesis

The ecological-feedback account explains how ecology could amplify a pre-existing developmental potential: Peterson, McPeek, and Evans propose that macrophagy and pelagic–benthic coupling increased ecological pressures, while Marshall argues that predator avoidance and new feeding roles increased the selective problems animals faced and thus the number of viable forms.[76][77] Fossils show predation, prey defenses, preserved feeding interactions, and complex food webs near and after the transition.[47][75][78] The broader pattern favors ecological feedback as a contributor to Cambrian diversification, especially to skeletal and behavioral innovation, rather than a simple rule that more predators directly produced more origination throughout the Cambrian–Ordovician radiation.[80]

The listed predator ratio is 37.5% higher at Chengjiang (0.44) than at the Burgess Shale (0.32). This comparison is consistent with both assemblages containing substantial predatory components, while Chengjiang has the higher reported ratio.[80]

Evidence of predation predates the Cambrian, including increasingly spiny acritarchs, holes drilled in *Cloudina* shells, and burrows interpreted as predator avoidance.[82]

The appearance of predation was therefore unlikely to have triggered the Cambrian Explosion, although predation may have influenced its subsequent diversification.[82]

## Chengjiang, Burgess Shale and molecular clocks

### How have fossil discoveries from the Chengjiang deposits shifted knowledge

Judgment — Chengjiang matters because it records an Early Cambrian community before the better-known Burgess Shale, while preserving bodies that ordinary shell-rich deposits usually lose. It therefore tests whether the apparent Cambrian diversification was only a spread of mineralized skeletons or a broader biological expansion.

The Chengjiang biota of Yunnan, China, is assigned to Cambrian Epoch 2, Stage 3, at approximately 518 Ma in recent site descriptions; a U–Pb zircon date of 518.03 ± 0.69/0.71 Ma supplies a robust *maximum* depositional age, not a direct date for each fossil. An earlier account dated the Lagerstätte to 530 Ma, illustrating continuing refinement of the regional timescale. Chengjiang was discovered in 1984 and preserves complete soft-bodied animals, non-mineralized structures, and internal anatomy alongside shells and carapaces.[36][83][84][4]

This preservation changed the known composition of Early Cambrian life. A 2003 inventory listed about 120 described animal species, whereas a later review reported more than 280; the increase records continuing collection and description, not a single fixed census. Arthropods dominate the named fauna, but Chengjiang also contains diverse lobopodians, priapulids, sponges, brachiopods, cnidarians, and chordate-grade animals. The fauna indicates that almost all major metazoan clades, including vertebrates, had appeared by about 518 Ma.[85][8][1]

Chengjiang also supplies anatomical connections that isolated skeletal fossils cannot. Complete specimens linked the net-like plates of *Microdictyon* to a lobopodian body, overturned the original Burgess Shale reconstruction of *Hallucigenia*, and showed robust paired ventral appendages in anomalocaridids. These discoveries converted several apparently bizarre fossils into evidence about the stepwise assembly of living animal lineages, especially Panarthropoda, the broader group containing arthropods, tardigrades, and onychophorans.[86][85][8]

The fauna records a structured ecosystem, not merely a taxonomic list. Its animals occupied benthic, endobenthic, and swimming habitats and included predators, suspension feeders, detritivores, herbivores, and other feeding roles; reconstructions place the fundamental structure of metazoan marine food webs in place by Stage 3. Comparisons recognize 30 life modes in Chengjiang and Burgess Shale biotas, against 12 in three selected Ediacaran macrofossil assemblages.[24][87]

Chengjiang is not an undistorted census. Exceptional preservation depended on restricted-shelf conditions, limited scavenging and bacterial decay, salinity fluctuations that reduced burrowers, and rapid burial; some anatomical decay still occurred. Moreover, the affinities of important fossils remain contested: *Yunnanozoon* has been proposed as anything from a stem bilaterian or protostome to several deuterostome and chordate-related forms. **The decisive result is that Chengjiang exposes a broad, ecologically organized soft-bodied fauna by about 518 Ma, showing that the skeletal record alone drastically understates Early Cambrian diversity.**[4][12][88]

South China supplies more than one isolated snapshot: dozens of exceptionally preserved biotas occur in sequence across its Ediacaran–Cambrian successions, and new taxa continue to be recovered from established and new localities. Chengjiang in particular has documented the oldest described pterobranch hemichordate and anatomical evidence supporting a deuterostome relationship for vetulicolians; it also preserves the probable venomous predatory arthropod *Isoxys curvirostratus* and the stem-arthropod *Fuxianhuia protensa* with a tripartite brain. These finds add early evidence of deuterostome diversity, predation, and nervous-system complexity to the case that Early Cambrian diversification was anatomical and ecological, not merely skeletal.[37]

The distribution of first occurrences places Chengjiang within a broader stepped Early Cambrian pattern. One synthesis records two bona fide metazoan phyla in the late Ediacaran, a rise of novel phyla in the Terreneuvian, and a further burst in Stage 3; 20 of 38 extant phyla in that compilation had fossil occurrences by Stage 3. It also records a gradual latest-Ediacaran-to-Early-Cambrian accumulation of lophotrochozoan lineages, but Stage-3 appearances of many ecdysozoan and all deuterostome lineages. This pattern shows that the fossil explosion comprises temporally uneven clade histories rather than one identical pulse across all animals.[37]

The principal Chengjiang exposures are the Maotianshan Shales near Chengjiang in Yunnan. Chordate-related fossils including *Myllokunmingia* and *Haikouichthys* made this fauna central to reconstructing the early history of vertebrate-related anatomy.

### How have fossil discoveries from the Burgess Shale deposits shifted

The Burgess Shale of British Columbia is a Middle Cambrian Burgess Shale-type Lagerstätte that records an approximately 508-million-year-old Cambrian ecosystem, after the principal Early Cambrian rise in disparity and diversity.[89][90] It preserves carbonaceous remains of cuticle, guts, eyes, and gills, plus locally mineralized features, revealing organisms and characters ordinarily absent from shell-based fossil assemblages.[4][91][15]

The Burgess Shale thereby expanded the record from familiar hard-part taxa to an anatomically rich community containing arthropods, worms, sponges, priapulids, mollusks, echinoderms, chordates, hemichordates, and problematic forms. A hard-part-only sample would represent about 40% of Burgess Shale genera. The importance is evolutionary as well as numerical: many so-called weird wonders are now interpreted as stem-group relatives—extinct branches outside but close to living crown groups—rather than as wholly separate vanished phyla.[86][92][8]

Burgess Shale and Chengjiang show continuity at high taxonomic rank but substantial turnover below it. About 85% of Burgess phylum-level groups and 15% of its genera are also represented at Chengjiang, including genera such as *Naraoia*, *Hallucigenia*, and *Anomalocaris*, but species mostly differ; one comparison found only six shared species among 152 listed Chengjiang species. Echinoderms and mollusks are scarce or absent at Chengjiang, a difference interpreted as more likely environmental than simply biogeographic.[4][85][84]

Later work also replaced the image of Burgess as a single locality with evidence of substantial within-region variation. The 2014 Marble Canyon assemblage, about 40 km from Walcott’s original locality, yielded 3,067 specimens, 55 taxa, and 11 metazoan body plans; 22% of its diversity consisted of new species. Two-thirds of its genera occur at Walcott Quarry, yet its composition is distinct, and *Primicaris* and *Misszhouia* extended formerly Chengjiang-only taxa into a younger Canadian assemblage.[93]

That variation has a direct bearing on interpretations of diversity. Burgess Shale-type deposits preserve unusually many soft-bodied forms because rapid, mud-rich burial transported organisms into conditions that inhibited decay; the Burgess escarpment setting aided that process. Differences between nearby localities also reflect palaeoenvironmental, ecological, and taphonomic controls. **Thus the Burgess Shale demonstrates extensive Middle Cambrian diversity and anatomical disparity, but as a later and selectively preserved window it cannot by itself date the beginning of the radiation.**[94][93][15]

Using the approximately 518 Ma age for Chengjiang and approximately 508 Ma for the Burgess Shale, their depositional ages differ by approximately 10 million years.[90][89] Their comparison therefore samples Cambrian communities separated by a substantial interval, not a single contemporaneous fauna.[36][4]

Reanalysis of Burgess Shale material by Harry B. Whittington, Derek Briggs, Simon Conway Morris, and Jean-Bernard Caron established the evolutionary importance of its soft-part anatomy. Taxa such as *Opabinia* and *Pikaia*, alongside *Anomalocaris* and *Hallucigenia*, became key evidence for the assembly of arthropod- and chordate-related body plans.

### How do molecular clock recalibrations using Bayesian relaxed-clock methods infer

Judgment — A molecular divergence date estimates when two living lineages last shared an ancestor; a fossil first appearance records when a diagnosable member of a lineage was preserved, recovered, and recognized. They answer different chronological questions.

A Bayesian relaxed clock estimates a dated evolutionary tree from molecular sequences while allowing substitution rates—the rates at which sequence changes accumulate—to vary among branches rather than imposing one constant rate. In Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo analysis, the result is a posterior distribution: a set of date estimates conditional on the sequence data, the evolutionary-rate model, the tree model, and calibration assumptions.[95][96][1]

Fossils convert relative molecular branch lengths into absolute geological time. The oldest securely identified crown-group fossil generally supplies a minimum age for a node. Maximum bounds rely on qualified negative evidence, such as records of out-group lineages in older strata that demonstrate that suitable preservational conditions were obtained while in-group members are absent. These bounds are often *soft*: probability outside the stated limits is low but not zero.[6][16] A 95% highest posterior density interval, or 95% HPD, is then the shortest posterior range containing 95% probability under the model; it is not an observed duration of fossil history.[6][16][97]

The method’s strength is that it recognizes rate variation and calibration uncertainty. Its constraint is that absolute dates remain heavily dependent on fossil interpretation: molecular sequences chiefly provide relative timing, while fossils anchor the timescale. Simulations show that relaxed-clock estimates work best when the assumed pattern of lineage-rate change matches the real one; using an incorrect rate model reduced 95% interval coverage to 83% in one test.[98][99]

This distinction prevents a common error in the Cambrian debate. A lineage split may precede the origin of its recognizable body plan by many tens of millions of years, and a fossil first appearance is a conservative minimum for clade age. **Bayesian relaxed clocks therefore estimate evolutionary origins, whereas Chengjiang and Burgess Shale date the presence and morphology of already fossilized animals.**[5][8][1]

A fossil placed on the stem lineage of a living clade also constrains the relevant living-lineage split: that split must be older than the stem fossil. Assigning a stem fossil incorrectly to a crown-group node makes the inferred divergence artificially young.

A soft maximum can be inferred from an older fossil-bearing assemblage that contains relatives of the focal clade but lacks the focal clade despite suitable preservation; poor preservation can make that absence misleading.[100]

### How have Bayesian relaxed-clock recalibrations shifted the debate about the

Earlier molecular-clock scenarios often placed the last common ancestor of living animals near 800 Ma and the stem lineages leading to most living phyla by the end of the Ediacaran, around 541 Ma. These results encouraged the view that much animal history was cryptic in the Neoproterozoic. At the other extreme, some early estimates placed major-phyla divergences 100–400 million years before the Cambrian.[2][101]

Recent recalibration has shifted many estimates younger. In *Science Advances*, 《Ediacaran origin and Ediacaran-Cambrian diversification of Metazoa》, the authors used 203 nuclear genes, 66 taxa, 46 calibrated nodes, and revised 23 minimum and 30 maximum calibration bounds. Under their conventional topology, uniform calibration scheme, and autocorrelated-rate model, they estimated crown Metazoa at 598.6–590.2 Ma, crown Eumetazoa at 587.4–579.7 Ma, crown Bilateria at 577.6–567.9 Ma, and Nephrozoa, Deuterostomia, Ecdysozoa, Protostomia, and Spiralia between 574.2 and 553.4 Ma.[6][16]

The authors attribute the younger outcome primarily to revised fossil calibrations, not to a wholly novel clock model. Their sensitivity analyses found relatively little overall change across independent- and autocorrelated-rate models, calibration-density schemes, and eight alternative deep-animal topologies, although independent-rate models produced wider HPD intervals.[6][16]

The reconciliation is partial, not complete. Their analysis still leaves, for example, an approximately 40-million-year gap between the deuterostome divergence estimate and its fossil minimum; yet the fossil sequence itself requires a deuterostome ghost lineage because protostome fossils are about 20 million years older than the oldest deuterostomes. In contrast, clock estimates for durably skeletonized crown groups such as Euarthropoda and Brachiopoda agree more closely with their earliest fossils.[16][1][8]

**The recalibrated clocks move the debate away from a choice between wholly Cambrian origins and an immensely ancient hidden radiation: they support late Ediacaran origins for deep animal lineages, while retaining a real Cambrian concentration of recognizable crown groups, body plans, and ecological communities.** The remaining date ranges are too broad to tightly correlate individual lineage splits with any one proposed environmental or ecological trigger.[16][102][77]

An influential earlier Bayesian analysis, dos Reis and colleagues’ 2015 study *Uncertainty in the Timing of Origin of Animals and the Limits of Precision in Molecular Timescales*, estimated crown Metazoa at roughly 833–650 Ma in its 95% credible interval. This older Neoproterozoic result illustrates why revised fossil calibrations, rather than molecular sequences alone, have been central to the later shift toward younger estimates.

### Synthesis

Chengjiang and Burgess Shale show that Early and Middle Cambrian seas contained far more soft-bodied anatomical diversity, ecological structure, and stem-group experimentation than shelly fossils alone reveal. Their shared high-rank groups, but mostly distinct species, document both early establishment of major lineages and later community turnover. Bayesian relaxed-clock analyses place the relevant lineage splits earlier than their first conspicuous fossils, but revised calibrations bring many deep divergences into the late Ediacaran rather than the distant Cryogenian. Together, the evidence supports a pre-Cambrian evolutionary prehistory followed by a genuinely consequential Cambrian expansion in morphology, ecology, and fossil visibility; neither the Lagerstätten nor molecular clocks justify treating fossil first appearances as literal lineage origins.[84][93][16][1]

## Rapid radiation or preservation bias?

The apparent abruptness of the Cambrian Explosion is inseparable from how organisms entered the fossil record. Fossils record both biological history and **taphonomy**—the post-mortem processes of decay, transport, burial and chemical alteration that turn remains into fossils. The central question is therefore not whether preservation mattered, but whether preservation alone can produce the documented early-Cambrian transformation of animal forms, behaviours and ecosystems.[3]

### What do Butterfield's taphonomic studies show about preservation bias in

Nicholas Butterfield’s 1995 and 2003 analyses establish that fossilization is not a neutral census of ancient life. Burgess Shale-type preservation is the exceptional preservation of non-biomineralizing organisms as flattened carbonaceous compressions in marine shale; each preservational pathway is restricted to particular sedimentary environments and early-diagenetic conditions. Butterfield therefore argued that the fossil record cannot be read literally: any one fossil type captures only part of the contemporaneous biota.[3]

The conventional Cambrian record is especially biased toward biomineralized, or hard-part-bearing, organisms. Butterfield noted that the great majority of taxa and individuals in the Burgess Shale and Chengjiang biotas did not biomineralize, whereas ordinary fossil assemblages are dominated by shells and skeletal fragments. In a census of organisms known from the Chengjiang deposit, more than 97% lacked hard parts; fewer than 3% of those Chengjiang organisms are represented in contemporaneous strata that preserve shelly fossils only. This is a comparison between the Chengjiang exceptional-preservation assemblage and a shelly-only preservation window, not an estimate for all Cambrian animals.[3][4]

Exceptional preservation corrects this bias but does not remove it. In Burgess Shale-type deposits, decay and flattening preferentially preserve resistant extracellular structures such as arthropod cuticle, polychaete chaetae and jaws. Organisms lacking such structures—including acuticular flatworms, nemerteans, shell-less molluscs and other soft-bodied forms—may still fail to preserve. Thus the abundance of cuticular arthropods, priapulids and polychaetes, and the apparent absence of other groups, cannot by itself establish the original community’s ecology or evolutionary composition.[3][103]

The physical process was highly selective. Burgess Shale-type fossils lost more than 99% of their original volume to decay, yet incomplete decomposition and fine sediment retained organic films and some delicate structures. The preservation mechanism involved rapid burial in fine clay-rich sediment, oxygen-poor bottom conditions and restricted oxidant access during early burial; anoxia alone was not enough because anaerobic microbial decay could continue.[94][91] At Chengjiang, finely laminated mudstones and rapid burial under dysaerobic or anaerobic conditions limited destruction by scavengers, currents, burrowers and bacterial decay.[85]

Nor is Chengjiang a uniform snapshot. A probability-based study found that the eleven animal groups examined preserved contrasting numbers of tissue types and contrasting proportions of internal organs. Internal-organ preservation ranged from zero in hyoliths to one in chordates at the genus level, while euarthropods had a value of 0.44, radiodonts 0.37, priapulids 0.91 and brachiopods 0.87. These differences were not correlated with generic diversity or specimen abundance, and may partly reflect mode of life and transport: sediment-dwelling animals were more readily buried alive than active swimmers able to escape sediment flows.[12]

Butterfield did not, however, treat the Cambrian Explosion as merely a preservational illusion. He argued that the terminal Proterozoic absence of phosphatic steinkerns—phosphate internal moulds—indicates a genuine absence of even non-mineralizing equivalents of Cambrian shelly fossils, although long-term changes in phosphogenesis limit the scope of that inference. He also emphasized a correlation between shells, phytoplankton cysts, trace fossils and exceptional assemblages containing unmistakably Phanerozoic-style animals.[3]

A disagreement remains over how representative exceptional deposits are. Butterfield stressed that exceptional fossils are unreliable measures of large-scale evolutionary timing because they are rare, environmentally restricted snapshots; a Chengjiang synthesis instead proposed that Chengjiang and Burgess Shale may provide fair and proportional samples of Cambrian life. The latter interpretation does not erase their tissue- and transport-selective preservation.[3][4][94]

**Butterfield’s work therefore shows that preservation bias changes the apparent membership, abundance and timing of Cambrian faunas, but it does not convert all evidence of early Cambrian biological change into an artifact.**[3]

Marshall (2010) separates a phylum’s history into an undiagnosable stem phase, after lineage splitting but before recognizable phylum-specific characters evolved; a diagnosable stem phase; and the crown group, beginning with the last common ancestor of living representatives. Fossils can document diagnosable stem and crown phases when preservation permits, whereas molecular clocks can date total-group and crown divergences; a first fossil occurrence therefore need not date the lineage split.[19]

Ecdysozoan dominance in many early–mid-Cambrian soft-tissue Konservat-Lagerstätten is one concrete taphonomic distortion. Ecdysozoans possess tough, periodically moulted cuticles that experimental taphonomy finds decay-resistant, and moulting creates more preservable cuticles than there were individual animals. Their fossil abundance cannot be read directly as their living abundance.[18]

The preservation advantage does not erase their early behavioural record. Possible ecdysozoan traces extend into the latest Ediacaran, and unequivocal total-group scalidophoran body fossils occur by approximately 535–532 Ma in the Fortunian; together with their extensive bioturbation record, these observations place part of ecdysozoan history before the better-known Stage-3 Lagerstätten.[18]

The Kuanchuanpu biota, dated to 537–532 million years ago, preserves scalidophorans, molluscs, chaetognaths, cnidarians and a proposed ctenophore.[6]

Butterfield’s work on Cambrian organic-walled microfossils also showed that suitable preservational windows can recover important small, non-biomineralizing components of early ecosystems that are largely absent from shelly-fossil assemblages.

The Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary is a stratigraphic convention, not a date for the origin of animals or all animal lineages. Its Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point (GSSP), the agreed reference horizon for a geological boundary, is at Fortune Head, Newfoundland. The ratified Cambrian base was tied to the first recorded occurrence of the trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum* at Fortune Head; the current International Chronostratigraphic Chart places the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition at approximately 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma. Numerical ages are revised estimates and do not define Phanerozoic or Ediacaran units: Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points (GSSPs) define them.[104][105]

That definition has been challenged because *T. pedum* is a behavioural trace rather than a body fossil, and its occurrence varies among sedimentary settings. It is strongly lithofacies controlled in some accounts, making precise correlation from Newfoundland to Siberia and South China difficult. Moreover, *T. pedum* was subsequently found at 16.25 m and 17.55 m in the Fortune Head succession—4.41 m and 3.11 m below the designated GSSP at 20.66 m.[25][26] A formal boundary and a fossil’s first local appearance are consequently not interchangeable.

High-precision zircon uranium–lead dating in Nevada’s lower Wood Canyon Formation places the boundary inferred from trace-fossil and carbon-isotope correlation at no older than 532.83 ± 0.98 Ma, at least six million years younger than prior age models for that section.[143]

Other markers also shift the apparent onset. On the Siberian Platform, diverse Cambrian-type skeletal assemblages occur in levels dated 543.9 ± 0.24 to 529.7 ± 0.3 Ma; in some areas they precede both the basal Cambrian negative carbon-isotope excursion and the basal Terreneuvian *Trichophycus pedum* assemblage. Ediacaran cloudinids also co-occur there with early Cambrian anabaritids, demonstrating continuity rather than a clean faunal replacement at a single boundary.[35][11]

The Namibian record illustrates both the power and limitation of high-resolution dating. Its authors calculated that the extinction of rangeomorphs and erniettomorphs and the beginning of the recorded Cambrian radiation occurred within 410 ± 400 thousand years. That interval includes an erosional unconformity and an ash bed that predates *T. pedum*, so it documents a short recorded turnover without resolving every biological event within it.[106][107]

At Fortune Head, the biological transition is substantial but not point-like. Trace-fossil abundance, diversity and disparity rise within a 14.0 m interval across the boundary; maximum burrow width increases from 0.3 cm in late Ediacaran strata to 1.0 cm in the Fortunian, and maximum depth from 0.8 cm to 3.5 cm. The succession shows no identified facies change or major hiatus beneath the boundary, yet a bed-by-bed reassessment found that ichnofossil diversity appeared more gradually and that the ecosystem transition was more protracted than earlier accounts envisioned.[38][26][41]

Regional correlations add a further complication. Earliest trilobite assemblages are provincial and diachronous, commonly overlie unconformities, and occur in shallow marine to nearshore facies. Carbon-isotope excursions can assist correlation, but they are not necessarily perfectly coeval and may be altered by diagenesis.[27] In East Yunnan, differing fossil and isotope interpretations have placed the boundary at several levels within or near the Daibu and Zhongyicun members, while local sections vary between conformable contacts and depositional discontinuities.[108]

The small shelly fossil *Watsonella crosbyi* occurs across South China, Australia, Mongolia, Siberia, Avalonian North America and France, offering a potential correlation marker despite differences between observed local and true biological ranges.[27]

**Stratigraphic revision weakens a literal picture of all innovation erupting exactly at the traditional Cambrian base: it extends or compresses the observed interval according to marker choice, but it does not eliminate the biological transition documented in continuous sections.**[35][38][41]

A revised Wood Canyon calibration places many small shelly fossil occurrences formerly assigned to approximately 540–533 million years ago in an interval younger than 533 million years ago; it implies diversification rates at least twice those inferred under the older age models.[143]

### What paleontological evidence supports a genuinely rapid radiation?

The strongest evidence for a real radiation is the convergence of multiple fossil records that have different preservation biases. A recompilation of first occurrences found a dramatic rise in metazoan phyla, classes and comparably disparate stem classes over roughly 25 million years from the latest Ediacaran through the first Cambrian stages, continuing into the Ordovician. This pattern appears in skeletal, trace and soft-bodied fossil records.[2]

The event was not confined to shells. Between 538 and 520 Ma, all major groups of durably skeletonized marine animals entered the fossil record, alongside soft-bodied forms and extinct clades in exceptional deposits. Reviews conclude that most bilateral clades appeared during the Cambrian’s first 20 million years and that, by approximately 518 Ma, Chengjiang recorded almost all major metazoan clades, including vertebrates.[1][8]

Trace fossils provide an independent behavioural record. Simple bilaterian traces occur by about 560 Ma or earlier, so bilaterian activity predates the formal Cambrian. But during the Fortunian, approximately 538.8–529.0 Ma, trace-fossil morphologies rose by 433%, followed by a further increase of about 40%; the full suite of metazoan traces, including active vertical burrowing across broad marine environments, appears only after 529 Ma.[8][1] This is evidence for changing animal-substrate interaction, ecosystem engineering and body-plan-related behaviour, not simply improved preservation of skeletons.

Morphological disparity also supports a biological signal. Disparity means the range of body forms rather than the number of species. Quantitative analyses find that most clades attained their greatest morphological breadth near their first fossil appearances, while the overall metazoan range of form was already broad in the early Cambrian.[1][29] Ecologically, Ediacaran macrofossil assemblages have 12 recognized life modes, compared with 30 in Chengjiang and Burgess Shale biotas: a gain of 24 life modes and a loss of 6 across the transition.[8]

The radiation was episodic rather than a single instantaneous pulse. Biomineralization proceeded in three phases: calcareous tubes and modular forms at about 550.0–538.8 Ma; broad lophotrochozoan and non-bilaterian mineralization in two pulses at 538.8–521.0 Ma; and widespread ecdysozoan mineralization with echinoderms at 521.0–514.5 Ma.[8] The Siberian skeletal record likewise identifies two phases: a stem-group-dominated phase from approximately 542 to 513 Ma and a crown brachiopod–mollusc phase beginning around 513 Ma and extending into the Ordovician.[35]

This evidence distinguishes the “explosive” claim in its proper geological sense. Tens of millions of years allow ordinary evolutionary processes, but they are short relative to the preceding history of animal life. By about 520 Ma, a review concludes that the pronounced burst of morphological innovation had shifted toward more conventional patterns of speciation and extinction.[8][1]

**The paleontological record supports a geologically rapid expansion in animal disparity, ecological roles and behaviours, while showing that this expansion began before the formal boundary and unfolded in successive pulses rather than in one instant.**[2][8]

The Burgess Shale in British Columbia, Canada, is about 508 Ma old and, like Chengjiang in Yunnan, China, preserves predators, mobile prey, suspension feeders, deposit feeders and infaunal animals. These assemblages document complex food webs and use of multiple marine habitats.

### How much of the apparently “explosive” radiation could be an

Preservation bias can make the fossil record’s onset look sharper than evolutionary history. Ordinary strata preferentially record durable skeletons, while soft-bodied lineages may remain unrecorded until a rare preservation window opens. This is why fossil first occurrence is a minimum age for a clade, not its evolutionary origin: preservation, sampling and the later acquisition of recognizable characters can all delay its appearance in the record.[3][13]

Exceptional deposits reveal the magnitude of this effect. Without Burgess Shale-type localities, shelly fossils would represent only an estimated 3%–20% of Cambrian biota; separately, the Chengjiang assemblage census found that more than 97% of organisms known from that deposit lacked hard parts.[4] Yet exceptional deposits are themselves selective.

Chengjiang and Burgess Shale contain the greatest numbers of soft-bodied forms partly because unusually energetic depositional settings transported whole assemblages across chemical gradients and buried them rapidly; taxonomic richness among deposits is strongly shaped by transport and burial before fossilization.[94]

The preservation window also changed through time. Butterfield showed that Burgess Shale-type preservation was widespread in Lower and Middle Cambrian strata but later declined as a significant mode; this temporal pattern means the initial appearance and later disappearance of Burgess Shale-type taxa cannot be directly read from those fossils alone.[110] The record of soft-bodied animals is therefore uneven, but the window’s timing cannot itself demonstrate when the animals originated or went extinct.

Changes in preservation also affect apparent Ediacaran losses. Ediacaran-type fossils continue to the Cambrian base, where their preservational mode largely disappears. Butterfield proposed that intensified early Cambrian bioturbation—sediment mixing by animals—could have destroyed microbial-mat conditions needed for many Ediacaran impressions, without requiring a simultaneous mass extinction.[3] Conversely, Ediacaran soft-bodied organisms occur in multiple preservational windows, and Daley and colleagues argued that Ediacaran taphonomic conditions were not generally inferior to Cambrian conditions.[8]

That disagreement defines the appropriate limit of the preservation explanation. Preservation can plausibly hide small, soft-bodied, non-burrowing or geographically restricted ancestors, and it changes the observed abundance and taxonomic composition of Cambrian communities. But preservation alone does not explain the concordant rise in shells, trace-fossil diversity, infaunal activity, pelagic ecological change and exceptional faunas with Phanerozoic-style metazoans.[3] It also does not explain why terminal Proterozoic exceptional-preservation pathways yield animals of a more primitive aspect than those seen through comparable Cambrian windows.[3]

A genuinely earlier origin remains possible for particular clades. For example, molecular-clock estimates and fossils need not coincide: one recent study places the minimum age of crown Metazoa at 593.4 Ma, about 19 million years before fossil evidence cited at around 574 Ma. Other groups have wider divergence-to-fossil gaps, including approximately 20–40 million years for Bilateria, Protostomia and Deuterostomia.[16] These are gaps between lineage origin and fossil recognition, not evidence that all later Cambrian ecological and morphological diversification was illusory.

**No defensible numerical fraction divides the Cambrian pattern into preservation artifact versus true radiation; the evidence instead supports a mixed signal in which preservation sharpened first appearances, while rapid biological diversification produced the cross-cutting fossil and ecological changes.**[2][3][13]

Rock exposure, collecting intensity, taxonomic practice and the uneven availability of radiometric dates also affect recorded fossil ranges and apparent diversity independently of original fossilization.

Rock-record loss adds a separate preservational filter before fossilization is considered. Cunningham and colleagues identify the Great Unconformity as prolonged latest-Neoproterozoic continental denudation followed by marine transgression: shallow-marine latest-Ediacaran sequences, environments expected to hold high animal diversity, were eroded in many regions; plate-tectonic reconstructions also indicate destruction of much remaining latest Precambrian and early Cambrian sediment at the Cambrian-initiated Delamerian–Ross subduction zone.[20]

### Synthesis —

The Cambrian Explosion was neither a literal instant nor merely an illusion made by shells and rare fossil beds. Butterfield’s taphonomic framework shows that ordinary and exceptional records both filter organisms by body tissue, burial history, chemistry and environment; stratigraphic revision further shows that formal boundaries and fossil first appearances do not date lineage origins.[3][25][13] Yet shells, traces, phytoplankton cysts, soft-bodied Lagerstätten, morphological disparity and ecological life modes converge on a substantial early-Cambrian transformation.[2][3][8] The most accurate conclusion is therefore a real, geologically rapid radiation with a preceding late-Ediacaran buildup and an artificially sharpened fossil onset: preservation affects the apparent tempo, but cannot account for the full scale or coherence of the biological transition.[35][8]

## Deep metazoan relationships

Phylogenomics reconstructs evolutionary relationships from hundreds to thousands of genes. It matters here because a molecular-clock date can only be assigned to a branching pattern—the animal tree—and because that tree determines which developmental and ecological traits are inferred to have belonged to early animals. The broad framework is stable, but several of its deepest branches remain disputed.[1][111]

### What have phylogenomic studies found about deep metazoan relationships?

Bilaterians—the animals with a basic left–right axis—are consistently divided into Lophotrochozoa, Ecdysozoa and Deuterostomia; the first two together are protostomes. The five principal living animal lineages are Porifera (sponges), Ctenophora (comb jellies), Placozoa, Cnidaria and Bilateria.[1][112] **The unresolved issue with greatest consequences is whether sponges or comb jellies are sister to all other living animals—that is, which lineage first diverged from the rest.**[113][114]

Sequence-based studies have yielded conflicting answers. Simion and colleagues’ 2017 *Current Biology* phylogenomic analysis of 1,719 orthologous gene clusters from 97 species recovered Porifera-sister: sponges first, then ctenophores, then placozoans, followed by a cnidarian–bilaterian clade. The authors found that Ctenophora-sister results appeared under settings that increased long-branch attraction, the erroneous grouping of rapidly evolving lineages.[113] Redmond and McLysaght’s 2021 *Nature Communications* reanalyses likewise favoured Porifera-sister when using site-heterogeneous mixture models and amino-acid recoding, although one dataset retained maximal comb-jelly-first support even under its best-fitting amino-acid models.[115]

A different genomic character supports the rival tree. Schultz and colleagues’ 2023 *Nature* analysis used synteny—chromosomal linkage of genes regardless of their precise order—and identified seven derived chromosome fusions shared by sponges, cnidarians and bilaterians but absent from ctenophores; four involved subsequent gene mixing, treated as effectively irreversible. Its Bayesian analysis gave posterior probability 1.0 for Ctenophora-sister.[116] This disagreement is substantive: sponge-first makes the absence of neurons, muscles, synapses and a gut in sponges more parsimoniously ancestral, whereas comb-jelly-first requires either losses in sponges and placozoans or independent origins of these traits.[113][115]

Other backbone nodes remain sensitive to methods: Xenacoelomorpha has been placed either as sister to Nephrozoa or with Ambulacraria, and Deuterostomia has been tested as both monophyletic and paraphyletic.[16] Thus, phylogenomics establishes much of the animal family tree but makes reconstruction of the animal ancestor—and hence claims about the origins of developmental complexity—conditional on unresolved deep branches.[10][117] (Babonis and Martindale, 2017)[118]

Dunn and colleagues’ 2008 phylogenomic study, 《Broad phylogenomic sampling improves resolution of the animal tree of life》, and Ryan and colleagues’ 2013 genome study of the comb jelly *Mnemiopsis leidyi* recovered Ctenophora-sister. Neither result implies that a living sponge or comb jelly is a literal model of the last common animal ancestor; both lineages have undergone their own long subsequent evolution.

### How have phylogenomic studies informed estimates of deep metazoan divergence

Bayesian relaxed-clock dating fits molecular change to a phylogenetic tree while allowing substitution rates to differ among branches; fossil occurrences supply age constraints, usually minimum ages for the lineages to which fossils can be assigned. It estimates lineage splits, not the first fossil appearance of a diagnostic body plan, which can arise tens of millions of years later and must then be preserved.[1][16][5][6]

Earlier clock studies commonly placed major animal divergences far before their conspicuous Cambrian fossils. Erwin and colleagues’ 2011 *Science* analysis, using seven genes, 118 taxa and 24 soft-bound fossil calibrations, estimated the last common ancestor of living animals near 800 Ma and stem lineages leading to most extant phyla by the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary; it placed most phylum-level crown divergences between the end of the Ediacaran and end of the Cambrian.[2] Lozano-Fernandez and colleagues’ 2017 Bayesian phylogenomic relaxed-clock study instead inferred a relatively rapid radiation of the principal animal lineages before the Sturtian glaciation, a pattern recovered under three alternative placements of ctenophores.[112]

Calibration revisions have moved some estimates younger. A 2024 Bayesian relaxed-clock analysis with revised node calibrations estimated Nephrozoa, Deuterostomia, Ecdysozoa, Protostomia and Spiralia between 574.2 and 553.4 Ma in the late Ediacaran; it estimated most animal phyla in the Cambrian or with 95% highest-posterior-density intervals spanning the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary.[6][16] Its result illustrates why clocks and fossils are not independent tests: changing the geological ages or phylogenetic interpretation of fossils changes the posterior dates.[6][16]

Clock models also matter. In the 2017 analysis, uncorrelated-rate models produced generally older mean dates and substantially wider 95% credibility intervals than autocorrelated-rate models; different fossil-calibration sets similarly shifted deep-node ages.[112] Across eight alternative animal-tree topologies in the 2024 analysis, node ages differed somewhat but overall changed little.[16] The resulting conclusion is not that every animal phylum had a long invisible Precambrian history: skeletonized crown groups such as Euarthropods and brachiopods show closer agreement between clock estimates and earliest fossils.[1][8]

The phylogenetic placement of a calibration fossil is decisive. A Cambrian fossil assigned to the stem group of Euarthropoda—an extinct branch closer to euarthropods than to their living sister group but outside the living crown group—constrains the total-group split, not automatically the divergence of all living euarthropods.

The practical link between phylogenomics and dating is fossil calibration. A calibration density is a user-defined distribution of plausible ages for a tree node based on fossil evidence; after topology constraints are enforced, the resulting effective priors for different nodes are interdependent.[119] In node calibration, an earliest fossil supplies a minimum bound because the lineage must be at least as old as that occurrence; relaxed-clock models combine such bounds while accommodating rate heterogeneity among branches.[120] Calibration is the largest reported source of variation among molecular-clock dates, and its interpretation depends on the phylogeny because changing clade boundaries changes a fossil’s placement and dating role.[121][100]

This dependence is directly relevant to Cambrian Lagerstätten. Putative hemichordates from Chengjiang and the Burgess Shale were not used to constrain crown-Hemichordata because their affinities were not sufficient for that node; by contrast, the Chengjiang fossil Haikouichthys ercaicunensis was used as a 514 Ma minimum constraint for crown Chordata and crown Olfactores, with a 636.1 Ma soft maximum.[100] The same 636.1 Ma soft maximum was based on the absence of recognizable total-group eumetazoans in the exceptionally preserved Lantian and Doushantuo biotas.[100]

A Bayesian ecdysozoan time-tree study used 55 fossil calibrations and tested two relaxed-clock models, four matrix-partitioning schemes, four calibration densities and five phylogenetic hypotheses.[18] It estimated crown Ecdysozoa at 636–578 Ma, crown Scalidophora at 617–534 Ma, crown Panarthropoda at 616–562 Ma, and crown Arthropoda at 589–540 Ma.[18] It therefore supports a cryptic Ediacaran origin of parts of bilaterian history before a conspicuous terminal-Ediacaran–Cambrian diversification.[18]

Carlisle and colleagues’ 2024 molecular-clock analysis, incorporating revised fossil calibrations and newly designated Ediacaran crown-metazoan fossils, estimated crown Metazoa in the early Ediacaran at 613.2–593.4 Ma when averaged across analyses.[72] This younger estimate differs from dos Reis and colleagues’ 2015 relaxed-clock estimates of 833–650 Ma for animals and 688–596 Ma for bilaterians.[119]

Fossil calibrations depend on phylogeny: changing clade boundaries changes a fossil’s placement and its role in fixing divergence dates, so sponge-first and ctenophore-first topologies can place constraints on different branches.[100]

### Which competing explanations do phylogenomic findings support or complicate?

Phylogenomics supports the genetic-toolkit hypothesis most directly in its regulatory-complexity form. Comparative genomes indicate that much of the developmental gene repertoire predates bilaterians and even occurs in single-celled opisthokont relatives; Davidson (2010, Nature, 'Emerging properties of animal gene regulatory networks') and Erwin (2006, Science, 'Gene Regulatory Networks and the Evolution of Animal Body Plans')’s account therefore emphasizes changed spatial and temporal deployment of conserved genes, hierarchical developmental gene-regulatory networks, and co-option of existing circuits rather than wholesale Cambrian invention of protein-coding genes.[62][67][31][1] Hox/ParaHox-class genes are shared by bilaterians, placozoans and cnidarians, but Hox-related capacities and other signalling components predate many Cambrian body plans.[116][88]

This weakens a simple claim that a newly evolved Hox toolkit alone triggered the radiation. Developmental capacity was necessary in Davidson and Erwin’s formulation, but not sufficient: similar genetic components can be retained, redeployed, independently co-opted, or lost in different animal lineages.[31][1][8] The unresolved placement of sponges and ctenophores further changes whether neurons and muscles are inferred as single ancient innovations, repeated convergences, or ancestral traits subsequently lost.[116][114]

Phylogenomic timing complicates the environmental-trigger hypothesis if it is framed as one instantaneous Cambrian oxygen threshold causing animal origins: several deep lineages are inferred to have diverged in the Ediacaran or earlier. It remains compatible with oxygenation promoting subsequent ecological expansion or crown-group diversification near the boundary, because lineage origin does not measure abundance, ecological dominance or fossil visibility.[6][10][122] **Consequences for inherited developmental traits.** Sponge-first makes the lack of a gut, neurons, synapses and muscles in sponges the ancestral animal condition most parsimoniously; it makes the later origin of these traits compatible with Davidson and Erwin’s account of regulatory co-option and increasingly hierarchical gene-regulatory networks.[113][31][1] Ctenophore-first instead requires losses of complex traits in sponges and placozoans or independent origins in ctenophores and the cnidarian–bilaterian lineage, so it complicates any single inherited sequence from a simple ancestor to Cambrian complexity.[115][114] In both trees, Wnt and TGFbeta signalling, epithelia, obligate clonal multicellularity, and eggs and sperm are inferred for the last common animal ancestor; this supports a pre-Cambrian regulatory capacity rather than a Cambrian invention of an entire toolkit.[117][31]

**Consequences for predator–prey timing.** Ctenophores are voracious predators of other zooplankton, so ctenophore-first places a predatory living lineage at the earliest split; sponge-first does not.[1][113] Neither rooting alternative dates the Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') and Valentine predator–prey feedback mechanism: mapping feeding strategies onto a time-calibrated animal tree finds that the last common ancestors of Bilateria, Deuterostomia, Ecdysozoa and Lophotrochozoa were unlikely to have been carnivorous, and carnivory cannot be constrained confidently before crown-group Nemertea.[14][47] A tree and divergence dates identify when lineages existed; predator–prey arms races require evidence of feeding, predation and ecological interaction.[47][112]

Phylogenomic trees do not measure ancient oxygen concentrations or demonstrate predation. Testing an oxygen trigger requires geochemical proxy evidence, while testing ecological escalation requires fossil evidence such as predatory damage, repair scars, defensive skeletons, sediment mixing, mobility, feeding structures and community organization.

### Synthesis —

Deep-metazoan phylogenomics supports a pre-Cambrian ancestry for key animal lineages and a later Ediacaran–Cambrian clustering of many crown divergences, but it does not turn fossil first appearances into direct divergence dates. It strengthens the case that regulatory capacity preceded the Cambrian and that later co-option could generate morphological novelty, while ruling against a Hox-only explanation. It also limits a single instantaneous environmental trigger for all animal origins, without excluding oxygen and ecological feedback as drivers of later success. The principal disagreement—sponge-first sequence analyses versus comb-jelly-first synteny—still affects reconstructions of early nervous systems, muscles and the animal ancestor, although recent clock analyses find that alternative deep topologies need not greatly alter the broad timescale.[31][6][16][116]

## Where the explanations meet and what remains open

### What does each of the three hypotheses explain

The three explanations address different parts of the same historical problem. The environmental hypothesis asks when animal life became physiologically and geographically feasible; the genetic-toolkit hypothesis asks how animal bodies could generate new forms; and the ecological-feedback hypothesis asks how interactions among animals could intensify selection and diversification. They should therefore not be treated as three mutually exclusive accounts of one single switch.

**Environmental trigger — oxygen as permission and habitat.** Planavsky, Reinhard and colleagues used chromium-isotope signatures in sediments from China, Australia and North America to infer that mid-Proterozoic atmospheric oxygen was at most 0.1% of the present atmospheric level. They argued that such low oxygen probably lay below the requirements of early animals and could have delayed their emergence and diversification. That result identifies a long-standing environmental constraint, not a direct measurement of Cambrian oxygen.[123][52] A higher-oxygen world could support larger bodies, active locomotion, bioturbation—sediment mixing by animals—and muscular carnivory, while expanding oxygenated seafloor and continental-margin habitat.[44][45]

The environmental account explains why diversification may have become possible at a particular time, especially if oxygen or redox stability crossed thresholds important for active animals and carnivores. It does not explain how new body plans originated. Sperling and colleagues explicitly note that oxygenation can remove a barrier to evolution but supplies no intrinsic mechanism for generating new species or fundamentally new body architectures.[47][46] Moreover, Neoproterozoic oxygen did not simply rise in one direction: one reconstruction predicts oscillation between roughly 1% and 50% of present atmospheric level, and concludes that the first animals evolved amid extreme variability.[53]

Geochemical reconstructions also disagree about the severity and duration of oxygen limitation. Shennongjia chromium-isotope data are consistent with atmospheric oxygen above 1% of present levels; petrographic-carbon work places minimum estimates at 2–24% across a broad late Paleoproterozoic-to-Neoproterozoic interval and concludes that early eukaryote evolution was not likely oxygen-hindered over that interval.[124][125] Early Cambrian Siberian Platform records further show primarily dysoxic—oxygen-poor but not oxygen-free—water columns, with modest oxygenation events between about 527 and 519 Ma corresponding to regional increases in richness and habitat expansion, chiefly among endemic species.[126] **Oxygen therefore best explains changing constraints and opportunities, not a demonstrated global atmospheric trigger acting alone.**

Genetic toolkit — developmental capacity for morphological novelty. Davidson (2010, Nature, 'Emerging properties of animal gene regulatory networks') and Erwin (2006, Science, 'Gene Regulatory Networks and the Evolution of Animal Body Plans')’s hypothesis centres on developmental gene regulatory networks (GRNs): interacting regulatory genes, transcription factors and DNA control elements that translate genomic information into the spatial patterning of an embryo.[62][67] Because animal body plans are constructed by GRNs, changes in network architecture can alter major morphology; because GRNs are hierarchical, changes in different components can have effects ranging from terminal traits to large body-plan features.[67][127][63] Hox and ParaHox genes are prominent toolkit components: they are ancient developmental patterning genes that emerged before the cnidarian–bilaterian split, rather than innovations confined to the Cambrian.[59]

This hypothesis explains a route by which selectable variation in axes, segments, tissues and organ systems could arise without inventing entirely new protein-coding gene families for every new form. Davidson and Erwin identified deeply conserved GRN kernels—densely connected regulatory subcircuits—as especially resistant to change; other network components can be more readily modified, co-opted or redeployed.[67][128][63][64] Erwin and colleagues further argued that much of the protein-coding developmental repertoire arose before the sponge–eumetazoan split, with later expansions in some families near the base of Eumetazoa; the availability of this toolkit was necessary but insufficient for the Cambrian radiation.[2][31]

That temporal lag is decisive. A genetic toolkit already present in Cryogenian or Ediacaran ancestors cannot, by its presence alone, explain why large-scale ecological success became conspicuous in Cambrian rocks. Nor does shared Hox expression prove that the last common ancestor possessed the full later anatomy associated with a gene: deep genetic homology can be followed by independent co-option and convergent morphological evolution.[1][88] Davidson and Erwin proposed that major phylum-level differences required unusual changes in conserved developmental pathways, whereas Coyne (2006, Science, 'Comment on "Gene Regulatory Networks and the Evolution of Animal Body Plans"') argued that ordinary natural selection adequately accounts for the origin of phyla.[70] **The toolkit hypothesis supplies developmental possibility and a mechanism for changing form, but it does not date the relevant network changes or independently establish their Cambrian causal role.**

Ecological feedback — selection becomes self-reinforcing. Marshall (2006, Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences, 'EXPLAINING THE CAMBRIAN “EXPLOSION” OF ANIMALS') and Valentine’s ecological model emphasizes macrophagy, feeding on relatively large food items, predation, competition, ecosystem engineering and new niches.[14] In one formulation, macrophagy and increased benthic predation selected for larger bodies and eventually skeletons; predation also helped connect benthic seafloor communities with pelagic water-column ecosystems through the evolution of mesozooplankton.[76] In Marshall’s morphogenetic fitness-landscape framework, an existing developmental system contains possible forms, while ecological interactions alter which forms are successful.

Predators, prey, competition and trade-offs create more selective demands and more locally successful designs, thereby increasing disparity and diversity even without an immediate new developmental invention.[129][15]

The fossil record supports the existence of the ecological ingredients. Terminal Ediacaran *Cloudina* tubes contain drillholes interpreted as predatory attacks; some populations have about 20% drilled individuals.[23] Defensive prey structures, including punctae in mickwitziid brachiopods and wiwaxiid spines, occur at or just before the Cambrian base.[75] Chengjiang and Burgess Shale food-web reconstructions find complex trophic networks with broadly modern-like topology, while the two biotas record 30 life modes compared with 12 in the three compared Ediacaran macrofossil assemblages.[78][8][87]

Predation evidence does not by itself demonstrate that an escalating predator–prey arms race initiated the whole radiation. Fossil predation signals can be ambiguous, early predators were commonly soft-bodied or lightly skeletonized, and long-term morphology series that would document escalation remain scarce.[130][131][75][132] A genus-level Cambrian–Ordovician test found a negative, non-significant association between predator ratio and origination rate (correlation −0.346; permutation-test *p* = 0.773), although its time series comprised only seven data points.[80] Alternative accounts propose that early mineralization was initially advantageous for biomechanical and energetic reasons, with predator–prey arms races following the expansion rather than starting it.[133] **Ecology clearly reorganized animal communities and could amplify diversification, but the evidence does not make predation a singular initiating cause.**

Marshall and Valentine’s ecological account makes a stronger initiating claim than the evidence summarized above: they identified macrophagy as the most likely cause of the Cambrian Explosion, proposing that benthic predation selected for larger bodies and eventually skeletons, and that it also drove the evolution of mesozooplankton that linked pelagic and benthic ecosystems into the Phanerozoic ocean.[81] Marshall (2010) frames the same process as reciprocal feedback: claws, jaws, armour, spines and scalids enhanced crawling, swimming and burrowing; these innovations both recorded and increased the complexity of biotic interactions, exposing phenotypic possibilities already latent in animal genomes.[19]

Additional environmental timing evidence. Lyons, Reinhard and co-authors (2021) place an oxygenation shift around 800 Ma within a protracted Neoproterozoic Oxidation Event, conventionally framed as roughly 800–600 Ma. Chromium-isotope, selenium-isotope, iodine, sulfate and trace-metal records indicate changing redox conditions, including increasingly oxidizing ocean conditions from around 770 Ma and locally sustained surface-ocean oxygenation; the proposed event could have oxygenated the atmosphere, shallow sea and intermediate continental-margin waters while the deep ocean remained anoxic.[55] This adds a specific pre-Cambrian environmental history to the oxygen hypothesis: oxygenation may have changed the extent and distribution of habitable aerobic environments well before the conspicuous Cambrian fossil record.[55][134]

The same evidence makes the environmental account compatible with an ecological and developmental lag. Lyons, Reinhard and co-authors describe oxygen as low and unstable in many Proterozoic shallow waters, with oxygenated surface-water oases above generally oxygen-free bottom waters, and identify coeval more- and less-oxygenated regions as a recurring feature.[55] Early animals may have tolerated oxygen concentrations well below the traditional 1–10% present-atmospheric-level threshold, whereas changing oxygenated-habitat availability could still have selected for later energetic innovation and diversity.[55]

### Can the environmental trigger

Yes. The most coherent causal model assigns the hypotheses complementary roles: environmental change alters what animals can physiologically do and where they can live; developmental regulation provides the capacity to construct heritable new forms; and ecological interactions select, diversify and stabilize successful forms. Marshall’s synthesis similarly assigns environmental evolution a principal role in constraining onset, developmental innovation a major role in duration, and the interaction of developmental potential with ecological demands a role in producing disparity and diversity.[14][135]

An oxygen-to-ecology pathway is especially plausible. Modern oxygen-minimum-zone comparisons show that animals can persist at very low oxygen, but carnivorous polychaetes generally do not; carnivore abundance and diversity increase with oxygen.[122][136] Sperling and colleagues consequently proposed that rising or stabilizing oxygen enabled active muscular carnivores, after which predator–prey arms races could generate the rapid increase in animal diversity and disparity.[47][46] This is not merely an oxygen account with predation appended: it identifies a physiological bridge between a changing environment and an ecological source of selection.

Developmental evidence fits this sequence because it does not require Hox genes or most developmental gene families to originate during the Cambrian. Existing regulatory components could be assembled into new networks, co-opted for new roles, and selected within a changing fitness landscape.[2][1][47][46] The same logic applies to biomineralization: inherited organic scaffolds and genetic capacities could be independently co-opted in several lineages, while environmental chemistry lowered costs and predation later favoured more controlled defensive skeletons.[137][45][138]

The causal arrows may also run both ways. Oxygenation may have expanded habitable space and supported active animals, while suspension-feeding sponges, planktonic animals and burrowing animals could alter carbon, nutrient and oxygen cycles in ways that promoted or stabilized oxygenation.[45] One alternative specifically proposes that early filter-feeding animals disrupted cyanobacterial redox stratification and thereby promoted marine oxygenation.[6][16] **The compatible interpretation is a feedback system, not a one-way chain from oxygen to genes to predators.**

The mechanisms nevertheless need not have carried equal weight everywhere. The high-oxygen model that invokes active carnivory differs from the Siberian Platform interpretation, which finds that high oxygen was not required and instead attributes diversification to modest oxygen pulses, persistent low oxygen, and cycles of geographic isolation and expansion.[47][13] Ecological feedback can therefore be an amplifier in some habitats and intervals without implying that the same oxygen level or ecological sequence governed all Cambrian basins.

### Does the evidence support a genuinely rapid radiation

The best-supported answer is elements of both: a real, geologically rapid radiation occurred, but preservation, sampling and stratigraphic framing sharpen its apparent abruptness. The radiation was not instantaneous. Fossil and trace evidence supports a progressive two-stage pattern, with terminal Ediacaran to early Cambrian Stage 2–3 records dominated by stem groups—extinct relatives outside the living group’s crown—and definitive crown-group representatives becoming conspicuous later.[35][11] On the Siberian Platform, skeletal stem lophotrochozoans appear by about 542.5 Ma; crown gastropods appear around 537 Ma, crown brachiopods and bivalves around 535 Ma, and crown molluscs and brachiopods begin radiating around 522.5 Ma.[11]

The ecological transition was also staged. A broad behavioural repertoire and many body plans appeared by the Fortunian, but the major agronomic revolution—replacement of microbial-mat seafloors by bioturbated sediment ecosystems—occurred in Cambrian Stage 2, about 529–521 Ma. By Stage 3, about 521–514 Ma, complex metazoan food-web structure is evident at Chengjiang, dated near 518 Ma.[40][8][87] These patterns support genuine diversification and ecological transformation over tens of millions of years, rather than a single boundary event.

Butterfield’s taphonomic argument matters because taphonomy is the study of how remains decay, are buried and become fossils. New skeletons certainly improved fossilization, and Burgess Shale-type soft-tissue preservation depended on special local conditions, including proximity to an oxycline—an oceanic depth zone with a sharp oxygen gradient.[3][94] Yet exceptional preservation is not a sufficient explanation for the biological pattern.

Burgess Shale-type biotas are dominated by non-biomineralizing taxa, implying that animal diversification was not simply an artefact of the acquisition of hard parts.[23] Chengjiang itself shows taxon-selective preservation: among 11 studied animal groups, the proportion preserving internal organs was unrelated to generic diversity (*p* = 0.6294) or specimen abundance (*p* = 0.9298).[12] **Preservation filters alter which anatomies are visible, but they cannot reduce the independently recorded expansion of forms, behaviours and ecological roles to a preservational illusion.**

Molecular-clock results reinforce the distinction between evolutionary origin and fossil first appearance. Bayesian relaxed clocks estimate divergence dates from molecular sequences while allowing evolutionary rates to vary among branches; fossil calibrations impose age constraints on nodes in the tree. Revised calibration work estimates crown Metazoa at 613.2–593.4 Ma, crown Eumetazoa at 590.7–578.2 Ma, and Bilateria at 581.8–569 Ma, while many crown phyla remain estimated near the Ediacaran–Cambrian transition.[16] In that analysis, the Metazoa-node minimum of 593.4 Ma predates the approximately 574 Ma *Charnia* record by roughly 19 million years, and several major bilaterian clades require 20–40 million years of inferred prehistory before their known fossils.[6]

Those revised dates are markedly younger than older strict-clock estimates exceeding 900 Ma, illustrating that fossil identity, calibration minima and maxima, and rate models alter the inferred timescale.[16] The record therefore supports an extended Ediacaran–Cambrian diversification history, but not a uniform hidden history of identical length for every clade.

Stratigraphic analyses further challenge a single traditional Cambrian boundary narrative: the Siberian diversity record rises and falls repeatedly, including an early Stage 4 turnover near 513 Ma rather than one uninterrupted increase.[11] **The Cambrian Explosion is best understood as a real but structured radiation whose fossil onset is sharper than the origins of all its constituent lineages.**

The Chengjiang fauna, about 518 Ma old, and the Burgess Shale fauna, about 508 Ma old, reveal soft-bodied lineages and lifestyles that an ordinary hard-part record misses. Their exceptional preservation makes this diversity visible, but the expansion of trace fossils, sediment mixing, feeding modes and structured communities shows that the radiation was not solely a fossilization effect.

The conventional Cambrian base is defined at Fortune Head by the first appearance of the trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum*. Challenges to the global synchrony and facies independence of this datum mean that a boundary-centred timescale can compress a geographically and environmentally staggered diversification sequence into an apparently sharper event.

### What open problems would settle the competing explanations?

Four linked tests would most directly resolve the debate.

1. **Date divergences against stratigraphy.** Fossil calibrations remain a major source of relaxed-clock uncertainty, because a calibration requires defensible fossil placement and justified age bounds. New analyses should test whether crown clades truly originated tens of millions of years before their first diagnostic fossils or whether some older molecular estimates reflect calibration choices. [16]

2. **Map oxygen locally and globally.** Marine redox is spatially variable, influenced by circulation, nutrients and local oxygen demand; shallow-marine sediment records are not straightforward measures of atmospheric oxygen. Integrated fossil and geochemical sections across multiple palaeocontinents can test whether oxygenation pulses consistently preceded habitat expansion and diversification. The Siberian study explicitly calls for comparable records outside that region. [43][139][13]

3. **Measure preservation rather than assume it.** Facies-matched sampling—comparing deposits formed in equivalent environments—combined with decay experiments, character reassessment and taphonomically corrected community analyses can distinguish true absence, local ecology, biogeography and non-preservation. The Chengjiang evidence already demonstrates that quality of soft-tissue preservation differs among groups in ways not explained by abundance alone. [12][94]

4. **Test ecological escalation and regulatory change as time series.** Predation requires repeated, stratigraphically ordered evidence for changing prey defenses, damage frequencies, predator diets and predator morphology; current studies call for more long-term data on shelly lineages. Developmental explanations require phylogenomic resolution of deep animal relationships and comparative functional tests of when GRN components, Hox/ParaHox systems and regulatory co-option arose. [132][80][21][116]

Deep phylogeny is central to this last test. Alternative placements of ctenophores, placozoans and Xenacoelomorpha alter reconstructions of which developmental and neural features belonged to the animal ancestor, whether certain genes were gained or lost, and how molecular-clock calibrations are distributed across the tree.[1][21][116] The unresolved question is therefore not simply which lineage branched first, but whether the inherited animal toolkit was already capable of producing later complexity or was extensively assembled independently in descendant clades.

Resolving whether *Treptichnus pedum* and other first-appearance datums are diachronous requires directly dated ash beds tied to fossil-bearing sections and stronger global stratigraphic correlation. This would establish how much of the apparent boundary pulse reflects the placement of the boundary itself.

Phylogenomic sampling and models that accommodate compositional heterogeneity and branch-rate variation can resolve the sponge-first versus ctenophore-first alternatives. The result determines which genes, neural traits and developmental capacities are reconstructed for the animal common ancestor and therefore changes the developmental inferences drawn from the genetic toolkit.

### Synthesis —

Environmental oxygenation, developmental GRNs and ecological feedback explain different causal levels of Cambrian change. Oxygen and redox dynamics could expand metabolically viable habitat; ancient but modifiable regulatory systems could generate new body architectures; and carnivory, ecosystem engineering and competition could amplify divergence once animals interacted in increasingly complex ecosystems.[14][47][13] The fossil record supports a genuine, geologically rapid and phased radiation, while selective preservation and the gap between lineage divergence and fossil recognition make its onset appear sharper than it was.[35][12][6][11] The strongest conclusion is consequently neither a literal instantaneous explosion nor a preservational mirage: it is an interacting Ediacaran–Cambrian cascade, with the relative timing and force of each mechanism varying among clades, habitats and basins.

### Open questions

The unresolved question is the relative causal weight and timing of oxygenation, gene-regulatory evolution, and ecological escalation in individual animal clades. Better globally correlated boundary sections, quantitative comparisons of Ediacaran and Cambrian preservation windows, securely placed early fossils, and molecular clocks calibrated with rigorously justified fossil constraints would discriminate more strongly among these explanations. Deep metazoan phylogeny also remains consequential: firmer resolution of the earliest animal branches and their divergence times would show whether the genetic capacities invoked for Cambrian innovation evolved well before the visible radiation or close to it.
