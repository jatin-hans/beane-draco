# Voice-Only and Hybrid Checkout for Blind and Low-Vision Grocery Shoppers

## Executive summary

Use a hybrid checkout: retain screen-reader touch with audio feedback for inspecting and committing product, quantity, and payment controls, and offer voice for search or longer text with an immediate structured fallback.

Explicitly speak the selected product, changed quantity, cart total, and authorization outcome, but require a distinct final activation after review rather than treating a spoken or touched value as confirmation.

For numerical quantities and payment-related values, expose an editable control with its current value, provide post-entry readback, and make correction local and reversible because speech-recognition errors and difficult correction are established risks.[1][2][3] Do not claim that voice-only or hybrid interaction has demonstrated superior completion, lower numerical-entry error, higher confidence, or lower cognitive load for blind or low-vision shoppers reviewing carts of 15 or more items before authorization.

The most applicable measured proxy favors audio-touch exploration with explicit activation for rapid access, while showing a control-confidence trade-off: Slide Rule was preferred by 7 of 10 blind participants but scored lower for feeling in control than button-based screen-reader interaction, 3.3 versus 4.7 out of 5.[4][5]

## The question and evidence landscape

Mobile grocery checkout combines a product-information problem with a consequential transaction problem. A blind shopper must be able to discover products, understand quantities and prices, move through a cart, and authorize payment without relying on an image-led layout or a sighted helper. The key open design question is not whether speech or touch can be made accessible in isolation, but how sequential voice commands and screen-reader-mediated touch with audio feedback behave across the same end-to-end grocery transaction.

**No identified study compares sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback workflows across mobile grocery product selection, quantity adjustment, and payment confirmation.** Millan, Phakamad, Rhodes, and Tipon evaluated desktop Instacart use, Kimura’s voice-based shopping task ended at cart addition, and the 2026 scoping review synthesizes 21 accessible e-commerce studies—14 direct and 7 adjacent—without establishing this mobile grocery-checkout comparison.[6][7][8]

A 2026 review uses “visually impaired people” for people with very limited or no functional vision, including blind people; totals labelled “visually impaired” therefore cannot always be split into blind and low-vision outcomes unless the original study reports that split.[63]

### What direct research exists on accessible mobile grocery checkout comparing

Millan, Phakamad, Rhodes, and Tipon’s November 2021 *Instacart - Accessibility Usability Evaluation of Grocery Delivery Website for the Visually Impaired* is the closest direct grocery-delivery usability study in the supplied material. It compared the experiences of four blind people and five sighted people using the desktop Instacart website. Participants logged in, found a store, searched for and added an item, and proceeded through checkout scenarios. The study concerned the desktop version specifically, not a native mobile app.[6]

That Instacart study is directly relevant because it covers the same consumer journey as grocery checkout: entering a service, locating a retailer, selecting an item, placing it in a cart, and moving toward checkout. Its interaction comparison is between blind and sighted users rather than between sequential voice-only interaction and hybrid touch with audio feedback.[6]

Kimura’s 2026 voice-based online-shopping agent work is closer to the requested voice-oriented product-selection pattern. Its task used a prepared mock account and included browsing, comparison, and adding the selected item to the cart. The task endpoint was cart addition; it excluded actual payment.[7]

Damm, Klutch, Osei, and Roman’s 2022 observational study examined four blind participants comparing products online with a screen reader. Participants selected a coffee maker as a gift rather than groceries, and the task investigated comparison shopping rather than a transaction flow.[9]

The 2026 scoping review *From Compliance to Decision Confidence* synthesizes 21 studies on accessible e-commerce for blind and low-vision shoppers and sellers, comprising 14 direct and 7 adjacent studies. It frames online shopping as encompassing activities including groceries, but it does not identify a controlled mobile grocery-checkout experiment that compares sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback workflows.[8]

The 2026 scoping review’s 21 studies were published from 2010–2025; its seven adjacent studies included work on voice assistants and checkout authentication.[8]

The direct grocery-shopping literature is stronger for product discovery than for checkout authorization. ShopTalk was a wearable system for visually impaired supermarket shoppers that used synthetic verbal route directions, store-layout descriptions, and barcode scanning to locate products on shelves. Two real-supermarket experiments included an earlier single-subject experiment and a later experiment with ten visually impaired participants; the authors report successful guidance to multiple shelf products in both experiments.[10]

ShopTalk’s interaction was neither a mobile voice-command checkout nor a touchscreen hybrid checkout. It combined a wearable computer, spoken instructions, a numeric keypad, maps, and barcode scans. In the earlier pilot, one blind guide-dog handler completed 16 shopping runs, each requiring travel to an aisle, location of three products, and travel to a designated cashier.[11]

The ShopTalk pilot provides a useful model of product-error recovery. In two of 16 runs, the participant entered an incorrect aisle; after scanning an item, the system announced the mismatch and supplied directions to the correct aisle. The participant located all products in all 16 runs and required an average of 4.2 barcode scans to locate each target product.[11]

Trinetra is another grocery-adjacent system rather than a mobile checkout comparison. Lanigan and colleagues’ 2006 prototype combined an Internet- and Bluetooth-enabled mobile phone, text-to-speech, and a portable barcode reader; its initial deployment and evaluation supported blind grocery shoppers in Carnegie Mellon University’s Entropy convenience store.[12][13]

Trinetra accepted barcode scans, RFID scans, or keypad events and returned synthesized speech. Its TALKS screen reader enumerated interface text and read the focused user-interface element aloud. These characteristics make it an early example of a hybrid shopping interaction in which physical input, spoken system output, and focus-based mobile navigation coexist.[14][13]

Trinetra also implemented staged feedback: it announced that input had been received, announced processing where required, and finally spoke either the product name and brand/type information or an item-not-found message. That staged pattern is directly relevant to the design of confirmation feedback because it distinguishes acknowledgement of input, system progress, and outcome.[13]

The Trinetra evaluation remained exploratory. The authors state that testing had been limited to one blind user who also advised the development team; in one test run, that shopper used the system to distinguish cookie packages and choose one.[13]

Several newer grocery-assistance systems address store navigation, product identity, and shelf retrieval rather than app checkout. VirtualEyez was described as a low-cost mobile grocery-shopping system that uses NFC tags, specifically NTAG 203 tags, to help visually impaired people shop independently. It conducted a preliminary mock-store study with visually impaired, partially impaired, and sighted participants; its prototype checked product availability, generated directions, and provided product information.[15]

GroZi proposed an audio alert when a shopper reached a selected product and then product confirmation through barcode scanning or object recognition, with a spoken indication of whether the item was correct or wrong. It used distinct sounds for correct and incorrect product confirmation.[16]

A 2022 grocery-assistant system compared a scanned product’s barcode with the shopping-list item. When the barcode mismatched, it gave return instructions; when it matched, it announced that the product was correct and told the shopper the required quantity.[17]

These systems show a consistent physical-shopping pattern: identify the target, collect input, check the result against the intended item, then announce a recoverable mismatch. They do not test quantity editing in a mobile cart, payment confirmation, voice-only checkout dialogue, or touch-with-audio checkout controls.[16][13][17]

The commercial landscape includes a limited mobile checkout example. A September 2019 Retail Customer Experience report described 365 Retail Markets’ 365Pay app as using iOS VoiceOver and Android TalkBack to read aloud selected items and permit checkout for visually impaired micro-market customers. The report describes an accessible transaction path but gives neither participant outcomes nor a voice-command comparison.[18]

A 2022 assessment of 12 supermarket apps used Apple VoiceOver and included test participation by a very visually impaired VoiceOver user, who conducted 8 of the 12 tests. It is a field-oriented accessibility assessment rather than a controlled experiment of alternative modalities.[19]

The assessment found that Albert Heijn’s app supported product searching and cart addition through VoiceOver. It read the full product name, price, and weight in one sequence, and search suggestions were available during text entry.[19]

The same assessment found that speech ordering was not a dependable substitute for screen-reader operation. In the Jumbo app, a speech-ordering feature was offered but unusable at the time of testing. In the Plus app, activating microphone search stopped VoiceOver, and the Dirk app similarly silenced VoiceOver when voice search opened.[19]

**For the requested comparison, direct research reaches product selection only indirectly: desktop grocery usability, voice-mediated cart addition, and screen-reader comparison shopping each address portions of the journey, but none measures the complete mobile grocery flow under both sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio conditions.**[6][7][9][8]

### What adjacent shopping or accessibility research can inform the accessible

Adjacent evidence is valuable when its task mechanics match a checkout mechanism. Product selection relies on finding and disambiguating an item. Quantity adjustment relies on entering or changing a number. Payment confirmation relies on detecting an error, preserving control, and making a consequential commitment explicit. Touchscreen interaction, speech recognition, screen-reader navigation, text entry, product identification, and payment-terminal access therefore provide useful—but bounded—design evidence.

Lazar, Allen, Kleinman, and Malarkey’s 2007 *What Frustrates Screen Reader Users on the Web: A Study of 100 Blind Users* examined frustrating web interactions reported by 100 blind screen-reader users. It is adjacent screen-reader usability evidence, not a controlled comparison of voice-only and touch-with-audio grocery-checkout workflows.[22]

A recent interview sample of legally blind adults included participants with no vision or minimal light perception and others with acuities of 20/300, 20/400, 20/3,000, and 20/10,000.[120]

Park, Han, and Oh’s 2020 smartphone-shopping study, *Understanding Smartphone-based Online Shopping Experiences and Behaviors of Blind Users*, identifies the mobile context directly. It states that smartphones provide blind users with screen readers but that image-based and inconsistent online-shopping layouts make access difficult.[23]

The BarrierFreeShop user study involved 80 participants with visual impairments who used the application for one month. The authors report effectiveness in terms of speed and post-interview feedback, and they report that extracting content from images improved access to product information.[23]

BarrierFreeShop used layout automation and review summarization in its mobile shopping application.[8]

BarrierFreeShop matters for grocery design because grocery products are often differentiated by packaging, unit size, price, flavor, dietary label, and promotion rather than by a single product name. The study points to image-content extraction as a means of converting visually embedded product information into content a screen reader can expose during selection.[23]

Wang, Chen, Zhang, and Li’s 2021 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems paper *Revamp: Enhancing Accessible Information Seeking Experience of Online Shopping for Blind or Low Vision Users* addresses a related information-access problem.[24] It identifies inadequate image descriptions and difficulty filtering large amounts of information with screen readers as barriers for blind and low-vision online shoppers. Its browser integration uses customer reviews to support question answering on a reconstructed product page.[24]

Revamp’s evaluation with eight blind and low-vision participants found that the system provided useful descriptive information about product appearance and helped participants locate key information efficiently. The study concerns online product research rather than grocery cart management or payment confirmation.[24]

VisionAI was iteratively co-designed through weekly meetings with one blind, proficient screen-reader user; it accepts typed or dictated queries and relies on the device’s screen reader rather than separate text-to-speech.[121]

In VisionAI, a barcode scan produces an auditory success signal, opens product information and can supply the search query. Its authors describe reduced user input and errors but report no error count.[121]

Prakash, Nayak, Sunkara, and Jayarathna’s 2024 *All in One Place* describes another selection burden: product information spread across multiple sections and pages forces blind screen-reader users to navigate back and forth. InstaFetch generates an alternative screen-reader-friendly interface that brings description, specifications, and reviews together and supports natural-language item queries.[25]

InstaFetch’s query-response accuracy was 48.4% across 40 examined participant queries. When answers were inaccurate, participants rephrased the query in 64.7% of cases and returned to other interface segments for manual search in 35.3% of cases.[26]

That recovery pattern has a direct design implication for voice-assisted product selection: a failed spoken answer should not trap the shopper in repeated dialogue. The interface needs a visible-to-assistive-technology route back to structured product facts, filters, and alternatives. This is an inference from users’ observed rephrasing and manual fallback behavior.[26][25]

Voice-mediated shopping can make product discovery faster or less effortful for some tasks, but it introduces speech-recognition and discoverability dependencies. A 2025 qualitative retailing study interviewed 30 online shoppers—15 voice-assistant users in Nigeria and 15 in the United Kingdom—and identified convenience, companionship, literacy support, and anthropomorphism as motivations for voice-assistant adoption. Participants also developed trust and personal attachment despite challenges.[27]

Those retail voice-assistant participants were general online shoppers rather than blind or low-vision users. The findings therefore frame voice commerce as a consumer interaction model, not as evidence that voice commands reliably support nonvisual selection, numerical adjustment, or payment authorization.[27]

A 2023 review of mobile text-entry work explains why speech cannot be treated as a frictionless replacement for touch. It finds that mobile tools for visually impaired and blind people commonly rely on audio feedback and speech-to-text, sometimes supplemented by haptics, and identifies noisy environments and privacy in public settings as drawbacks of speech and audio dependence. It also identifies virtual-keyboard entry as desirable for practicality and privacy.[28]

In an in-store shopping session, a blind participant said speaking was easier than typing while holding a product, phone, and cane because typing required both hands; supermarket noise also led the system to mishear or misinterpret requests, requiring repetition and rephrasing.[122]

Gaines and Vertanen’s 2025 interviews with 12 legally blind adults provide a user-centered account of that trade-off. Speech dictation was the most commonly used mobile text-input method, followed by an onscreen keyboard used with a screen reader.[2]

In a 2013 study of eight blind people using an iPod, speech input was nearly five times as fast as onscreen keyboard entry with VoiceOver. A survey of 169 blind and sighted people found that blind respondents used speech input more often and entered longer messages with it.[123]

All ten participants who reported dictation as a primary or secondary method mentioned speech-recognition accuracy problems. They attributed errors to background noise, accents, uncommon words, and speech artifacts such as coughs or hiccups.[2]

Participants described speech as advantageous for entering substantial amounts of text, but they preferred the keyboard for shorter content such as Google or Amazon searches. They also reported that generated-text errors were difficult to correct and that repeated recognition errors could lead them to abandon voice input and switch to the keyboard.[1]

This distinction matters for grocery checkout. Product search may tolerate a voice command followed by candidate selection, because the shopper can inspect results. Quantity and payment values are compact, high-consequence inputs for which correction cost can outweigh the speed benefit of speech. This is a design inference from the reported voice-entry and correction behavior.[1][2]

Speech-recognition review research reinforces that caution. In a study of 1,920 utterances, Google’s recognizer had a 15% word error rate; word error rate was 17% for challenging phrases and 12% for common phrases.[3]

The same work reports that users correctly located 49% of recognition errors overall: 2% of insertion errors, 49% of substitution errors, and 62% of deletion errors. A cited earlier study found no statistically significant difference in speech-recognition error identification between blind users at 42% and sighted users at 38%.[3]

Selective slowing of synthesized speech when the recognizer was uncertain produced 85% error-detection accuracy, compared with 76% when all recognition output was slowed. It yielded a 12% relative improvement in detection and reduced listening-and-decision time by 11% compared with uniformly slow playback.[29]

The selective-slowing result did not significantly outperform normal-rate playback: 85% versus 80%, with p = 0.1. It supports uncertainty-sensitive spoken review as a candidate safeguard, particularly where the system recognizes a quantity, price, address, or payment-related value.[3]

Hybrid touch-with-audio interaction has a different strength: it lets the user spatially inspect a control before activating it. Slide Rule, developed by Kane, Bigham, and Wobbrock at the University of Washington’s AIM Research Group, used touch gestures as input and speech/audio output without visual feedback or physical buttons.[30][4]

In Slide Rule, a user scans a list with one finger, hears a newly encountered object’s name and summary, then selects that already announced object by holding the first finger in place and tapping elsewhere with a second finger. This creates an exploration–activation separation: touching a target does not itself commit an action.[5][4]

That separation is also characteristic of platform screen readers. With screen-reader software enabled, touching an item can speak its description without activating it because the screen reader changes the device-control gestures. VoiceOver and TalkBack both use touch-or-drag exploration to hear items, swipes for sequential navigation, and double tap to activate selected content.[31][32]

Observed VoiceOver navigation strategies include sweeping a finger across the display and dragging down its centre while listening for elements.[124]

Kane, Bigham, and Wobbrock’s 2008 Slide Rule study involved ten blind participants completing contact dialing, email reading, and music-playing tasks on Slide Rule and on a button-based Pocket PC running Mobile Speak Pocket. Participants completed five trials for each task on each device, with task and device order randomized.[20][4]

Six of Slide Rule’s ten participants classified as blind had some residual vision, and its results were reported for the group rather than separately by vision level.[20]

Six of the ten blind participants in the Slide Rule study had residual vision.[20]

Slide Rule was significantly faster than the button-based comparator and was preferred by 7 of 10 participants. Participants made more errors with Slide Rule, while the Pocket PC had no errors under the study’s strict error definition.[33][5][34]

In a separate BrailleTouch evaluation, audio feedback was held constant across smartphone-touchscreen, tablet-touchscreen, and physical Braille-keyboard conditions; the comparison therefore varied input technique rather than audio output.[125]

The study’s “felt in control” rating illustrates why preference and speed cannot be treated as the same outcome. On the 5-point scale, Pocket PC scored 4.7 (0.48), compared with 3.3 (1.16) for Slide Rule; the difference was significant in a Wilcoxon test, z = −2.35, p < .05.[5]

Participants nevertheless preferred Slide Rule’s rapid and non-linear access. Seven of ten chose it overall, even though they described the Pocket PC as more familiar and somewhat easier to use. Some participants explicitly suggested combining Slide Rule’s quick access with the accuracy of button-based interaction.[4][5]

The important comparison for a grocery app is therefore not “touch versus speech” in the abstract. It is “rapid non-linear access with explicit audio-confirmed activation” versus “serial navigation with a familiar and more controlled action model.” Slide Rule’s comparator was a button-based Pocket PC system running the Mobile Speak Pocket screen reader, so it supplies a hybrid-touch design proxy rather than the requested modality result.[5]

Slide Rule’s list-navigation observations map especially well to large grocery catalogues. Pocket PC users stepped through lists from the first item and were less likely to make errors or miss items. Slide Rule users could jump non-linearly toward an intended target, but they sometimes skipped items or searched randomly.[5]

The mean listening time per item was 0.95 seconds with Slide Rule and 1.42 seconds with the Pocket PC, a statistically significant difference, F(1,8) = 68.88, p < .001. This was a list-scanning measure rather than a cognitive-load measure, but it shows how a non-linear audio-touch technique can reduce serial listening time.[20]

In Roussille’s nonvisual six-item word-list study, 12 blindfolded participants tested five touch interactions with audio feedback; mean selection times ranged from 7.4 to 9.9 seconds, and mistaken-item rates ranged from 3% to 10%. The participants were blindfolded, so these are not blind or low-vision grocery-selection error rates.[126]

Touch accessibility depends on accommodating blind users’ movement patterns. Kane and Wobbrock’s 2011 gesture work found that blind participants preferred gestures using edges, corners, and multi-touch. Many used a second finger or hand as a mode switch and chose areas corresponding to familiar QWERTY-key locations.[35]

The same study found that blind participants’ gestures were larger, slower, and more variable than sighted participants’ gestures. Blind participants took 925.90 milliseconds on average to perform tested gestures, compared with 462.58 milliseconds for sighted participants, and their average offset from specified tap targets was 110.97 pixels compared with 48.84 pixels for sighted participants.[35]

The authors therefore recommend using screen edges, corners, and other landmarks; increasing target size; and permitting approximate initial targeting followed by finger exploration. They also advise against unnecessary time-sensitive gesture processing because differences in gesture pace can create additional errors.[35]

A 2023 target-selection study with 13 legally blind participants gives a practical touch-target range. Its authors recommend targets 12–18 mm wide for blind users; smaller targets produced multiple overshoots, longer movement time, and higher error rates.[36]

The target-selection study separates three audio states that a checkout designer should retain: spoken direction or guidance, notification that a finger has reached a target, and feedback that a target has been selected. A confirmation action is not equivalent to merely reaching a control.[36]

FetchAid provides the most directly applicable hybrid proxy beyond Slide Rule. Guan, Xiong, and Fan’s CHI 2024 system is a standalone mobile app for blind and low-vision people operating parcel lockers. It detects the user’s fingertip and relevant touchscreen buttons, guides QR-code scanning and compartment opening, then uses context-aware audio feedback to guide the user to the correct physical compartment.[37]

In a study with 12 blind and low-vision participants, FetchAid substantially improved task accomplishment and efficiency and reduced frustration and overall effort regardless of vision condition or prior experience. The system concerns parcel retrieval rather than grocery checkout, but its interaction problem closely resembles finding an uncertain touchscreen control, understanding whether the action succeeded, and recovering from a mismatch.[37]

In an Android object-recognition study, eight blind participants and 18 blindfolded sighted participants trained on versions with and without vibration before locating three objects among three to five objects; audio feedback remained available when vibration was disabled. Blind participants rated the vibration-enabled version more highly, including an approximately 18.34% increase in usefulness responses.[127]

The Food-delivery implementation literature provides operational context. Kumar (2025, American Journal of Technology, 'Accessibility First Design Systems for Multi-Brand Food Apps')’s 2025 study, *Accessibility First Design Systems for Multi-Brand Food Apps*, evaluated a shared component library across multiple restaurant brands within one food-delivery application.[38] The library incorporated screen-reader compatibility, adequate color contrast, and optimized touch target sizes.[38]

Kumar reports a 35-percentage-point increase in accessibility compliance, a 33% reduction in abandonment, and a 17% increase in order completion after implementation. These are application-level outcomes rather than blind- or low-vision-specific task results, but they show that accessibility-first components can accompany commercial conversion improvements.[38]

**Adjacent evidence supports a multimodal architecture rather than replacing one modality with another: speech is useful for search and long text, screen-reader touch provides spatial inspection and explicit activation, and structured fallback routes are necessary when recognition or automated answers are wrong.**[1][2][26][5][37]

Azenkot, Prasain, Borning, and Wobbrock’s 2012 ASSETS paper 《Exploring the Use of Speech Input by Blind People on Mobile Devices》 identifies recognition uncertainty, the need to communicate what the system heard, correction burden, and circumstances in which speaking commands is undesirable. It informs requirements for voice-command recovery and non-speech correction, rather than establishing comparative grocery-checkout completion, numerical-error, confidence, or workload outcomes.

### How have screen-reader navigation

Screen readers are not merely audio output. They create an alternate interaction model in which controls must expose a programmatic name, role, state, and value; focus must move predictably; and actions must be announced at a time that lets the user act on them. In a mobile grocery app, this determines whether a shopper can locate a product, understand the cart, modify a quantity, and know whether payment has been authorized.

Screen-reader software reads smartphone content aloud and supports touch-based gestures, enabling eyes-free use. VoiceOver is the default iOS screen reader and TalkBack is the default Android screen reader.[1][31]

VoiceOver and TalkBack share basic concepts but not a single universal gesture vocabulary. Both allow touch or drag exploration, left/right swipes for navigation, and tap/double tap selection and activation. TalkBack was described as having around a dozen specific navigation gestures, many involving right-angle swipes, while VoiceOver was described as having more than 20 gestures involving different numbers of fingers and tap counts.[32]

Platform variation matters because a grocery checkout should not require shoppers to learn a retailer-specific gesture language. Kane, Wobbrock, and Ladner noted that VoiceOver and Android’s Eyes-Free Shell used different layouts and interaction primitives, with no lingua franca for blind touchscreen users.[39][35]

Expert blind Android users demonstrate that screen-reader touch is commonly hybrid rather than purely serial. In a four-week logging study of eight experienced TalkBack users in India during May–August 2020, participants had an average of 8 ± 2.9 years of TalkBack experience and more than five years of touchscreen use.[1]

The users employed Explore by Touch 104,526 times across 209 logged days, including 56,433 text-entry events. They used single-item exploration 61,123 times and multiple-item exploration 43,403 times; the former was significantly more frequent, t(7) = 2.6, p < .05, which the authors interpreted as primarily target acquisition rather than broad exploration.[1]

Participants described a practical recovery sequence: explore by touch to target a remembered item, double tap if the target is reached, and use a quick next/previous gesture if they miss. This sequence combines spatial memory with sequential repair rather than forcing either purely direct touch or purely serial navigation.[1]

The study also reports a learning burden. Participants said that they learned TalkBack through experimentation and took 3–6 months to become comfortable using it.[1]

For a checkout designer, that learning period means familiar platform behaviors should carry the critical flow. Retailer-specific gesture conventions create avoidable relearning during transactions that may involve money, identity, delivery, substitutions, or time-sensitive slots. This is an inference from the reported platform variation and learning experience.[35][1]

Transaction-centric navigation can be especially demanding because screen readers present content serially. Islam, Ahmed, Borodin, and Mahmud’s 2010 work on transaction-centric web objects describes online transactions as spanning multiple pages and requiring users to find essential clickable objects such as add-to-cart buttons. It states that blind screen-reader users experience strain because they must listen sequentially through irrelevant information and have no dedicated interface for identifying transaction-critical controls.[40]

Online-shopping research identifies a related burden. Revamp reports that blind and low-vision shoppers struggle with inadequate image descriptions and filtering large information volumes through screen readers. InstaFetch identifies back-and-forth navigation among dispersed product information as tedious and cumbersome.[24][25]

A grocery app can reduce this burden by presenting the information needed for a product decision as one coherent accessible unit: product name, size or weight, unit price and total price where applicable, dietary or substitution state, selected quantity, and add/change action. That is a design inference from the reported burden of dispersed and fragmented information, together with the supermarket-app observations that product information can be announced either coherently or in confusing fragments.[25][19]

The 2022 supermarket-app assessment illustrates both outcomes. Albert Heijn allowed VoiceOver users to search, receive suggestions, hear product name, price, and weight together, and add a searched product to the cart.[19]

Jumbo similarly read product information as a whole, including name, price, and weight. Dirk instead announced product information as separate fragments, including product name, a number, price, and volume.[19]

Numeric presentation is a checkout concern even before a shopper enters a quantity. Coop’s app read €1.80 as “80 and 1,” which the assessment says caused confusion. EkoPlaza broke monetary values into separate euro and cent components, and the coexistence of old and promotional prices made it unclear which amount was current.[19]

Screen-reader confirmation must also report the effect of an action. In Coop, testers received no feedback indicating whether a product had been added to the cart. DekaMarkt similarly provided no feedback after a product was added to the shopping list.[19]

Quantity controls expose another implementation issue. Albert Heijn allowed the user to adjust the number to add by swiping up on the quantity. Hoogvliet permitted screen-reader users to decrease product quantity but not increase it.[19]

These observations make quantity adjustment a transaction control rather than merely a cart display field. Both increase and decrease must be discoverable, named, operable, and followed by a spoken update that identifies the affected item and the resulting quantity. This is an inference from the observed one-directional quantity access and absent cart-add feedback.[19]

Navigation recovery is equally important. In several apps, including Ekoplaza, the VoiceOver zigzag gesture for returning to a previous screen did not work; testers could reach a dead end and could not return. The Deen app was described as unusable with a screen reader, and completing its shopping list was virtually impossible in the assessment.[19]

A reliable “Back,” “Cancel,” “Edit cart,” and “Return to product” route should therefore be available as explicit accessible controls rather than relying solely on a platform gesture. This is a direct design conclusion from users reaching inaccessible navigation dead ends.[19][41]

WCAG 2.2 provides a transaction-relevant baseline but does not itself prescribe a checkout dialogue. W3C’s understanding material defines a process as a series of actions required to complete an activity and uses online shopping as an example involving product selection, order submission, shipping information, and payment information.[42]

The practical implication is that grocery checkout should be treated as one accessible process, not as a collection of individually accessible screens. A shopper who can read a product card but cannot locate an add control, recover from quantity validation, or return from a payment-provider view does not have an operable transaction. This is an interpretation of WCAG’s process concept and the observed transaction-navigation barriers.[42][40][19]

WCAG’s error-identification principle is particularly relevant to numerical and financial inputs. Error feedback needs to identify that an error occurred and describe the error in a form that can be programmatically exposed to assistive technology. The mobile implementation must connect the error message to the affected input, return focus predictably, and preserve the entered values that remain valid.Judgment — This is the practical accessibility pattern for an error state: identify the field, explain the constraint, retain recoverable data, provide a direct edit action, then announce the corrected state before the user resumes checkout.

WCAG 2.2, published in 2023, specifies web-content accessibility success criteria rather than measured task-completion, input-error, or user-confidence outcomes for grocery checkout.

The evidence on mobile financial accessibility makes the consequence clear. A 2026 scoping review of 23 studies published from 2011 to 2026 found recurring barriers in authentication and one-time passwords in 18 studies, assistive-technology compatibility in 17, and navigation in 15. Screen readers were the most frequently described assistive technology; TalkBack appeared explicitly in six studies, VoiceOver in five, and both in four.[43]

Within that 23-study financial-accessibility review, PIN-entry difficulties appeared in 7 studies and inconsistent or inaccessible error messages in 8 studies.[128]

The review characterizes the financial-accessibility evidence as small, geographically concentrated, and dominated by cross-sectional and qualitative studies. It identifies a disconnect between technical design literature and end-user experience research and reports an absence of longitudinal or intervention-based evidence.[43]

A participant in the expert smartphone study described avoiding trial and error in Paytm because accessibility issues could lead to financial loss. The participant sought sighted help to label buttons rather than experimenting with consequential controls.[1]

This account supports conservative interaction design at payment: a checkout must make the selected payment method, total, delivery fee, discounts, authorization state, and final action unambiguous before any irreversible submission. The required interaction cost is justified by the cost of an accidental or uncertain financial action. This is a design inference from the participant’s avoidance of trial and error and the financial-accessibility barriers.[1][43]

The mobile implementation guidance itself is evolving. W3C’s *Guidance on Applying WCAG 2.2 to Mobile Applications (WCAG2Mobile)* is an informative Group Draft Note dated 6 May 2025; it describes application of WCAG 2.2 to native, mobile web, and hybrid apps but does not set normative requirements.[44]

For low-vision shoppers, its resizing guidance recommends that app text resize to 200% without loss of content or functionality, or to the platform’s supported text-sizing capability where that platform cannot achieve 200% for all displayed text. This affects cart verification and payment confirmation because totals, unit prices, quantity values, fees, and authorization prompts must remain legible at enlarged text sizes without truncation or hidden actions.[45]

Accessibility conformance is not equivalent to usable checkout. A review of accessible e-commerce notes that the European Accessibility Act applies to e-commerce services from June 2025 while stating that compliance does not ensure usability. A separate study cited in a mobile-app accessibility thesis recorded 1,382 user-problem instances across 16 websites and found that WCAG 2.0 success criteria covered 50.4% of the problems; on 16.7% of sites, some guideline-covered problems remained despite use of WCAG 2.0 techniques.[46][41]

Manual testing is necessary because automated tools cannot determine whether an error is announced at the right moment, whether focus works in a payment modal, or whether a real purchase can be completed. A mobile-checkout accessibility article recommends real-device testing with VoiceOver and TalkBack and sessions with assistive-technology users in addition to audits.[47]

**Screen-reader navigation and WCAG-based implementation together make hybrid touch-and-audio checkout feasible, but they make predictable labels, structured information, explicit state changes, and recoverable transaction controls non-negotiable.**[1][31][40][19][42][47]

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 3.3.4, Error Prevention (Legal, Financial, Data), Level AA, applies to financial transactions: before final submission, the shopper must be able to reverse the submission, have data checked with an opportunity to correct errors, or review, confirm, and correct the information. This makes a revisit-able cart and an explicit pre-authorization review-and-correction step essential for payment confirmation.

WCAG 2.2 also adds Success Criterion 3.3.7, Redundant Entry, Level A, requiring information already supplied in the same process to be auto-populated or selectable except where re-entry is essential, security-required, or no longer valid; reuse does not remove the shopper’s need to verify the value. Success Criterion 3.3.8, Accessible Authentication (Minimum), Level AA, limits authentication steps based on cognitive-function tests, while 3.2.6 Consistent Help, 2.5.7 Dragging Movements, and 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) affect predictable assistance and operable cart controls.

Cart-review and payment layouts therefore need to preserve both enlarged visual readability and complete nonvisual semantics.

### Synthesis

Direct grocery evidence covers accessible desktop delivery, physical product retrieval, and screen-reader supermarket-app use; it does not measure a mobile grocery checkout under both sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio conditions. The adjacent evidence nevertheless gives a clear design direction. Voice is valuable for product search and substantial text input, but recognition errors, noise, privacy, and difficult correction make it unsuitable as the sole control channel for consequential values. Screen-reader touch supports direct spatial targeting, inspection before activation, and explicit selection, but needs adequately sized controls, platform-consistent gestures, and serial-navigation shortcuts. Grocery and transaction studies converge on an action-state pattern: acknowledge input, expose the resulting value or cart state, identify mismatches specifically, and provide a direct correction route. WCAG 2.2 conformance supplies the baseline for operability and error communication; blind and low-vision usability depends on testing the full transaction with actual carts and real payment states.[1][2][5][36][13][17][42][47]

## Product selection

### How do sequential voice-only workflows versus hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback patterns compare

Product selection has two distinct operations: finding or naming the intended product, and committing that product to the cart. Voice can reduce the effort of expressing a known request; touch-with-audio can let the user inspect, locate, and deliberately activate a specific result. The important design question is therefore not whether speech or touch is universally better, but which channel carries search, disambiguation, and final commitment safely.

Judgment — **No controlled study directly compares a sequential voice-only mobile grocery-selection workflow with an integrated hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback workflow.** The closest product-selection study is robot-assisted shopping: five blind and five blindfolded sighted participants selected products from a database of 11,147 household products by browsing, keypad typing, or speech. Speech was fastest, at a mean 37.5 seconds per selection, versus 74.1 seconds for typing and 85.5 seconds for browsing; speech was significantly faster than both alternatives. That finding supports voice for a known-item query, not for inspecting a long grocery-result list or safely committing a selected variant.[48]

The speech interface constrained recognition to one spoken word at a time, used press-to-talk, and used recognition alternates to form possible queries. These measures were explicitly intended to reduce false positives and recognition errors. Yet participants reported speech-recognition errors as a source of frustration; although speech was fastest, nine of ten participants said they would prefer typing if no hybrid interface were available, chiefly because typing was more discreet in a public supermarket.[48] Voice-only product search should therefore be an efficient entry route, but not the sole route for resolving an uncertain recognition or reviewing alternatives.

In a real-supermarket study with one blind participant, voice commands reduced typing while the participant’s hands were occupied, but repeated misrecognition and rephrasing interrupted shopping.[122]

In a one-participant supermarket session, the blind participant preferred speaking to typing while holding a phone, product and cane; supermarket noise nevertheless caused repeated mishearing and misinterpretation, prompting repeated or rephrased requests.[122]

This distinction appears in present mobile use. Expert blind smartphone users reported using Google Assistant because it was faster for selected tasks such as web searches, calls, messages, opening apps, and reminders, although the study did not log those commands. The same users reported that voice input was faster for lengthy text but that generated-text errors were difficult to correct; for short searches on Google or Amazon, they preferred the keyboard.[1] In supermarket-app assessments, voice search could also interrupt the screen reader: enabling microphone search in the Plus app stopped VoiceOver, and the Dirk voice-search function similarly silenced VoiceOver and did not work.[19] A voice command that suppresses the channel needed to hear search state, alternatives, and corrections is unsuitable as the only selection path.

Hybrid touch-with-audio patterns provide a different interaction structure. In Slide Rule, a blind user scans a list with one finger, hears the item name and summary beneath that finger, and then selects the already-announced target by tapping anywhere with a second finger. This is an explore-then-commit pattern: touching an item does not itself activate it.[20][4] Screen-reader touch exploration similarly lets users glide across controls while hearing labels, whereas repeated next/previous swipes serially access targets and become slow for a long list.[36] The pattern makes product-result inspection spatial rather than entirely command-based, while retaining an explicit activation step.

The closest controlled mobile comparison shows a speed–accuracy trade-off rather than an unconditional hybrid advantage. In the 10-blind-participant Slide Rule study, mean task time across calling, email, and music tasks was 11.69 seconds (SD 5.77) with Slide Rule and 12.79 seconds (SD 7.58) with the button-based Pocket PC screen-reader system; Slide Rule was faster for six participants. However, Slide Rule users sometimes accidentally selected items, while Pocket PC users made no selection errors.[4][5] The comparison is not voice versus touch and not grocery shopping, but it demonstrates a relevant mechanism: direct nonvisual touch can accelerate target access while increasing accidental commitment risk.

That risk is particularly relevant to product-result lists. Pocket PC users moved sequentially from the first item and were less likely to miss an item or make an error. Slide Rule allowed non-linear jumps toward an intended target, but users sometimes skipped items or searched randomly.[20][5] In contrast, four of five experienced screen-reader users in a touchscreen-question study preferred linear left/right swiping because it felt more reliable for reaching every element.[49] Hybrid product selection should therefore retain both direct exploration and a sequential next/previous route; the latter is a verification control, not merely a legacy navigation mode.

In a non-commerce experiment with blindfolded participants selecting from six-item word lists, five touch interaction types yielded selection times of 7.4–9.9 seconds and wrong-item rates of 3%–10%; the grid condition had the highest wrong-item rate, 10%.[126]

In a four-item word-selection comparison with blindfolded participants, prior audio feedback reduced the mean number of items browsed from 3.7 to 2.0; selection time was 5.6 seconds with prior audio versus 5.3 seconds without it, while wrong-item rates remained 2% in both conditions.[126]

Physical-grocery studies reinforce the importance of explicit identity feedback before commitment. In the seven-participant VizWiz::LocateIt study—four totally blind and three low-vision participants—users searched for a desired cereal box among 15 unique boxes. Mean completion time was similar for audio-guided LocateIt, 92.2 seconds (SD 37.7), and a talking barcode scanner, 85.7 seconds (SD 55.0). Yet the scanner identified the correct box in every case, whereas LocateIt found the correct box on the first try in 12 of 21 cases and on the second try in seven more; the authors summarize LocateIt as comparable in time but more error-prone.[50] Time alone is thus a poor criterion for product selection: a user needs the system to speak the discriminating identity—product name, brand or variant, size or weight, and price—before add-to-cart.

Product information must also reflect the current selected state. In mobile e-commerce testing, a screen reader gave no indication that a selected clothing size was selected, and revisiting the options did not announce a selected state. In another reported case, changing color updated the visual display but left the product title and image description spoken as the original color.[51] For groceries, an add-to-cart confirmation that says only added is inadequate when variants differ by pack size, flavour, dietary attribute, or price.

Touch controls require nonvisual targeting tolerance. In a 13-legally-blind-participant target-selection experiment, targets at 12 mm or narrower produced relatively more overshoots, while targets at 18 mm or wider had comparatively fewer; the authors recommend widths of 12–18 mm. The interface announced both reaching the target and whether it had actually been selected, and announced Passed the Target so users could move back after an overshoot.[36] Separately, 36 visually impaired participants preferred simple one-finger, one-stroke gestures: 22 preferred one finger, 19 preferred one stroke, and the overall perceived gesture-difficulty mean was 1.49 on a five-point scale where 1 meant very easy.[32]

**Design implication — Speech has a measured speed advantage for a known-product request in robot-assisted shopping, while Slide Rule demonstrates non-linear audio-touch access with accidental-selection and missed-item trade-offs in other mobile tasks.** A proposed grocery-checkout combination is voice search followed by inspectable audio-touch results and deliberate activation: speech expresses the known request; touch exploration announces candidates; and a separate activation commits the chosen product. The proposal retains sequential next/previous review because sequential navigation reduced errors and missed items in the Pocket PC comparison.[48][4][5]

1. **Use voice for search, not irreversible selection:** accept a press-to-talk product query, announce the recognized query, and offer edit, retry, and touch-result alternatives before searching.
2. **Use touch exploration for result choice:** speak the full discriminating product identity under focus, then require a separate, simple activation gesture to add the item.
3. **Expose sequential verification:** provide next/previous result navigation and a repeat-current-result command so users can check that no candidate was skipped.
4. **Confirm the committed variant:** after add-to-cart, announce the exact selected product, variant, package size, quantity of one, price, and cart state; make the selected state persistently discoverable.
5. **Design recovery into the control:** use sufficiently large targets, announce reached versus selected states, report overshoots, and provide an immediate Undo add action.

In a simulated-supermarket study of 15 visually impaired and 15 sighted participants, the visually impaired group took longer to recognize products, identified them less accurately, and had more difficulty navigating, retracing steps and maintaining spatial orientation.[63]

For a voice-led search, the 2026 CHI voice-based computer-using-agent study adds specific information-presentation requirements. Participants preferred a limited option set, with one participant identifying around three choices as easiest to remember; they wanted product name, brand, price, rating, and review count first, rather than all available detail at once. Participants described that double-checking as reassuring and as reducing the perceived chance of error.[55] This supports a voice result sequence of two or three spoken candidates, each with name, one distinguishing detail, and price, followed by a refinement turn that retains accumulated constraints.[56]

Voice selection also requires an explicit repair route. In the National Federation of the Blind telephone-shopping demonstration, an initially unsuccessful spoken query returned irrelevant results; reformulating it as Meta Ray-Ban smart glasses produced relevant Ray-Ban Meta results.[57] The AssemblyAI e-commerce implementation identifies short replies such as yes and ten as recognition-sensitive and supplies the assistant’s immediately preceding question as recognition context for short confirmations, sizes, and spelled-out entities.[56] A press-to-talk query should consequently be read back, with retry, reformulation, and touch-result navigation available before any product is added.[56][57]

Hybrid access is strengthened when each product card carries its identity as a coherent unit. Accessibility.com’s screen-reader checkout protocol specifies one focusable product card announcing title, price, and availability together, while keeping Add to cart as a separate interactive element; this avoids requiring users to reconstruct the relationship among six separately announced elements.[58] In the National Federation of the Blind’s Innosearch web demonstration, JAWS announces product results as heading-level links; the narrator moves between results by heading and can reach controls to add an item to the cart or buy it immediately.[57] WCAG input provisions reinforce this architecture: WebAIM’s WCAG 2 checklist states that content must support alternatives to a required input type and that non-essential multipoint or path-based gestures must also be available through single-point activation.[59] Mobile-interface elements must expose role, state, boundary, name, description, and available actions programmatically to assistive technologies.[60]

Audio feedback must track the user’s current touch position. In a TalkBack touchscreen text-entry study, 64% (SD 9.8%) of week-eight substitution-error paths entered the intended key boundary but selected the wrong key; video inspection associated many of these errors with delayed speech feedback mismatching the key currently touched. Over the eight weeks, mean visited keys per keystroke declined from 4.9 (SD 1.9) to 2.0 (SD 0.3), while mean keystroke movement time declined from 4.1 seconds (SD 1.4) to 1.9 seconds (SD 0.3).[61] For touch exploration, immediate focus speech, a distinct committed-selection announcement, and a separate Add to cart control directly address this timing mechanism.[61][58]

Adjacent grocery systems show viable multimodal divisions of labour rather than a single-input workflow. BlindShopping lets a user invoke navigation by drawing L or saying Location and invoke recognition by drawing P or saying Product; it then combines verbal guidance with camera recognition of embossed QR or UPC codes and spoken product features. In its one-participant basic usability study, the blind participant described RFID-mark navigation as intuitive and vocal navigation commands as useful.[62] A 2020 system used voice input for category selection and tactile embossed tags plus a smartphone camera for product identification; its seven visually impaired participants achieved 85% product-identification accuracy under optimal imaging conditions.[63] Barcode Speak combines camera scanning, voice synthesis, virtual-cart add, update, and remove functions, voice-guided purchasing, and audio product confirmation before purchase.[63] These systems support assigning voice to request formulation and audio-supported physical or touch interaction to identity verification and commitment.[62][63]

VisionAI plays a sound when it detects a barcode, then opens product information and uses the scanned product identity as a search query.[121]

Sequential screen-reader navigation remains valuable as a stable review path but imposes serial listening. The 2023 screen-reader study characterizes screen-reader access as linear and sequential, and reports that users fall back to sequential navigation when shortcuts and keyword search fail; participants nevertheless described keyboard interaction as more efficient than voice in some contexts and anticipated conversational support for shopping and image questions.[64] Participants also preferred dynamic conversational speech rates while identifying that faster speech could increase errors.[64] Product-selection interfaces should therefore let users control speech rate and repeat the current result rather than accelerate a long spoken list.[55][64]

Product information should include grocery-specific attributes that would otherwise be trapped in images: expiry date, ingredients, nutritional values, and allergens.[53][63] A simulated smart-supermarket evaluation found that visually impaired, blindfolded, and sighted participants valued product details and customer reviews and requested those attributes, tactile markers, more accurate localisation, smaller devices, and RFID tags on individual products; participants also reported difficulty operating the RFID reader and navigating accurately.[63]

Recent design work adds several voice-led product-discovery architectures. CeeUI proposes that a voice assistant describe shopping-centre structure, areas, nearby and new products, shelf contents, and product information; a user can ask which buttons are available and instruct the system to press a button. It also proposes voice assistance for setup and for reading descriptive text aloud.[65] GaBuy is described as a “multimodal sensory-integrated (MSID)” assistive device that combines text-to-speech with streamlined product identification.[66] Packaged varieties can be indistinguishable by touch, while camera- and barcode-based identification requires correct alignment, is affected by product-design changes, and requires contact with the product.[65]

A follow-up RoboCart experiment compared browsing, typing, and speech recognition with five blind participants and five sighted participants who were blindfolded. Participants rated speech the most efficient and least cognitively demanding method, and rated browsing the most frustrating and least effective.[63]

## Quantity adjustment

### How do sequential voice-only workflows versus hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback patterns compare

In a sequential voice-only workflow, the user proposes an item and numeral by speech, receives acknowledgment of the interpreted item, options, and quantity, then receives a separate announcement that the quantity has been added to the cart. Blind voice-shopping-agent participants wanted this double-check and described it as reassuring and as reducing the chance of a mistake.[69] Voice-only quantity adjustment needs an Edit or Cancel route after acknowledgment and before application, followed by spoken confirmation of the quantity actually stored in the cart.

Expert blind smartphone users reported that errors in voice-generated text were difficult to correct and preferred keyboard input for short Amazon and Google searches.[1]

In a hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback workflow, the user proposes a quantity through an increment/decrement control or numeric field, hears the item and entered or stepped-to numeral, then hears the separately applied cart quantity and revised total. The acknowledgment of an entered or recognized numeral is distinct from the announcement that the cart has stored that value: mobile-commerce testing recorded quantity changes that remained unregistered when users missed an Update action or proceeded before a focus-loss update occurred.[70] Mobile shopping observations found controls that could not be activated with a screen reader, and one supermarket app allowed quantity decreases but not increases.[51][19] Hybrid controls need directly operable increment/decrement actions and an editable numeric field, with an Edit or Cancel route before commitment; after commitment, they need to announce the resulting quantity and revised total without requiring a separate Update action.

In a UPI payment usability study, 15 visually impaired smartphone users were tasked with transfers of Rs.3, Rs.3, and Rs.4 through different routes. Reported accessibility failures included a screen-reader announcement merging distinct fields and a login field ambiguously announced as both password and mobile-number input; an experienced participant inferred the field from its numeric keypad but warned that a less experienced user might require help or multiple attempts.[129][130]

In supermarket-app observations, one screen-reader user could decrease a product quantity but could not increase it.[19] Baymard found buttons efficient for one-step adjustment and found that an accompanying open text field gives another route for larger quantities.[70] General mobile-commerce testing found that users overlooked Save/Update steps and that focus-loss updating could leave quantity changes unregistered even when users proceeded to submit an order.[70] WCAG Status Messages guidance similarly says immediately perceivable dynamic updates need dynamic announcement.[71]

For multi-digit quantities, buttons plus an editable numeric field offer two recovery paths. Baymard found buttons efficient for one-step adjustment and recommends immediate cart-summary and total updates, while allowing a 200–300 ms delay before auto-update for double-digit typing.[70] Its testing also observed replacement-entry errors such as unintended 21 and recommends selecting the existing value on focus.[70]

Confirm the item and interpreted quantity together.[69][55][56]

Voice recognition and product-reference ambiguity differ from touch-control targeting and state-feedback failures; comparative error rates are unknown.[56][69][55][70][72]

Judgment — Hybrid controls have an error-reduction design advantage, not a measured grocery-checkout modality advantage. Fixed, enlarged numeric keys, paced spoken labels, familiar keypad structure, large targets, and simple one-finger gestures make numerical changes easier to locate, inspect, undo, and repeat; voice remains a useful alternate command path where privacy, noise, and recognition conditions permit.[72][28][73][39][32][68]

1. Provide both spoken commands and screen-reader-operable quantity controls.
2. Speak the item name, new quantity, and revised total after every committed change.
3. Provide immediate decrement/increment, editable numeric entry, and an explicit Remove action rather than requiring quantity zero. [74]

AssemblyAI’s voice-commerce tutorial states that “Short utterances carry almost no acoustic context” and supplies the assistant’s latest question as `agent_context` before every reply, so a one-word “ten” or “yes” is interpreted as an answer to that question; it identifies the greatest effect for short confirmations, sizes, and spelled-out entities.[56] Because short spoken answers such as quantity numerals can be hard to recognize and voice-entry errors can be difficult to correct, contextualizing the prompt and repeating the interpreted item and quantity before applying the change can help users detect a misrecognition.[56][55][1]

AssemblyAI’s mocked cart returns `insufficient_stock`, the available quantity, and variants with sufficient stock when a requested quantity exceeds stock; its proposed agent behavior is to say that a variant is out of stock and offer what is in stock.[56] When a requested quantity exceeds stock, reporting the available stock and alternatives gives users information they can use to revise the quantity rather than leaving the constraint unexplained.[56][75]

A quantity control should expose its current value and limits to assistive technology, and announce dynamic quantity and total changes, so users can perceive both the value they are editing and the result of a committed change.[71][75] In the November 10, 2023 W3C WCAG discussion, a commenter said that the current form-field value—the number of products—should be perceptible with a screen reader, identified a spinbutton (`role=spinbutton` or `input type=number`) as the best fit, and said that a +/− implementation is not optimal because its value cannot automatically be perceived.[71] The DeveloperUX checklist says quantity controls must expose labels, values, selection states, and minimum and maximum limits; add-to-cart, quantity, and remove controls must identify their product and variant; and changed quantities, stock, and recalculated totals must be announced without unexpected focus shifts.[75] A WooCommerce remediation case reported that custom quantity steppers and variant swatches had no programmatic name or state and were addressed after triage of purchase-blocking issues.[76]

The W3C answer posted April 13, 2026 in the Status Messages discussion says dynamic updates or content changes that are immediately obvious or perceivable to a sighted user who is not using screen magnification “need to be announced dynamically under 4.1.3.” WCAG2ICT’s application of WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 sets a 24 by 24 CSS-pixel minimum pointer-input target size, except when an exception applies.[71][68] TestParty’s May 12, 2026 mobile-checkout article identifies quantity stepper buttons as a common failure, states that the 24×24 CSS-pixel floor has exceptions for inline targets and genuinely space-constrained small UI elements, and recommends increasing padding and adopting the iOS Human Interface Guideline 44×44 minimum for mobile targets.[77] Larger, adequately spaced quantity targets address the small-target problem identified in checkout audits and reduce the risk of mistaken taps and retries.[77][78][79]

The CNR keypad prototype reports that smooth touch screens lack tactile reference points: participants regarded locating options and using a numeric keypad as particularly worrying, and said they relied on the marked 5 key to orient themselves while editing a number.[72] The authors of the blind-gesture study recommend reproducing familiar spatial layouts, specifically noting that QWERTY keyboards and telephone keypads are instantly familiar to many blind people.[39] Fixed, enlarged keypad positions, a tactile landmark such as the marked 5 key, and paced spoken number labels can jointly help users orient on a keypad and distinguish adjacent numeric choices.[72]

Baymard observed that long, high-maximum quantity dropdowns caused participants to struggle to target their desired quantity and says dropdowns require dedicated dexterity to manipulate.[70] Buttons support efficient one-step quantity changes, while long dropdowns can make the desired value difficult to target, so a stepper with direct numeric entry offers alternatives to dropdown selection.[70]

Model grocery quantity as a constrained state rather than free-form numeric entry where possible, validating range, stock, and unit rules before committing the change. After a committed change, provide Undo that restores the prior quantity.

## Payment confirmation

Judgment — Payment authorization is a safety-critical action: the user must know what will be charged, deliberately authorize it, and know whether it succeeded. The relevant comparison is therefore not speech versus touch speed, but whether each route supports independent verification, private input, deliberate commitment, and recovery when recognition or navigation fails.

### How do sequential voice-only workflows versus hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback patterns compare

No controlled grocery-checkout study reports payment-confirmation completion, unintended authorization, correction or cancellation, or confidence scores for sequential voice-only versus hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback. A 2026 scoping review of digital financial applications similarly identifies minimal end-to-end transaction evidence and no adequate evaluations of feedback mechanisms, transaction safety, or recovery.[43]

A simulated mobile-money comparison reported transaction success of 65–75% with traditional screen-reader navigation versus more than 90% with its proposed voice-driven system; reported transaction times were 40–60 seconds and 12–15 seconds, respectively. Its voice workflow accepted a recipient and amount, validated the input, handled PIN entry, and returned audio feedback, but did not test grocery carts.[131]

Sequential voice-only confirmation provides hands-free, eyes-free control. Voice-command interfaces are useful for spoken-language control, but background noise, language differences, and speech-recognition quality affect success. Blind smartphone users also reported that voice-produced text can be difficult to correct; for short searches they preferred the keyboard. Spoken output and speech input can expose passwords, amounts, or decline messages to bystanders, especially when headphones are impractical.[73][1][28] Voice should therefore be an available authorization route, not the sole route for a consequential purchase.

Research on voice-based authentication reports concerns that spoken payment information could be overheard or recorded and reluctance to say passwords aloud; reported trust in voice biometrics varies with transaction size and repeat purchasing.[132]

Hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback has the stronger payment-adjacent implementation precedent. French and UK terminal patterns let a customer explore a blanked virtual keypad by finger slide, receive beeps and vibration at key transitions, and double-tap to confirm; the French pattern also offers high-contrast modes and adjustable text for partially sighted users.[80] Financial-app evidence identifies audio feedback, haptic confirmation, and clear error messages as confirmation-stage enablers.[43] **This pattern gives the user both local orientation during input and a separate, deliberate commitment action.**

A voice-driven money prototype used text-to-speech for transaction results and haptics for state feedback.[131]

For either modality, the authorization must remain non-final until the shopper has reviewed and can edit the order and total. WCAG 2.2 technique G98 provides review and correction before submission; G168 requests confirmation before continuing; G164 provides a stated post-submission amendment or cancellation period; and G199 provides success feedback.[81] This matters because payment-terminal users report that an amount notification delivered only after payment, without an itemised receipt for comparison, is not an independent pre-authorization check.[80]

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 2.5.6 says content must not restrict platform-available input modalities except for essential, security, or user-setting reasons.[67] Audio should be user-activatable rather than mandatory: UK Finance notes privacy concerns about speaking amounts or declines aloud, while busy environments can make audible cues inaudible.[82] A status announcement should communicate accepted or declined payment and, where applicable, direct the user into PIN recovery; a blind Instacart participant reported having to search the post-checkout page because completion was not announced.[82][6]

1. Provide an editable pre-authorisation review of items, delivery, fees, discounts, and total, followed by an explicit Pay/Authorize action. [83][80]
2. Offer speech and screen-reader/touch routes, with optional haptic confirmation and private authentication such as accessible biometrics. [67][82]
3. Announce each recoverable failure, preserve entered data, offer re-entry or cancellation, and explicitly announce accepted or declined status. [82][43]

Recent assistive-shopping evidence reinforces that independent price verification is a payment-confirmation requirement. The assistive-technology review identifies checkout price verification as essential to trust, financial control, and consumer protection, and reports that visually impaired people can have limited means to detect a discrepancy between a previously supplied price and the amount charged. An app-based payment flow can present the purchased items, their individual prices, and the amount to be charged on the user’s own device, rather than requiring reliance on a cashier or companion.[63][84]

Voice-commerce implementations operationalise a sequential confirmation gate by stating the item count and total before asking to place an order, rejecting an ambiguous phrase, and re-prompting until an accepted confirmation is received. They also recommend speaking a price as words—for example, “one hundred thirty-nine dollars,” rather than “139.00”—and permit interruption that stops current text-to-speech output and cancels the pending reply.[56] AssemblyAI reports a 14.4% entity error rate, 5.19% word error rate, 10.0% codes/IDs error rate, and 2.4% phone-number error rate for Universal-3.6 Pro Realtime on its 12,460-scenario English voice-agent benchmark; the corresponding entity-error rates reported for Deepgram Flux EN, ElevenLabs Scribe v2, and Deepgram Nova-3 are 30.1%, 18.5%, and 26.1%.[56]

Screen-reader/touch confirmation is not merely touch input: TalkBack and VoiceOver support touch exploration, sequential swipe navigation, and double-tap activation. Payment controls therefore need programmatically determinable names, roles, states, boundaries, descriptions, and available actions so assistive technology can expose and, where security permits, execute the action.[85][60] This supports a hybrid pattern in which the shopper explores the amount, payment method, and authorization control through touch with spoken feedback, then activates a separately labelled commitment control.[85][86]

Voice-shopping research has concentrated on product queries and adding products to a virtual cart rather than final purchase decisions; it reports that voice shopping is used less frequently to complete transactions, indicating reluctance at the final buying stage.[87] The 2026 voice-based computer-using-agent shopping task used a prepared mock account and ended when the participant added the selected product to the cart; it did not include actual payment.[7]

The Instacart accessibility usability evaluation reported mean checkout completion times of 9:41 for blind participants and 2:11 for sighted participants; for finding a store, searching for an item, and adding it to the cart, the respective means were 8:34 and 1:20.[6] In that evaluation’s 1-to-5 difficulty scale, where 1 was easiest and 5 most difficult, the four blind participants rated checkout 2, 2, 4, and 5; none rated any task 1, and three rated the store/item task very difficult.[6]

A bare spoken confirmation such as Place order is not an adequate authorization pattern unless the shopper has first been able to inspect the cart, substitutions, promotions, delivery address, payment method, and final total. Let voice commands request a summary, navigate directly to a discrepancy, or invoke correction, rather than making serial spoken review the only way to inspect the transaction.

Keep correction controls adjacent to the associated cart section and announce accessible updates when a price, fee, substitution, or authorization state changes. Permit cancellation or return to editing until the authorization is transmitted, then report the outcome and the next available recovery action.

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 3.3.4 requires financial submissions to be reversible, checked with an opportunity to correct, or reviewed and confirmed before they become final.[47]

### What input error frequencies

No reported result supplies a blind- or low-vision-participant error rate for spoken product selection, numerical quantity adjustment, or payment-field entry in either requested checkout modality. Self-checkout research identifies accessibility barriers among 86 surveyed BLV respondents and 10 contextual inquiries, but does not publish numerical error frequencies.[89] An informal RNIB survey found that 49 of 75 respondents said they could not enter a PIN on a touchscreen Chip and PIN device; that is an access-barrier count, not a PIN error rate.[82]

The closest blind-participant hybrid input comparison is a speech-feedback text-entry study of 15 blind users. Its reported average minimum-string-distance error rates were 15.6% for MultiTap, 10% for NavTap, and 4% for NavTouch.[90] The same paper separately reports session values of 47%, 60%, and 71% for MultiTap; 17%, 6%, and 4% for NavTap; and 27%, 17%, and 14% for NavTouch.[90] Those summaries are not reconciled in the supplied results, so the stable finding is that error performance differs materially by nonvisual touch-entry technique rather than that one percentage generalizes to checkout.

Blind users’ speech-input risk is clear in adjacent work. All ten interview participants who used dictation reported speech-recognizer accuracy problems, including errors linked to background noise, accents, uncommon words, coughs, and hiccups.[2] A related speech-recognition study reports that blind users identified 42% of recognition errors, compared with 38% for sighted users; the difference was not significant.[29] Recognition alone therefore cannot function as quantity or payment confirmation.

In an audio-only speech-input review task involving 12 blind screen-reader users and 12 sighted participants, blind participants correctly identified 42% of speech-recognition error instances in short trials and missed 52.1%; their error-identification recall fell to 0.25 for longer messages. Errors resembling the intended words were harder to identify, and participants identified number-format and other formatting errors in only two of four instances.[134]

Text-entry studies also show why final-value accuracy must be separated from correction burden. In an eight-week study of five novice blind touchscreen users, total character-error rate fell from 26% (SD 11.7%) in week one to 7.4% (SD 1.7%) in week eight, while uncorrected errors were 1.6% (SD 1.4%) in week eight.[91] Participants corrected 98.4% of their errors, but correction consumed about 13% of typing time.[91] Omission errors are especially hazardous: 68% went undetected and uncorrected.[91]

For low-vision interaction, Tilt-Explore reduced accidental taps in a visual-search task from 3.72 (SD 4.17) with baseline gestures to 0.15 (SD 0.20) per task, a significant difference.[92] This demonstrates that interaction design can sharply reduce unintended activation, although it is not a quantity-entry measure.

General mobile-commerce testing identifies three consequential quantity failures: users can append a replacement digit to an uncleared existing value, make a typo that requires deletion and re-entry, or overlook a separate Save/Update action so the order retains the old quantity.[70] Mobile quantity controls should therefore measure invalid values, omitted values, out-of-range values, corrected errors, uncorrected errors, total error rate, and correction time.

WCAG2ICT defines an input error as 《information provided by the user that is not accepted》, including required information omitted by the user and information outside the required data format or values.[68] Mobile text-entry research defines corrected, non-corrected, and total error measures separately.[28]

### Synthesis —

The measured pattern is practical rather than modality-deterministic. Product retrieval and identification can reach complete success in bounded assistive tasks, but independent grocery checkout completion has not been quantified for either sequential voice-only or hybrid touch-with-audio workflows.[11][50][6][43] Nonvisual touch methods can reduce final text-entry errors, while speech recognition leaves errors that users do not reliably identify; both modalities therefore require explicit value verification rather than assumed correctness.[90][2][29][91] Confidence improves when users can understand and cross-check product information, but preference, familiarity, and felt control can conflict.[93][20][46]

1. Treat every quantity and payment value as provisional until the interface presents an independently reviewable confirmation.
2. Log corrected and uncorrected numerical-entry errors separately, alongside completion, recovery time, and perceived control.
3. Test the two workflows with blind and low-vision users using independent 15-plus-item cart verification and payment authorization, rather than inferring checkout performance from product-finding or text-transcription tasks.

## Confirmation and error recovery by evidence stream

A checkout confirmation is not merely a message that a control was activated. For a blind or low-vision shopper, it must establish the current cart state, preserve a way to inspect and amend it, and distinguish a reversible edit from the irreversible authorization of payment. Error recovery likewise must return the shopper to a known state with the failed value, explanation, and a usable correction route still available.

### What confirmation strategies does evidence from WCAG 2.2 implementation case

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion (SC) 3.3.4, Error Prevention (Legal, Financial, Data), applies to financial transactions. It requires at least one of three safeguards: reversibility; input checking with an opportunity to correct; or a mechanism to review, confirm, and correct information before final submission. A grocery order authorization is such a transaction because it creates a financial commitment. The direct checkout pattern is therefore an accessible review of basket contents, quantities, delivery or collection details, price total, and payment method, with edit controls before the final authorization action.[67][83]

The standard does not require a specific confirmation modality. A confirmation dialog, a screen-reader-operable review screen, an accessible voice-confirmation step, or a reversible order are all potentially compatible with the criterion. **Implementation example.** The Digital Policy Office’s before-and-after example replaces forced order placement with an opportunity to confirm and change details before final submission.[83] WCAG techniques additionally include review-and-correction before submitting, requesting confirmation for a selected action, success feedback after submission, and a stated cancellation or amendment period after a transaction.[81]

Status feedback has a separate function from final authorization. WCAG 2.2 SC 4.1.3, Status Messages (Level AA), requires status messages in markup-based content to be programmatically determinable through roles or properties so assistive technologies can present them without receiving focus. For non-web software, WCAG2ICT says that status messages must be programmatically exposed so assistive technologies can present them without receiving focus, typically through accessibility services of the user agent or other platform software. W3C’s shopping example is directly relevant: after Add to Shopping Cart, a screen reader announces the updated item count, such as “Shopping cart, five items,” without moving focus. In a mobile grocery app, this supports feedback such as “Organic bananas added. Cart: 12 items. Total $43.70,” while the shopper remains on the product page.[94][67][68]

A blocking dialog is different. If it takes focus, it changes context and is outside the narrow definition of a status message; it must instead preserve orientation through correct focus placement, a usable dismissal method, and a predictable return point. Changes of focus, viewport, or content can disorient a user who cannot inspect the whole screen at once.[94][68] Transaction dialogs should therefore place focus on their heading or first meaningful content when opened, keep focus within the dialog while active, and return focus to the invoking control if dismissed.[95]

**The WCAG-supported confirmation architecture is two-layered: announce routine cart and validation state without stealing focus, then require a deliberate, editable review before payment authorization.** This separates low-risk awareness from high-consequence commitment.[94][67][83]

For native mobile software, WCAG 2.2 SC 4.1.2 requires each user-interface component’s name and role to be programmatically determinable; user-settable states, properties, and values to be programmatically settable; and changes to those items to be available to user agents, including assistive technologies. WCAG2Mobile identifies platform accessibility services as the usual best-practice implementation route. Do not rely on a visual badge, changing colour, or an animation as the only evidence that quantity, delivery method, or total changed.[44][68]

WCAG conformance establishes accessible mechanics, not a measured outcome of confidence, completion, or safety. The 2026 accessible-e-commerce scoping review explicitly separates decision-confidence outcomes, such as time-to-confidence and reliance on sighted assistance, from WCAG conformance.[67][46]

This means that a conformant confirmation pattern makes independent review possible; it does not establish that a dialog, spoken readback, or audio-haptic prompt is the universally most usable implementation.

In a WooCommerce remediation case, WCAG World reported 41 open WCAG errors with six weeks to Black Friday, triaged issues by whether they blocked purchase rather than by audit order, and reported zero open errors by the week before launch after keyboard testing of the checkout.[76] The case addressed custom quantity steppers and variant swatches with no programmatic name or state after the higher-priority purchase-blocking issues.[76]

A home-goods-store before-and-after test reported that successful order completion among screen-reader users rose from roughly 40 percent to over 90 percent after checkout accessibility fixes; overall checkout conversion rose about 6 percent as error messages became clearer.[96]

### What confirmation strategies does mobile-commerce usability research suggest?

Mobile-commerce research supports a distinct review-before-authorization stage. The review must make each line item, quantity, subtotal, discounts, taxes, fees, shipping, delivery choice, payment method, and final total easy to find and edit. NN/g recommends placing the order summary prominently near the top of mobile checkout pages because charges lower on the screen can be missed; unclear or unexpectedly increased prices can prompt shoppers to abandon and buy elsewhere.[74]

The review stage must also be unmistakably different from an order-confirmation page. Baymard reports that shoppers sometimes mistake a review page for confirmation, close the page, and unintentionally abandon their order. Its testing also found that users can hesitate or revisit prior steps when accordion checkouts lack an efficient way to inspect previously entered information; Baymard recommends collapsing completed stages into summaries that remain reviewable.[97][98] A visible screen-reader heading and an explicit authorization label—such as Place order and authorize payment, not Continue—make this state boundary audible as well as visible.

Editing must be local to the review. Baymard reports that 68% of benchmarked sites send users backward in the checkout flow to edit from review, a flow it observed as highly problematic. The appropriate design is an Edit quantity, Edit delivery, Edit payment, or Remove item control adjacent to each reviewable group, followed by a return to the same position in review.[97]

Quantity is an especially important confirmation case because the shopper must verify both the intended number and the recalculated price. Baymard found increment/decrement buttons the most efficient of its tested quantity-selector alternatives; it recommends optionally pairing them with an open numeric text field for larger quantities. Quantity changes should apply automatically and update the cart summary and total immediately. When typed entry is available, a 200–300 ms delay before auto-update gives time to enter a multi-digit value such as 21 rather than committing after the first digit.[70]

The documented failure mode is concrete. A participant intending to replace 1 with 2 instead appended the digit and obtained 21 because the existing value remained in the field. Baymard also reports quantity changes that participants did not realize had registered, resulting in orders with incorrect quantities.[98] Thus, quantity confirmation needs to state the resulting value and the total, not merely announce that the plus, minus, or field control was activated.

Payment routing is also part of confirmation. NN/g reports that participants used PayPal or Apple Pay partly to avoid error-prone manual card entry and perceived these routes as more secure and trustworthy than directly entering card details. It recommends clearly identifying each payment option, avoiding excessive options, and telling users that an external payment service will temporarily take them away and return them.[74] The Souq.com evaluation similarly found a Slide to pay securely control confusing because it required a slide rather than a press, and recorded inconsistent confirmation-page language as severe problems.[99]

**The mobile-commerce pattern is review, edit in place, then explicitly authorize: it rules out interfaces whose review state can be mistaken for successful purchase or whose corrections require a disorienting backward journey.**[97][74][98]

This evidence is general mobile-commerce usability research rather than a blind or low-vision modality comparison. It nevertheless supplies an important interaction rule for the accessible app: screen-reader status text must describe the resulting state—quantity, total, and payment boundary—not merely the control event.

### What confirmation strategies do assistive-technology evaluations suggest?

Assistive-technology systems support staged confirmation. Trinetra announces first that a scan was received, then that processing is occurring if necessary, and finally speaks either the product description or an item-not-found result. A touchscreen target-selection experiment similarly distinguished reaching a target from selecting it, with spoken feedback for reached, missed, and selected states.[13][36] This State Ladder—received, processing, result, correction if needed—maps well to scan product, change quantity, apply promotion, and submit authorization actions.

For high-consequence numeric input, payment-terminal deployments provide the closest evaluated hybrid pattern. The Spanish ONCE Point of Sale combines accessible software, tactile markers, haptic signals, and tactile screen marks; after each double-tap, it announces only ordinal position—first digit, second digit—rather than the digit itself.[80] French Cartes Bancaires terminal specifications use a blanked screen, a voice-guided keypad, beeps and vibration while sliding across keys, and double-tap confirmation. Partially sighted users receive high-contrast alternatives and adjustable text size.[80]

The UK RNIB and UK Finance speech-and-beeps method similarly uses a private or blanked PIN surface, auditory location cues, and explicit double-tap selection. It was refined through years of testing with blind and partially sighted users.[80] UK Finance recommends a clear explanation of the PIN-entry steps, confirmation that each digit was entered, an incorrect-PIN message with re-entry instruction, and a final accepted-or-declined payment status.[82]

These patterns distinguish three different confirmations that a grocery app often collapses:

- **Input acknowledgement:** the system received the intended touch, spoken command, or scan.
- **State confirmation:** the system applied the quantity, selection, or delivery choice and can state its current value.
- **Commitment confirmation:** the user is about to authorize payment, followed by an accepted, declined, or pending outcome.

The UK terminal findings also establish that privacy changes what should be spoken. Spoken amounts, PIN feedback, and decline messages can expose financial information in public; UK Finance therefore suggests audio as an independently accessible option rather than universally audible output. Headphones and a haptic parallel channel are practical ways to preserve access in noisy or socially sensitive settings.[82]

A hybrid touch-with-audio/haptic pattern is the only payment-confirmation approach here refined with blind and partially sighted users, but it is not a head-to-head demonstration that hybrid interaction outperforms sequential voice-only grocery checkout. The terminal report says trained users can complete transactions independently, while first use in busy shops remains difficult and some users do not use the method accurately and confidently even after several attempts.[80]

Granular confirmation also has a cost. In a Braille touch-entry study, some participants wanted to cancel erroneous dots before committing a character or confirm each dot, and some used backspace as a self-check. Yet many considered Split-Tap confirmation slow or hard to learn, and the second-finger gesture could itself register the wrong Braille pattern.[100] Confirmation should therefore be proportional to consequence: brief immediate state feedback for ordinary cart edits, with an intentional review-and-authorize action for payment.

Screen-reader shopping observations show why state confirmation is necessary. Reported failures include a selected clothing size not announced as selected, changed colour remaining incorrectly announced after addition to cart, quantity controls that claim to be interactive but do not open a keyboard or menu, and product additions with no cart feedback.[51][19] Price output can also be misleading: one grocery-app assessment reported €1.80 read as “80 and 1,” while promotional price components were read separately and could become unintelligible.[19]

**For checkout, hybrid nonvisual feedback should confirm the actual selected state and outcome, while the final amount must be independently reviewable before—not only announced after—authorization.** The European Blind Union reports that a post-payment mobile-wallet notification is not equivalent to an independent amount check because it occurs after payment and may provide no itemized receipt for comparison.[80]

### What error recovery mechanisms does evidence from WCAG 2.2 implementation

WCAG’s recovery baseline is field-specific, textual, and actionable. Under SC 3.3.1, when an input error is automatically detected, the interface must identify the item in error and describe the problem in text. Under SC 3.3.3, if a correction is known, it must be suggested unless disclosure would jeopardize security or the purpose of the content.[67][68]

For a quantity or other bounded numeric input, silent correction is not acceptable. If the interface changes an out-of-range value automatically, it still must tell the shopper what was wrong and state the replacement value. For example: “Quantity for 12-pack sparkling water cannot exceed 8. You entered 12. Quantity remains 8.” This lets the user decide whether the revised state is acceptable rather than discovering it only at authorization.[101][42]

Error presentation may be inline, in an error summary, alert, or dialog. The criterion does not prescribe one visual or spoken presentation.[42] On mobile, the robust pattern is to preserve an inline message associated with the control and, for multiple failed fields, move focus to an error summary or first invalid field.

A transient toast or auto-dismissing snackbar must not be the only error channel, because a screen-reader user may miss it or be unable to revisit it.[102]

For touch, Pointer Cancellation adds a separate recovery mechanism. Where a control uses a single pointer, activation should occur on release rather than touch-down where feasible, with an option to abort before completion or undo afterward.[67][45][103] This matters for Remove item, decrement quantity, and payment authorization controls. It does not replace transaction review: it mitigates accidental touch activation; SC 3.3.4 protects against consequential submission errors.

Data preservation is part of recovery. WCAG 2.2 requires that repeated information within the same process be auto-populated or available for selection except for security, essential re-entry, or invalidated information. It also requires timing adjustments for content-imposed time limits, including warning and a simple extension option where applicable.[67] For an expiring payment or authentication session, SC 2.2.5 at Level AAA further requires users to continue after re-authentication without loss of data.[67]

**WCAG recovery combines prevention of accidental activation, persistent identification of the exact error, direct correction, and preservation of completed work; it does not support forcing a shopper to reconstruct a failed checkout from the beginning.**[67][83]

The CREDENTIAL WCAG 2.0 prototype assessment above shows the consequence of omitting textual error identification: invalid entries were marked with a red X or checkmark without an error description, and Error Identification was one of five failed criteria.[41] That is a screen-reader recovery failure even if a sighted shopper can infer the symbol.

### What error recovery mechanisms does mobile-commerce usability research suggest?

Mobile-commerce research favors local, immediate recovery over a separate save cycle. Quantity controls should update automatically; NN/g warns that an Update button adds an extra action that shoppers can forget or fail to understand.[74] Baymard likewise recommends buttons or buttons plus an open text field rather than text-field-only or dropdown selectors, and reports that 61% of benchmarked sites used only the latter less effective patterns.[70]

For typed quantities, preserve the entered content long enough to inspect and correct it; do not clear the field on failure. The user needs an accessible indication of the current quantity, the new applied quantity, and the changed order total. When correction is possible through plus/minus controls, these should be clearly named with the product and current value—for example, “Increase bananas, current quantity 2”—rather than announced as unlabeled buttons.

Payment recovery should identify the cause where the system can do so. In online ticket-marketplace data from January 2024 through June 2026, 23.0% of payment attempts were declined; 55.7% of initially declined orders were retried, and 43.7% eventually completed. Recovery differed materially by cause: incorrect CVV recovered in 68.5% of cases, insufficient funds in 66.2%, failed authentication in 42.6%, and UnableToProcess in 28.6%.[104]

The same dataset indicates that retry should not always mean switching payment method. Among recovered orders, 80.1% completed with the same instrument, 8.4% used a different issuer within the same scheme, and 11.5% switched scheme. Instrument changes were more common after expired-card failure, 23.5%, and failed authentication, 25.3%, than after insufficient funds, 9.7%, or CVV error, 12.9%.[104]

**Payment recovery should be cause-specific: preserve the valid checkout state, describe the failure in the payment context, offer correction or retry for correctable entry errors, and surface an alternative instrument when the failure suggests the original one may no longer work.**[104][47]

Observed timing also differs: the median pause before the next attempt was 3 minutes, 68.1% of retries occurred within 5 minutes, and retries more than one hour after failure accounted for 4.2% of retries. Retries after a 5–15 minute pause succeeded in 74.8% of cases versus 25.0% for immediate retries.[104]

### What error recovery mechanisms do assistive-technology evaluations suggest?

Assistive-technology research supports separating exploration from activation. Slide Rule lets blind users explore with one finger without changing state and requires a second-finger action for selection. Its risk-free exploration principle reserves multi-finger gestures for state-changing operations.[20][5] Tilt-Explore similarly uses touch exploration to focus an element without clicking it, followed by double tap to activate.[92] These patterns make it possible to inspect product, quantity, or removal controls without unintended cart mutation.

Recovery after a touch-target miss should be short and spatially stable. Expert blind smartphone users reported directly targeting remembered locations with explore-by-touch and then using a quick previous/next gesture if they missed.[1] In controlled gliding selection, the screen reader announced “Passed the Target” after overshoot and allowed the participant to move back; when the finger lifted outside the target, it announced failure and required a retry.[36] For checkout, a quantity stepper or cart-row control should similarly retain focus and offer an adjacent-item route rather than changing page context after a minor targeting error.

Voice input needs a distinct recovery path because detection and correction are different tasks. Speech-recognition work found 15% word error rate across 1,920 utterances, with 17% for challenging phrases and 12% for common phrases. Participants correctly located only 49% of recognition errors overall, including 2% of insertion errors, 49% of substitutions, and 62% of deletions.[3] Selectively slowing low-confidence recognitions produced 85% error-detection accuracy versus 76% for uniformly slowed playback, although the difference from normal-rate playback, 80%, was not statistically significant.[3]

These results support confidence-sensitive readback for a voice command such as “set milk quantity to four,” but not a voice-only correction loop. The shopper needs a persistent visible-to-assistive-technology command record and direct edit alternatives: repeat command, edit quantity with touch controls, or cancel the proposed change. Audio can help detection, yet noisy environments, public privacy concerns, and the difficulty of correcting recognized text limit audio-only recovery.[2][29][105]

For numeric entry, auditory acknowledgement of accepted input and explicit error signals are useful. The cited BlindSight comparison asked 12 participants to enter a 10-digit number, supplied feedback for correct input and errors, and reported the ear interface as best among ear, flip, and visual interfaces.[106] Payment-terminal guidance extends that pattern with per-digit acknowledgement, wrong-PIN re-entry, and final payment outcome.[82]

Assistive evaluations also show that feedback timing matters. In a blind touch-typing study, by week eight 64% of substitution errors involved paths that entered the intended key but did not select it in time; video inspection associated many of these cases with delay between spoken feedback and touch position. Participants corrected 98.4% of their detected typing errors, but correction consumed about 13% of typing time, while 68% of omission errors remained undetected and uncorrected.[91]

**The recovery design implication is to make feedback prompt, synchronized, persistent, and locally actionable: a user should hear or feel what happened, inspect the current state, and correct it without replaying the entire checkout journey.**[91][102][47]

### What do these evidence streams suggest about optimal confirmation strategies

Across WCAG, mobile-commerce, and assistive-technology evidence, the appropriate checkout pattern is not a single universal confirmation prompt. It is a staged design: first, expose cart edits and validation results as non-disruptive, programmatically available status; second, retain direct edit, undo, and error-navigation paths; third, present an explicit pre-authorization review; fourth, require a deliberate authorization; and fifth, announce accepted, declined, or pending outcome. Hybrid touch with audio/haptic feedback has practical advantages for private spatial numeric entry and target confirmation, while sequential voice input needs confidence-sensitive readback and a parallel touch or screen-reader correction route. WCAG defines the safeguards; mobile-commerce research defines review and editing failures; assistive-technology evaluations show how nonvisual confirmation and recovery can be made operable.[67][94][97][70][80][82][47]

1. Provide persistent, screen-reader-readable cart-state updates after every add, removal, quantity, price, or delivery change.
2. Put editable order, total, payment, and delivery summaries immediately before a clearly named final authorization action.
3. Preserve failed-payment context and valid entries; return focus to the failed step with a specific correction path.
4. Offer both voice and touch/screen-reader correction routes, with private audio or haptic feedback for sensitive payment actions.

## Cognitive load during cart verification

### How do cognitive-load measurements differ between modalities when users must

Pre-authorisation review is a state-verification task: the shopper must establish that every item, quantity, substitution, price, fee, delivery choice, and payment total is correct before an irreversible transaction. For a blind user, the design question is whether serial spoken review or touch-linked audio lets that state be checked with less memory, effort, and uncertainty.

Judgment — No published result in this set measures cognitive workload for blind or low-vision people verifying a 15-or-more-item mobile grocery cart before payment authorisation in sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback conditions. A direct claim that either pattern produces lower workload for that task would therefore be premature.

NASA-TLX is suitable for such a comparison because it produces subjective ratings for mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, self-rated performance, effort, and frustration; its overall score may be weighted or calculated as an unweighted mean, and studies must report which method they use.[107][108] That distinction matters: weighted and raw TLX totals are different calculations and should not be pooled.

SUS measures perceived usability, while USE measures perceived usefulness, satisfaction and ease of use; neither is a direct substitute for a transaction-confidence score, task-completion rate or input-error frequency.[6][122][127][135]

The closest shopping-assistant workload study used NASA-TLX but compared normal vision with cataract-simulated impairment, not checkout interaction patterns. Its four participants completed navigation and two-product detection tasks; navigation scores were 35.9 under normal vision and 40.4 under simulated impairment, whereas detection scores were 50.7 and 44.8 respectively.[109] These opposing task results show that workload is sensitive to task structure, rather than providing a direction for voice-only versus touch-with-audio cart review.

Serial screen-reader presentation creates a credible working-memory risk during long-cart checking. Nonvisual browsing is constrained by information-processing speed and the serial-audio interface, while visual product grids and carousels become long linear reading orders under screen readers.[110][46] **This makes a voice-only cart review most demanding when the shopper must retain earlier announcements while interpreting later quantities, substitutions, prices, and totals.**

Hybrid evidence does not yet demonstrate a cart-review advantage. In an acoustic-touch evaluation, the device did not significantly increase NASA-TLX workload during object-search and reaching tasks, but those tasks involved locating physical household objects rather than checking transactional state.[111] The appropriate design conclusion is narrower: touch-plus-audio can be evaluated as a way to anchor feedback to an item, but it should not be credited with reducing 15-item cart-verification workload until tested in that task.

### What do smaller-cart or related-task cognitive-load measurements indicate about verifying

Related blind-user studies identify feedback placement and externalised state as important workload controls. In a website study, structure with in-place feedback was perceived as lower workload, less effortful, and less frustrating than structure alone; participants also rated it less mentally, physically, and temporally demanding. The authors attributed the difference to avoiding context switches in which users had to remember their previous location and navigate back.[112] For cart review, attach confirmation, edit status, and recovery messages to the affected cart line rather than sending focus to a distant alert or refreshed page.

Screen-reader users report high cognitive load when interface contents and object state are insufficiently conveyed. They described having to remember incoming information, decide what mattered, and perform calculations mentally; limited manipulation feedback left some users checking repeatedly and others guessing whether an action succeeded.[113] A cart interface should therefore provide an immediately queryable external record: item count, current line position, quantity, unit price, substitution state, subtotal, fees, total, and whether a requested edit succeeded.

The most useful long-list proxy comes from a 30-product virtual-reality grocery task with 22 unimpaired young adults. Adding an auditory 2-back number-memory task raised the mean NASA-TLX mental-demand-and-effort score from 22.05 to 74.77, a 52.73-point increase, and reduced products placed in the cart from 18.68 to 10.68 per 122.4-second trial. Participants spent 9.89 additional seconds looking at the shopping list and often serialised the tasks, pausing one to perform the other.[114] The result supports stepwise review and persistent cart-state access, because added memory maintenance sharply degraded performance in this related task.

A second proxy shows that high auditory demand can raise workload even where task output remains stable. In the same VR study, an auditory-high condition increased mental demand and effort by 17.05 points relative to baseline, without a statistically significant product-collection difference of −1.18 products.[114] Spoken confirmation should consequently be concise, interruptible, and paced by the user rather than layered with competing audio.

A mathematical-expression recall experiment with 16 blind daily screen-reader and braille users provides the closest controlled information-complexity comparison. Across the experiment, auditory presentation produced 2,403 recall errors, tactile presentation 1,606, and auditory–tactile presentation 1,399. The auditory error count was significantly greater than tactile (p = 0.022) and auditory–tactile (p = 0.018) error counts.[116]

In that experiment, the recall-error slope per expression element was 0.570 for auditory, 0.449 for tactile, and 0.389 for auditory–tactile presentation. Participants performed equally across modalities for expressions of up to 10 elements; tactile and especially auditory–tactile performance exceeded auditory performance for 11–35 elements; and all modalities performed equally poorly for the 46-element expression.[116]

The authors link the rising error rate with expression complexity to augmented cognitive load and recommend dividing lengthy audio into smaller complete structures, with user pausing and automatic structural segmentation. The auditory–tactile condition was the most time-consuming because its reduced tactile-reading time did not offset the audio time.[116]

### Synthesis

Judgment — The stronger design direction is not “voice” or “touch” alone; it is low-memory, item-anchored verification. Sequential audio imposes a serial retention burden when a shopper must compare many cart lines and states, while in-place feedback reduces perceived workload in related blind-user web tasks.[110][112][113] Hybrid touch-with-audio is therefore the better candidate pattern for a 15-plus-item review when touch selects or revisits a stable cart line and audio states that line’s status; it is not a demonstrated workload winner over voice-only review.[111][20]

1. Provide a cart overview first, then user-controlled line-by-line review with a stable position such as item 7 of 18.
2. Bind audio confirmation and edit recovery to the selected line; preserve focus and announce the updated quantity, price, and total.
3. Offer spoken summaries on demand, not as an uninterruptible recital; require an explicit final confirmation after total and payment method are reviewed.
4. Test both modalities with blind and low-vision participants using identical 15-plus-item carts, verification accuracy, recovery success, review time, and identically scored NASA-TLX dimensions.

## Conflicts and open questions

Judgment — The central design question is not whether speech or touch is accessible in isolation. It is whether users can select, amend, verify, and authorize a consequential grocery order without losing track of current state or being unable to recover from an input error. The answer remains task- and context-dependent rather than a general victory for sequential voice-only or hybrid touch-with-audio interaction.

### Where do findings on sequential voice-only versus hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback patterns

No identified study directly compares sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback for mobile grocery product selection, quantity adjustment, payment confirmation, or pre-authorization review of a 15+-item cart. The closest touch-and-audio comparison, *Slide Rule: Making Mobile Touch Screens Accessible to Blind People*, tested calling, email, and music; its comparator was a button-based Pocket PC screen reader, not voice commands. The closest voice-shopping study stopped when participants added an item to a mock cart and did not include payment.[20][5][7]

The most concrete conflict is therefore a **speed–accuracy–control trade-off** in adjacent nonvisual list interaction. In the Slide Rule study of 10 blind participants, the audio-based multitouch system had mean completion time of 11.69 seconds per task, versus 12.79 seconds for the Pocket PC, and 7 of 10 participants preferred Slide Rule. But Slide Rule averaged 0.20 errors per trial, with errors in 17 of 120 tasks; Pocket PC users made no errors. Pocket PC users also stepped through lists sequentially and were less likely to miss items, while Slide Rule permitted non-linear jumps that sometimes caused skipped items or random search.[20][5]

This is not a contradiction in the statistical sense: each outcome measures a different property. Faster access and majority preference make hybrid touch-with-audio attractive for finding a known product or moving among cart items. The no-error result and lower omission risk make sequential navigation attractive when an item must not be missed. The finding does not select voice-only or hybrid checkout, because the supposedly sequential condition used physical buttons and screen-reader output rather than voice commands, and Slide Rule’s broad error definition included harmless second-finger taps and gesture activations.[5]

Perceived usability also conflicts with stated preference. Slide Rule participants rated the Pocket PC significantly higher for ease of use, feeling in control, ease of learning, and familiarity; the feeling-in-control means were 4.7 for Pocket PC and 3.3 for Slide Rule on a 1–5 agreement scale. Yet 7 of 10 still preferred Slide Rule. This separates preference for speed and direct access from confidence that the interaction is predictable.[20]

Speech-based product selection shows a different split. In *User Intent Communication in Robot-Assisted Shopping for the Blind*, speech had the lowest NASA-TLX mental-demand mean, 13.4, and overall-workload mean, 7, compared with browsing at 45.6 and 12.88 and typing at 35.9 and 8.33. Speech nevertheless produced more frustration than typing—34 versus 1.8—and participant comments attributed that frustration to speech-recognition errors.[48] **The implication is that lower mental demand does not remove the need for highly visible recognition confirmation and low-cost correction before a quantity or payment command takes effect.**

Environmental conditions add another conflict. In a blind-participant navigation experiment, auditory turn commands were faster than tactile-belt guidance in quiet settings, but street noise significantly increased auditory completion time from 46.68 seconds on trial 5 to 53.96 seconds on trial 6; tactile time rose from 52.21 to 55.98 seconds, a marginal increase. Auditory guidance still had fewer major errors overall, but its performance advantage narrowed under noise.[117] A checkout whose confirmation is solely spoken is consequently exposed to auditory masking and competing sound, whereas a touch or haptic supplement can preserve a separate acknowledgement channel.

Results called modality effects are also influenced by familiarity. Slide Rule participants had about 15 minutes of practice per device, and their first trials were excluded because learning effects were significant. Separately, tactile-navigation researchers proposed that a novel tactile belt imposed extra cognitive load because participants had little or no prior tactile-navigation experience.[5][117] This makes first-use performance a poor basis for choosing a permanent checkout modality.

For number-related interaction, an audio-versus-tactile feedback study found more errors under auditory-only than tactile-only feedback, with very small completion-time differences. It was a number-dialing task, not a voice-command quantity-entry or mobile checkout comparison.[118] It supports making a quantity’s changed value available through more than one cue; it does not demonstrate that touch-plus-audio is inferior to voice-only quantity adjustment.

Finally, speech-rate findings for detecting recognition errors are unsettled. A later eyes-free speech study reported 85% detection with selective slowing and 80% at normal playback, but found no significant difference between those conditions; it contrasts this with earlier work reporting improved detection at slow speech.[3] The practical consequence is to let the user replay, slow, or inspect uncertain recognition rather than imposing a universally slow spoken confirmation.

Because participants correctly located only 49% of speech-recognition errors in the adjacent study, 51% of recognition errors were not correctly located; a spoken quantity or payment command consequently needs a reviewable correction route rather than relying on recognition alone.[3]

In robot-assisted product selection, speech’s mean NASA-TLX mental-demand score was 32.2 points lower than browsing, a 70.6% reduction relative to the browsing score. This measured workload advantage coexisted with speech-recognition-related frustration.[48]

ShopTalk adds direct grocery product-finding evidence for spoken guidance combined with barcode scanning: its two real-supermarket experiments included an earlier single-participant pilot and a later study with 10 visually impaired participants, each shopping for three products from aisle shelves. The authors tested whether verbal route descriptions and barcode scans enabled independent shopping for shelved items.[54]

In ShopTalk, completely blind participants averaged 590.5 seconds and low-vision participants averaged 280.8 seconds, F(1,8) = 21.45, p = 0.0017; the authors reported that completely blind participants increased their efficiency over time and approached low-vision performance. This establishes a visual-status difference in a spoken-guidance grocery product-finding task.[54]

These physical-object-reaching results do not conflict with favorable touch-based mobile-navigation findings because the FAD required active scanning with auditory feedback, whereas the clock and speaker conditions supplied precise prior position information, and none tested touch-screen controls, voice-only checkout, or cart authorization.[115][111]

### What remains open about the preferable modality for accessible mobile

The preferable modality remains unresolved for the requested grocery workflow because direct comparisons have not tested all three stages—product selection, quantity adjustment, and payment authorization—with blind and low-vision shoppers. Existing direct grocery-related work is mostly needs research or prototypes for in-store navigation and product identification; one grocery-app concept remained unimplemented, and another planned user study did not recruit blind or low-vision participants.[119][105]

Comparable rates are also missing for the requested decision. There are no directly comparable figures here for successful independent checkout, numerical-entry errors, recovery attempts, or scaled confidence scores under sequential voice-only versus hybrid touch-with-audio. General mobile text-entry research uses inconsistent error measures, and deletions cannot be treated as errors: corrected error rate, non-corrected error rate, total error rate, minimum string-distance error rate, and keystrokes per character capture different phenomena.[1][28]

The 15+-item cart-verification question is especially open. No supplied study measures cognitive load for either requested modality while users verify a cart of that size before authorization. Related screen-reader research does show that retaining object state, deciding which information matters, and mentally combining it can create high working-memory demand and uncertainty about whether a manipulation succeeded.[113] **For long-cart review, the decisive comparison is not raw review speed but whether users detect product, quantity, substitution, price, and total discrepancies without unacceptable workload or reliance on sighted assistance.**

Judgment — A decisive counterbalanced study should hold the grocery catalogue, a cart containing at least 15 items, seeded product/quantity/substitution/price/total discrepancies, and payment states constant across three paths: sequential voice-only, hybrid touch-with-audio-feedback, and an ordinary accessible VoiceOver/TalkBack grocery flow. The ordinary baseline should retain explore-by-touch, target activation, and next/previous-item navigation available in current screen-reader use; VoiceOver-style finger exploration with object callouts and TalkBack-style explore-by-touch followed by double-tap or next/previous-item movement provide the relevant interaction patterns.[1][113] The study should counterbalance path order and report blind and low-vision participants separately, with independent completion, time, corrected numerical errors, residual uncorrected numerical errors, recovery turns, seeded-discrepancy review accuracy, and a pre-specified decision-confidence measure for each workflow stage.

Corrected error rate (CER) and non-corrected error rate (NCER) distinguish fixed from unfixed erroneous characters; total error rate (TER) additionally captures correction effort. Decision confidence is judging product suitability and transaction risk sufficiently to buy or sell without ad hoc sighted assistance.[28][46] It should administer the same NASA-TLX or equivalent workload instrument and scoring procedure after each 15+-item pre-authorization review, under stated quiet/noisy and private/public conditions.

Background noise, language obstacles, speech-recognition quality, privacy in non-private settings, and social appropriateness affect voice-command use; auditory performance also changes when competing street noise is introduced.[28][73][117] This design would distinguish a fast command that users cannot safely verify from a genuinely independent checkout workflow.[46][1]

### Synthesis

Read together, the adjacent evidence favors **user-selectable multimodality, not a single mandated interaction path**. Hybrid touch-with-audio can provide quick nonvisual access and is often preferred, but it can increase targeting and omission errors relative to sequential navigation. Speech can lower mental demand for known-item selection, yet recognition errors increase frustration and are harder to correct; its reliability also changes with noise and privacy conditions.[33][5][48][117]

For a grocery checkout, first offer voice for search and concise commands, then provide touch-explorable, screen-reader-accessible controls for inspection and correction. Second, make every quantity or state change immediately confirmable and reversible before payment. Third, use a structured cart review that supports item-level navigation and an explicit final summary, rather than requiring users to retain a long spoken sequence. Those are defensible safeguards; superiority of either requested workflow over the other remains unmeasured for the full checkout journey.[113][1][51]

### Open questions

The unresolved comparison is an end-to-end mobile grocery experiment in which blind and low-vision shoppers complete identical product selection, quantity adjustment, 15-plus-item cart verification, and payment-authorization tasks using sequential voice-only and hybrid touch-with-audio workflows. It should measure completion, numerical-entry and correction errors, authorization errors, confidence, workload with a stated instrument, review time, and recovery success under realistic speech-recognition uncertainty.
