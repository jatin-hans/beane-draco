# Statutory language and scholarship on slavery in the 1730s British Empire

## Executive summary

The Molasses Act, 6 Geo. 2 c. 13 (1733), was a customs and trade statute, and it does not call enslaved people property or chattels. Its usable 1733 rule is a duty of sixpence per gallon on foreign molasses imported into British American colonies after 25 December 1733, payable before landing.

Jamaica, Barbados, and South Carolina maintained coercive slave-law regimes in the 1730s, but the familiar Barbados goods-and-chattels formula is from 1661 and South Carolina’s explicit chattels-personal formula is from 1740, not the requested decade.

Parliament’s 1730s measures chiefly protected imperial commerce and slave value as credit property, while colonial plantation laws supplied the routine machinery of surveillance, forced labour, punishment, pursuit, and local adjudication. Recent scholarship therefore treats the Molasses Act as directly a trade-regulation measure embedded in a slave-plantation economy, rather than as an express slave-control code.

## The Molasses Act: text and scope

Property is an asset which the law treats as capable of being owned or made answerable for a debt; a chattel is movable property. The distinction matters here because a statute can regulate commodities as goods without classifying a human being as either property or a chattel. A separate statutory debt provision concerning North America and the West Indies treated “Houses, Lands, Negroes, and other Hereditaments and real Estates” as assets liable for debts, but that is not language from the Molasses Act.[1]

The statute usually called the Molasses Act was Parliament’s 1733 customs statute for the Atlantic sugar trade, not a colonial slave code. Its citation, **6 Geo. 2 c. 13**, means chapter 13 enacted in the sixth regnal year of King George II; its 1733 enactment date is distinct both from the date of a later printed collection and from the 2015–2025 publication window for modern scholarship. For example, Owen Ruffhead’s *The Statutes at Large*, volume 6, covering 3 Geo. 2 to 19 Geo. 2, was printed in 1764; that printing date does not change the Act’s 1733 date.[2][3]

That distinction matters for dialogue and narration: the Act’s legal language concerns rum, molasses, sugar, ships, duties, customs officers, and forfeiture—not enslaved people’s status.Judgment — A character may plausibly call it the Molasses Act, but a formal recital would use its long title and regnal citation.[2]

### What is the actual statutory language from the Molasses Act

The measure is 6 Geo. 2 c. 13 (1733), formally titled 《An act for the better securing and encouraging the trade of his Majesty’s sugar colonies in America》. It received royal assent on 17 May 1733; it commenced in part on 24 June 1733 and in its entirety on 25 December 1733.[4] Some catalogues call it the Trade of Sugar Colonies Act 1732 because Parliament’s regnal session straddled 1732–33; for a novel, “the Act of 1733” or “6 Geo. 2 c. 13” avoids confusion.[2][4]

Its preamble stated the statutory purpose in expressly commercial and imperial terms:

> “Whereas the Welfare and Prosperity of Your Majesty’s Sugar Colonies in America are of the greatest Consequence and Importance to the Trade, Navigation, and Strength of this Kingdom: And whereas the Planters of the said Sugar Colonies have of late Years fallen under such great Discouragements, that they are unable to improve or carry on the Sugar Trade upon an equal Footing with the Foreign Sugar Colonies, without some Advantage and Relief be given to them from Great-Britain;” [2]

The core operative provision imposed the famous molasses duty. It began:

> “That from and after the Twenty-fifth Day of December, One thousand Seven hundred and thirty three, there shall be raised, levied, collected, and paid, unto and for the Use of His Majesty, His Heirs, and Successors …” [2]

The 1733 Act specified that the rum or spirits came from American colonies or plantations “not in the Possession or under the Dominion of His Majesty,” while the covered molasses, syrups, sugars, and paneles were likewise of foreign produce or manufacture.[2]

The same clause then set out the rates. To avoid separating numbers from their legal setting, the following table gives the covered imports and duties imposed when they were brought into the Crown’s American colonies.[2]

| Commodity and origin covered by the Act | Duty, from 25 December 1733 |
|---|---:|
| Foreign rum or spirits | 9 pence per gallon [2] |
| Foreign molasses or syrups | 6 pence per gallon [2] |
| Foreign sugars or paneles | 5 shillings per hundredweight avoirdupois [2] |

For the 1733 rum duty, the Act specified “Money of Great Britain” valued at five shillings and sixpence per ounce of silver.[2]

The molasses language itself was: “upon all Molasses or Syrups of such Foreign Produce or Manufacture, as aforesaid, which shall be imported or brought into any of the said Colonies or Plantations of or belonging to His Majesty, the Sum of Six pence of like Money, for every Gallon thereof.”[2]

The Act did not merely declare a tariff. First, it required an importer to make an entry with “the Collector or other proper Officer of the Customs, or the Impost Officer, or proper Officer of the Excise.” Second, it required that the duties “shall be paid down in ready Money by the Importers thereof, before the landing of the same respectively.” “Ready money” here means payment in cash, rather than a promise to pay later.[2]

Third, the Act made goods landed without the prescribed entry, payment, or warrant liable to forfeiture: “all such Goods as shall be so landed or put on Shore, or the Value of the same, shall be forfeited.” It authorized seizure by a governor, commander in chief, magistrate-authorized person, or customs, impost, or excise officer, and allowed proceedings in a colonial Admiralty court or court of record.[2] An owner claiming seized goods bore the onus probandi—the burden of proving lawful shipment or production, proper entry, and payment of duty.[2]

The Molasses Act also imposed liability equal to three times the value of the goods for assisting an illicit landing or knowingly receiving illegally landed goods.[2][37]

Forfeitures recovered under the Molasses Act were divided into thirds among the Crown’s colonial government, the governor or commander in chief, and the informer or prosecutor.[2][37]

The statute also regulated trade beyond the American colonial ports. Section IV prohibited imports into Ireland of American sugar, paneles, syrups, molasses, rum, and spirits unless they were bona fide loaded and shipped in Great Britain in lawfully navigated ships; the exception was for rum or spirits of the growth or manufacture of His Majesty’s sugar colonies there.[2] The sanction was forfeiture of the goods or value and of the vessel with its “Guns, Tackle, Furniture, Ammunition, and Apparel.” It offered drawbacks—repayment of duties—on certain colonial sugar or paneles re-exported from Great Britain within one year, and an additional two shillings per hundredweight for qualifying British-refined exported sugar.[2][5]

The Act was expressly a public Act and was initially to continue for five years from 24 June 1733 and until the end of the next parliamentary session.[2] **Thus, for a scene after 25 December 1733, the immediately usable legal consequence is a sixpence-per-gallon charge on foreign molasses, payable before landing, with forfeiture exposure if the customs process was evaded.**[2]

The Act followed an unsuccessful 1731 bill: British sugar-island planters had protested the prosperity of French colonies, and the Commons passed a bill to prohibit foreign sugar, molasses, and rum imports into Britain and the American colonies, but the Lords rejected it. The 1733 import duties were the alternative to the rejected 1731 prohibition bill, pursuing its trade-restriction object through duties on the specified foreign commodities.[8][2]

For the 1733 compromise bill, the candidate’s cited account gives 21 March 1733 as the date it passed the Commons and 17 May 1733 as the date it passed the Lords.[2][90][88]

In practice, colonial smuggling diminished the measure’s effect. Lax enforcement by the small customs bureaucracy allowed earlier commercial routes to continue, and New England distilled more rum than legally imported molasses could have produced.[9]

The Act’s relief for British sugar-colony planters took the form of duties on foreign rum, molasses or syrups, and sugar or paneles. It aimed to shift North American demand away from cheaper French West Indian molasses, which was essential to New England rum production, toward the British sugar colonies.[10][11]

The printed long title continues by describing the revenue purpose: “for applying the produce of the duties on rum and molasses, &c. towards defraying the expenses of defending, protecting, and securing the said colonies and plantations.” Thus the measure joined commercial protection to imperial defence finance.

A 1733 printed copy identifies the Act as 6 George II, chapter 13, 《An Act for the better securing and encouraging the Trade of His Majesty’s Sugar Colonies in America》, at pp. 283–290.[12]

### Does the Molasses Act itself explicitly classify enslaved people as

No. **The Molasses Act contains no express statement that enslaved people were property or chattels.** Its title, preamble, duty provisions, customs-entry requirements, seizure machinery, forfeitures, and Irish shipping restriction all regulate commodities and trade routes.[2]

That negative answer should not be softened into a claim that the Act had no relationship to plantation slavery as an economic system; that larger historical question belongs in “Why the Molasses Act? Scholarly interpretations.” The legal point here is narrower and decisive: the statute’s named objects are foreign rum, spirits, molasses, syrups, sugars, and paneles, then the ships and goods involved in customs violations.[2]

The Act did impose a punishment on people, but it was not a slave-control punishment. A person who hindered, molested, or resisted customs, impost, or excise officers performing seizures forfeited £50 and, if convicted, faced three months’ imprisonment “without Bail or Mainprize.” That is enforcement of customs law against obstruction, not authority to discipline enslaved people.[2]

A customs officer who colluded in evasion of the Molasses Act forfeited £50 and became incapable of Crown employment.[2][37]

The law’s confiscatory language also applies to imported goods. It speaks of “such Goods” being forfeited and permits the seizure of those commodities; it does not use enslaved persons as the object of forfeiture, sale, discipline, or proprietary classification.[2] **A novelist should therefore not put “chattel,” “property,” or equivalent slave-status wording into a quotation, proclamation, or courtroom argument attributed to 6 Geo. 2 c. 13.**[2]

### Synthesis —

Read together, the Act’s structure is consistent throughout: Parliament protected and channelled sugar-colony commerce through duties, customs entry, cash payment before landing, seizures, forfeitures, shipping rules, and export rebates. Its own preamble expressly links sugar-colony prosperity to Britain’s trade, navigation, and strength; its operative clauses implement that aim through commodity regulation.[2] The statute therefore supplies authentic language for a merchant, customs officer, governor, shipmaster, or smuggler, but not for a planter or magistrate defining an enslaved person’s legal status. That latter legal vocabulary belongs to the colonial legislation addressed in the next section, “Colonial slave laws: dates and chattel language.”

### What is the actual statutory language from Barbados slave laws

**1730s enactments and citation.** The London 1739 second part of *Acts of Assembly, passed in the island of Barbadoes* covers Assembly acts from 1717–18 through 1738, but no identified Barbados enactment dated 1730–1739 provides an express classification of enslaved people as property or chattels.[20] **Verified property wording and older law.** The preamble to the Barbados Code of 1661—not a 1730s enactment—states that enslaved people were to be protected 《as Wee doe mens other goods and Chattells》, while adding that they were to receive some further protection 《as being created men》.[21] It can be used only if a character is invoking or paraphrasing an old Barbadian legal tradition, not if the narration says that Barbados enacted those words in 1733 or 1739.[22]

Barbados’s 1661 enactment was titled “An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing of Negroes.”[25]

The 1730s statute collection is locatable. The 1732 printing of *Acts of Assembly, passed in the island of Barbadoes, from 1648, to 1718* has a second part, separately titled *Acts of Assembly … from 1717–18, to 1738, inclusive*, printed in London in 1739 and beginning after page 282. It also contains an abridgment of Acts passed from 1717–18 to 1738.[20] Thus, the colony had an accessible published legislative series reaching through 1738, but the cited material supplies no individual 1730–1738 clause with an express “property” or “chattels” classification.

The Barbados compilations printed in 1732 and 1739 are contemporary collections, not the enrolled original Acts.[75][95][20][96]

1739 enactment. Barbados’s 1739 《An Act for amending an Act of this Island, entitled an Act for the Governing of Negroes, and for providing a proper Maintenance and Support for such Negroes, Indians, or Mulattoes as shall be hereafter manumitted or set free, and also for preventing certain Inconveniences from them arising to the Inhabitants of this Island》 amended the 8 August 1688 《An Act for the Governing of Negroes》.[23] It referred to 《any Negro or other Slave tried before them》 and to 《the owner or possessor of such Negro or other Slave》.[23] The 1739 amendment therefore governed within the rewritten 1688 code’s framework, but its owner-or-possessor wording was not an express classification of an enslaved person as property or a chattel.[23][24]

Older property wording. The 1661 enactment was an older predecessor, not the unchanged text of the code governing Barbados in the 1730s: it was amended through 1688 and comprehensively rewritten in that year.[21][24] In the earliest surviving, amended 1667 manuscript version of the 1661 act, the preamble stated that enslaved people should be protected 《as Wee doe mens other goods and Chattells, and also somewhat further as being created men》.[21][13] That goods-and-chattels wording belongs to the older 1661 code tradition, not to the operative 1688 act or the 1739 amendment.[21][23]

The National Archives copy of the Barbados code is dated 1661 but incorporates later amendments; the Huntington manuscript is an amended 1667 version. These surviving copies should not be treated as identical texts of the original enactment.[21]

A separate older Barbados statute, dated 29 April 1668, was titled 《An ACT declaring the Negro-slaves of this Island to be Real Estates》.[13] The 1668 real-estate classification is distinct from both the surviving 1667 version’s goods-and-chattels wording and the 1739 act’s phrase 《the owner or possessor of such Negro or other Slave》.[13][21][23]

The Barbados succession rule of April 1668 specified that enslaved people “shall be held, taken, and adjudged to be Estates Real, and not Chattels, and shall descend unto the heir and widow…”[25]

Plain-English implication. Jerome Handler explains that Barbados treated enslaved Africans as chattel property, subject to lifelong servitude and maternal descent of status in custom, and that owners’ property rights appeared through bequests, sales, gifts, mortgages, loan security, and inventories alongside movable property and livestock.[25] He identifies three customary features of slave status: chattel property, lifetime servitude, and descent of status through the enslaved mother; he says these features existed in custom before slave legislation and were implicit in laws mentioning enslaved people.[25] Handler likewise says owners’ property rights appeared in legal practice—enslaved people were bequeathed, sold, given as gifts, used in mortgage payments and loan security, and listed with movable property and livestock in inventories—even when statutes did not set out those rights as a formal definition.[25]

There is a genuine interpretive tension in the older Barbados material. Handler says no Barbados law defined slave status apart from one 1668 exception, while a later discussion reads the 1661 phrase 《protect them as we do many other goods and chattels》 as sanctioning the enslaved person’s classification as a patrimonial good.[25][22] For a Barbados scene, the owner’s payment on manumission is a 1739 detail; the quotable goods-and-chattels wording is the older 1661 formulation.[26][21] **A Barbados scene set in 1739 may accurately involve an owner paying upon manumission, but should not attribute the 1661 “goods and chattels” phrase to that year.**[26][22]

Plain-English implication. For a 1730s South Carolina scene, use the 1735 Act No. 586 as the identified slave-governing enactment, not the express chattels-personal wording of 1740.[27][28] It paired its express property classification with controls over movement: a person found away from town or plantation without a signed pass, ticket, or white companion could be whipped, up to twenty lashes.[28] It provided slave trials before small panels of justices and freeholders, authorized capital punishment for listed offences, organized capture and return of fugitives, and made a negligent Charleston workhouse warden answer to an owner for the person’s value or resulting loss if recovery did not occur within three months.[28]

The later code also exposed the economic logic of slave law. It treated the taking away of another person’s enslaved person as a felony, controlled enslaved commerce in Charlestown through forfeiture and up to twenty lashes, and imposed monetary penalties—not ordinary homicide punishment—for killing or mutilating enslaved people, while expressly excepting various ordinary disciplinary practices from the listed category of prohibited cruelty.[28] Those details belong to 1740, not 1735, and should not be placed in a 1730s South Carolina courtroom without a separate basis for doing so.

For South Carolina in the 1730s, Act No. 586 of 1735 is the identified slave-governing enactment; the exact statutory formula “chattels personal” is a 1740 formula and must be dated accordingly.[27][28]

South Carolina’s earlier slave-law regime included a 1712 slave act, which continued to shape the legal position of enslaved people during the 1730s. Act No. 670 is commonly known as the 1740 Negro Act.

South Carolina’s 1690 *Act For the Better ordering of Slaves* said enslaved people “but as to payment of debts shall bee deemed and taken as all other goods and Chattells” and that “all nigros and slaves shall be accounted as freehold In all other Cases Whatsoever and descend accordingly.” The proprietors repealed the Act in September 1691, and its reported later reuse is unevenly documented.[98]

South Carolina’s 1690 slave act treated enslaved people as goods and chattels for payment of debts but as freehold for other purposes of descent. These were different property classifications for creditors and inheritance under an older law still relevant to the 1730s, not a clause newly enacted in that decade.[98]

### Synthesis

Across the three jurisdictions, assemblies actively regulated enslaved people during the 1730s, but they did not enact one uniform formula calling them chattels in that decade. Jamaica’s laws addressed regulation, firearms, rebellion, smallpox, and the treatment of enslaved people as inheritable, transferable, and saleable estate.[14] Barbados’s 1739 amendment concerned manumission, maintenance, capital punishment, and the relationship of owner or possessor to enslaved person; its famous “goods and chattels” wording belongs to the older 1661 code, and the 1668 statute classified enslaved people as real estates.[23][19][22][13] South Carolina’s Act No.

586 of 1735 belongs in a 1730s scene, including its six-month removal rule for privately manumitted people, but the unambiguous “chattels personal” text belongs to Act No. 670 of 1740.[29][28] The pattern is coercive continuity, but the dates decide the dialogue.

The 1730 index also lists 《An Act for the better ſuppreſſing and reducing the rebellious and runaway Negroes》, at p. 236.[14] In 1733, it lists 《An Act for the ſpeedy fitting out of a Party or Parties, to ſuppreſs the rebellious * runaway Slaves in the North-eaft Part of this Iſland》, at p. 261.[14]

A 1730 highway law authorized collection of a defaulting person’s penalty 《by Diſtreſs and Sale of the Negroes, Cattle, Mules, and Goods of the Perſon ſo offending, neglecting, or refuſing, for the Uſe of the Highways》.[14] A 1731 public-records act made recorded patents, deeds, conveyances, and wills 《ſufficient Evidence of the ſeveral Perſons Titles to any Lands, Tenements, Negroes, Hereditaments, or Eftate whatſoever, Real or Perſonal》.[14] The 1731 《An Act for rendering the Bath, in the Pariſh of St.

Thomas in the Eaſt, more ſerviceable》 made void leases or grants of the Bath springs and land, together with 《all Negroes, Mules, and other Neceſſaries to the ſame belonging, or in any wiſe appertaining》.[14] The 1731 《An Act for the further quieting Poſſeſſions, and regulating Reſurveys》 provided that qualifying holders 《ſhall have and enjoy ſuch Lands, Tenements, Negroes, or other Hereditaments, to them and their Heirs for ever》; its rule took effect on 1 May 1732.[14]

## Parliament and colonial slave control

Judgment — In the 1730s British Empire, a novelist should picture two connected but different legal systems. Parliament at Westminster chiefly made imperial rules about trade, customs, shipping, credit, and the status of property; colonial assemblies made the day-to-day rules that governed enslaved people’s movement, work, trials, punishment, escape, and resistance. The systems overlapped, because colonial laws needed royal assent and could be reviewed in London, but they did not perform the same job.

**The essential distinction is this: the Molasses Act was an imperial customs statute, while Jamaican and Barbadian slave laws were local instruments of plantation policing.** A customs officer might seize rum or molasses; a parish justice, planter, militia party, or slave court might order lashes, branding, confinement, mutilation, execution, or pursuit of a runaway. The same empire supported both forms of power, but they should not be confused in a scene.

### How did British Parliamentary law in the 1730s address slave

Parliament did legislate about slavery in the 1730s, but not usually by writing a single empire-wide slave code governing ordinary plantation discipline. Its principal interventions in the materials for this period concern imperial commerce, customs enforcement, debt recovery, and legal property. The most important distinction is between the 1732 debt-recovery law, which affected enslaved people as assets, and the 1733 Molasses Act, which regulated imported commodities and customs violations.[30][1][2]

The relevant Parliamentary debt statute is commonly called the Colonial Debts Act or Recovery of American Debts Act, 5 Geo. II c. 7 (1732). It made houses, land, enslaved people, and other real estate in the British American colonies and West Indies answerable for debts. Its material wording included 《Houses, Lands, Negroes, and other Hereditaments and real Estates》, made liable for 《all just Debts, Duties and Demands, of what Nature or Kind soever》. In plain terms, an enslaved person could be reached by a creditor as part of the debtor’s estate.[1]

This was a Parliamentary property-and-credit intervention, not a detailed slave-management code. The Act’s own words placed 《Houses, Lands, Negroes, and other Hereditaments and real Estates》 among property liable for 《all just Debts, Duties and Demands, of what Nature or Kind soever》; it did not use the phrase chattels personal.[1] A modern legal-history account characterizes the Act’s effect as making enslaved people chattel property for debtor-creditor purposes and binding the colonies within a common regulatory framework.

That is the account’s characterization of legal effect; the statutory text itself groups 《Negroes》 with houses, lands, hereditaments, and real estates liable for debts.[1] The same account distinguishes that function from the Yorke–Talbot Opinion of 1729, which addressed the status of an enslaved person brought to Britain rather than debt enforcement in the colonies.[1]

The Yorke–Talbot Opinion was influential in the 1730s, but it was not legislation. It was advice by the Attorney General, Sir Philip Yorke, and Solicitor General, Charles Talbot, dated 14 January 1729. It stated that a person brought from the West Indies to Great Britain or Ireland did not thereby become free; baptism did not change that person’s temporal condition; and the master could compel a return to the plantations. The key words were: 《a Slave, by coming from the West-Indies to Great Britain or Ireland … doth not become free》 and 《his Master may legally compel him to return again to the Plantations》.[1]

For fiction, that distinction matters. A London lawyer, merchant, or West Indian planter in 1733 could invoke the Yorke–Talbot Opinion as powerful legal advice about a master’s claimed right over an enslaved person in Britain. But he would not accurately call it an Act of Parliament or a court judgment. It was an opinion of Crown law officers, circulated widely and deeply influential, but not itself positive legislation.[1][31]

The 1732 Act and the 1729 Opinion operated in the same broad legal direction: the Opinion asserted that a master’s property or right was not altered by an enslaved person’s arrival in Britain or Ireland, while the Act made 《Negroes》 among the assets liable for colonial debts.[1] Holly Brewer (2021, Law and History Review, 'Creating a Common Law of Slavery for England and its New World Empire')’s account describes the 1732 Parliamentary law as establishing slave ownership as property and as enshrining the Yorke–Talbot position.[32] In that account, the Act was a crucial step in legitimising ownership of people as property.[30]

That does not mean that English law in Britain was simple or settled. Historical accounts identify a long legal argument over slavery’s place in English common law. One prevailing interpretation holds that common law was confused but that coerced servitude was legal in England before 1772 and that slavery was legal in the empire because English slave law did not directly reach overseas colonies. Holly Brewer instead argues that English high-court judges, merchants, and Crown policy helped create an imperial common-law foundation through which people could be treated as absolute property.[32][33][34][30][35]

The disagreement is about the degree and mechanism of metropolitan legal authority, not about whether plantation slavery existed in Jamaica or Barbados. One view emphasizes legally plural colonies operating beyond the ordinary reach of English law; the other emphasizes the English judicial and imperial practices that made colonial slave property legally workable. For a 1730s character, the practical result is the same: English legal ideas, royal policy, and local statutes could all be invoked to protect slaveholding interests.[33][30]

Older English decisions had pulled in different directions. Chief Justice Holt’s court was later described as rejecting a claim that an enslaved person could be demanded as a chattel under English law: 《one man cannot have an absolute property in the person of another man》. Holt’s position drew a distinction between English and colonial law rather than simply abolishing slavery throughout the empire.[1]

The Yorke–Talbot Opinion and the 1732 debt statute came after that uncertainty. They gave planters, merchants, and creditors language and procedures more favourable to property claims. In 1749, outside the decade of this novel, Lord Hardwicke invoked the Yorke–Talbot Opinion in *Pearne v. Lisle* and described a slave as 《as much property as…any other thing》. That later case should not be put into a 1730s scene, but it helps explain why contemporaries treated the 1729–32 legal developments as significant.[30][1]

The Molasses Act was a different kind of Parliamentary measure: a trade and customs statute, not legislation controlling enslaved people’s ordinary movement, trial, discipline, or punishment.[2][36] Its proper citation is 6 Geo.

II c. 13, usually called the Molasses Act of 1733 or the Trade of Sugar Colonies Act 1732. It received royal assent on 17 May 1733. It began partly on 24 June 1733 and came fully into operation on 25 December 1733. Its formal title was 《An Act for the better securing and encouraging the trade of his Majesty’s sugar colonies in America》.[4][5][2]

Its preamble announced a commercial and strategic purpose. It said that the welfare and prosperity of Britain’s sugar colonies were of great importance to the kingdom’s 《Trade, Navigation, and Strength》 and that British sugar planters could not compete with foreign sugar colonies without relief from Great Britain. That is the Act’s own declared rationale: protection for the British sugar-colony economy, understood as part of British imperial strength.[36][5][37][2]

The Act imposed duties on goods imported from colonies outside the King’s dominions into British American colonies, including a duty of six pence on every gallon of molasses imported from non-British colonies.[37][38][2][39] From 25 December 1733 it charged nine pence per gallon on foreign rum or spirits, six pence per gallon on foreign molasses or syrups, and five shillings per hundredweight avoirdupois on foreign sugar and paneles. These were very high duties intended to make foreign West Indian supplies unattractive or effectively prohibitive.[37][38][2]

The Act also created a customs regime. Importers had to enter the goods with the appropriate customs, impost, or excise officer and pay the duties in ready money before landing them. Goods landed before entry, payment, or a proper warrant could be seized and forfeited; the prosecution could take place in a colonial Admiralty court or a court of record, at the informer’s or prosecutor’s election.[37][2]

A character who obstructed customs enforcement faced a penalty. The Act provided that anyone who hindered, molested, or resisted a customs, impost, or excise officer enforcing the Act could forfeit £50 and, on conviction, be imprisoned for three months without bail or mainprise. That was a real punishment under the Act, but it was a punishment for obstructing revenue officers—not a rule for disciplining enslaved people.[37][2]

The Act also shifted the burden of proof in specified customs litigation. An owner or claimant had to establish that goods had been lawfully loaded and shipped, that the rum or spirits came from British sugar colonies where relevant, or that imported goods had been properly entered and the duties paid before landing. This is a useful detail for a port, wharf, warehouse, or smuggling scene: the defendant could be required to prove lawful origin and lawful entry rather than leaving the Crown to prove illegality in the ordinary way.[2]

The Act extended beyond colonial imports. It prohibited specified American sugars, paneles, syrups, molasses, rum, and spirits from being imported into Ireland unless they had been fairly and bona fide loaded in Great Britain in ships navigated under the applicable laws; the penalty could include forfeiture of the goods, ship, guns, tackle, furniture, ammunition, and apparel. It also created drawback arrangements—repayment of duties—for certain sugar re-exported from Britain.[5][2]

Nothing in the Molasses Act’s stated subject or operative provisions makes enslaved people property or chattels. The provisions concern rum, spirits, molasses, syrups, sugar, paneles, customs entries, warrants, seizures, forfeitures, and prosecutions. The Act is therefore not the right source for dialogue about an enslaved person’s legal status, a master’s disciplinary authority, a slave trial, or punishment for running away.[2][40][4]

**Parliament’s 1730s law underwrote slaveholding chiefly through imperial trade and credit, while the Molasses Act itself created a customs-and-forfeiture regime for goods rather than a code for controlling enslaved people.** The legal language for slave property is more directly found in the 1732 debt statute and in colonial laws, not in 6 Geo. II c. 13.[30][1][2]

Parliament nevertheless asserted authority over colonial affairs. In the years 1714–39 it passed twenty-nine Acts concerning colonial trade, customs, and piracy. Its focus remained America and India; it tightened navigation legislation, addressed piracy and smuggling, and altered charters of companies trading in America, Africa, and Asia.[41]

That parliamentary jurisdiction was contested in political language. In debate over the Sugar Colonies Bill in March 1733, Sir William Yonge argued that the House of Commons had power to tax colonies and make laws regulating their affairs. Opponents and colonial petitioners objected that such taxation was imposed without colonial representation. Those arguments anticipated later constitutional disputes, but in 1733 they appeared inside an active Parliamentary claim to authority over colonial commerce.[42][43]

Contemporaries themselves often described the Molasses duties as trade regulation rather than fiscal revenue. Sir John Barnard said the measure was not intended to supply the government with money, but was 《rather in the Nature of a Prohibition》. He distinguished duties for the 《Regulation of Trade only》 from duties raised for current public service.[42][43]

That characterization fits the statute’s architecture. The duties were high; the preamble concerned British sugar-colony prosperity and competition with foreign colonies; the enforcement mechanisms focused on prohibited or improperly landed commodities. The measure was meant to channel commerce toward British sugar islands and away from French, Dutch, and Spanish competitors.[36][5][37][44]

In practice, the Act was often evaded. Historical summaries describe it as systematically disregarded by English colonies and largely a dead letter; another account says the tax was rarely paid because smuggling, bribery, and intimidation of customs officers effectively nullified it. That weak enforcement should shape a novelist’s port scene: the law could be on the books, a customs officer could threaten seizure, and merchants could still expect evasions, private arrangements, or collusion.[5][4]

The Act was originally to endure for five years from 24 June 1733 and until the end of the next parliamentary session. Parliament continued it in 1737 through the Continuance of Laws Act 1737, 11 Geo. II c. 18. Thus a story set late in the decade may still treat the Molasses Act as legally current, although its practical enforcement could be patchy.[37][2][40][4]

The constitutional structure was neither simple central command nor colonial independence. Colonial legislatures made laws locally, but their enactments had to receive gubernatorial assent and could be reviewed by imperial institutions. Colonial laws were understood to be vulnerable if “repugnant”—that is, contradictory—to English law as then understood.[30]

The imperial review structure mattered because it supplied leverage rather than daily supervision. A governor could refuse assent; legislation could go to the secretary of state and Board of Trade; the Privy Council could confirm, disallow, or leave it without action; and colonial cases could, in principle, reach the Privy Council or English courts on appeal. That framework did not prevent colonial assemblies from creating severe slave laws, but it meant those laws existed within an imperial legal order.[30][16][17]

Francis Williams brought a successful appeal to the Privy Council, an example of an imperial appeal operating within the unequal legal system governing enslaved people.[91][50][99][15]

For the novelist, Parliament should therefore appear not as absent from slavery but as operating at another level. A Westminster Act could make plantation property more marketable to creditors, impose trade duties on plantation produce and rival goods, or authorize colonial customs enforcement. A Jamaican or Barbadian Assembly, by contrast, supplied the close-grained legal machinery for coercing enslaved people in daily life.[30][1][45][16]

This difference decides the tone of legal scenes. Parliamentary law produces petitions, merchants, sugar planters’ agents, shipmasters, collectors, Admiralty proceedings, bonds, duties, seizures, and forfeitures. Plantation law produces passes or tickets, parish patrols, workhouses, slave courts, militia parties, corporal punishment, and the direct legal power of masters and white officials over Black people.[36][37][24]

The commercial pressure behind the Molasses Act was already visible in 1731, when a bill to prohibit imports of foreign sugar, molasses, and rum into Great Britain and the American colonies passed the Commons but was rejected by the Lords. British sugar-island planters had protested French-colony competition after France’s 1717 commercial change; the 1733 Act used practically prohibitory duties to pursue the earlier bill’s object.[8]

The northern mainland colonies had a material interest in resisting that protection: fish, lumber, and agricultural produce were exchanged for West Indian sugar, molasses, and rum, and this commerce supplied money for manufactured goods from England. That commercial pattern explains both the statute’s trade focus and the persistent incentive for smuggling.[8]

The later history of the debt statute made its property language explicit: on 19 July 1797 Parliament enacted 37 Geo. III c. 119, titled 《An Act to repeal so much of an Act, made in the fifth year of the Reign of His late Majesty King George the Second, intituled, An Act for the more easy Recovery of Debts in His Majestys Plantations and Colonies in America, as make Negroes Chattels for the Payment of Debts》.[1]

The Yorke–Talbot judgment deeply influenced legal opinion until Lord Mansfield’s judgment of 1772.[31]

Brewer states that later courts upheld the 1729 guidelines, including *Pearne v. Lisle* in 1749.[30]

Between 1714 and 1739, Parliament passed twenty-nine Acts on colonial trade, customs and piracy; its main focus remained America and India.[41]

### How did Jamaica’s plantation codes differ from British Parliamentary law

Jamaica’s plantation regime was locally made, locally administered, and strongly shaped by the island’s security crisis. In the 1730s, Jamaica was fighting the First Maroon War, a conflict with communities of formerly enslaved people who had established autonomous settlements in difficult interior country. The Assembly’s legislation and spending show that plantation security, not merely commercial regulation, was one of the island government’s central concerns.[46][47]

Jamaica had a representative colonial legislature with English-looking forms but local planter power. Its legislature consisted of governor, Council, and Assembly. The Assembly claimed powers and privileges like those of Westminster; the Council acted as an upper chamber and revised bills; the governor accepted or rejected them for the Crown. Most Assembly members were planters, with some merchants, doctors, and attorneys.[48][16]

After 1718, Jamaica’s Assembly met at least annually; its legislation passed readings and often a committee stage before requiring Council and gubernatorial approval.[16]

That institutional resemblance should not mislead a novelist into treating Jamaican law as merely English law transplanted without alteration. Jamaica’s 1664 act declared English laws and liberties to be in force on the island as the birthright of the King’s subjects, but the same act allowed the governor, Council, and Assembly to mitigate, alter, lessen, or enlarge those laws according to local circumstances.[49]

Jamaica’s 1664 reception statute excepted English fiscal statutes imposing subsidies, loans, aids, or impositions from the English law it applied on the island.[49]

The 1730s Jamaican slave-law system rested substantially on older colonial codes rather than a wholly new comprehensive code enacted in that decade. Jamaica had adopted a version of the Barbados slave law soon after representative government began, revised it after unrest, and had a major code in force by 1696. Scholarship characterizes most Jamaican slavery legislation before the 1790s as leaving the basic seventeenth-century framework intact while adjusting it to particular challenges.[15][16][47]

That layered legal structure is important. A Jamaican character in 1734 did not inhabit a legal vacuum awaiting a new Act of Parliament. He or she lived under inherited local regulations concerning slavery, local courts, policing, labour, movement, runaways, military security, and the authority of masters; new Assembly acts tended to add emergency powers or particular mechanisms rather than replace the whole system.[16][17]

A number of Jamaican measures were enacted in 1730. Their titles included 《An Act for the better regulating Slaves, and rendering free Negroes and Mulattoes more usesful》; 《An Act to prevent the selling of Powder to rebellious, or any other Negroes whatsoever》; and 《An Act to suppress the rebellious Negroes in the Windward Parts of this Island》. These titles show that the Assembly legislated directly on slave regulation, weapons, rebellion, and the status of free people of colour.[15][50]

Jamaican Acts of the 1730s also addressed arson as a matter of local security and alleged offences.[50][99][100][14][15]

The law was reinforced by military legislation. Between 1723 and 1738, the Jamaican Assembly authorized at least twenty-four Acts to raise parties against the Maroons, compared with six such Acts between 1699 and 1719. The scale of the increase shows that the 1730s Assembly was not simply administering settled plantation life; it was repeatedly mobilising coercive force against a sustained challenge to white control.[46]

In October 1734, the Assembly passed an Act putting martial law into force and levying Black and white labour to support military deployments. This is particularly useful for fiction because martial law was not merely an abstract threat. It could authorize emergency mobilisation in a colony where the state faced a real military struggle and where enslaved and free Black labour could be requisitioned alongside white labour.[46]

Jamaica’s fiscal structure reflected the same priority. Governor John Ayscough estimated that the Assembly had spent £105,000 on expeditions against the Maroons between 1729 and 1734. The island’s reported tax revenue was £27,107 in 1730, £40,000 in 1731, £22,221 in 1734, and £20,344 in 1737. The comparison shows why the Maroon war was a major colonial burden: Ayscough’s five-year estimated expenditure exceeded any one of those annual revenue figures several times over.[46]

Jamaican government devoted a very large share of revenue to security. One account estimates that Jamaicans spent around 60 or 70 percent of this money on defence against slave insurrections and foreign invasions. The two threats were linked in planter thinking: an external enemy might exploit a large enslaved population, while Maroon communities and rebellion threatened the colony from within.[46]

The Deficiency Acts formed another older mechanism of plantation security. These laws dated from the late seventeenth century and fined estate owners when white-to-Black ratios fell below prescribed levels. Funds could be diverted to military needs, including war against the Maroons; the laws rarely achieved their demographic aim, partly because employing white overseers could cost more than paying the statutory fine.[46]

The Deficiency Acts show how local law joined plantation management to public security. The owner’s failure to employ enough white people was not merely a private business choice. It triggered a colonial fiscal penalty because the Assembly saw the racial balance of plantation labour as a matter of insurrection risk.[51][46]

That regime did not operate only through statutes directed at the enslaved. Jamaican law and administration also used parish institutions. Parish vestries had responsibilities that included tracking down runaways, managing workhouses and jails, policing, local security, regulating markets, and collecting parish taxes. A runaway could therefore encounter not only a private owner or overseer but also parish officials and a public institutional system designed to return people to bondage.[48]

Jamaica’s slave courts were a distinctive local institution. The Assembly established separate courts for trying and sentencing enslaved people in 1664. The first capital tribunals consisted of one justice of the peace and two “sufficient Neighbors,” who adjudicated capital cases and passed sentence for owners to carry out; the number of judges increased in 1696.[47]

Through much of the eighteenth century, slave crimes were tried before irregular tribunals made up of three freeholders and two magistrates, the magistrates usually being prominent planters. There was no jury until 1788 and no opportunity for appeal even after later jury reforms. That is a fundamentally different procedural world from an ordinary English criminal trial idealised around jury participation.[47]

**Jamaican plantation justice was not ordinary English criminal justice applied to enslaved defendants; it was a separate, planter-dominated system built for rapid control and exemplary punishment.** Its composition—magistrates and freeholders rather than a twelve-person jury—and its lack of appeal made the legal authority of local white elites immediate and difficult to challenge.[47]

The source material describes Jamaican slave courts as ordering corporal punishments much more frequently than English courts. Whipping was used extensively, and mutilation for non-capital offences—including cutting ears, noses, or feet—continued in Jamaica after comparable punishments had ceased in Europe. Such punishments should not be presented as routine English metropolitan practice in the 1730s; they belonged to the colony’s racialised system of control.[47]

For capital offences, Jamaican authorities could intensify punishment beyond death itself. The courts commonly imposed post-mortem punishments for crimes including assault on a white person and rebellious conspiracy. The effect was to adapt the harshest symbols of treason punishment to the colonial slave order, treating violence against a white person as a danger to the entire system of white authority.[47]

This development drew upon, but radically enlarged, the English doctrine of petit treason. Under that English household doctrine, the murder of a person with legitimate authority—such as a master by a servant—could be treated as treasonous within the household. Jamaican lawmakers expanded the principle by treating every white person as possessing legitimate authority over every enslaved person.[47]

The legal logic was therefore racial and collective. In Jamaica, an enslaved person’s violence against a white person was not treated merely as an assault between two individuals. It could be read as an implicit threat of rebellion and the overthrow of white power. The point for fiction is that a white stranger, not merely the person’s own master, could be imagined as occupying a legally protected position of authority.[47]

The terror of the system was intended to be visible. In 1731, landowners in Carpenter’s Mountains petitioned the Assembly for a court nearer to them. They complained that transporting defendants roughly forty miles to Lacovia allowed many accused people to escape what they called 《just punishment》.[47]

The petitioners argued that holding the trial and punishment near the place of the alleged crime would create a stronger example. They stated that punishment in the neighbourhood would strike greater terror into other enslaved people than merely hearing that it had happened far away, even if a condemned person’s head could be displayed where the offence was committed.[47]

That petition is not proof that the Assembly granted the requested court. It is, however, direct evidence of how planter petitioners understood slave justice: it was meant not only to decide an individual accusation but also to intimidate an enslaved population through local spectacle.[47]

Jamaican execution culture differed from English public execution in the interpretation of one study. English executions are described as regular public dramas of state authority, whereas Jamaican executions were sporadic and local demonstrations of masters’ power. The difference matters for staging: a Jamaican execution scene need not look like an orderly metropolitan ritual before a broad civic public; it can be a concentrated assertion of planter authority over a specific district and enslaved community.[47]

Planters also used internal plantation hierarchy. Attorneys and overseers appointed drivers and headmen, almost always men, to subdue enslaved people, enforce labour discipline, and implement the work regime. These roles made the plantation’s coercive system work below the level of statute or court: the law authorised the structure, but daily discipline was exercised through people placed between owner and field labourer.[47]

Some drivers had privileges, including greater movement, larger dwellings, extra provision land, and sometimes guns for hunting or policing. The source describes armed drivers as a clear breach of Jamaican slave codes. That gap between written law and plantation practice is useful for fiction: a trusted driver might possess authority or mobility that an ordinary enslaved person legally could not claim, while still remaining enslaved and vulnerable to the system.[47]

The Assembly’s 1730–45 journal index records sustained legislative attention to enslaved people, including laws regarding slaves, more effectual punishment for crimes, prevention of enslaved people buying horses, raising enslaved people in His Majesty’s service, suppressing runaways, restricting access to gunpowder, and military parties. An index is not the full statutory text, but it demonstrates the breadth of matters treated as plantation security.[17]

Jamaican law did not operate in complete isolation from Britain. Locally initiated bills went through the Council and governor; after a session, Acts were sent to the secretary of state and Board of Trade; the Privy Council could confirm, disallow, or let them lie. In the period 1714–60, Jamaica had 531 Acts, of which 78 were confirmed and 30 were disallowed by the Privy Council.[16][17]

The figures do not mean that London directed ordinary slave discipline every day. They show a layered constitutional order. The colonial Assembly had substantial initiative; the governor, Board of Trade, and Privy Council could impede or review legislation; and Parliament asserted imperial authority. Jamaican planter law was local in operation but imperial in constitutional setting.[16][17][30]

Imperial intervention could be concrete. An Order in Council dated 13 October 1732 forbade Jamaica’s governor to approve Acts imposing duties on enslaved people imported into Jamaica or exported within twelve months after arrival. That order concerned duties on enslaved people as objects of commerce; it did not regulate plantation punishment. Still, it demonstrates that London could limit a Jamaican governor’s legislative authority in a matter touching the slave trade.[52]

The Crown repealed Jamaica’s Francis Williams measure in 1732, another instance of imperial review checking colonial legislation.[91]

The 1739 peace settlement with the Maroons changed the island’s security system. It ended a costly phase of warfare and enrolled Maroons in a new role as military auxiliaries and policemen for the Jamaican state. The arrangement was funded by colonial taxation and, according to a later description, involved suppressing insurrections and returning runaway enslaved people to their masters.[46][53]

The 1739 treaties should not be treated as slave-code legislation. They were political and military agreements. But they belong in the legal and coercive landscape of late-1730s Jamaica because they transformed former enemies into an organised force that helped enforce plantation control, particularly by recovering fugitives.[46][53]

The contrast with Parliamentary law is therefore functional. The Molasses Act imposed duties, created customs procedures, and punished resistance to customs officers. Jamaica’s Assembly authorised military parties, emergency martial law, local slave tribunals, coercive policing, and the punishment of alleged rebellion and escape. Parliament and imperial government supplied constitutional authority, property rules, trade rules, troops, and review; local planter institutions supplied much of the immediate machinery of terror.[37][46][47]

The difference was not that English law was humane and Jamaica’s law cruel. That would be historically misleading. English legal and imperial structures helped make slavery legally and commercially viable, while Parliament’s 1732 debt legislation strengthened property claims in enslaved people. The distinction is that Jamaican law made the bodily and local consequences of slavery unusually explicit and administratively immediate.[30][1][47]

Jamaica’s political circumstances sharpened that local severity. The island had a very large enslaved population—one table gives 96,946 enslaved people out of a total population of 105,000 in 1730—and it faced the Maroon war. This demographic and military setting helps explain why legislators treated slave control as a matter of military security, fiscal policy, and emergency law rather than merely private plantation management.[46]

The 1730s Jamaican Assembly was also unusually active. Estimates put its output at about fifteen to twenty Acts per year from the 1730s to the 1760s, compared with about five per year in 1714. Conflict between Assembly and governors had previously obstructed legislation, but the First Maroon War and Governor Edward Trelawney’s tenure from 1738 helped produce a more unified political environment and more efficient lawmaking.[16]

For a novel, this means that a Jamaican planter, magistrate, militia officer, or Assembly member can plausibly speak of lawmaking as active and urgent. The period is not one of static inherited rules alone. Older slave codes remained foundational, but emergency measures, military acts, petitions, and local regulations were being debated and enacted against a background of warfare and fear.[16][17][46]

What follows for Jamaica is clear: a 1730s character encounters plantation control chiefly through local law—courts without ordinary juries, plantation discipline, anti-Maroon expeditions, patrols, martial law, and spectacular punishment—while Parliamentary law forms the wider commercial and constitutional frame rather than the everyday code of coercion.[47][46][30]

In 1730, enslaved people made up 92.3% of Jamaica’s recorded total population—about 12.0 enslaved people for every non-enslaved person—placing the Maroon-war security policies in a colony with an exceptionally stark demographic imbalance.[46]

Ayscough’s £105,000 estimate for anti-Maroon expeditions over 1729–34 was 2.6–5.2 times each of the reported single-year tax revenues for 1730, 1731, 1734, and 1737. This puts the expenditure in proportion to the colony’s available annual revenue and underscores the fiscal intensity of the war.[46]

Jamaica’s 1733 Act No. 336 gives the local coercive system a particularly concrete form. Enacted by the Governor, Council, and Assembly, it ordered armed parties 《to march after, attack, purſue, and deſtroy all runaway and rebellious Ne- groes, and root up, burn, and deſtroy all Kinds of Proviſions they ſhall meet with》. Its preamble linked suppression to settlement and cultivation and to British revenue, trade, navigation, and consumption of British manufactures.[14]

A Jamaican act of 1735 was titled “An Act for the Encouragement of good and able Men to march from the Barracks against the rebellious Negroes, during Martial-law; and for making Satisfaction to such Persons as may lose any Slaves in any Engagement or March after the rebellious Negroes; and for providing for the Sick and wounded.” Its title expressly linked anti-rebellion expeditions to compensation for owners who lost enslaved people.[14]

The same 1733 Act required parish justices and vestries to value enslaved men raised for service so that a master, owner, proprietor, agent, or attorney could be paid their value if they were killed, died, or were maimed; the owner then had to supply another fit and able person within ten days after notice. It also provided mile-money for taking up a skulking or runaway Negro, payable by the known owner or otherwise by the precinct deputy marshal. These provisions joined military coercion to monetary valuation and replacement of enslaved labour.[14]

Other 1730s Jamaican Acts show control in ordinary commercial and transport settings. A 1735 Act allowed a justice to impose corporal punishment not exceeding thirty-one lashes on an enslaved person who sold or gave away sugar or sugar-canes without the owner’s ticket. A 1736 Assembly law required every entering shipmaster or commander to swear that he had not and would not hire an enslaved person except from the owner or from a person holding the specified certificate.[14]

Jamaican legislation also treated enslaved people as assets available for enforcement against their owners. The 1730 highway statute authorized collection of a penalty by 《Diſtreſs and Sale of the Negroes, Cattle, Mules, and Goods》 of the offender. Under Act No. 363 of 1735, where sale of enslaved people or other effects produced more than the debt and costs, the provost marshal had to return the surplus to the owner within ten days if demanded.[14]

The older statutory framework supplied explicitly harsh criminal machinery. Under the 1696 Act for the better Order and Government of Slaves, a person enslaved in Jamaica for at least three years who ran away for twelve months was deemed rebellious and could be transported by two justices and three freeholders even if no other crime appeared. On return after transportation, or if the owner failed to send the condemned person away, a justice could cause execution by hanging or otherwise. In capital proceedings, the tribunal could sentence death, transportation, dismemberment, or another punishment it judged fit, and one enslaved person’s evidence against another was deemed sufficient proof.[14]

The older Jamaican code in force in the 1730s was the *Act for the Better Order and Government of Slaves*, consolidated in 1696. It was not a new 1730s enactment.[11]

Jamaica did not significantly revise its slave code in the 1730s; its regime rested on foundational late-seventeenth- and early-eighteenth-century Acts, amended and supplemented through the era. The chattel provision sometimes quoted for payment of debts and legacies—《all slaves shall be deemed and taken as all other goods and chattels are in the hands of executors or administrators》—belongs to Jamaica’s 1809 Act, 50 Geo. III, with a 1756 abridgment also identified, not to a 1730s Jamaican statute.[54]

In the Jamaican punishment process, the court that condemned an enslaved person also fixed the owner’s compensation: it could award no more than £100 for each enslaved person sentenced, or no more than £50 where the conviction was for running away. When an enslaved person was sentenced to death or confinement to hard labour for life and was valued, the receiver-general paid that valuation from public funds.[11]

The Assembly’s 1733 Act No. 336 made anti-Maroon warfare an express statutory object.Its preamble linked that suppression to opening valuable land to settlement and cultivation and to protecting 《His Majesty's Revenue in Great Britain, as well as the Trade, Navigation, and Consumption of the British Manufactures in this Island》.[14] The Act also provided rewards differentiated by sex and age for rebellious people killed or captured alive: £40 for a man, £20 for a woman, and £10 for a person under fourteen.[14]

Act No. 336 also treated compelled enslaved labour as economically compensable property of an owner. Parish justices and vestries had to value each person raised for service, so that a master, owner, proprietor, agent, or attorney could receive the person’s value from the Receiver-General if that person was killed, died, or was maimed in service; the owner then had to send another fit and able enslaved person within ten days after notice.[14] This is valuation and compensation language, not an express statutory declaration that enslaved people were chattels.[14]

A Jamaican martial-law measure of 1735 promised owners “Satisfaction” for enslaved people lost during operations against rebels.[16][17][14]

Jamaican enactments also regulated enslaved people’s commercial dealings and hiring. Act No. 364 of 1735 provided: 《That no Slave Scheu presume to sell or give away any Sugar or Sugar-canes, without a Ticket from the Owner of Proprietor of such Slave, on Pain of such Corporal Punishment, to be inflicted on such Slave or Slaves by order of any Justice, as such Justice shall think fitting, not exceeding Thirty One Lashes.》 A 1736 Assembly law required a ship’s master or commander, on entry, to swear that he had not and would not hire an enslaved person except from the owner or from a person holding the specified certificate of liberty to hire himself or herself.[14]

### How did Barbados’s plantation codes differ from British Parliamentary law

Barbados offers the clearest contrast between a Parliamentary trade statute and a local slave-control system. In 1731, the Barbados Assembly enacted a new Act specifically titled 《an act for the punishment of run-away slaves》. At the same time, older slave-code provisions continued to govern movement, capture, confinement, corporal punishment, death penalties, and masters’ disciplinary immunity.[24]

The key point is chronological. The island did not receive a wholly new comprehensive slave code in 1731. Its major code had been rewritten in 1688; that 1688 Act remained on the books until 1826. Thus a Barbados setting in the 1730s should combine new measures, such as the 1731 runaway Act, with older provisions accumulated from the seventeenth century.[21][24]

The August 1688 Barbadian *Act for the Governing of Negroes* nullified earlier governing laws and remained in force, with modifications, until 1826.[75]

The original Barbados slave code had been enacted in 1661, though the best surviving text is an amended 1667 version and the law was comprehensively rewritten in 1688. Contemporary and later writers often treat the Barbados code as one of the earliest comprehensive English colonial slave codes and as an important model for Jamaica and other colonies.[21][55][22]

The 1661 preamble itself stated that English law gave Barbados lawmakers no 《Tract to guide Us》 and no 《Rule set Us how to Governe such Slaves》. That sentence does not mean English legal ideas had no influence. It means the local legislature claimed that the English legal corpus lacked a ready-made operational code for governing a plantation slave population, so colonial lawmakers would create provisions suited to island conditions.[21][55]

The same preamble used property language. It said enslaved people should be protected as masters protected 《mens other goods and Chattells》, while also acknowledging them as 《created men》. This language is important because it captures the central legal contradiction of the code: it treated people as property while conceding, in the same breath, that they were human beings.[21][25][55]

Barbados also developed special property classifications in older law. A 1668 Act provided that enslaved people in intestate estates should be 《Estates Real, and not Chattels》, descending to the heir and widow rather than being sold separately; a 1672 modification maintained a real-estate treatment in one setting while providing that they should 《continue chattels for the payment of debts》 and could be recovered in personal lawsuits. That distinction reflects inheritance and debt law, not any recognition of personhood.[25]

The Barbadian rule allowing enslaved people to “continue chattels for the payment of debts” was renewed and made perpetual in 1681; it also allowed their recovery through personal actions.[25]

Those older classifications matter because a legal category could change according to transaction. An enslaved person could be treated as real estate in intestacy, chattel for debt payment, an asset in a lawsuit, and a person criminally punishable under a slave code. This was not an accidental inconsistency; it let owners and colonial government use different legal rules as needed for inheritance, credit, sale, recovery, labour, and punishment.[25][30]

In 1731, Barbados imposed an escalating corporal regime upon runaways and enslaved people who concealed them: twenty-one lashes for a first offence, thirty-nine lashes for a second, and thirty-nine lashes plus branding with an R on the right cheek for a third. The statute is cited in Richard Hall’s 1764 collection at pages 286–87 and in John Baskett’s 1732 collection at page 223.[24][19]

That law illustrates the public character of plantation punishment. The lashes were numerically specified. The branding was permanent and legible: an R on the right cheek, imposed with a hot iron. The punishment did not merely correct conduct; it marked the body in a way that assisted surveillance and attached the identity of “runaway” to the person’s face.[24]

Branding itself was not new in Barbados. It had been common for serious offences by the mid-seventeenth century. The significance of 1731 is that branding appears to have been legislated specifically as a punishment for repeat offending in this runaway context. The 1731 law was formally repealed only by the 1826 consolidation Act.[24]

The same 1731 legislation continued authority for armed patrols to pursue runaways, limiting them to ten men. That limit remained until 1826, when a later law raised the maximum to twenty. A 1730s Barbadian scene can therefore plausibly include a formally authorised armed party of up to ten men searching for fugitives.[24]

This was part of a much older legal system for controlling movement. Barbados required enslaved people away from their owner’s property to carry written authorisation—a ticket—signed by the owner or an authorised agent. The ticket was meant to show both permission to be absent and that the person was on lawful or necessary business. The system originated in 1652 or earlier and was incorporated into the 1688 Act.[24]

The ticket system was not a trivial administrative formality. It converted movement into a legally supervised privilege. A person without a ticket could be treated as a runaway or as unlawfully absent; a written slip, signed by an owner or overseer, could make the same journey lawful. That makes a ticket an excellent prop for a novelist: a small piece of paper could determine whether a Black person travelling along a road was treated as a servant on business, a fugitive, or a target for arrest.[24][55]

Older Barbados law also required regular searches of slave houses for fugitives. The 1661 code directed plantation management to search twice a week; the 1688 Act repeated the requirement but reduced it to twice monthly. The search requirements remained unrepealed until 1826, although one historian considers regular enforcement unlikely except during short periods following discovery of revolt conspiracies.[24]

The difference between written law and practice should be preserved in fiction. It would be accurate to portray the legal right and duty to search enslaved people’s dwellings as part of the 1730s structure. It would be less accurate to assume that every plantation faithfully searched twice each month in ordinary times. A search may therefore be especially plausible after a rumour of conspiracy, a nearby escape, stolen weapons, or fear of a planned rebellion.[24][56]

Barbadian law also created a public system for capturing and returning fugitives. A captured runaway who was not directly restored to the owner could be taken to the provost marshal for confinement. The owner had to reimburse the treasury for the capture reward and pay custody charges; the provost marshal could owe compensation if a captive died from lack of food or escaped; and an unclaimed captive could be sold at public auction.[24]

That system shows that escape was not solely a private dispute between owner and enslaved person. It involved public officers, confinement, treasury payments, fees, liability, and eventually sale. It turned the recovery of human beings into an organised governmental process, with monetary incentives for capture and financial responsibility for custodians.[24]

The legal sanctions for flight could be lethal. A death penalty for specified long absences was first enacted in 1676, was inadvertently repealed in 1688, renewed in 1692 after a revolt conspiracy and continued absconding, and was not repealed until 1819. It was therefore part of the legal background still in force in the 1730s.[24]

The precise condition described in the older law was severe. Barbados laws of 1676 and 1692 prescribed death for captured Maroons who had lived in Barbados for at least a year and had been absent for a month or more. The term “Maroons” in this context refers to enslaved people who had run away and lived outside their owners’ control, not necessarily members of a permanent independent settlement like the Jamaican Maroons.[24]

The 1730s Barbados legal order therefore contained a graduated system: ticket requirements and patrols to prevent unauthorised movement; arrest, confinement, and return for fugitives; lashes and branding for harbouring runaways; and, in defined long-absence cases under older law, death. This was detailed public policing, not merely an owner’s informal discipline.[24]

Masters nevertheless had broad disciplinary authority. A provision first enacted in 1661 and renewed in 1688 stated that if an enslaved person died under punishment inflicted by the master or the master’s agent for running away or another crime, no person was legally accountable. That provision was not repealed until 1826.[24]

The wording was stark. The code stated that if a person enslaved 《under punishment by his Master or his order》 for running away or another offence suffered in life or limb, 《no person whatsoever shall bee accomptable to any Law therefore》. This was not a mere failure to prosecute in practice. It was a formal statutory immunity for death or bodily loss caused in specified disciplinary punishment.[21][55]

The code made an important distinction when the victim belonged to another owner. If one person killed another owner’s enslaved person, the consequences could include compensation to the owner and payment to the public treasury. The structure protected the owner’s economic interest rather than treating the enslaved person’s death as a wrong committed against that person as a legal individual.[55][24]

This is one reason property law and punishment cannot be separated in plantation society. A master’s violence against his own enslaved person could be insulated from legal responsibility; violence against someone else’s enslaved person could create a payment obligation because it damaged another owner’s property. The law thereby allocated power and loss among white proprietors.[55][21][25]

Older Barbadian law imposed severe punishment for violence against white Christians. Under the 1661 code, a first offence of striking or other violence brought severe whipping. A second offence brought severe whipping, slitting the nose, and burning part of the face. For a third offence, the governor and Council could impose greater corporal punishment.[21]

The code also assigned lesser misconduct to the master in the first instance. Petty felonies under twelve pence and small broils or misdemeanours were to be heard and determined by the enslaved person’s master. If the injured complainant was dissatisfied with the master’s punishment, the matter could be taken to a justice of the peace, who could impose corporal punishment not injurious to life, limb, or member.[21]

This arrangement divided authority between private plantation discipline and public magistracy. The master was the ordinary tribunal for minor misconduct, but a white complainant could demand satisfaction from a justice if the master did not punish enough. The enslaved person’s own grievance was not the relevant legal trigger; the system was designed around the claims of owners and injured parties.[21][25]

For serious accusations, Barbados used a special criminal procedure rather than a jury trial. The 1661 code provided for two justices and three freeholders to hear evidence in capital cases. It explicitly contrasted that procedure with trial by a jury of twelve, saying enslaved people did not deserve the legal trial of twelve men enjoyed by English subjects.[55]

That older procedure is vital to the comparison with Parliament and England. Colonial law did not simply impose English criminal law on Black defendants. It constructed a separate tribunal, populated by local white officeholders and freeholders, for the swift adjudication of capital offences. The procedure itself marked enslaved people as outside the ordinary legal safeguards claimed for English subjects.[55][25]

The 1661 code also authorised military law for rebellion. If enslaved people made insurrection, prepared arms, powder, bullets, or offensive weapons, or held a council or conspiracy for rebellion, the governor or superior officer could call military officers to proceed by martial law and punish actors, planners, supporters, and concealers by death or other penalties.[21]

Again, this older law remained part of the historical foundation of the 1730s. Barbados faced a major alleged conspiracy in 1736, after earlier conspiracies in 1692 and 1701. That context makes renewed patrols, searches, weapons fears, sudden arrests, and exemplary punishments historically plausible, even though the specific statutory response to the 1736 episode is not supplied here.[56][24][21]

The law also authorised pursuit violence. Under the 1661 code, a justice, constable, or company captain who learned the hiding place of fugitives could raise and arm up to twenty men to seize runaway enslaved people alive or dead. The Act set sugar rewards for capture or killing during capture, including different amounts according to the period of absence.[21]

The 1731 patrol law’s ten-man limit should be read beside this earlier twenty-man authority with care. They are not necessarily the same legal mechanism. The older code described a specified pursuit power; the 1731 Act continued an armed-patrol authorization limited to ten men. For fiction, use the exact statutory setting: a general patrol may be capped at ten, while an older emergency provision might support a different form of armed pursuit.[24][21]

The differences from Parliamentary law are sharp. Parliament’s Molasses Act regulated imports of molasses, rum, sugar, and paneles; required customs entries and payment; and authorised seizure and forfeiture of goods. Barbados’s plantation laws regulated people’s movement, tickets, dwellings, capture, imprisonment, compulsory return, corporal punishment, branding, mutilation, death, and masters’ immunity.[37][24]

The contrast should not be stated as if Parliament had no connection to slavery. Parliament legislated in 1732 to make colonial slave property reachable for debt, and its trade law protected British sugar-colony interests. But Parliament did not provide, in the Molasses Act, a list of lashes for a runaway, a ticket rule, a slave tribunal, a patrol limit, or immunity for a master whose punishment killed an enslaved person. Those were colonial legal functions.[1][5][37][2][24]

Barbados’s local slave laws operated within an imperial framework. Colonial laws were expected not to be repugnant to English law, and imperial authorities could approve, disallow, or otherwise review colonial legislation. The history of a Barbadian detinue law illustrates that such review could matter: the Board of Trade overturned a Barbados law designed to secure possession of enslaved people in 1701 and again acted against it between 1700 and 1702.[30]

That oversight did not make the island’s slave law mild or uniform with England. Barbados’s 1661 preamble itself asserted that English law lacked a rule for governing slaves, and local statutes filled the gap with a detailed coercive regime. Imperial review was a constitutional constraint and a route for property and commercial policy; it was not a substitute for local plantation law.[21][30][22]

The Barbadian code also reveals the mixed nature of slave law. It contained some rules that could be described as protective—for example, provisions relating to food for captured runaways in custody, and language warning against arbitrary cruelty. Yet its central machinery focused on public order, runaway recovery, and protection of owners’ authority. Of its twenty-three clauses in the 1661 Act, well over half addressed fugitives or runaways.[25][24]

That mixture should not be romanticised. A formal food duty for a captive in the provost marshal’s custody did not alter the larger regime of capture, sale, whipping, branding, and execution. Likewise, the preamble’s acknowledgment that enslaved people were “created men” coexisted with the statutory treatment of them as goods and chattels, assets, and subjects of punishments that could disfigure or kill.[21][24]

Barbados’s legal structure made plantation control public in another sense: it enlisted white society beyond the individual owner. Constables, justices, captains, freeholders, provost marshals, treasury officials, patrols, and courts all had roles. A white person who encountered a Black traveller without a ticket or suspected a runaway did not need to be the owner to participate in enforcement.[21][24][55]

That broad enforcement structure explains why escape was so dangerous even on a small island. Flight challenged an owner’s command over labour and property, but it also became a public concern because fugitives were imagined as sources of disorder, theft, conspiracy, and rebellion. The legal response therefore combined incentives for capture with highly visible deterrent punishments.[24]

**In Barbados in the 1730s, slave control was a public legal system of passes, searches, patrols, capture, confinement, judicial punishment, branding, and in certain cases death; it was not merely the private will of a master.** Parliament’s trade and debt measures supported the wider plantation economy, but the detailed law of the enslaved body was made and administered on the island.[24][45]

A novelist should also distinguish between enactment date and law in force. The 1731 punishment for harbouring runaways was new in the requested decade. The ticket system, slave-house searches, special capital tribunals, martial-law rebellion procedures, master disciplinary immunity, and some death penalties were older rules carried forward from the seventeenth century and still operative in the 1730s.[24]

That distinction avoids a common anachronism. It would be inaccurate to write that Barbados suddenly invented plantation coercion in 1731. It is accurate to write that the Assembly added or continued specific anti-runaway measures in 1731 within an already mature legal regime whose older provisions remained central to everyday power.[24]

What follows for Barbados is that a 1730s scene should make the law layered: a new 1731 anti-runaway Act can supply exact lash-and-branding penalties, while the older 1688 framework supplies tickets, searches, capture machinery, special trials, and wider owner authority.[24]

The 1731 alteration did not end all statutory capital exposure for flight in the accounts before the Court. One account states that the Barbados legislature removed execution as a legally prescribed punishment for running away in 1731 and substituted whipping and branding; it gives twenty-one lashes for a first offence, thirty-nine for a second, and, for a third, thirty-nine lashes and an R-brand, with further punishment at a justice’s discretion, 《life and limb excepted》.[19] This account differs from the older-law account that describes a death penalty for defined long absences as continuing until 1819.[24][19]

A 1739 Barbados amendment directed courts to impose lesser sentences on first-time Black offenders whose crimes were not 《of a heinous nature》. It made a punishment short of death a matter for negotiation between the slaveholder and prosecutor, with the governor deciding if they could not agree; the stated practical context was the cost to public revenue of compensating owners after executions and owners’ objections to execution.[19]

A further Barbados enactment in the requested decade was the 1739 Act amending the 1688 《An Act for the Governing of Negroes》 and regulating the maintenance of people thereafter manumitted. It confirms that the 1688 Act supplied the live procedural framework in 1739: under the older Act, a death sentence imposed by justices and freeholders against 《any Negro or other Slave》 had been executed immediately, without stay or respite.[23]

The 1739 Act altered that procedure in property-centred terms. Where a capital charge was not heinous and the accused was not an old offender, justices and freeholders were to seek a compromise between prosecutor and 《owner or possessor》, aimed at saving the enslaved person’s life while repairing the prosecutor’s loss or injury. If a party insisted on condemnation, the enslaved person was committed to the common gaol while the governor or commander in chief and Council considered the evidence; after a death judgment, an owner or possessor could obtain a ten-day stay to seek a writ of error.[23]

The same 1739 Act gave a slave’s corroborated testimony against a free Negro, Indian, or Mulatto the legal force it would have if the witness were free, baptized, and not in bondage. It also made a person who manumitted someone deposit £50 current money with the churchwarden for each person freed, from which the parish vestry was to direct £4 current money annually for that person’s maintenance.[23] Thus Barbados in 1739 combined a narrowly enlarged colonial procedure in capital cases with a financial barrier to manumission.[23]

The 1739 Barbadian manumission payment requirement was associated with the abandonment of elderly or sick people.[26]

The Barbados code’s 1661 preamble described enslaved people as 《heathenish, brutish, and an uncertaine dangerous kind of people》. It also said that lawmakers would 《protect them as we doe many other goods and Chattels, and alsoe somewhat further as being created Men》.[11]

Clause 1 of the Barbados code barred enslaved people from leaving the plantation on Sabbath or holy days without a written ticket from the master. The penalty was 500 pounds of Muscovado sugar, recoverable before a justice of the peace, and an enslaved person found without a ticket was subject to 《moderate whipping》.[11]

Barbados’s 1739 amendment changed the older 1688 capital-case procedure. Under the 8 August 1688 *Act for the Governing of Negroes*, a death sentence imposed by justices and freeholders was immediately executed without stay or respite, preventing the owner or possessor from bringing a writ of error.[23]

The 1739 Act directed justices and freeholders in a capital trial, where they found the charge not heinous and the accused not an old offender, to try to compromise the offence between the prosecutor and owner or possessor. Their stated objects were saving the enslaved person’s life and satisfying and repairing the prosecutor’s loss or injury.[23]

Where either party insisted on condemnation and execution, the 1739 Act required the justices to commit the enslaved person to the common gaol and certify all evidence, proceedings, and their opinion to the governor or commander in chief and Council. Those colonial authorities could confirm, disapprove, or alter the justices’ decision, prevent execution, or direct strict enforcement; the owner or possessor bore the cost of continued imprisonment pending that determination.[23]

The 1739 Act also provided that an enslaved person’s evidence against a free Negro, Indian, or Mulatto, whether baptized or not, was to be received and have the same legal effect as free testimony when supported by 《very good and sufficient corroborating circumstances》.[23]

The same 1739 Act required anyone manumitting an enslaved person by deed, will, or other means to deposit £50 current money with the parish churchwarden for that person’s support.
In Barbados, private manumission rested on custom rather than a general statute granting owners permission to free enslaved people. It derived from the property holder’s right to alienate, dispose of, or relinquish title to property; after fees were established in 1739, the Barbados legislature tried to curtail manumission.[57]

The 1731 Barbados change also marked a departure from the earlier prescribed penalty for flight: the legislature removed execution as a legally prescribed punishment for running away. Thereafter the law prescribed whipping and branding for runaways, and execution was never again mandated for that offence.[19] For additional attempts to escape, the 1731 law allowed a justice of the peace to impose further punishment judged sufficient, 《life and limb excepted》.[19]

### What about the British Empire’s 1730s division between Parliamentary trade

The empire’s legal division was institutional: Parliament’s 1732 debt law and 1733 Molasses Act addressed colonial credit, property, trade, customs, and forfeiture, while Jamaican and Barbadian assemblies supplied the local rules for slave control and punishment.[1][2][45][16] Parliament passed trade, customs, credit, and property legislation that helped structure an Atlantic economy based on enslaved labour.

Colonial assemblies created and maintained the immediate rules by which enslaved people were controlled, punished, recovered after escape, and tried for alleged crimes.[45][30][1][16][22]

The Molasses Act belongs on the Parliamentary side of that division. Its title, preamble, duties, entry requirements, forfeiture provisions, and penalties all concern trade. It protected British sugar-colony commerce against foreign competition by taxing foreign rum, molasses, sugar, and paneles imported into British American colonies.[5][37]

The law’s own subject should guide the novelist. A merchant, sugar factor, customs collector, shipmaster, rum distiller, or New England trader would care about the Molasses Act because it altered the price and legality of imported supplies. A plantation owner might care because it promised protection from foreign sugar-colony competition. An enslaved field worker would experience any consequence only indirectly, through the plantation economy, prices, labour demands, or the owner’s wealth—not because the Act itself created a new corporal punishment or defined that person as chattel.[36][37][51][2]

The direct enforcement tools under the Act were commercial. Customs officers could demand entry and duty payment before landing; improperly landed goods could be seized; goods and their value could be forfeited; cases could go to colonial Admiralty courts or courts of record; and those who resisted officers faced £50 and three months’ imprisonment.[37]

The direct enforcement tools under Jamaican and Barbadian plantation law were bodily and local. They included slave courts, martial law, armed pursuit parties, tickets, searches, confinement, whippings, branding, mutilation, public execution, and the authority of masters and overseers. This is the practical distinction the novel needs.[47][46][24]

The Molasses Act did involve colonial officials. Governors, magistrates, customs officers, Admiralty courts, and courts of record could participate in its enforcement. That overlap does not turn it into a slave code. It shows that imperial trade law relied on colonial institutions for execution, just as plantation law relied on those institutions in other capacities.[37][2]

The fact that both kinds of law used colonial courts should not obscure their subjects. A customs prosecution asked whether rum, molasses, sugar, or paneles had been lawfully imported, entered, and duty-paid. A plantation prosecution or tribunal could ask whether an enslaved person had run away, struck a white person, conspired, carried weapons, or committed another alleged offence under local slave law.[37][2][47][24]

The economic connection between the two legal worlds was nevertheless close. The Molasses Act was advanced by British West Indian plantation interests seeking protection against French and Dutch competition. British sugar islands mattered to the kingdom’s trade, navigation, and strength; sugar plantations depended on enslaved labour; and Parliament’s intervention was intended to sustain the commercial position of those colonies.[36][58][41][51]

That connection supports a careful formulation. The Molasses Act was not a law of slave control in the narrow sense of setting discipline, punishments, or legal status for enslaved people. It was, however, a law designed to protect an economy in which plantation production depended on enslaved labour. Trade regulation and slaveholding were economically interdependent without being legally identical.[5][37][59][51]

The same point applies to the Navigation Acts generally. Enforcement of Navigation Act tariff protection shielded Jamaican planters from cheaper French sugar. Parliament’s commercial policy therefore mattered greatly to plantation wealth and to the political power of slaveholders, even where a statute did not mention enslaved people.[46][44][41]

For a novelist, this means that a Jamaican planter might sincerely speak of the Molasses Act as essential to “our sugar interest,” “the trade,” or “the strength of the kingdom,” while an enslaved person would more immediately know the local rules on movement, work, punishment, and escape. These are different lived relationships to the same imperial order.[36][5][47][24]

The 1732 debt statute is the closer Parliamentary analogue to colonial property law: it made 《Houses, Lands, Negroes, and other Hereditaments and real Estates》 liable for colonial debts, while a modern account describes its effect as chattel property for debtor-creditor relations.[1] It expressly placed 《Negroes》 among houses, lands, hereditaments, and real estates liable for debts; a later legal-history account describes its effect as making enslaved people chattel property for debtor-creditor relations.[1] That statute gives a far better basis than the Molasses Act for a scene involving a creditor’s claim, bankruptcy, estate settlement, mortgage pressure, or sale to satisfy debt.[1][31]

The distinction becomes especially important in a scene involving sale or seizure. If a merchant seeks payment from a Jamaican or Barbadian planter, the 1732 debt law can explain why enslaved people are treated as assets reachable by creditors. If a customs officer seizes a cargo of French molasses, the 1733 Act explains the forfeiture. If a plantation manager hunts a runaway, the relevant law is local slave law.[1][37][24]

Colonial legislatures were separate lawmaking bodies from Parliament. Scholarship tracking legislation across the British Atlantic distinguishes British and Irish parliamentary legislation from the legislation of colonial assemblies in North America and the West Indies. That institutional distinction is basic: a Jamaican Act was not an Act of Parliament, even if it later passed through imperial review.[60][16][17]

The colonies were also not legally independent states. Colonial legislation had to pass through local constitutional stages and was exposed to review by metropolitan authorities. Royal governors, the Board of Trade, the Privy Council, and sometimes Parliament itself formed a network through which colonial laws could be supervised or blocked.[30][16]

Jamaica illustrates this mixed structure particularly clearly. Its Assembly claimed Westminster-like privileges and had real local initiative. Yet bills required the governor’s assent, and Acts were sent to London for Board of Trade and Privy Council review. In 1714–60, only 15 percent of Jamaican Acts were confirmed and 6 percent disallowed by the Privy Council; the balance could remain unconfirmed or lie without final action.[16]

The figures show that formal imperial oversight was substantial but not necessarily intrusive in every case. A study of Jamaican legislation says imperial institutions exercised their authority relatively sparingly: more than forty Jamaican Acts were disallowed between 1664 and 1760, about 4 percent of the total. Acts concerning slavery were twice as likely to be disallowed as other Acts, though this broad figure is not limited to the 1730s.[16]

That pattern makes good dramatic sense. A Jamaican Assembly could be confident in its power to legislate locally, while still fearing a governor’s refusal, a Board of Trade objection, a Privy Council disallowance, or a clash with imperial commercial policy. The law was locally made but never entirely local in political consequence.[16][17][48]

The constitutional language of “repugnancy” is useful for dialogue. A law “repugnant” to English law meant one deemed contradictory to it. Colonial lawmakers and lawyers could argue that a local measure conformed to English legal principles, adapted them to local necessity, or required special treatment because colonial conditions differed.[30][16]

Barbados’s 1661 preamble dramatizes precisely that problem. It asserted a local legislative power to make provisions not contradictory to English law, while also saying English law offered no rule for governing slaves. The colonial slave code thus presented itself as both within English legal authority and necessary because English law did not supply a practical plantation regime.[21][55]

Jamaica used a comparable structure. Its 1664 declaration brought English laws and liberties to the island but authorised local modification by the governor, Council, and Assembly. A Jamaican lawyer in the 1730s could therefore speak in the language of English liberty and local necessity at the same time, even when defending severe plantation measures.[49][16]

This is why it is misleading to imagine a simple opposition between English law and colonial law. English common law, royal policy, Parliamentary debt law, and colonial slave codes were interwoven, although each operated differently. The empire was legally plural: each colony had a distinct or “bespoke” legal system, but colonial norms were constrained in important respects by English law and imperial institutions.[34][30][1]

The scholarly disagreement about English law should be stated narrowly. One account says that slavery in the empire was legal because English law on slavery did not reach it overseas; another argues that English judges and Crown policy supplied an imperial common-law foundation that made people absolute property. The difference is whether one emphasizes legal separation or legal construction across the empire.[33][34][30][35]

Both positions reject the simplistic notion that the colonies invented slavery in complete isolation. The first still recognises that colonial slavery was legally secure in the empire; the second traces that security more directly to English judges, merchants, and Crown policy. For historical fiction, either interpretation supports the same practical warning: do not portray a planter code as an autonomous world disconnected from Britain.[61][33][30]

The Molasses Act makes that connection visible at the level of political economy. Its preamble called the welfare of the sugar colonies essential to British trade, navigation, and strength. Parliament legislated because plantation colonies were economically and strategically important to the kingdom.[36][5][37]

Its primary stated objective, however, remained commercial protection. Parliamentary proceedings in April 1733 focused on sugar and rum production, conditions in the sugar colonies, northern-colony lumber and fisheries, shipping, trade with French and Dutch colonies, and prices. Those are the topics of a trade bill, not the deliberations of a proposed universal slave-discipline code.[62]

A contemporary Commons speaker described the duties as regulation rather than revenue, and another argument described them as near-prohibitory. Later historical accounts agree that the Act was meant to favour British products over French West Indian competition and was pressed by British West Indian plantation interests.[42][51]

The law’s commercial purpose should not be diluted into a vague claim that it was “about slavery” because sugar was slave-produced. Such a claim loses the necessary distinction between a statute’s immediate legal operation and the wider economic system it supported. The immediate operation was duties and customs enforcement; the wider context was a slave-based plantation economy.[37][59]

For a scene set in a colonial port, the overlap can still be vivid. The ship may carry molasses whose sale supports rum production; rum may be part of a wider Atlantic economy entangled with the trade in enslaved people; a planter may use protected sugar profits to service debts secured on enslaved property; and the same colony may maintain slave patrols with local taxes. But none of this changes the statutory fact that the Molasses Act regulated goods, not enslaved persons.[40][63][1][46][2]

A character may also distinguish the Crown’s revenue officers from colonial plantation officials. A customs collector’s authority comes from the customs Act and the Crown’s commercial interest. A justice, slave-court freeholder, militia officer, overseer, provost marshal, or planter’s driver exercises a different kind of authority derived from colonial statute, office, property, or plantation hierarchy.[37][47][24]

This distinction can produce conflict. A colonial merchant may resent customs interference while supporting severe plantation discipline. A planter may invoke English liberties against revenue policy while demanding extraordinary local authority against enslaved people and Maroons. Jamaican white colonists, according to one study, defended colonial prerogatives while relying on imperial protection.[46]

That apparent contradiction is historically useful rather than inconsistent. Colonial elites could seek local control over taxation and legislation while also asking Britain for military aid, tariff protection, credit security, and commercial privileges. Their commitment was not to a consistent abstract theory of liberty but to the preservation of planter power and property.[46][30]

The 1739 Maroon settlement shows the same combination. Jamaica’s local government spent heavily fighting the Maroons, then negotiated a treaty that made them state-supported auxiliaries tasked in part with recovering runaways. Imperial troops and colonial taxation, local law, treaty arrangements, and plantation interests all converged in one coercive system.[46][53]

The law also had different temporal layers. Parliamentary measures could be enacted, expire, and be continued. The Molasses Act began in 1733, was originally limited to five years, and was continued in 1737. Colonial slave law could persist for decades or centuries: Barbados’s 1688 code lasted until 1826, and some provisions renewed in 1692 remained until 1819.[37][4][24]

That temporal contrast matters in a novel. A merchant may ask whether a customs Act is in force, when it began, or whether Parliament will renew it. A plantation magistrate may apply an old local rule as settled law, without caring that its first enactment predated his grandfather. Law on the ground was an accumulation of old code provisions, recent local amendments, administrative practice, and imperial statutes.[16][24][4]

The same distinction explains why older codes must not be mistaken for laws enacted in the 1730s. Barbados’s 1731 Act added specific punishment for concealing runaways, but the ticket regime, search requirements, tribunals, martial-law provisions, and master immunity were older. Jamaica’s 1730s Assembly enacted security measures while relying on a seventeenth-century slave-law framework and older judicial institutions.[24][47][16][46]

South Carolina provides a useful constitutional comparison, though its main slave code in the supplied material is later than the requested decade. In March 1736–37, South Carolina’s common-pleas legislation stated that local courts could not execute an English or British statute in America unless the plantations were specifically named, the statute extended to them by its terms, or provincial law made it effective there.[27] That clause captures a practical colonial rule of statutory applicability: not every English statute automatically governed a colony.[27]

The Molasses Act did expressly extend to British colonies and plantations in America, so it was the kind of Parliamentary statute that could operate there by its own terms. It referred repeatedly to colonies and plantations in America under the King’s dominion and authorised enforcement in colonial courts. This is a useful contrast with a general English statute not directed to the plantations.[37]

South Carolina’s 1735 Act for the better ordering and governing Negroes and other slaves appears in the statutory contents, alongside earlier slave laws of 1690, 1712, 1714, 1717, and 1722. The contents establish that the province had its own continuing sequence of locally enacted slave laws before the more famous 1740 code.[27]

The 1740 South Carolina Act should not be presented as a 1730s enactment. It was assented to on 10 May 1740, outside the decade. Its wording is useful only as a later comparator: it declared specified people and their descendants 《absolute slaves》 and 《chattels personal》, required tickets for movement, authorised whipping, created special tribunals, and imposed death for enumerated offences.[28]

The proper point is not that South Carolina lacked slave control in the 1730s. Its 1735 statute and earlier codes show that it had a local slave-law tradition. But a writer setting a novel before 1740 should not put the 1740 Act’s exact chattel-personal language, twenty-lash ticket penalty, or provisions into a 1730s scene as if they were already enacted.[27][28]

South Carolina also demonstrates that colonial law could regulate enslaved labour in public works. Earlier Acts authorised officials to press enslaved people for Charleston fortification work, imposed financial penalties on owners for failure to supply labour, and authorised “moderate correction” for refusal to obey work directions. These are colonial, not Parliamentary, mechanisms for converting enslaved labour into public military infrastructure.[27]

The broader pattern is consistent across the colonies named in the request. Parliamentary law set imperial terms for trade, shipping, customs, and colonial debt. Colonial law supplied the intensive legal apparatus of plantation society: owner authority, slave status in local property regimes, racialised criminal procedure, mobility restrictions, runaway recovery, militia and patrol powers, labour compulsion, and corporal punishment.[45][1][37][16][24][30]

That division should not be overstated into an absolute boundary. Parliament’s debt law affected colonial slave property directly. The Yorke–Talbot Opinion addressed the claimed status of enslaved people brought to Britain. Imperial officials reviewed colonial Acts. The Crown and English courts formed part of the wider legal environment in which slavery developed.[1][30]

Yet the difference in legal texture remains decisive. The Molasses Act speaks in the language of commodities, duty, entry, warrant, seizure, forfeiture, and prosecution. Barbados and Jamaica slave law speaks in the language of Negroes, slaves, masters, overseers, tickets, runaways, patrols, courts, whipping, branding, martial law, and death. Those vocabularies identify two distinct modes of imperial governance.[37][24][21][47]

**The 1730s empire therefore combined commercial legislation that protected and organised plantation wealth with colonial statutes that organised domination over enslaved people.** Trade law and slave law supported one another economically, but only the latter normally dictated how an enslaved person could move, be tried, be punished, or be forced back into bondage.[5][1][24][45]

### Synthesis

The evidence establishes a connected but divided legal order. Parliament’s 1732 debt statute strengthened the treatment of enslaved people as assets for creditors, while the 1733 Molasses Act protected British sugar-colony commerce through duties, customs entry, seizure, forfeiture, and penalties for resisting officers. Jamaica and Barbados, by contrast, used local plantation law to regulate bodies and movement: special tribunals, martial law, patrols, tickets, searches, confinement, lashes, branding, mutilation, execution, and runaway recovery.[1][37][46][47][24]

The colonies were not legally detached from Britain: governors, the Board of Trade, and the Privy Council reviewed local laws, and English legal ideas shaped colonial property claims. But the immediate coercive regime was principally colonial, layered from older codes and newer measures. For a 1730s novel, use the Molasses Act for cargo and customs disputes; use Jamaican or Barbadian law for plantation discipline and punishment.[30][16][24][2]

Judgment — The most useful recent scholarship does not treat legal dehumanization as a single rule that simply made enslaved people “things.” It examines the connected legal processes that made people saleable property, imposed inherited status, regulated their conduct and punishment, and linked enslavement to racial hierarchy. ## Why the Molasses Act? Scholarly interpretations

### What do recent scholars argue for the trade-regulation interpretation of

The Act’s operative provisions regulate trade; protecting markets for slave-produced sugar is a possible political-economic interpretation, not a slave-discipline clause.[38][2][59][63] Its formal name answers much of that question: 6 Geo.

II, c. 13, *An Act for the better securing and encouraging the Trade of His Majesty’s Sugar Colonies in America* (1733). The later 1764 Act recited the earlier statute under that title.[81] Its machinery was customs machinery—duties, shipping rules, forfeitures, and export repayments—not plantation discipline.[81][38]

The Act and the contemporary parliamentary speakers support a protectionist trade reading: Parliament used high customs duties to favour the British sugar colonies against foreign sugar-colony competition.[2][42] The Act’s preamble expressly linked the welfare of Britain’s sugar colonies to “the Trade, Navigation and Strength” of the kingdom and portrayed their planters as unable to trade “upon an equal Footing with the foreign Sugar Colonies” without advantage and relief from Great Britain.[36][38]

The principal import duties, which began on 25 December 1733, show the mechanism. They applied to commodities brought from American colonies outside the Crown’s dominions into British American colonies.[38]

| Imported foreign commodity | Duty under 6 Geo. II, c. 13, from 25 December 1733 | What the charge did |
|---|---:|---|
| Rum or spirits | 9d. per gallon | Raised the price of foreign Caribbean spirits in British American markets. [38] |
| Molasses or syrups | 6d. per gallon | Targeted the molasses on which northern colonial rum distilling relied. [38][58] From 25 December 1733, 6 Geo. II, c. 13 imposed the charge on molasses or syrups of foreign produce or manufacture imported into British American colonies. [36][77] |
| Sugars or paneles | 5s. per hundredweight avoirdupois | Protected the market position of sugar produced in the British islands. [38] |

The statute also regulated routes and re-export trade. It prohibited the import into Ireland of specified American sugars, syrups, molasses, rum, and spirits except those from the Crown’s sugar colonies and shipped through Great Britain in legally navigated vessels; breach meant forfeiture of the goods or value and of the vessel with its equipment. It gave drawbacks—repayment of duties—when qualifying colonial sugar was re-exported from Britain within one year, and added a repayment of 2s. per hundredweight for qualifying refined sugar exported from Britain.[38] These are features of mercantilism: the imperial policy of channelling colonial commerce through a protected British system.

Contemporary advocates described the measure not as a normal revenue tax but as an almost prohibitory trade restriction. In the Commons debate of March 1733, Sir John Barnard said the proposed duties concerned “the Regulation of Trade only” and were “rather in the Nature of a Prohibition”; he added that supporters did not expect or desire ordinary public revenue from them.[42] Colonel Bladen similarly said that the duties were intended to come close to an absolute prohibition because northern colonists’ commerce with the French islands enriched those islands at the British islands’ expense and raised the northern colonies toward independence.[43]

The hearings in the Lords likewise concentrated on the commercial facts of the case: northern-colony exchange with French and Dutch possessions; dealings with Indigenous peoples; shipping and its uses; lumber and its sale; and sugar prices in the sugar colonies.[62] The Board of Trade then circulated the enacted measure to American governors for execution as an Act for securing and encouraging the sugar-colony trade.[52]

Rönnbäck’s 2020 article concerns British sugar protection more broadly, rather than specifically interpreting the 1733 Molasses Act. It argues that British refiners, later in the sugar value chain, may have captured the principal gains from protectionist policies. London wholesale muscovado prices were generally not significantly higher than Amsterdam’s, while refined sugar was consistently dearer in London; Rönnbäck attributes the refined-sugar difference in part to prohibitive barriers against foreign refined sugar and limited competition among refiners.[59]

This qualification does not turn the Molasses Act into a slave-control statute. It sharpens the trade interpretation by warning a novelist not to assume that every protective imperial measure delivered its greatest profit to the Caribbean planter rather than to metropolitan merchants and refiners.[59]

The fiscal label has been expressed differently. Barnard described the proposed duties as trade regulation and near-prohibition rather than ordinary public revenue, while Parliament’s modern historical account calls the Molasses Act Parliament’s first tax intended to raise revenue from the colonies.[42][41] The statute’s title, preamble, commodity duties, route rules, and drawbacks concern the protective regulation of imperial trade.[2][38]

The commercial pressure was specific. France’s more liberal policy of 1717 enabled French West Indian sugar to compete in European and English-colonial markets; France’s prohibition on rum imports, protecting French brandy, also drove French-colony molasses producers toward New England and New York, where demand was high.[8][9] Northern colonies exchanged fish, lumber, and agricultural produce for West Indian sugar, molasses, and rum, using that trade to obtain money for English manufactured goods.[8]

The Act followed organised lobbying and a failed attempt at outright prohibition. From 1730 to 1733 British West Indian sugar planters, led by Barbados, petitioned Parliament to prevent mainland-colony commerce with the foreign West Indies.[9] In 1731 the Commons passed a bill to prohibit imports of foreign sugar, molasses, and rum into Great Britain or the American colonies, but the Lords rejected it; the 1733 duties pursued that protective object by another means.[8]

Ken Shumate identifies attempted outright prohibitions in both 1731 and 1732: the measures passed the Commons but failed in the Lords before supporters turned to the sixpence-per-gallon duty.[58]

The competing interests were commercial on both sides. West Indian planters invoked soil exhaustion, a recent hurricane, export taxes, and Navigation Act restraints; mainland colonists answered that the British islands could neither absorb all their fish, lumber, flour, cheese, and other produce nor furnish the rum supply they needed.[9] Boston alone produced more than one million gallons of rum annually by the 1730s.[9]

Shumate identifies horses among the mainland provisions exchanged for French and Dutch molasses.[58]

In practice, the high duty was widely evaded. The Act was systematically disregarded in the English colonies and largely became a dead letter; smuggling, bribery, and intimidation of customs officers undercut enforcement.[8][82][83] Thus its protective design did not necessarily produce a protected British West Indian market in operation.[8][82][83]

Accounts of the Act’s evasion also identify relabelling and customs corruption; little revenue was collected.[101][86]

The later statutory contrast helps explain the revenue dispute without changing the 1733 Act’s subject. A 2020 account of Thomas Whately’s 1765 argument describes the 1733 regime as commercial regulation and the 1764 Sugar Act as adding taxation for revenue; the 1764 Act amended and made perpetual the 1733 Act, expressly clarified a revenue purpose, and reduced the molasses duty to 3d.[84]

The 1764 Sugar Act also strengthened anti-smuggling enforcement; Boston merchants discussing it described a chain linking molasses, rum, and purchases of African captives.[102][103]

The immediate commercial target was the mainland colonies’—especially New England’s—trade with the French sugar islands, whose molasses was cheaper and plentiful. New England distillers used that imported molasses to make rum for Atlantic commerce.

### What do recent scholars argue for the slave-control interpretation of

The Molasses Act legislated customs duties and trade rules, not the movement, punishment, work, legal status, or resistance of enslaved people. It imposed no slave patrol, trial procedure, corporal punishment, restriction on assembly, or rule defining an enslaved person as property.[2]

The Act affected an Atlantic plantation economy built on enslaved labour. Sugar and rum formed parts of commercial circuits in which European manufactures were exchanged in West Africa for enslaved people, who were sold in Caribbean and other American destinations; proceeds were then used to buy colonial raw materials.[63] Rum also had a role in the New England slave trade.[44] Those facts make slavery essential background to the Act’s economic world.

Brown University describes a Rhode Island–West Africa trading circuit connected to Caribbean molasses and rum, while Korzeniewicz and Payne treat slave-produced Atlantic sugar as a wealth-generating commodity chain.[103][102][104]

But background is not legislative purpose. The Act’s stated object was relief for British sugar-colony planters facing foreign competition; its operative provisions taxed rum, molasses, syrups, sugar, and paneles, regulated the route into Ireland, and provided customs drawbacks on exported sugar.[36][38] Bladen’s explanation focused on preventing northern commerce from aiding French sugar islands, while Barnard characterized the duties as commercial regulation rather than a device for ordinary public supply.[43]

**No developed recent scholarly argument represented here treats the Molasses Act itself as an instrument of direct slave control.** The slavery-related reading is instead indirect: by seeking to preserve or redirect markets for British plantation commodities, the Act sought to sustain an imperial economy whose Caribbean production rested upon enslaved labour.[38][63]

This distinction matters for fiction. A Jamaican or Barbadian planter might reasonably welcome the Act because it promised a more protected market for plantation output; he could not accurately cite it as the legal authority for whipping, trying, selling, or otherwise disciplining an enslaved person.

The indirect slavery-related interpretation concerns economic support, not legal discipline. The duty-bearing molasses and rum circuits included rum sent to Africa to purchase enslaved people for West Indian markets, while enslaved labour produced the plantation sugar from which molasses came.[85][86][84] Protecting British sugar-colony commodity markets could therefore support the profitability of slave-based plantation economies, although the Act did not define an enslaved person’s legal status or prescribe control over any person.[54]

Trade evasion could itself be entangled with trafficking: one account describes smugglers carrying enslaved Africans to Spanish colonies, exchanging them for sugar and molasses, selling those goods to New England distillers, and using the proceeds to buy more enslaved people.[9] That describes an illegal commercial circuit affected by the law, not a statutory power to govern enslaved people.[9][38]

On this structural reading, protecting British West Indian commodity markets also protected the revenues, credit, and political position of slaveholding planters. The affected networks connected mainland North American rum distilling and food provisioning with Caribbean plantations and the wider Atlantic slave economy.

### Synthesis —

Read together, the evidence establishes a protectionist trade law, not a plantation police law. The Act’s title, preamble, commodity duties, route restrictions, and export drawbacks all aimed to channel sugar, molasses, rum, and related commerce within the British imperial system. Its supporters described the sixpence molasses duty as a near-prohibition intended to divert northern-colony trade away from French islands, even as later accounts differ over whether it should also be called a revenue tax.[36][38][42][43][41]

For the novel, use the following distinction:

1. Cite 6 Geo. II, c. 13 for a 1733 dispute about cargo duties, commodity imports, shipping routes, forfeitures, or sugar-export drawbacks. [2][38]
2. Cite the correctly dated local colonial provision for plantation coercion, punishment, or the legal treatment of enslaved people; enslaved labour was the foundation of the plantation economy connected to the trade, but it was not the Molasses Act’s express legal subject. [2][63]
3. Do not present Barbados’s 1661 predecessor as a new 1730s enactment: the surviving text is an amended 1667 version, and the code was comprehensively rewritten in 1688. Do not present South Carolina’s chattels-personal formula as a 1730s enactment: the cited Act is dated 10 May 1740; the 1735 South Carolina slave-governing Act is Act no. 586, beginning at page 385 of the printed statutes.
