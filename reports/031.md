# Monitoring and disposition thresholds for recurrent near-syncope

## Executive summary

Arrange continuous ECG monitoring now and do not discharge him merely on the basis of the denied Holter order or a six-week cardiology wait. Admission or monitored observation is warranted if telemetry or expert ECG review shows Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete atrioventricular block, symptomatic bradycardia, a pause >3 seconds, recurrent near-syncope or impaired responsiveness, hemodynamic instability, or a persistent focal neurological deficit.

If monitored assessment is unrevealing, he returns fully to neurological baseline, no high-risk rhythm is documented, and reliable rapid follow-up is actively secured, discharge may be reasonable only after fitting and activating an extended 15–30-day auto-triggered monitor, preferably mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry where an alert-response pathway exists.

A 24–48-hour Holter is a poor substitute when events are not reliably daily and access has already failed. The pallor and 30-second inability to speak make these spells potentially arrhythmic or focal neurological events until symptom–rhythm correlation and focused neurological assessment clarify them; variable PR intervals with dropped beats alone do not establish a specific degree of AV block.[1][2][3][4]

## Immediate risk assessment

Case facts: This 68-year-old man has made three ED visits for near-syncope and fatigue during the past month; at the prior visits, troponins were normal and chest radiographs were unremarkable. He has also had two approximately 30-second home spells in which he became pale and could not talk but recovered spontaneously. Today, temperature is 36.9°C, heart rate 52/min, blood pressure 118/76 mm Hg, respiratory rate 16/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air; hemoglobin is 13.8, creatinine 1.1, potassium 4.2, magnesium 2.0, and thyroid-stimulating hormone 2.4. He takes metoprolol 50 mg twice daily for hypertension and atorvastatin. A Holter monitor ordered 3 weeks ago has been denied twice, its appeal is pending, and the next new-patient cardiology appointment is 6 weeks away. Arrange monitored evaluation now before any discharge decision.[5][3][6] Hospital admission is warranted if assessment identifies a serious medical condition potentially related to the events; patients with neither high- nor low-risk features should remain in ED or syncope-unit observation rather than be routinely hospitalized, while discharge is reasonable only after reassuring assessment and a verified external-monitoring and clinical-review pathway within 48 hours—not a pending authorization or a six-week cardiology appointment.[7][8][9][6]

Today’s troponins, chest radiograph, electrolytes, thyroid-stimulating hormone, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation are reassuring. The question is whether recurrent cerebral-hypoperfusion symptoms—reduced brain blood flow causing near-fainting or impaired responsiveness—could arise from an intermittent bradyarrhythmia or conduction disorder that a single ECG and episodic ED testing may miss. Recurrent near-syncope merits the same careful ED risk assessment as syncope, particularly in an older man with an abnormal rhythm description.[8][10][11]

### What can be concluded from sinus bradycardia with occasional dropped

Sinus bradycardia means that the sinus node, the heart’s usual pacemaker, is setting a slow rhythm. The ECG is reported as sinus bradycardia at 52/minute. The conventional adult slow-sinus-rate description uses <60/minute, whereas the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline defines sinus bradycardia as a sinus rate <50 beats/minute; 52/minute does not meet that guideline definition.[12][13] Neither a slow rate nor a sinus pause >3 seconds alone establishes sinus-node dysfunction or a pacing indication; temporal symptom–rhythm correlation is required.[12] The rate therefore does not independently identify a dangerous bradyarrhythmia, but neither does it make the recurrent episodes safe.[1][14][3]

The PR interval measures conduction time from atrial activation to ventricular activation through the atrioventricular (AV) conduction system. A conducted PR interval of 240 milliseconds shows prolonged AV conduction on that beat. The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline defines first-degree AV block, more accurately termed first-degree AV delay, as 1:1 AV conduction with a PR interval >200 milliseconds; no P waves are blocked. It defines second-degree AV block as a constant P-wave rate <100 beats/minute with AV conduction that is present but not 1:1. First-degree AV delay does not classify the reported dropped beats.[13][12] His reported range of 180–240 milliseconds therefore includes both normal-range and prolonged AV conduction times; the PR measurement itself does not explain why ventricular beats are intermittently absent.[15]

Potential contributors to PR prolongation also include aging, increased vagal tone, coronary disease, myocarditis, and infiltrative disease; the conduction delay can arise in the AV node or farther downstream in the His–Purkinje system.[80]

A “dropped beat” should mean a P wave—atrial activation—that is not followed by a QRS complex, the electrical signature of ventricular activation. That relationship must be verified on the tracing. Second-degree AV block requires nonconducted P waves; third-degree, or complete, AV block has no conducted P waves, so the P-wave rate exceeds the QRS rate.[15][16]

Mobitz type I second-degree AV block, also called Wenckebach block, has a specific sequence: the PR interval progressively lengthens before one atrial impulse fails to conduct, and the first subsequent conducted beat has the shortest PR interval. A PR interval that merely varies between 180 and 240 milliseconds is not that sequence.[16][17][15]

Additional surface clues to Mobitz I include relatively constant P–P timing, the greatest PR increase early in a group, progressively shorter R–R intervals, and a pause shorter than twice the usual R–R interval.[81]

Mobitz I can appear in grouped 3:2, 4:3, or 5:4 conduction cycles, indicating the number of P waves relative to conducted QRS complexes.[82][81]

Mobitz type II second-degree AV block has a different defining pattern: consecutively conducted beats have unchanged PR intervals, then a sinus P wave fails to conduct; the pause containing the blocked P wave is twice the sinus cycle length. The reported PR variability does not match the defining constant-PR feature, but Mobitz II cannot be excluded from the brief description because PR prolongation and narrow QRS complexes can coexist with an infranodal lesion, meaning disease below the AV node in the His–Purkinje ventricular conduction system.[16][17]

Apparently constant PR intervals alone do not establish Mobitz II: atypical Wenckebach may show little visible PR change, and assessment of the intervals before and after a blocked P wave requires enough conducted beats for comparison.[83][84][22]

A 2:1 AV-block pattern—every other P wave blocked—requires particular caution. Because two conducted P waves do not occur consecutively, progressive PR prolongation cannot be assessed reliably; 2:1 block can occur at the AV node or in the His–Purkinje system and is not automatically Mobitz II. QRS width helps with probability but does not definitively localize the block: a narrow QRS makes AV-nodal block more likely, whereas a wide QRS more often accompanies His–Purkinje disease.[16][17][15]

In 3:2 conduction, three P waves produce two QRS complexes, but this ratio can occur with either Mobitz subtype; fixed 2:1, 3:1, or 4:1 patterns can be nodal or infranodal.[83][85]

A 4:1 conduction pattern can produce a ventricular rate near 35 beats/minute and should be assessed as potentially high-grade block rather than assumed to be more pronounced Wenckebach.[85]

A wide QRS, conventionally more than 120 milliseconds, heightens concern for His–Purkinje disease even in a Mobitz I pattern; AV-nodal treatments such as atropine may not improve infranodal block.[16]

The necessary diagnostic object is therefore a long, high-quality multilead rhythm strip showing the conducted beats before and after each nonconducted P wave, P–P timing, R–R timing, QRS width, and the relation of sinus rate to AV block. Nonconducted atrial premature beats and concealed His-bundle or ventricular extrasystoles can mimic AV block; movement, poor electrode contact, loose leads, and electrical noise can create pseudo-pauses that resemble sinus arrest or high-grade AV block. Simultaneous multichannel ECG identifies most artifacts.[17][15][3]

The sinus-rate behavior around a pause also matters. A sudden interruption of AV conduction without slowing of sinus discharge supports transient or paroxysmal high-grade AV block, whereas sinus slowing with the block points toward a vagal or neurocardiogenic mechanism.[3] Significant PR lengthening before and after block with prolonged P–P intervals during ventricular asystole supports vagal block rather than dangerous pause- or bradycardia-dependent paroxysmal block.[3][15]

Metoprolol, a beta-blocker, is clinically relevant because beta-blockers can prolong the PR interval and can contribute to bradyarrhythmia or AV block. Medication exposure must prompt a careful reversible-cause review, but symptomatic AV block at therapeutic medication doses is often irreversible and should not simply be attributed to the drug without rhythm characterization.[16][15][14][18]

**The reported ECG is an undifferentiated conduction/rhythm abnormality, not a safely assignable degree of AV block: tracing-level assessment must distinguish Wenckebach-type AV-nodal block, Mobitz II or high-grade infranodal block, complete block, pseudo-block, and artifact.**[17][15][3]

That distinction decides urgency. Mobitz II and third-degree AV block are major high-risk ECG features, and severe acquired AV block—Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete block—is closely related to syncope because escape rhythms may be unreliable. If acquired Mobitz II, high-grade, or third-degree AV block is confirmed and is not reversible or physiologic, permanent pacing is recommended regardless of whether a symptom is captured.[1][19][2][20][13][21]

By contrast, Mobitz I block and markedly prolonged first-degree AV delay are minor high-risk ECG features only when the history is consistent with arrhythmogenic syncope. In asymptomatic people, isolated PR prolongation, Mobitz I, and AV-nodal 2:1 block generally have a benign course with low progression to complete block; this patient is not asymptomatic, so symptom–rhythm correlation remains decisive.[2][18]

The immediate consequence is that the rhythm description cannot support routine reassurance or a definitive pacemaker diagnosis. It supports continuous observation now and a plan to capture the actual rhythm during symptoms, addressed in **Rhythm monitoring and access**.[3][5]

For suspected or documented bradycardia or conduction disease, the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that cardiac rhythm monitoring is useful to establish whether heart-rate or conduction abnormalities correlate with symptoms, with monitor selection based on symptom frequency, symptom nature, and patient preference. It specifically calls ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring useful in symptomatic conduction-system disease when AV block is suspected.[22]

The 2018 bradycardia guideline specifically supports ambulatory ECG monitoring for patients with lightheadedness or dizziness and first-degree AV delay or Mobitz I block to determine whether symptoms coincide with conduction abnormalities.[86]

There is no established minimum heart rate or pause duration that independently mandates permanent pacing for sinus-node dysfunction; temporal correlation between symptoms and bradycardia is the relevant pacing principle.

The anatomical level of true AV block changes its immediate significance: AV-nodal block generally progresses more slowly and has a faster, more reliable escape rhythm, whereas intra-Hisian or infra-Hisian block can progress rapidly and unpredictably with a slower, less reliable ventricular escape rhythm. If ECG and telemetry cannot distinguish Mobitz I from Mobitz II, the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that electrophysiology study can establish whether block is AV-nodal, intra-Hisian, or infra-Hisian.[12]

Improvement of AV conduction during exercise supports AV-nodal block, whereas worsening during exercise suggests infranodal disease; electrophysiology testing can identify the site of block directly.[87][88]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that fatigue or exertional intolerance from loss of AV synchrony, sometimes called pseudo-pacemaker syndrome, may occur with profound first-degree AV delay when the PR interval is >300 milliseconds. His reported maximum PR interval of 240 milliseconds is below that value.[12]

High-grade, high-degree, or advanced AV block means that at least two consecutive P waves at a constant physiologic rate fail to conduct while some AV conduction remains. This pattern is generally intra- or infra-Hisian and treated with pacing; at night, accompanying sinus slowing and a narrow QRS support a possible vagal cause.[13][12]

Ambulatory ECG (AECG) can detect transient or paroxysmal high-degree AV block as sudden interruption of AV conduction without slowing of sinus discharge.

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that frequent dropped QRS complexes in Mobitz I block can cause symptoms from loss of AV synchrony even without bradycardia.[12]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that confirmed infranodal AV block warrants a pacemaker regardless of symptoms because sudden complete AV block can cause syncope and harm.[12]

For selected patients with lightheadedness or syncope suggestive of intermittent bradycardia, ECG-identified conduction-system disease, and no demonstrated AV block, the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline states that an electrophysiology study is reasonable.[12][22]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline adds that intermittent complete AV block causing syncope or presyncope can occur even without baseline heart disease or evident conduction abnormalities, although it is more typical with underlying heart disease or baseline bundle-branch block.[12] Thus, a reassuring baseline evaluation cannot exclude a paroxysmal AV-conduction event.[12]

### How do the two episodes at home where he went

Syncope is abrupt, transient, complete loss of consciousness with inability to maintain postural tone and rapid spontaneous recovery, usually from transient global cerebral hypoperfusion. Presyncope or near-syncope is the syndrome of impending faint or altered consciousness without complete loss of consciousness. The daughter’s report of pallor and inability to speak for 30 seconds describes a transient neurological or responsiveness event, but it does not identify whether he lost awareness, lost postural tone, or retained memory.[2][7]

Transient loss of consciousness (TLOC) requires four features: short duration, abnormal motor control, loss of responsiveness, and amnesia for the period of loss of consciousness. An eyewitness determines loss of responsiveness by whether the person responds to speech, touch, or pain. TLOC is certain when all four features are present and is ruled out when motor control or responsiveness was intact throughout, or when the person recalls the entire event. The daughter’s account is therefore central clinical data rather than a peripheral family observation.[19][23]

Pallor is compatible with autonomic activation before reflex syncope, including vasovagal syncope. Pallor, sweating, nausea, warmth, visual dimming, and a recognizable precipitant such as prolonged standing, emotion, pain, dehydration, micturition, cough, or swallowing favor a reflex pattern; pallor alone is not diagnostic and does not resolve the coexistence of bradycardia and dropped beats.[1][19][2][5]

An abrupt event with no meaningful prodrome, no clear trigger, or occurrence while supine or during exertion instead increases concern for arrhythmic syncope. Arrhythmic syncope is associated with absent or very brief, <3-second, prodromes, whereas reflex syncope can have a prodrome lasting up to 3 minutes.[24][25][2]

The inability to speak is disposition-critical. It may represent impaired responsiveness during global cerebral hypoperfusion from an arrhythmia, but it may also represent dysphasia or aphasia, a focal language deficit that raises concern for a transient ischemic attack (TIA) or other neurological process. Sudden difficulty speaking is a BEFAST stroke symptom; suspected TIA involves acute-onset, transient symptoms referable to a neurovascular territory with complete return to neurological baseline.[19][26]

A focused neurological examination must therefore document speech, facial symmetry, limb function, gait when safe, mental status, and residual deficit. Persistent focal deficit, ongoing altered consciousness, severe headache, or failure to return to baseline requires urgent neurological evaluation; routine head CT, brain MRI, electroencephalography, or carotid imaging is not appropriate for ordinary syncope without focal neurological findings, but the reported speech symptom means that exception must be actively assessed rather than assumed absent.[7][10][5]

The witness history should reconstruct posture, activity, trigger, prodrome, palpitations, color, breathing, movements, duration, injury, responsiveness, confusion, focal symptoms, and time to normal speech, behavior, and walking. Ask specifically whether he could understand speech, follow commands, move both sides, remained upright, recalled the episode, had head turning, rhythmic jerking, lateral tongue injury, incontinence, post-event confusion, headache, or weakness. Immediate clear-headed recovery favors syncope; prolonged confusion favors epileptic seizure, while urinary incontinence does not distinguish epileptic seizures from syncope or from psychogenic TLOC.[27][19]

**Rapid spontaneous recovery does not make either event benign: pallor may fit a reflex event, but brief recurrent incapacitation without a confirmed benign mechanism must be treated as potentially arrhythmic or focal neurological until the event phenotype and rhythm are clarified.**[27][25][10][5]

Near-syncope should not be dismissed because complete loss of consciousness was not reported. ESC-oriented guidance gives presyncope and syncope equal consideration because they have the same prognosis, and an ED review similarly states that near-syncope shares mechanisms and causes with syncope despite fewer serious outcomes. One practical review argues that presyncope should not automatically be managed as syncope because its definition is less standardized and its prognosis is less studied; that disagreement supports individualized risk assessment rather than downgrading this patient’s recurrent spells.[28][8][10][29]

The immediate consequence is that the two spells add recurrence, possible sudden incapacitation, and possible focal neurological symptoms to the risk picture. They require observation with cardiac rhythm capture and focused neurological reassessment before a disposition is chosen in **Disposition thresholds**.[3][11][5]

If the inability to speak was abrupt focal language dysfunction rather than inability to respond during global hypoperfusion, treat the event as suspected transient ischemic attack (TIA) until proven otherwise. TIA is temporary neurological dysfunction caused by focal ischemia without acute infarction; its diagnosis is often history-based because the neurological examination may be normal after symptoms resolve.[30][31]

A current or recurrent focal deficit requires immediate acute-stroke assessment, including a standardized neurological severity screen such as the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and brain plus vascular imaging by CT or MRI. Suspected stroke and TIA require immediate access to brain and vascular imaging; a normal chest radiograph neither evaluates nor excludes this possibility.[32]

The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline requires a thorough history, physical examination, and baseline ECG in all patients. The witness account should specifically establish the circumstances, prodrome, post-event symptoms, and whether he had loss of responsiveness, loss of postural tone, amnesia, abnormal motor control, or complete return to baseline.[33][34][35]

If the spells represent TLOC, patient-triggered ambulatory ECG (AECG) activation can fail because temporary loss of consciousness prevents activation. Outpatient cardiac telemetry with continuous recording independent of patient activation is recommended, and an implantable loop recorder can ensure capture of syncopal events in some circumstances.[3]

For suspected stroke, Canadian Stroke Best Practices recommends a rapid examination for focal deficits using FAST—Face, Arm, Speech, Time—and an initial standardized stroke-severity assessment using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS).[32]

Obtain a bedside glucose measurement as part of the immediate assessment of transient impaired speech or responsiveness, because hypoglycemia can mimic a neurologic or syncopal event.

When available, video recordings of a spontaneous home event, including smartphone recordings, can help characterize the episode; ESC guidance gives home recordings a class IIa recommendation.[36]

For suspected transient ischemic attack, assess the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale together with confusion, receptive aphasia, eye movements, visual fields, limb weakness, and sensory loss to confirm return to neurological baseline.[26]

Ask specifically about syncope during exertion or while supine, sudden palpitations immediately followed by syncope, family history of unexplained sudden death at a young age, and known structural heart disease or coronary artery disease; each supports concern for cardiac syncope.[2]

### The subject is a 68-year-old man with three ED presentations

This 68-year-old man has had three ED presentations for near-syncope and fatigue in one month, followed by two additional 30-second home spells. The presently normal blood pressure, oxygen saturation, hemoglobin, creatinine, potassium, magnesium, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and troponins lower concern for several acute reversible contributors but do not resolve an intermittent arrhythmia.[7][6][37][38]

A resting 12-lead ECG can reveal bradyarrhythmia, sinus pauses, or high-grade conduction block, but intermittent AV block can escape standard tests and may only appear with continuous or prolonged recording. ## Rhythm monitoring and access

Rhythm monitoring must be arranged as an active part of today’s care, not left to a pending insurance appeal or a routine cardiology queue. The purpose is **symptom–rhythm correlation**: documenting the electrocardiogram (ECG) rhythm at the time of pallor, near-syncope, inability to speak, or fatigue, so that an intermittent slow rhythm or conduction disturbance can be confirmed or excluded. Ambulatory ECG (AECG) recording has two objectives: capture a serious arrhythmic event that explains syncope, indicates the possibility of more serious arrhythmia, or itself warrants treatment or intervention; and correlate recurrent symptoms with an arrhythmia, or demonstrate that recurrent symptoms occur without arrhythmia and thereby exclude an arrhythmic basis for syncope.[3] A resting ECG is a short snapshot; ambulatory ECG (AECG) monitoring records rhythm over ordinary activities and can detect cardiac arrhythmias or arrhythmia patterns not readily obtained from a standard ECG. Accurate and timely AECG characterization directs therapies affecting diagnosis, prognosis, and symptoms.[50][3][51]

The reported variable PR interval and dropped beats raise concern for an intermittent conduction problem, but they do not identify a specific degree or mechanism of atrioventricular (AV) block. The monitoring plan should therefore aim to capture the actual rhythm during symptoms and identify any serious bradyarrhythmia—an abnormally slow rhythm—or higher-grade AV block.[1][3][4]

### What monitoring strategy should be arranged before any discharge decision?

**Begin monitored ECG observation now.** Continuous ECG monitoring, usually called telemetry, should start during the current ED evaluation because there are recurrent near-syncope presentations, an abnormal ECG description, and a plausible intermittent arrhythmic cause. If high-risk features are present, monitor in a setting able to resuscitate deterioration; studies summarized in syncope guidance suggest up to 6 hours in the ED and up to 24 hours in hospital, although monitoring must continue when the clinical course requires it. The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline gives continuous ECG monitoring for hospitalized patients admitted for syncope evaluation with suspected cardiac etiology a Class I, Level of Evidence B-NR recommendation.[44][1][7][5][42]

Reported diagnostic yield for in-hospital ECG monitoring in suspected arrhythmic syncope ranges from 1.9% to 17.6%; its immediate purpose is also to detect rhythms that require intervention during observation.[1]

Telemetry and ambulatory monitoring have different jobs. Telemetry provides immediate detection during the current period of risk, with staff and resuscitation capability available if the rhythm deteriorates. Ambulatory ECG monitoring extends the observation window after discharge to capture intermittent events that do not recur during ED observation.[2][3][52]

The immediate monitoring objective is event capture, not ECG-label assignment. If pallor, inability to speak, near-syncope, fatigue, or a recurrent dropped beat occurs in the ED or observation unit, obtain and preserve the telemetry strip and a repeat 12-lead ECG during or immediately after the event. Compare it with previous tracings. Continuous ECG monitoring, ECG review against prior tracings, and a repeat ECG or rhythm strip with recurrent symptoms are part of the suspected-cardiac-syncope approach.[27][5]

The two witnessed home spells matter to monitor selection because he may be unable to press a button or describe symptoms while they are occurring. A patient-activated recorder requires the patient to recognize symptoms and activate the device promptly. Temporary loss of consciousness or sudden incapacitation prevents reliable activation, and external loop recorders that rely on patient activation have limited usefulness around such episodes.[3][7]

If discharge becomes appropriate after monitored assessment, fit and activate an extended continuous or auto-triggered monitor before he leaves. The preferred outpatient strategy is a 15–30-day extended ambulatory ECG monitor with automatic arrhythmia detection, ideally mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry (MCOT) when a real-time alert pathway is locally available. MCOT is a continuously worn system that can detect significant arrhythmias automatically, transmit ECG data to a central monitoring station, and generate alerts without requiring the patient to activate it.[3][7][53][54]

An acceptable alternative is an automatically triggered external loop recorder, which continuously records and stores rhythm data over weeks to months. It can preserve 3–14 minutes before, and 1–4 minutes after, a patient- or automatically triggered event and is intended for frequent spontaneous symptoms likely to recur within 2–6 weeks.[22][44][45]

A continuously recording patch monitor for 7–14 days is a practical alternative where MCOT or a loop recorder cannot be obtained promptly. Patch devices record continuously and do not require patient activation, although they are generally single-lead devices and may not provide real-time clinical notification.[7][55][18][56]

The duration should match the recurrence pattern. The 2017 ISHNE-HRS consensus gives a Class I, Level of Evidence B-NR recommendation for 24–48-hour Holter monitoring when frequent symptomatic events are anticipated within that recording window, and a Class I, Level of Evidence B-R recommendation for extended ambulatory ECG (AECG) monitoring, for example 15–30 days, when symptomatic events are less than daily or their frequency is uncertain.[4][3]

Longer recording gives more opportunity to document intermittent events. The consensus estimates syncope diagnostic yield at 1–5% for a 24–48-hour Holter, 5–10% for 3–7-day external monitoring, and 15–25% for 1–4-week external monitoring. In one trial cited by the consensus, symptom–rhythm correlation occurred in 22% with 48-hour Holter monitoring and 56% with one month of loop recording.[3]

ESC syncope guidance reports that Holter monitoring has a diagnostic yield of only 1–2% in an unselected syncope population because symptoms often do not recur during the recording window.[1][89]

An external-loop-recorder registry reported a 24.5% diagnostic yield for syncope, predominantly identifying bradyarrhythmias; starting monitoring early was its strongest predictor of diagnosis.[1][90]

The ED-based monitoring literature reinforces this duration match. In a study of 174 ED patients with symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia, the median time to detection was 4.2 days for sinus pause and 5.8 days for significant heart block. In a manufacturer registry of 122,454 patients wearing a continuous patch for a mean 9.6 days, 52.5% of potentially high-risk arrhythmias were detected by 24 hours, 65.5% by 48 hours, and 92.9% by seven days.[40]

A standard short Holter therefore should not be the default monitor merely because it was originally ordered. A Holter is well suited to daily or very frequent symptoms; a 7–14-day continuous patch or 15–30-day auto-triggered monitor is better matched to this patient’s recurrent but not clearly daily spells.[57][58][4]

A monitor fitted at discharge must have device-specific result ownership and response routes. For MCOT, name the ordering clinician, the 24/7 monitoring station, the physician available to review transmitted data outside the set parameters, the after-hours recipient of urgent automatic detections, the alert thresholds, and the route for directing the patient to emergency assessment. MCOT can continuously transmit data to a reading centre, automatically generate an alarm without patient activation, and uses a monitoring station staffed continuously with immediate physician access for transmissions outside set parameters.[3][59]

For an auto-triggered external loop recorder, name the ordering clinician or monitoring service that receives automatically detected event segments, the recipient and response route for device-generated alarms where that capability is provided, and the clinician responsible for final interpretation. External loop recorders store selected ECG segments around automatic or manual events rather than continuously documenting the entire rhythm.[3][40]

For a continuously recording stored-data patch, name the clinician responsible for confirming device return or data upload, reviewing the final report, contacting the patient with the result, and arranging emergency assessment if interpretation identifies a dangerous rhythm. A patch continuously stores rhythm data for 2–14 days but ordinarily requires retrieval and interpretation after the recording period; it is not a real-time alert system.[7][41]

The ordering clinician should not rely uncritically on vendor-default alerts. The ISHNE-HRS consensus advises practices to develop specific notification criteria because nominal vendor criteria can generate unnecessary calls while still requiring prompt review of truly dangerous recordings.[4]

Examples of urgent monitor notifications include a reported syncopal episode, Mobitz II second-degree AV block, third-degree AV block, and a symptomatic pause of at least 4 seconds. These are notification examples, not stand-alone disposition rules, but they support a prearranged response plan.[3]

This makes a 24–48-hour Holter a conditional option, not the preferred discharge monitor: use it only when events are expected within its short window; otherwise arrange an extended, automatic-recording system before discharge or continue monitored observation.[3][40][4]

The one-month loop-recorder strategy increased symptom–rhythm correlation by **34 percentage points** versus a 48-hour Holter (56% versus 22%) in the cited study, quantifying why a short recording window is a poor default when events are not daily.[3]

In the cited continuous-patch registry, **27.4 percentage points** more potentially high-risk arrhythmias were detected by day 7 than by 48 hours (92.9% versus 65.5%), illustrating the incremental capture available beyond a conventional two-day recording.[40]

Among potentially high-risk arrhythmias detected within seven days in that registry, **29.5% were first detected after 48 hours** (27.4 divided by 92.9 percentage points); this supports selecting multiday monitoring when discharge is otherwise appropriate.[40]

The ACC/AHA/HRS 2018 bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline gives ambulatory ECG monitoring a Class I recommendation when the initial evaluation is nondiagnostic, with monitor selection based on symptom frequency. It also recommends a 12-lead ECG to document rhythm, rate, and conduction and to screen for structural heart disease or systemic illness.[22]

In pooled data from nine studies of 506 patients with unexplained syncope after complete conventional investigation, 176 patients (35%) had symptom–ECG correlation; among those 176 patients, 56% had asystole or, in a few cases, bradycardia, 11% had tachycardia, and 33% had no arrhythmia. In pooled data from seven studies, presyncope was much less likely than syncope to be associated with an arrhythmia; presyncope is not a substitute for documented syncope in establishing an arrhythmic diagnosis, but a significant arrhythmia recorded during presyncope is diagnostic.[60] This makes capture of one of his typical spells particularly important.[42][60]

Do not use a consumer smartwatch or other non-ECG wearable as the sole monitor in this pathway. A smartwatch photoplethysmography algorithm predicted vasovagal syncope up to five minutes before transient loss of consciousness with 84.6% accuracy during head-up tilt testing; the wearable evidence described for syncope is primarily atrial-fibrillation detection or vasovagal-syncope prediction rather than diagnosis of intermittent AV block.[35]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline identifies correlation between symptoms and bradycardia as the diagnostic gold standard. Ambulatory-monitoring yield for significant bradyarrhythmias is typically less than 15%; in populations with nonspecific symptoms considered potentially arrhythmic, one-third have their presenting symptoms during continuous ambulatory monitoring without an associated arrhythmia. A symptomatic recording can establish a rhythm diagnosis; a recording of the patient’s typical symptom without an associated arrhythmia can also exclude an arrhythmia or conduction disorder as its cause.[47][12]

Intermittent AV block can be found on long-term monitoring even after initially negative physical examination, ECG, and echocardiography. In one study cited by the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline, paroxysmal idiopathic AV block was found in 8% of syncope patients with normal ECG and echocardiogram. This supports obtaining a diagnostic tracing rather than assigning an AV-block degree from the variable PR interval and dropped beats described here.[12]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline specifies the frequency-based device choice: a 24- or 48-hour Holter is appropriate for daily symptoms; a 2–14-day continuously recording patch is an external-loop-recorder alternative; and an external loop recorder, which may auto-trigger and retains rhythm before, during, and after an event, fits symptoms likely to recur within 2–6 weeks. MCOT records and transmits data for up to 30 days and automatically transmits significant arrhythmias to a central station staffed by trained technicians 24 hours a day.[12]

The same guideline reports higher diagnostic yield from external loop recorders, transtelephonic event recorders, adhesive patches, and MCOT than from 24–48-hour Holter monitoring because they monitor longer. In the syncope/presyncope comparison cited in the 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline, MCOT diagnostic yield was 89% versus 69% with an external loop recorder.[12][34]

In the cited syncope/presyncope subgroup monitored for up to 30 days, MCOT produced a diagnosis in 89% versus 69% with an external loop recorder—a 29.0% relative diagnostic-yield increase. This supports choosing MCOT when real-time automatic detection and a staffed escalation pathway are actually available.[61] (ROTHMAN et al., 2007)[61]

In a 2007 randomized multicentre study of patients with syncope or presyncope after a nondiagnostic 24-hour Holter, clinically significant arrhythmias were detected in 52% with 30-day mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry versus 15% with loop recorders.[90]

In the 146-patient full monitoring-period comparison, the 14-day patch detected 57.4% more arrhythmias than the 24-hour Holter (96 versus 61). This illustrates the added capture opportunity from extending recording beyond a one-day Holter window.[40]

In the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline, an implantable cardiac monitor is reasonable after a nondiagnostic initial noninvasive evaluation when suspected bradycardia symptoms are infrequent, with more than 30 days between symptoms.[12] The implantable-loop-recorder table describes approximately three years of monitoring with automatic bradycardia/tachycardia storage and patient-triggered episodes, but lists cost and lack of availability at all centres as disadvantages.[62]

Ambulatory ECG monitoring is also indicated when medication effects on sinus-node automaticity or AV conduction are suspected. Metoprolol makes this indication directly relevant because the monitoring question includes whether reduced automaticity or conduction contributes to his symptoms.[3]

The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline gives external cardiac monitoring and implantable cardiac monitoring Class IIa recommendations for selected ambulatory patients when an arrhythmic cause of syncope is suspected; monitor type and duration should reflect the frequency and nature of the episodes.[36][63]

MCOT should be selected only when its real-time monitoring is essential for safety and is expected to prompt immediate clinical change, such as medication or an emergent procedure, in addition to the other specified criteria: infrequent symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia, suspected arrhythmia as the most likely diagnosis, and a nondiagnostic or contraindicated Holter or external ambulatory event monitor.[40] No randomized studies have evaluated management with versus without mobile cardiac monitoring, and available evidence does not demonstrate a clinically significant incremental benefit from adding MCOT real-time monitoring to outpatient ambulatory monitoring.[40]

The monitor result remains clinically interpretable only when it is linked to an actual spell. A typical symptom occurring during continuous ambulatory monitoring without an associated arrhythmia is evidence against bradyarrhythmia or conduction disease as the cause, whereas absence of a recurrent spell leaves symptom–rhythm correlation unresolved. Syncope failed to recur during long-term monitoring in almost one third of patients in the implantable-loop-recorder review, including patients who had reported frequent episodes before implantation.[12][64]

If recurrent unexplained events become genuinely infrequent, occur more than 30 days apart, and initial noninvasive evaluation is nondiagnostic, the 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline supports an implantable cardiac monitor. It is subcutaneously implanted, has a 2–3-year battery life, can automatically detect significant arrhythmias with remote monitoring, and can be triggered by the patient or often by a family-member witness.[12]

### How should the twice-denied outpatient Holter order be addressed?

The twice-denied Holter order is an access failure, not a reason to defer rhythm evaluation. Arrhythmia detection is most likely when monitoring is applied soon after the index syncopal presentation, and the likelihood of detection declines with delay. ED-initiated external monitoring at discharge can substitute for hospitalization only when the patient is otherwise appropriate for discharge and hospitalization would otherwise be solely for cardiac monitoring.[9]

**Do not discharge him with only a pending appeal.** Before any discharge, the ED should either fit an available monitor or retain him in ED observation or a monitored setting while a safe plan is arranged. Continuous ECG monitoring during ED observation is an immediate option when longer-term monitor access is delayed.[38][11][29]

The authorization request should seek the device that matches the clinical problem, rather than simply resubmitting the original short-Holter request. The documentation should state:

- **Recurrent unexplained near-syncope and possible transient loss of consciousness:** three ED presentations in one month, plus two witnessed episodes of pallor and inability to speak lasting about 30 seconds. The 1999 ambulatory-electrocardiography guideline gives external ambulatory event monitoring a Class I recommendation for unexplained syncope, near-syncope, or episodic dizziness when the cause is not obvious; it does not distinguish monitor types and directs that symptom frequency dictate the recording type. The UnitedHealthcare Medicare Advantage policy lists AECG monitoring for unexplained syncope, near-syncope, vertigo, chest pain, palpitations, or dyspnea and for assessment of documented or suspected bradycardia. [3][65][57]

- **Suspected intermittent bradycardia or conduction disturbance:** sinus bradycardia, variable PR interval, and dropped beats on the reported ECG require symptom–rhythm correlation rather than an assumed AV-block subtype. Ambulatory monitoring is useful for suspected bradycardia or conduction disorders because it can correlate symptoms with heart rate or conduction abnormalities. [65][20][4]

- **Why a short Holter is poorly matched:** symptoms are not stated to be daily, whereas 24–48-hour monitoring is intended for daily or near-daily symptoms. Extended monitoring is recommended when symptoms are less than daily or their timing is uncertain. [57][4]

- **Why automatic capture is needed:** the inability to speak during an episode may prevent operation of a patient-triggered device. Auto-triggered event monitors and continuous monitors record without relying on patient action. [41][52][56]

- **Why an immediate monitoring pathway is needed:** the original order has already been denied twice, appeal remains pending, and current symptoms continue. For autotriggered loop recorders and ambulatory cardiovascular telemetry that transfer data wirelessly to an independent diagnostic testing facility through a cellular network, the 2017 ISHNE-HRS consensus requires practices to have a 24/7/365 mechanism to handle incoming data so potentially life-threatening arrhythmias are rapidly identified and treated. The request should document the service responsible for alert review and the action expected if a dangerous rhythm is found. [4][59]

Several insurer policies recognize longer external monitoring as an alternative to a Holter for infrequent symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia. Anthem’s utilization guideline considers an external ambulatory event monitor medically necessary as a diagnostic alternative when symptoms occur less often than once every 48 hours. Other policies describe continuous monitors storing data for more than 48 hours, or patient- or auto-activated event monitors, as alternatives for presyncope or syncope occurring less often than every 48 hours.[65][40][52][56]

The appeal should therefore request **extended continuous monitoring, auto-triggered external loop monitoring, or MCOT**, depending on local availability and the needed alert capability. A 7–14-day patch may be easiest to place immediately, while a 15–30-day auto-triggered monitor better covers uncertain event frequency. Clinical input received in 2025 supports outpatient cardiac telemetry for selected people with symptoms less frequent than every 48 hours when recurrent unexplained clinically significant presyncope, syncope, severe palpitations, or dizziness is thought most likely due to arrhythmia, Holter monitoring or external ambulatory event monitoring is nondiagnostic or contraindicated, and real-time monitoring is essential for safety and will lead to immediate clinical changes such as medication or emergent procedures.[57][4][52][56]

Coverage policy differs by insurer and member plan. The member-specific benefit document determines coverage, exclusions, and limitations, so the denial letter and plan-specific criteria should be reviewed rather than assuming that a generic policy or a pending appeal will result in approval.[65][63][57]

Health Quality Ontario found no studies directly comparing long-term continuous ambulatory event monitors with external loop recorders. Its indirect comparison, using 24-hour Holter monitors as the common comparator, estimated a risk difference in arrhythmia detection of 0.01 (95% confidence interval -0.18 to 0.20) between long-term continuous monitors and external loop recorders; both were more effective than a 24-hour Holter monitor, and Health Quality Ontario rated the evidence quality poor using GRADE criteria.[52][56]

A patch is not identical to a Holter in signal characteristics. In one 146-patient comparison, the 14-day patch detected 96 arrhythmias over the full monitoring period versus 61 with the 24-hour Holter; during the simultaneous first 24 hours, the Holter detected 61 events versus 52 with the patch. This supports extended monitoring for duration while preserving the need to choose the monitor according to the suspected rhythm and available technical support.[40]

Judgment — If the insurer will not authorize a monitor promptly but the health system can supply one through an ED, observation-unit, charity-care, or contracted-monitor pathway, arrange the available appropriate monitor first and pursue authorization in parallel. A clinically necessary monitoring plan cannot depend on an appeal outcome that has already been delayed twice.

If an external monitor is fitted, confirm before discharge that the patient and daughter understand its device-specific result route. For MCOT, confirm activation, transmission, telephone connectivity, the monitoring-station contact number, and the route for an urgent call or recurrent spell. For an auto-triggered external loop recorder, confirm activation, electrode adherence, any transmission method, symptom-marker use, and the service or clinician receiving event alerts and final recordings. For a stored-data patch, confirm the return or upload method, the named clinician who will review the report after retrieval, and the route for immediate reassessment if another spell occurs rather than waiting for the report. Check skin contact, charging or electrode-change requirements as applicable. Artifact from poor skin-electrode contact, loose leads, movement, muscle signal, or ambient noise can mimic pauses and high-grade AV block; multichannel ECG can help recognize many artifacts.[3][41][59]

If external monitoring is nondiagnostic and unexplained recurrent events continue, cardiology or electrophysiology should consider an implantable loop recorder. In the Reveal LINQ Usability study, infection and procedure-related serious adverse-event rates were 1.3% and 0.7%, respectively; in the Reveal LINQ Registry, they were 1.6% and 1.6%, respectively.[52] The usual staged pathway is short-term continuous ambulatory ECG, followed by external loop recording over weeks to months, then an ILR when prolonged noninvasive monitoring does not establish a diagnosis.[3][4][59]

The denied Holter should be replaced with a fitted, appropriate-duration monitor or with continued observation; it should not remain the uncompleted prerequisite for meaningful rhythm assessment.[9][27]

The authorization request should state that the ACC/AHA/HRS monitoring indication is symptom–rhythm correlation in suspected bradycardia or conduction disease and that the 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guidance directs the choice of cardiac monitor by the frequency and nature of the syncope events.[22][42][63] An external loop recorder is specifically suited to symptoms likely to recur in 2–6 weeks; MCOT provides automatic transmission for up to 30 days to a monitoring station attended 24 hours a day.[22][42][63]

The ED should document the daughter’s ability to assist with device operation and event logging, but should not select a patient-activated-only device. In a randomized external-loop-recorder study, 24% of assigned patients failed to activate the recorder properly, and a retrospective comparison found diagnostic-event yields of 36% with auto-triggered loop recording, 17% with patient-activated loop recording, and 6.2% with Holter monitoring.[64]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia and conduction-delay guideline supports ambulatory ECG monitoring in symptomatic patients with conduction-system disease when AV block is suspected. Its monitoring options extend from a 10-second ECG and 24-, 48-, or 72-hour continuous ambulatory recordings to event monitors and implantable loop recorders, selected to uncover symptom–rhythm correlation in fatigue, dizziness, or syncope with suspected AV block or sinus-node dysfunction.[12]

For a patch or auto-triggered external device supplied through an ED or contracted-monitor pathway, confirm adequate signal quality at fitting and ensure that all automated bradycardia or tachycardia detections undergo clinical review: automated detection and storage are affected by recording-quality artifact.[12]

In the retrospective 600-patient comparison, auto-triggered loop recording had a 2.12-fold diagnostic-event yield versus patient-activated loop recording (36% versus 17%). This quantifies why an automatic-capture device is preferable when the patient may be unable to activate a recorder during a spell.[64]

Before discharge, ED staff must confirm that the selected patch, external loop recorder, or mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry service can be supplied and activated promptly, because monitor selection must account for local availability as well as symptom frequency, automaticity, clinical condition, and the probability of a life-threatening arrhythmia.[63]

### How should the 6-week wait for a new cardiology appointment

A routine six-week new-patient cardiology appointment is not an adequate stand-alone follow-up plan for recurrent near-syncope with possible intermittent conduction disease. The relevant choice is between monitored hospital or observation care and a **verified rapid pathway** that includes a fitted monitor, named result ownership, specialist review, and clear escalation instructions.[27][24][29]

**Obtain direct ED-to-cardiology, electrophysiology, or syncope-service communication before discharge.** The ED should request review of the actual ECG and telemetry, discuss the need for extended monitoring, and secure a named clinician or service responsible for monitor alerts and final interpretation. A suspected-cardiac-syncope plan should make the admission, transfer, or rapid ambulatory-monitoring arrangement explicit and owned.[27][5][37]

The pathway should not be a generic referral placed into the existing six-week queue. High-risk patients may require urgent advanced investigation, ECG monitoring, specialist review, and treatment; they should not leave the ED unless that evaluation can occur during the ED stay, in a syncope decision or investigation unit, or through a rapid follow-up clinic.[48][24]

A structured syncope pathway can provide the required access. In the SYNERGY multicentre study, implementation of ESC syncope-guideline care with quick referral routes to a syncope unit produced diagnostic accuracy of 86% compared with 69% under usual care and diagnostic yield of 89% compared with 76%.[66][48]

For suspected cardiac syncope, outpatient workup may be considered in carefully selected patients with an acute presentation; the cited example is reliable patients able to follow up with a primary-care clinician or cardiologist within 48 hours.[6] The appointment should be with cardiology, electrophysiology, or a syncope service able to review monitor strips, assess medication-related bradycardia and conduction disease, and decide whether further testing, electrophysiology study, or pacing evaluation is needed.[44][22]

Rapid follow-up does not replace monitor-result ownership. An extended monitor may identify clinically important rhythms days after application: in the ED cohort of 174 patients, median detection occurred at 4.2 days for sinus pauses and 5.8 days for significant heart block. For MCOT, the monitoring station must transmit urgent automatic detections for prompt review by its designated physician and emergency escalation when needed. For a stored-data patch, the designated ordering clinician must obtain and interpret the report after retrieval and contact the patient with the result; the patient must seek immediate reassessment for another dangerous spell rather than await deferred patch interpretation.[40][41][59]

The daughter’s observations should be incorporated into the plan. She can maintain an event log, press a symptom marker if the device permits, note the exact time and duration of pallor, speech inability, loss of awareness, falls, chest symptoms, and recovery, and contact emergency services for recurrent focal neurologic symptoms or prolonged impaired responsiveness. A witness may also activate some implantable cardiac monitors, although this is relevant only if an ILR is later selected.[7][63]

The patient and daughter should also record medication timing in the event diary so it can be compared with symptom times.[87]

The monitor should not be used as reassurance during a potentially dangerous recurrent event. A patient who has another spell with collapse, persistent inability to speak, chest pain, dyspnea, sustained palpitations, injury, or prolonged recovery needs immediate reassessment rather than waiting for the device report.Judgment — This is especially important because inability to speak can represent cerebral hypoperfusion from an arrhythmia but can also represent a neurologic event; rhythm monitoring and neurologic assessment address different urgent possibilities.

If no rapid specialist review, monitor fitting, alert pathway, transportation support, or reliable contact method can be confirmed, retain the patient in ED observation or admit for monitored evaluation rather than discharge to the routine cardiology waitlist. Observation is a deliberate diagnostic intervention for patients who are neither clearly low nor clearly high risk: it permits ECG recording for arrhythmia, repeat assessment, and reassignment to a safer disposition once the early course is clearer.[29][25][37]

A six-week cardiology appointment becomes acceptable only as the later follow-up after a rapid, owned monitoring pathway has already been secured; it is not the pathway itself.[27][24][11]

In the cited ED patch-monitor cohort, median detection of significant heart block occurred **1.6 days later** than median detection of sinus pause (5.8 versus 4.2 days), reinforcing the need for a result-review pathway that remains active after discharge.[40]

The spells of pallor and transient inability to speak also require an immediate possible transient ischemic attack (TIA) pathway, not cardiac follow-up alone. A TIA evaluation pathway calls for evaluation within 24 hours with ongoing cardiac telemetry and observation for recurrent events, brain imaging, cerebrovascular imaging, and neurologic consultation; ED observation, same-day specialty clinics, and virtual acute-stroke consultation are access models when on-site stroke expertise is unavailable.[30][32]

If the ECG demonstrates conduction-system disease but no AV block is captured, and symptoms remain suggestive of intermittent bradycardia, electrophysiology study is a reasonable specialist-directed next test.[22]

A rapid-access pathway is a defined alternative to the routine queue. The Queen Elizabeth University Hospital (QEUH), Glasgow pathway stratifies patients as high, intermediate, or low risk and directs those needing specialist input to the rapid-access syncope clinic (RASCL).[67] A 2026 British Journal of Cardiology article proposes that acute hospitals use clear fast-track referral to clinics or observation units, supported by governance and quality oversight, and identifies access to ambulatory monitors, implantable loop recorders, tilt testing, and observation units as essential diagnostic infrastructure.[67]

A routine referral without structured follow-up is not an access plan. In the SEED study of 41 EDs in 14 countries, 75% of sites lacked a dedicated outpatient clinic and 10% offered structured follow-up; admission rates ranged from 20% to 84%. The same 2026 article identifies admission rate, bed-days saved, time to diagnosis, and recurrence as service-performance metrics.[67]

### Synthesis

**The recommended route is immediate telemetry now, followed—only if monitored assessment supports discharge—by an ED-fitted extended, continuous or auto-triggered ambulatory ECG monitor with a named 24/7 alert-review pathway and confirmed rapid specialist follow-up.** Telemetry addresses immediate deterioration; prolonged monitoring addresses intermittent events after discharge. A 24–48-hour Holter fits daily symptoms, while 15–30-day extended monitoring better fits uncertain or less-than-daily spells and avoids dependence on activation during incapacity.[1][3][4]

The denied Holter and six-week cardiology queue do not satisfy either requirement. They should be replaced by an active, documented ED-owned access plan or by continued monitored observation. If prolonged noninvasive monitoring remains nondiagnostic while recurrent unexplained events continue, electrophysiology should consider an implantable loop recorder as the longer-term escalation strategy.[9][27][3][53]

## Disposition thresholds

Disposition depends on whether today’s evaluation identifies an immediately dangerous rhythm, a serious non-rhythm cause, or a genuinely low-risk explanation. Normal troponins, chest radiography, electrolytes, thyroid testing, and resting blood pressure reduce some alternative concerns but do not resolve intermittent bradyarrhythmia or transient neurologic symptoms. Presyncope—near-syncope without confirmed complete loss of consciousness—should receive the same risk attention as syncope because their prognoses are similar.[1][8][10]

### Which clinical findings and symptoms warrant admission?

**Admit to a monitored, resuscitation-capable setting when a serious potentially causal condition is identified or strongly suspected.** The ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline recommends hospital evaluation and treatment when the initial assessment identifies a serious condition that may be relevant to the event. For suspected cardiac syncope, continuous ECG monitoring—telemetry, meaning continuous remote ECG observation on a ward—is useful after admission.[7][53]

The immediate physiologic triggers for escalation are persistent bradyarrhythmia causing hypotension, acutely altered mental status, or ischemic chest discomfort. The American Heart Association bradycardia algorithm uses these adverse effects, rather than a pulse number alone, to distinguish patients who need active treatment from those who can be monitored and observed.[14] Instability, recurrent deterioration, shock, persistent altered consciousness, or a serious arrhythmia is a higher-acuity threshold that may require resuscitation-area care, temporary pacing, or critical-care involvement rather than ordinary telemetry.[18][5]

Documented high-grade conduction disease warrants admission and urgent cardiology/electrophysiology assessment. Mobitz II second-degree AV block has constant PR intervals before conducted beats with intermittent nonconducted P waves; third-degree, or complete, AV block has no conducted P waves and an escape rhythm maintains ventricular activity.[16][15] Mobitz II may progress suddenly to complete heart block, and high-grade block has an unpredictable risk of complete block.[16]

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline recommends permanent pacing for acquired Mobitz II, high-grade, or third-degree AV block that is not due to a reversible or physiologic cause, regardless of symptoms. This is a treatment recommendation, but it makes these documented rhythms serious disposition findings rather than outpatient-Holter findings.[20][68] Severe acquired AV block—Mobitz II, high-grade, and complete AV block—is closely related to syncope because the patient may depend on unreliable subsidiary escape rhythms.[19][4]

Mobitz II block can progress abruptly to complete block with an escape rhythm of 20–40 beats/minute or to asystole.[16][91]

Newly identified advanced AV block also warrants echocardiography.[16][86][92]

The following ECG or telemetry findings require urgent rhythm interpretation in the context of symptoms, haemodynamic stability, and whether a serious concern remains unresolved. Admit to monitored hospital-level care when dangerous conduction disease is documented, a bradyarrhythmia or tachyarrhythmia is correlated with symptoms, haemodynamic instability is present, neurologic assessment identifies a serious concern, or a high-risk concern persists after ED or observation evaluation.[1][7][8][14]

- **Mobitz II, high-grade, or complete AV block:** These are major high-risk ECG features and make an arrhythmic cause of syncope highly probable. [1][2][29]

- **Awake persistent sinus bradycardia below 40 beats/minute:** The ESC syncope guideline identifies this threshold, in a person who is not physically trained, as making arrhythmic syncope highly probable and requiring urgent interpretation with the symptoms and clinical condition. [1][3][58] A pulse of 52 beats/minute does not reach that specific threshold, but the overall rhythm and symptoms remain relevant. [3][14]

- **Awake sinus pause longer than 3 seconds:** This is another ESC finding that makes an arrhythmic cause highly probable and requires urgent interpretation with symptoms, stability, and the rest of the rhythm record. There is no established minimum heart rate or pause duration at which permanent pacing is recommended for sinus-node dysfunction; symptom–rhythm correlation guides pacing decisions. [1][13]

For severe unpredictable syncope, spontaneous symptomatic asystolic pauses longer than 3 seconds due to sinus arrest or AV block support dual-chamber pacing; spontaneous asymptomatic pauses longer than 6 seconds can also support it.[75]

- **Symptomatic bradycardia or sinus pauses not attributable to a neurally mediated mechanism:** These are examples of serious conditions associated with syncope in the ACC/AHA/HRS guideline. A neurally mediated mechanism means a reflex vagal event that slows the sinus node and AV conduction; this distinction requires rhythm-pattern review rather than an assumption from the resting ECG. [7][4]

- **Alternating bundle-branch block, intraventricular conduction disturbance, sustained or nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, rapid paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, acute ischemia or ischemic heart disease or cardiomyopathy, type 1 Brugada pattern, repeated QTc longer than 460 milliseconds, slow atrial fibrillation below 40 beats/minute, or pacemaker or implantable-cardioverter-defibrillator dysfunction:** These are major high-risk electrical findings that require escalation because they can identify a potentially dangerous cardiac cause. [1][2][58]

### Under what conditions is expedited outpatient evaluation reasonable?

Expedited outpatient evaluation is reasonable for patients with only low-risk characteristics, no high-risk characteristics, and a likely reflex or orthostatic syncope diagnosis.[24] Stable intermediate-risk patients with unexplained syncope can instead undergo ED observation or enter a rapid cardiology/electrophysiology pathway rather than routine admission.[27][74] Patients with both high- and low-risk features should generally be managed as high risk unless a clear benign low-risk story explains the event.[24] The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS guideline rates outpatient management of presumptive reflex-mediated syncope without serious medical conditions as Class IIa, level C-LD, and supports outpatient care for selected suspected-cardiac-syncope patients without a serious condition when expedited syncope-specialist referral is available.[1][27][5][44] A normal troponin or a patient who feels better is not, by itself, a safe-discharge criterion.[27][5]

A low-risk reflex or vasovagal pattern usually has a clear trigger and prodrome, such as prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotion, nausea, sweating, pallor, or visual dimming, followed by complete recovery. Low-risk assessment also includes a normal cardiac examination and ECG, no significant structural heart disease, no dangerous associated symptom, no major injury, and safe mobility and social support.[27][69][29] Patients with a presumptive reflex-mediated cause and no serious medical condition can generally be managed as outpatients.[7][53]

Orthostatic hypotension is another possible outpatient diagnosis when it is documented and fits the event. Classical orthostatic hypotension is a sustained fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing, or a standing systolic pressure below 90 mmHg under the ESC definition.[19][74] Orthostatic measurements should be obtained after five minutes supine, then within one minute and again at three minutes standing.[58] Beta-blockers can contribute to orthostatic hypotension, but medication-associated hypotension does not exclude another simultaneous cause of syncope.[29]

### What could make the proposed monitoring and disposition plan unsafe

The principal safety risk is a **false reassurance error**: treating a brief, incompletely characterized dropped-beat pattern as benign before the actual tracings have been reviewed. Intermittent high-grade AV block can be transient, and telemetry or ambulatory ECG may be needed to show the event. Conversely, movement, poor electrode contact, loose leads, muscle activity, and electrical noise can create pseudo-pauses that mimic sinus arrest or high-grade AV block.[4][3]

The safety check is to require clinician review of the original ECG and telemetry strips, ideally with simultaneous multichannel recording when a pause or block is uncertain. Most artifact can be recognized from multichannel ECG, and false positive alarms can lead to inappropriate intervention.[3]

Judgment — **The operational rule should be “trace before label.”** Do not call a rhythm dangerous solely from a monitor alarm, but do not dismiss a recurring conduction pattern until a clinician has verified whether P waves are marching through, whether QRS complexes are truly absent, whether sinus rate changes around the event, and whether the recording quality is adequate.

A second safety risk is a **monitoring-gap error**: discharging him with a prescription, appeal, or order rather than with monitoring already in place. Ambulatory electrocardiographic (AECG) monitoring records heart rhythm during daily activities and can identify the existence and frequency of clinically significant rhythm disturbances and waveform abnormalities missed on a standard ECG. Detection is highest when monitoring begins near the index presentation and falls as time passes.[57][9][40]

The previously denied 24–48-hour Holter should not dictate the plan. A 24–48-hour AECG monitor is most appropriate for daily or near-daily symptoms; otherwise, select the AECG type and duration according to the evaluation, symptoms, and device-labeled indications. An external ambulatory event monitor is a medically necessary diagnostic alternative to Holter monitoring when arrhythmia-suggestive symptoms occur less often than once every 48 hours. The 2017 consensus recommends extended ambulatory ECG for 15–30 days when symptoms are not daily or their timing is uncertain, and continuous monitoring for 1–14 days to quantify arrhythmia burden and patterns. Device choice should reflect episode frequency, severity, suddenness of incapacitation, acceptance, local availability, and probability of a life-threatening arrhythmia.[59][65][40][53][3][57]

The daughter’s report is especially important for device design. A patient who becomes unable to speak, loses consciousness, or is otherwise incapacitated may not be able to press a button or initiate a patient-triggered event recording. Continuous recording and automatic triggering avoid reliance on patient action; mobile cardiac telemetry (MCOT) can transmit detected events to a staffed reading center and generate alerts without manual activation.[3][40][7]

If the patient ultimately qualifies for discharge after observation, the preferred external option is therefore an **immediately fitted continuous, auto-triggered monitor with defined escalation**, preferably MCOT if locally available and if real-time response can be provided. A 7–14-day continuous patch monitor is an alternative when continuous recording is needed but real-time transmission is unavailable; patches provide a single ECG lead and generally require later analysis.[7][41][4]

A purely patient-activated recorder is the option not chosen as the sole device. It is unsuitable as the only safeguard for this presentation because it depends on recognizing symptoms and activating the device in time. In one study, 18 of 32 patients with recurrent symptoms lacked a diagnosis because of device malfunction, non-compliance, or inability to activate the recorder; in a randomized comparison, 24% of loop-recorder patients failed to activate the device properly.[3][49][7][4]

Before discharge with any ambulatory monitor, complete the following safety checks:

1. **Device in hand and functioning:** Apply the monitor before departure, confirm signal acquisition, provide skin preparation and electrode-placement teaching, and document the monitor serial number and wear period. Incorrect electrode placement occurred in 26% in one cited study and impaired signal quality. [3][4]

2. **Automatic capture and event marking:** Confirm automatic detection is active. Instruct the patient and daughter to press the symptom marker if possible, but not to rely on it; record a diary of time, posture, symptoms, and witnessed features. Traditional ambulatory ECG systems often require a diary or symptom marker, while auto-triggering captures asymptomatic as well as symptomatic events. Several studies, including an analysis of a 100,000-patient database, found improved diagnostic yield with auto-triggered rather than patient-activated arrhythmia recordings. [3][4][56]

3. **Named results owner:** Identify the clinician, service, or electrophysiology team that receives alerts and final reports, and document who will contact the patient. Ambulatory notification criteria in the ISHNE-HRS consensus include reported syncope, symptomatic pauses of at least 4 seconds, and Mobitz II or third-degree AV block whether or not symptoms occur. [3]

A fourth safety risk is an **incomplete reversible-cause response**. Finally, more monitoring is not automatically better unless every apparent event is reviewed. Longer monitoring improves arrhythmia detection compared with 24–48-hour Holter recording, but it can increase false-positive findings. MCOT provides real-time transmission, yet randomized evidence has not established a clinically significant incremental outcome benefit from its real-time feature over other outpatient monitoring methods.[40][41][9]

The correct conclusion is not to avoid extended monitoring. It is to pair the chosen device with signal-quality review, a clinician response pathway, and symptom correlation.

The second unknown is whether the home events were syncope, presyncope, TIA, seizure, or another transient condition. Syncope requires loss of consciousness. The daughter described pallor and inability to speak, but not definite loss of consciousness. The third unknown is whether symptoms correlate with rhythm. A resting sinus rate of 52 beats/minute does not establish that bradycardia caused the fatigue or near-syncope. For sinus-node dysfunction, there is no minimum heart-rate or pause threshold that alone dictates pacing; temporal correlation between symptoms and bradycardia is important. The fourth unknown is whether orthostatic hypotension contributes. Older adults receiving antihypertensive medication are prone to orthostatic hypotension, including beta-blocker-related hypotension. Even a positive orthostatic test does not exclude coexisting intermittent arrhythmia or structural disease, especially in a patient with an abnormal rhythm description and recurrent spells.[29][39][69]

The fifth unknown is whether structural heart disease raises the stakes. Structural heart disease and primary electrical disease are major risk factors for sudden cardiac death and mortality in patients with syncope. Echocardiography, when indicated by history, examination, ECG, or monitoring, helps determine whether left-ventricular dysfunction, valvular disease, or another structural substrate is present.[8][75][1]

The sixth unknown is whether outpatient care can actually occur safely. The denied Holter order and six-week cardiology wait demonstrate that an outpatient plan has already failed operationally. A monitor order is not monitoring; a referral is not follow-up; and a future appointment cannot respond to a dangerous event tonight. If extended monitoring is eventually needed after a nondiagnostic observation or inpatient period, the escalation pathway is clear:

- **Frequent daily symptoms:** a 24–48-hour Holter may be appropriate. [3][59]
- **Weekly or uncertain symptoms:** a continuous patch monitor for 7–14 days, extended ambulatory ECG for 15–30 days, or auto-triggered external loop monitoring is more appropriate. [3][39][40][23]
- **Recurrent, infrequent unexplained events after nondiagnostic noninvasive monitoring:** an implantable loop recorder (ILR), a subcutaneous monitor with approximately 2–3 years of battery life and automatic arrhythmia detection, becomes a reasonable diagnostic escalation. [7][3][49]

Longer monitoring improves the opportunity to capture intermittent events. In one trial cited by the ISHNE-HRS consensus, symptom–rhythm correlation increased from 22% with a 48-hour Holter to 56% with one month of loop recording. In another cited study, the median time to correlation was 16 days, and 87% of correlations occurred by one month.[3]

However, an ILR is not a substitute for acute protection when immediate high-risk bradyarrhythmia is plausible. Implantable monitoring may diagnose an intermittent mechanism over time, but it may also leave the patient exposed to another spell while awaiting capture.[74][51]

### Synthesis

The pathway should begin with monitored evaluation, not another attempt to await outpatient services. The ECG description requires direct review because variable PR intervals and dropped beats do not specify AV-block degree; recurrent near-syncope and possible focal speech symptoms require telemetry, event reconstruction, neurologic assessment, orthostatic testing, medication review, and targeted structural assessment.
