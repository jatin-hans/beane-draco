Zimbabwe’s constitutional fast-track expropriation delivered by far the largest and quickest transfer of racially concentrated farmland, but at high immediate cost in production disruption, food insecurity among displaced farm workers, insecure tenure and land-linked political violence.

Zimbabwe’s annual maize output fell from about 1.7 million tonnes in 1991–2000 to about 0.7 million tonnes in 2001–10, then recovered to an average 1.6 million tonnes in 2017–21 as support and market connections improved, especially in tobacco.[1][2] South Africa’s mainly market-assisted, grant and state-acquisition approach has been less violent and less disruptive to aggregate commercial agriculture, but redistributed too little land to overturn concentration, with redistribution reaching 5.46% of commercial agricultural land by 2019 and redistribution plus restitution about 9–10% by March 2018.[3][2][1] Namibia’s purchase-and-resettlement and subsidised private-acquisition model has likewise produced gradual, limited redistribution, while proposals for stronger expropriation remain proposals rather than outcomes.

The decisive lesson is not that one legal label determines results, but that speed of transfer, tenure security, selection and inclusion of beneficiaries, finance, inputs, infrastructure, market access and political coercion determine whether land redistribution broadens productive wealth without undermining livelihoods and food security.

## Land reform outcomes

This comparison observes Zimbabwe from 2000 to the latest reported 2024 food-balance data, South Africa from 1994 to the latest reported 2023 food-security data, and Namibia’s implemented reforms and debates through the reported 2018 land statistics. Agricultural productivity is output per unit of land or another input; national crop figures are production or output, not productivity unless expressed per hectare. Food security is reliable access to sufficient food, rather than national crop output or food availability alone. Land ownership is control of land; broader wealth distribution includes income, productive and financial assets, and net wealth, not land area alone. Political violence here means violence tied to land acquisition, occupation or allocation, rather than general rural crime or unrelated political violence. A constitutional amendment changes the constitutional legal rules; negotiated acquisition is state or beneficiary purchase from a willing seller; and expropriation is compulsory state acquisition, with compensation determined by the applicable legal rule.[4][5][6][7]

Food insecurity can also be measured with the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), which records experiences of constrained food access, from reduced food quality or quantity to running out of food.[82]

The cost of a healthy diet measures the minimum daily price of a locally available 2,330-kilocalorie diet spanning six food groups, expressed in purchasing-power-parity dollars; it measures neither actual consumption nor food obtained from a household’s own farm.[82]

Colonial and apartheid land allocations left Zimbabwe, South Africa and Namibia with unequal landholding and different agricultural opportunities, complicating before-and-after production comparisons.[8][9][10] At Zimbabwe’s 1980 independence, white-dominated large-scale commercial farms occupied 11 million hectares, small-scale commercial farmers occupied 1.2 million hectares, and 1.3 million communal farmers occupied 16.3 million hectares.

At Namibia’s 1990 independence, 36.2 million hectares, or 52% of the 69.6 million hectares available for agriculture, were freehold or commercial land occupied by about 4,200 predominantly white farming households; 33.4 million hectares, or 48%, were communal land supporting about 70% of the population. Apartheid-era dispossession sharply restricted Black South Africans’ land rights; the 1994 democratic settlement committed the state to restitution, redistribution and tenure reform alongside constitutional property protection and compensated expropriation in the public interest.[11][9][8][12][7]

The post-independence property-and-redress settlements overlapped more than the labels constitutional amendment, market-based reform and expropriation suggest. Zimbabwe began under Lancaster House protections requiring prompt and adequate market-based compensation and a ten-year constraint on amendment, then moved to constitutional and statutory compulsory acquisition. Namibia adopted willing-seller–willing-buyer as its primary method under constitutional just-compensation protection, while retaining statutory compulsory-acquisition authority. South Africa combined constitutional redress duties and property protection with restitution, redistribution, tenure reform, negotiated acquisition and a constitutional power of expropriation.[13][7]

### Zimbabwe (2000-present)

Zimbabwe entered the Fast-Track Land Reform Programme (FTLRP) with an inherited dual agrarian structure. At independence in 1980, white-dominated large-scale commercial farms occupied 11 million hectares, while 1.3 million communal farmers occupied 16.3 million hectares; white-owned land generally had better rainfall and soils. By 1999, about 4,500 predominantly white commercial farmers still held 11 million hectares of the richest land, while more than one million Black families lived on 16 million hectares of poor communal land.[8][14][5]

An earlier Zimbabwean land baseline recorded 11.270 million hectares, or 35.1% of agricultural land, in large-scale commercial farms and 16.355 million hectares, or 50.9%, in communal areas.[83]

The post-independence settlement initially used willing-seller–willing-buyer acquisition. The Lancaster House constitutional safeguards limited compulsory acquisition for ten years and required prompt, adequate, market-based compensation. The early programme was orderly and supported with planning, infrastructure and specialist services, but it was slow, costly and often acquired scattered or marginal land.[13][15] The early resettlement programme nevertheless transferred close to three million hectares in its first decade, resettled 52,000 households affecting more than 400,000 people, and reduced the share of national land held by 6,000 white farmers from 42% in 1980 to 29% in 1990.[5]

One account reports that Zimbabwe’s planned resettlement programme had acquired about 3.4 million hectares before February 2000.[84]

After the Lancaster House restrictions expired on 18 April 1990, constitutional amendments and the 1992 Land Acquisition Act strengthened state power to acquire land compulsorily. The amendments retained compensation but replaced the earlier prompt-and-adequate market-value standard with fair compensation payable within a reasonable time; Parliament could prescribe compensation principles outside market value.[5][14] This did not initially generate redistribution at the intended pace: only 90,000 hectares were resettled between 1998 and 2000, against a plan of one million hectares annually.[13]

The FTLRP arose from this unresolved land pressure and from occupations that accelerated in 2000. It used two principal models: A1, intended mainly for poorer, land-short and landless households; and A2, intended to create Black small-, medium- and larger-scale commercial farmers. A1 formally began in late 2000; A2 gathered pace in late 2001 and 2002.[14][16] The programme was therefore not a uniform transfer from white farms to one homogeneous beneficiary group. It combined a broad smallholder settlement scheme with a commercially oriented scheme for more capitalised applicants.[17][18]

The scale of the transfer was large, though published counts differ because they count households, beneficiaries, plots, farms and acquired hectares differently. A Government of Zimbabwe table reported 145,775 A1 beneficiaries on 5,759,154 hectares and 16,386 A2 beneficiaries on 2,978,334 hectares in 2000–08, within a total of 237,858 beneficiaries and 12,404,196 hectares across all phases since 1980.[17] Another account reports that the FTLRP officially benefited 168,671 families across 9.2 million hectares, with A1 households receiving 70% of transferred land and average allocations of 20 hectares; by 2010, more than 22,000 A2 beneficiaries held plots averaging about 100 hectares.[18] A government resettlement manual gives a higher summary figure: over 12 million hectares acquired from white farmers and more than 170,000 households legally resettled.[19]

One administrative account reported that Zimbabwe’s Fast Track programme had transferred 6.4 million hectares to 134,452 beneficiaries by August 2003.[84]

A separate administrative table recorded 232,738 families resettled on 12.5 million hectares across 1980–2005; another account counted 134,452 Fast Track beneficiaries and 6.4 million hectares by August 2003.[85]

By August 2003, Fast Track had reportedly transferred 6.4 million hectares to about 134,452 beneficiary units.[84]

Zimbabwe’s tobacco production rebounded from roughly 49 million kilograms in 2008 to approximately 296 million kilograms in 2023.

Zimbabwe’s maize harvest fell to roughly half a million tonnes in the drought- and crisis-affected 2008 season. This was a steep decline from pre-2000 harvests, but it does not isolate the effect of redistribution from drought and the wider economic crisis.

The physical change in landholding was decisive. By 2009, government had acquired about 10.8 million hectares from 12.3 million hectares of commercial land.[17] A later assessment estimated that the former large-farm sector, which had occupied roughly 15 million hectares in 1980 and 12.5 million hectares in the 1990s, had fallen to perhaps three million hectares or less after fast track; communal areas, old resettlement areas and A1 areas together represented 62% of farming land.[20] One analysis estimates that small farm producers held over 70% of Zimbabwe’s agricultural land after redistribution, middle-scale farmers about 13%, and large farms and estates 11%.[18]

This was a substantial de-racialisation of land access and a transfer of control over productive land. The FTLRP transformed the agrarian structure from a bimodal system dominated by large-scale commercial farms and communal areas into a tri-modal structure that included A1 smallholders and A2 commercial farmers.[8][16] It also shifted much formerly freehold agricultural land into state ownership, with beneficiaries holding permits, offer letters and leases rather than ordinary private title.[16][17]

The legal route was consequential. Constitutional Amendment No. 16 in 2000 expanded compulsory acquisition and limited Zimbabwe’s compensation obligation for qualifying resettlement land to improvements where the former colonial power did not provide a compensation fund.[14][5] Constitutional Amendment No.

17, published on 16 September 2005, inserted section 16B. It vested listed agricultural land in the state, provided no compensation for the land itself except for pre-acquisition improvements, and barred court challenges to acquisition while permitting challenges to the amount payable for improvements.[6] The law therefore accelerated legal transfer, but it also weakened former owners’ legal remedies and reinforced state control over beneficiaries’ tenure.

The state did not convert most beneficiaries into secure freehold owners. Offer letters confirmed allocation but did not permit sale, rental or mortgage, because the state retained ownership.[17] A1 beneficiaries were intended to receive permits, while A2 beneficiaries were intended to receive 25- or 99-year leases; at the time of the 2005/06 baseline survey, 78.5% of beneficiaries reported only letters or verbal land offers rather than formal permits or lease documents.[16] The resulting tenure uncertainty mattered for investment.

Banks treated leaseholds as weak collateral, particularly because the state resisted granting lenders transfer rights over land when borrowers failed.[17] Farmers reported that insecure tenure, audit threats and possible removal reduced their willingness to make long-term improvements.[17][16]

Agricultural production initially fell sharply. National maize production, an output measure rather than a yield measure, averaged around 1.7 million tonnes annually in 1991–2000, around 0.7 million tonnes in 2001–10 during fast-track expropriation, and 1.6 million tonnes in 2017–21, including a large 2021 harvest.[1][2] The calculated change was a 58.8% fall from 1991–2000 to 2001–10, a 128.6% recovery from 2001–10 to 2017–21, and a 5.9% shortfall in 2017–21 relative to the 1991–2000 average.[1] The 2017–21 average therefore remained below the pre-fast-track average.[1]

Zimbabwe’s official 2024/25 Post-Harvest Survey covered approximately 58,000 farming households in eight rural provinces and used different sampling approaches for A1, A2, communal, commercial and irrigation farms.[64]

The FTLRP was one major cause of the fall, but it operated alongside drought, macroeconomic collapse, hyperinflation, input shortages, credit withdrawal, disrupted markets and state marketing controls. The 2005/06 AIAS baseline survey identified the FTLRP as a major source of agricultural decline while also identifying poor rainfall, drought, economic crisis, international isolation and disrupted input and output markets.[16] Zimbabwe’s real GDP fell by 5.5% in 2000 and 7.5% in 2001; a contemporary FAO account attributed the collapse to agricultural shocks including land invasions, bad rainfall and poor economic management.[15] The appropriate conclusion is that rapid ownership change disrupted production, but the size and duration of the decline reflected the interaction of redistribution with a much wider economic crisis.

Zimbabwe’s early Fast Track disruption coincided with drought in 2001/02 and 2002/03, compounding declines in maize, tobacco, wheat, soya, beef and horticulture.[86]

An account of Zimbabwe’s 2000–08 crisis reports that real GDP fell by more than 40% as the country moved from food exporter to food importer.[87]

Farm-level evidence shows varied results rather than a uniform collapse. In Shamva District in the 2004/05 season, reported maize yields were 1.1 tonnes per hectare in communal areas, 1.3 tonnes in old resettlement areas, 2.5 tonnes in A1 schemes and 3.2 tonnes in A2 farms. A1 farms accounted for 26% of district maize production and A2 farms for 6%.[17] In contrast, the AIAS baseline survey found that yields for almost all studied food and cash crops were below former large-scale commercial-farm yields and some communal-area yields. It attributed lower yields principally to inadequate access to improved seed, fertiliser, credit, draught power and machinery, with adverse weather also important.[16]

These findings are consistent. The Shamva comparison measures yields among locally observed sectors in one season; the wider survey compares new farms with a capitalised commercial sector built over decades and finds a lower national-level performance pattern. Beneficiaries could outperform communal farmers in particular local settings while remaining below former commercial-farm productivity overall. A cited econometric study reaches a similar conclusion: beneficiaries were more productive than communal farmers but less productive than commercial farms before and after redistribution.[8]

The tobacco sector demonstrates that production outcomes were not fixed by the legal form of redistribution. Tobacco output averaged about 220 million kilograms in 1996–2000, fell to lows of around 60 million kilograms after fast track, and then recovered as smallholder contract farming expanded.[2] A Chinese company introduced an input, extension and purchasing scheme for smallholders in 2006, with British companies also investing.

By 2019, about 160,000 growers produced output comparable with the best earlier years.[2][1] The recovery relied on connections to finance, inputs, extension and buyers rather than land transfer alone.[2]

Tobacco recovery also had distributional and food-security limits. Contract farming supplied credit and inputs, but it could expose growers to manipulated prices, expensive inputs, debt and weather risk. In some areas, expansion of tobacco reduced maize production, the local staple, and tobacco curing increased pressure on fuelwood resources.[21] A recovery in an export crop is therefore not equivalent to recovery in staple-food production or household nutrition.

The early resettlement years were marked by serious production constraints. In the 2005/06 baseline survey, 53.7% of beneficiary households identified unavailable inputs as a production grievance.[16] Fifty-five percent of surveyed beneficiaries cited no land grievance as a key issue, but 21% cited grievances over access to and secure use of land, including the risk that offer letters could be withdrawn without compensation for improvements.[16] Around 49% cited unavailable credit, and government had become the principal provider of credit and input support in resettlement areas, unlike communal areas where private firms and NGOs were also active.[16]

The disruption of productive infrastructure mattered. Some subdivisions made irrigation infrastructure unviable or dysfunctional and interfered with specialised operations such as tobacco rotations and dairy.[19] Land occupations and accelerated removals also damaged or idled irrigation systems, tobacco-curing facilities, machinery, pumps and generators.[17][16] The constraint was therefore not simply that new farmers lacked skill. It was that assets, finance, labour, machinery, market channels and tenure arrangements were reorganised at the same time as land ownership.

Food-security outcomes followed this mixed pattern. At household level, close to 90% of surveyed newly resettled households reported own agricultural production as their most important source of food, supplemented by market purchases; fewer than 5% relied principally on food aid, food-for-work or employer rations.[16] Many beneficiaries therefore gained a direct subsistence base.

Yet the AIAS survey found no significant difference in food insecurity, morbidity or school attendance between newly resettled and communal areas. It identified greater per-capita access to arable land and natural-resource foods as possible advantages for resettled households, except former farm workers who remained unemployed or land-poor.[16]

Former commercial farm workers were the most clearly disadvantaged group in the transition. At least 150,000 former farm workers were estimated to have been left without secure housing, land or jobs, and the baseline survey reported evictions and conflict between former workers and new settlers.[16] In the AIAS farm-worker sample, only 14.8% reported receiving a land allocation under the FTLRP. More than 70% of interviewed farm workers reported food-security challenges in resettlement areas, and their gazetted wages averaged below 10% of the rural poverty datum line during the survey period.[16] Redistribution improved land access for many rural households, but it displaced a wage-dependent population whose access to land and services was often insecure before reform and became more precarious after it.

Wealth distribution changed more clearly in landholding than in broader household wealth. The reform transferred large areas from a racially concentrated commercial sector to Black A1 and A2 beneficiaries.[18] It also created some asset accumulation among beneficiaries: in the 2005/06 baseline survey, 62% of households had built homesteads after resettlement, 13.4% had bought livestock, 10.3% had invested in irrigation equipment and 7.2% had acquired farm equipment or machinery.[16] Livestock provided meat, milk, draught power, cash income and a buffer in years of food shortage.[17]

The gains were unequally distributed. A2 farmers had greater access to tractors than A1 farmers, with reported ownership of 31.7% versus 4.8%.[16] Some beneficiaries accumulated multiple or oversized holdings through illegal, corrupt or pseudo-legal means, reducing the redistributive character of the programme.[18] Political and administrative links could also influence allocation.

A Supreme Court judgment held that targeting farmers believed to support the opposition while allocating expropriated land mainly to ruling-party supporters constituted unfair discrimination.[17] Land redistribution thus reduced racial concentration while also creating a differentiated Black agrarian structure.

Women gained more direct access than under the previous commercial-farm and communal systems, but remained underrepresented. Government sources cited in the baseline survey put women at about 17% of beneficiaries in their own right, while other studies gave a range of 10–28%.[16] In Mazowe sample surveys, women received 16% of A1 allocations in 2004 and 18% in 2007; in A2, their share rose from 7% to 15%.[17] The programme expanded women’s formal access, but occupation-based allocation and patriarchal household structures continued to disadvantage many women.[17][16]

One assessment reported that rural poor people constituted approximately 87% of Zimbabwe’s land-reform beneficiaries, while women constituted 18% of A1 and 12% of A2 beneficiaries and former farm workers about 2%.[85]

Political violence was directly connected to the implementation of fast-track redistribution. Occupations began before the formal programme and peaked between 2000 and 2002. In a survey of recorded occupations, 24.4% occurred in 2000, 46.3% in 2001 and 17.2% in 2002.[16] The term *jambanja* was used for occupations involving intimidation and violence, including all-night demonstrations, threats, beatings, killing or maiming livestock and pressure on police not to intervene.[17]

Human Rights Watch reported that commercial farms were used as bases by war veterans and ZANU-PF militia to intimidate alleged opposition supporters in nearby communal areas.[22] It also reported police failure to act against alleged violent crimes and, in some cases, active assistance to illegal actions; the army helped organise and facilitate occupations without checking violence.[22] The violence therefore cannot be treated as unrelated rural insecurity. It was part of the political machinery through which occupations, eviction, partisan mobilisation and access to land were organised.

Another analysis describes the farm invasions as evolving into a centrally endorsed and coordinated purge of commercial farms intended to weaken support for the opposition MDC among farmers and farm workers.[88][89]

The programme’s violence also had distributive effects. Men were more likely to participate in confrontational occupations and could thereby gain priority in allocation, while women who remained in communal areas to care for households were often excluded.[17] Former farm workers were sometimes driven from farms by violence and often excluded from resettlement, particularly workers of foreign descent.[22] At the same time, not all occupations followed the same pattern: some occupiers used courts and bureaucratic procedures to sustain land claims, and formal government allocation was the main reported route of access among beneficiaries in the 2005/06 survey.[21][16] The process combined coercive occupation, local political mobilisation and state-administered allocation.

**Zimbabwe’s fast-track programme achieved the largest and most rapid racial redistribution of agricultural land among the three cases, but its first decade produced a deep maize decline, serious food insecurity for many farm workers, insecure tenure and violent partisan implementation; later tobacco recovery shows that production can rebound when new producers receive finance, inputs, extension and market access.**[1][2][16][22]

The national maize averages imply a 58.8% fall from 1.7 million tonnes in 1991–2000 to 0.7 million tonnes in 2001–10, followed by a 128.6% recovery to 1.6 million tonnes in 2017–21; the later average was still 5.9% below the pre-fast-track average.[1]

Within the Government of Zimbabwe’s 2000–08 Fast-Track totals for A1 and A2, A1 accounted for 65.9% of acquired area but 89.9% of beneficiaries. This quantifies the programme’s dual structure: the smaller-holder A1 route reached far more people, while A2 used a disproportionately large share of land for comparatively few beneficiaries.[17]

Zimbabwe’s 2024 food-balance sheet measures national food availability, while ZIMSTAT identifies frequent droughts, Covid-19, El Niño and the Russia–Ukraine war as additional causes of declining agricultural production; it does not measure a stand-alone effect of redistribution.[4]

Zimbabwe’s 2024–25 rural food-security assessment projected that approximately 5.9 million rural residents would face food insecurity during the January–March 2025 lean season.

Zimbabwe’s 2024/25 Post-Harvest Survey projected a 456,937-tonne cereal surplus for April 2025–May 2026 at consumption of 7.7 kilograms per person per month, but only 42,937 tonnes at 10 kilograms and 312,937 tonnes at the SADC average of 8.5 kilograms.[64]

Zimbabwe’s 2024 maize harvest was estimated at about 635,000 tonnes after severe drought, showing that post-2000 agricultural output has not followed a single uninterrupted trend.

The Lancaster House settlement also made early large-scale redistribution dependent on willing sellers, willing buyers and external funding. By 2000, stalled redistribution, war-veteran claims and an electoral crisis combined with the inherited land grievance to produce the fast-track rupture.

FAO’s 2025 food-security estimates add a recent national access and dietary-energy measure to the household evidence. The prevalence of undernourishment, meaning habitual dietary-energy consumption insufficient for a normal, active and healthy life, declined from 29.5% in 2004–06 to 19.7% in 2022–24. Severe food insecurity declined from 35.5% in 2014–16 to 25.1% in 2022–24, while moderate or severe food insecurity rose from 64.7% to 68.8%.[23] The corresponding estimated numbers were 3.7 million and 3.2 million undernourished people; 5.1 million and 4.1 million severely food-insecure people; and 9.3 million and 11.2 million moderately or severely food-insecure people, respectively.[23] The 2025 Zimbabwe Livelihoods Assessment Committee projected that 15% of rural households, approximately 1.5 million people, would be cereal insecure in the January–March 2026 peak-hunger period and require 118,563 metric tonnes of cereal support.[24]

Human Rights Watch (2002) documented the early fast-track violence in more detail. The Commercial Farmers’ Union recorded at least 829 violent or hostile incidents on commercial farms through September 2001, while human-rights groups and the union reported at least seven farmers killed in political violence from the beginning of 2000. The National Employment Council for the agricultural industry reported in June 2000 that occupations had displaced at least 3,000 farm workers, killed 26, assaulted 1,600 and raped 11.[25]

Farm workers, who numbered an estimated 300,000–400,000 wage earners before the crisis, were not a principal target group for fast-track allocations. By October 2001, official statistics recorded 2,122 farm-worker households among 123,979 resettled households, or 1.7%; the Commercial Farmers’ Union estimated that 75,000 people or 13,636 families had been forced off land or laid off by that month, while the United Nations estimated 30,000 displaced farm-worker families by January 2002.[25]

Human Rights Watch (2002) found that informal war-veteran and ruling-party militia processes could supersede allocation through civil servants and rural district councils. There were no published records of reasons for accepting or rejecting applicants and no appeal after rejection; applicants reported that opposition affiliation or failure to produce a ZANU-PF card could prevent access to land.[25]

Women’s formal access also lagged behind their role in agriculture. In October 2000 government announced a 20% fast-track quota for women, but Human Rights Watch reported that it was not implemented and that no legal or administrative framework guaranteed gender equality in allocation. The report also documented sexual exploitation of women seeking allocation and rape during occupations.[25]

A later longitudinal study of Goromonzi and Zvimba provides evidence of differentiated, locally improving production after the initial crisis. The study recorded 1,274.85 tonnes of maize on 327 hectares among Goromonzi A1 beneficiaries in 2015–17, including a 928-tonne marketable surplus; between 2018 and 2021, maize production rose 52.3% among Goromonzi A2 farmers and 31.2% among Zvimba A2 farmers, while tobacco cultivation rose 72.4% among Goromonzi A1 farmers and 47.3% among Zvimba A1 farmers.[26]

The same study found cropping intensity in Goromonzi A1 areas increased by 8.2 percentage points between 2019 and 2022, and 89.28% of its 2022–24 beneficiaries had occupied their holdings continuously for about two decades. It also recorded average total crop production of 8.4 tonnes per Goromonzi A1 household and 6.1 tonnes per Zvimba A1 household.[26]

These local post-reform gains coexist with the national maize series: they show that A1 and A2 outcomes varied by district, crop, capital and market connection, rather than reversing the documented national production collapse of 2001–10.[1][2][26]

Later evidence reinforces the finding that outcomes within the redistributed sector are stratified rather than uniform. In research conducted in Goromonzi and Zvimba in 2015–2017 and 2018–2021, A2 farmers consistently earned higher absolute incomes than A1 farmers, and Goromonzi outperformed Zvimba in both tenure groups; the reported differences reflected resource endowments, scale, farmer capacity, technical expertise, support services and strategic partnerships.[26] In 2019/20–2020/21, gross income rose from US$474,329 to US$568,808 for Goromonzi A1 farmers and from US$1,512,864 to US$1,803,136 for A2 farmers; in Zvimba it rose from US$255,230 to US$317,740 for A1 and from US$558,860 to US$691,925 for A2.[26] These are local farm-group income totals, but they demonstrate both growth and continuing differentiation between A1 and A2 production systems.[26]

The same Goromonzi–Zvimba evidence records rising marketed maize among both farm types. A1 maize sales in Goromonzi increased from 790,300 kg in 2018/19 to 1,007,500 kg in 2020/21, and A2 sales from 2,559,000 kg to 3,897,900 kg; in Zvimba, the respective changes were 264,500 kg to 280,000 kg for A1 and 1,239,400 kg to 1,626,000 kg for A2.[27] Total quantities sold across the study districts rose from 3,741,665 kg in Goromonzi and 1,598,350 kg in Zvimba in 2018/19 to 5,313,265 kg and 2,018,200 kg respectively in 2020/21.[27] This local recovery is consistent with the national pattern of selective recovery, not with a claim that productivity was equal across beneficiaries or regions.[26][27][28]

Recent farm-level asset evidence also shows accumulation alongside inequality. In 2022–2024, truck ownership was 54% among Goromonzi A2 farmers and 19% among A1 farmers; in Zvimba it was 39% among A2 farmers and 12% among A1 farmers.[26] Borehole ownership in Goromonzi rose from 8% in the 2015–2017 study to 57% among A2 farmers and 45% among A1 farmers in 2022–2024, while storage-facility ownership reached 95% among Goromonzi A1 farmers and 50% among Zvimba A1 farmers, compared with 15.3% of Goromonzi A1 farmers owning storage sheds in 2015–2017.[26] These investments strengthened irrigation capacity, sanitation, climate resilience and storage in the groups concerned, but A2 farmers retained a marked advantage in capital-intensive assets.[26]

Gendered outcomes remained differentiated. In 2022–2024, female-headed A1 households recorded income growth of 16.8%, compared with 21.2% for male-headed households, but allocated 18.3% of income to education and 8.9% to health, compared with 12.7% and 5.4% respectively among male-headed households.[26] Sixty-seven percent of women beneficiaries reported primary or shared control over household income-allocation decisions; women’s participation in farmer associations rose from 23% in 2015–2017 to 41% in 2022–2024, and women held 35% of association leadership positions in the latter period.[26]

Food-security outcomes also depended on production support and agro-ecology. In the 2019/20 and 2020/21 seasons, the Goromonzi–Zvimba study recorded total maize retained rising from 812,651 to 850,700 units, including maize retained for consumption rising from 97,830 to 99,320 and food reserves from 411,147 to 421,285.[27] The same holdings retained maize for stockfeed, wage payments and gifts, demonstrating that staple output served household consumption, livestock production, labour remuneration and social transfers as well as sales.[27] Beneficiaries interviewed in the later study reported improved household food security, greater capacity to invest in education and health care, and lower exposure to external economic shocks.[26]

These later improvements followed substantial state agricultural-support interventions rather than land transfer alone. Pfumvudza/Intwasa supplied seed, fertiliser, pesticides and extension support to 1.6 million rural households in the 2022/23 season.[27] Earlier post-fast-track policy combined commodity-market controls, trade protection, regulated input and food prices, a Grain Marketing Board grain-buying monopoly, and Command Agriculture’s subsidised inputs and credit directed toward output targets.[28] The production record therefore confirms that access to land interacted with input finance, seed, fertiliser, irrigation, credit, markets and public support.[28]

The early productivity collapse was especially severe in capital-intensive and irrigation-dependent activities.Wheat area fell from a 1990s average of 58,000 hectares to 18,200 hectares in 2010; its 2005/06 rebound followed concentrated subsidised inputs, before output deteriorated from 2006/07 to 2010/11 as subsidies were lost and electricity supply for irrigation worsened.[28]
The mechanisms of unequal performance are measurable.

Redistribution also remade labour relations without eliminating exclusion.Landlessness persisted especially among farm labourers and residents of overcrowded communal areas; full-time hired labour declined while short-term hired labour expanded.[28] In the Goromonzi–Zvimba study, A1 farms’ casual-worker totals increased from 1,344 in 2020 to 1,526 in 2022 and A2 totals from 2,371 to 2,644, while permanent-worker totals changed from 135 to 140 for A1 and 271 to 278 for A2.[27] The new agrarian structure thus combined expanded self-employment and smallholder production with continuing class differentiation, casualisation and unequal access to productive resources.[28]

### South Africa (post-1994)

South Africa’s post-1994 settlement placed land reform within a constitutional framework that balances property protection with redress. The Constitution provides restitution or equitable redress for people dispossessed after 19 June 1913 by racially discriminatory laws or practices; requires reasonable measures to enable equitable access to land; provides secure tenure or comparable redress for people whose tenure became insecure through racial discrimination; and permits expropriation in the public interest.[7]

South Africa’s land administration distinguishes surveyed, registered land from communal or state-held land with weaker tenure; the state holds at least 17 million hectares of communal land in trust.[90]

The programme has three pillars: restitution, redistribution and tenure reform. Restitution is the return of land or another form of redress to qualifying claimants; redistribution is acquisition or transfer of land to improve access; tenure reform is the securing of rights for people whose occupation is legally insecure.[29][7] These pillars have different beneficiaries and cannot be treated as one single measure of farmland redistribution.

Redistribution initially relied mainly on grant-assisted market purchase. The Settlement/Land Acquisition Grant (SLAG) operated from 1994 to 2000 and encouraged communities to pool grants to purchase land through trusts and Communal Property Associations (CPAs). It was followed by Land Redistribution for Agricultural Development (LRAD) from 2000 to 2006, and then increasingly by the Proactive Land Acquisition Strategy (PLAS), introduced in 2006.[1] Under PLAS, the state acquired farmland and generally leased it to beneficiaries rather than transferring ownership directly, especially after 2011.[2][29]

One account dates the adoption of South Africa’s Proactive Land Acquisition Strategy to 2007 and reports that it enabled the state to identify, acquire, develop and allocate land, with a shift principally toward leasehold after 2009.[90]

This was not a pure willing-buyer–willing-seller system in which individual beneficiaries bought farms unaided. The state subsidised purchases, acquired land itself, made state land available, and used collective entities such as trusts and CPAs.[13][30][5][29] Nevertheless, market purchase at or near market compensation was the central route, and this limited the pace at which the state could acquire high-quality agricultural land.

The scale of transfer was much smaller than in Zimbabwe. The Presidential Advisory Panel on Land Reform and Agriculture reported that redistribution had transferred 5.46% of commercial agricultural land by 2019 and that the pace had declined since 2008.[3] A separate estimate records that by March 2018, state redistribution and restitution together had transferred 9–10% of agricultural land, about 8.4 million hectares: around 6% through redistribution and around 4% through restitution.[2][1] A 2021 estimate including private purchases put the transferred share at 17%, or 14.5–15 million hectares, but government does not publish a full count of market transfers.[2]

These figures use different scopes. The 5.46% figure refers to commercial agricultural land transferred through redistribution; the 9–10% figure combines state redistribution and restitution; the 17% estimate adds private purchases. They all point in the same direction: South Africa has made real transfers, but it has not met the long-standing objective of transferring 30% of agricultural land.[3][2][9]

Land ownership remains racially unequal. The 2017 Land Audit found that white individuals held 26,663,144 of 37,078,289 hectares of farmland and agricultural holdings registered to individuals, or 72%, while African individuals held 1,314,873 hectares, or 4%.[31] The racial composition of all land ownership remains difficult to calculate precisely because cadastral data do not fully capture communal, trust and public land; a 2013 audit found that 79% of all land may have been privately owned but did not establish owners’ racial composition.[32] The direction is nonetheless clear: redistribution has not yet transformed the inherited concentration of agricultural land at national scale.

South Africa’s 2015 Land Audit counted 114,223,276 hectares registered at the Deeds Office, or 94% of national land; its racial ownership calculation for farms covered only the 37,078,289 hectares owned by individuals.[31]

The audit’s 72% White and 4% African ownership figures refer to individually owned farms and agricultural holdings in 2015; they exclude land held through companies, trusts, communities and the state.[31][90]

Restitution has reached many people but has often delivered cash rather than land. The Restitution of Land Rights Act 22 of 1994 created a route for people dispossessed after 1913 through racial laws or practices to seek restoration, alternative land or financial compensation.[29][5] As of 2023, Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development data recorded 82,976 settled restitution claims, four million hectares secured at a cost of R25.6 billion, R22.3 billion in financial compensation, 456,089 beneficiary households and 2.3 million beneficiaries.[29]

South African restitution provides for investigation and negotiated settlement, with Land Claims Court adjudication where settlement fails.[91]

Most restitution claims have not resulted in land return. Around 88% of claims lodged by 1998 were urban, and most were settled through cash payments.[11] A more recent review reports that more than 90% of restitution claims were settled through financial compensation, with that pattern persisting through 2019/20.[9] Cash compensation can provide useful immediate resources, but it does not alter rural landholding or secure agricultural livelihoods in the way that restored land can.

Restitution has delivered secure tenure where land is transferred. Land acquired through restitution has generally been transferred with complete tenure security, often to community trusts or elected committees.[9] Yet collective ownership also creates governance challenges.

In Loeriesfontein, a CPA was established in 2008, but title to the first of three commonage farms was transferred only in 2014, and the state had not transferred all commonage land by the time described.[1] In Ravele, ownership of fruit and macadamia farms passed to seven CPAs or trusts in 2005, but early private partnerships failed and left communities in debt before recapitalisation and new management produced profitable years.[1]

The agricultural outcome on transferred farms was uneven because land acquisition was often not matched by post-settlement support, finance, management capacity and secure tenure. The former minister Gugile Nkwinti estimated in 2010 that production had declined on 90% of transferred farms.[1] In a North West Province study of 37 projects visited in 2010, ten farms had no farming activity; three had never produced since beneficiaries obtained land, while others were used for housing or settlement rather than agriculture.[33]

These failures were not inevitable consequences of collective ownership or Black farming. Productive cases show the importance of institutional design and support:

- **Grasslands dairy partnership:** Forty-nine Black dairy workers acquired a 485-hectare farm through a trust, with the state subsidising 35% of the transfer. The company supplied cows, inputs, skills, labour and market access, while the trust received 60% of profits; members received around R150,000 a year in recent years in addition to wages. [2]

- **Ravele restitution project:** After failed initial partnerships, recapitalisation and a later operator restored commercial performance. Turnover reached R9.7 million in 2016, and around 300 members chose reinvestment over a possible R30,000 cash distribution. [2]

- **Eastern Cape wool scheme:** The National Wool Growers Association scheme began in 1997 and supplied breeding stock, extension, training, wool sheds and marketing channels. By 2018, 25,000 participants earned R383 million from wool, averaging R15,000 per owner. [2][1]

These cases show that landholding produces income when it is combined with production systems, finance, technical knowledge, infrastructure and reliable market access. The same principle is visible in the state’s own extension shortfall: in 2021, South Africa had one agricultural extension officer for every 850 farmers, while the budget target was one officer for every 250 farmers after recruitment of 10,000 additional officers. Recruitment had been slow.[29]

Food security remains poor in many rural and farming-area households. Statistics South Africa recorded moderate-to-severe food insecurity in 19.7% of households nationally in 2023 and severe food insecurity in 8.0%. The corresponding rates in traditional areas were 26.8% and 10.8%; farm areas recorded 20.9% and 8.0%.[34] Food insecurity was highest among female-headed households in farming areas, where around one in four faced moderate-to-severe food insecurity and 10.4% experienced severe food insecurity.[34]

These national and settlement-type measures should not be treated as the effect of land redistribution alone. They show that access to rural or farm land has not automatically protected households from food insecurity. One review reports a 10% reduction in food insecurity among restitution-recipient households, including reported gains in output and community benefits in Buffalo City.[9] The overall picture is therefore mixed: particular land-return projects can improve food access, but national food insecurity remains high in the rural settings where land reform is most relevant.

Land reform has also not delivered a broad redistribution of wealth. Broader wealth includes income, employment, assets, financial claims and public services, as well as land. South Africa’s pretax income inequality in 2019 was as high as in 1993; racial disparities widened between 1993 and 2005, and later declines returned inequality only toward its 1993 level.[35] Restitution payments are programme redress, not a national net-wealth measure: as of 2023, R22.3 billion had been paid as financial compensation, alongside four million hectares secured at a cost of R25.6 billion.[29] Project earnings are likewise not national wealth measures: at Grasslands, trust members received around R150,000 annually in recent years in addition to wages.[2]

Programme benefits have also been unequal. The Presidential Advisory Panel found evidence that redistribution was increasingly benefiting elites and reported that women constituted only 23% of programme beneficiaries.[3] A literature review similarly reports that women generally constituted less than 25% of beneficiaries and that emerging farmers and elites increasingly captured benefits while vulnerable groups remained underserved.[9] The transition from SLAG to LRAD and PLAS also shifted policy toward larger farms and applicants with business plans, making reform more commercially selective.[1]

Tenure insecurity has limited the wealth effects of transfer. PLAS shifted the state toward ownership and oversight of land acquired for reform. It acquired 2.5 million hectares of farmland, but a survey of close to 2,000 farms representing 98% of PLAS farms between 2006 and 2018 found all were leasehold.[29][9] Leasehold can support farming where terms are secure and support is available, but it does not provide the same ownership rights as title and may reduce beneficiaries’ ability to use land as collateral or make long-horizon investments.

South Africa’s post-1994 land-reform disputes include conflict over resources, under-use, asset stripping and violence in some KwaZulu-Natal projects; farm attacks require separate attribution because the police definition and reported motives include land issues alongside robbery, labour disputes, poverty, unemployment, evictions and other factors.[36][37] At Malangskraal, a 5,200-hectare farm allocated in 2011 was informally divided among three parties; intermittent disputes concerned water access, fencing and the contested division before two beneficiaries received 30-year leases in 2019.[36] At Loeriesfontein, the minister acknowledged disputes regarding land access and the need to regularise the Communal Property Association in December 2019.[1]

Land reform has also sometimes reduced farm-worker livelihoods. At Nangkoos, the state purchased a 448-hectare farm in 2009 for 32 resident families. They formed a CPA, but by 2016 the state still owned the farm, residents had no lease or documented tenure rights, and local jobs and piece-work opportunities had disappeared.[36] This was a land-reform-linked loss of employment and tenure security, not evidence that all rural violence or deprivation is caused by reform.

The debate over expropriation without compensation (EWC) did not culminate in a constitutional amendment. In February 2018, the National Assembly adopted a motion to amend the Constitution to permit EWC, and the issue went to the Joint Constitutional Review Committee.[7] The committee recommended amendment in November 2018, but the amendment bill failed to receive the required two-thirds majority in the National Assembly on 7 December 2021.[1] The existing Constitution permits expropriation in the public interest with just-and-equitable compensation, which may be below market value; the separate proposal for zero compensation was not enacted through the failed amendment process.[11][1]

**South Africa’s market-led and constitutionally bounded programme has delivered restitution, some secure collective ownership and important productive successes, but it has transferred land too slowly and unevenly to alter national ownership and wealth inequality; its central failure is the gap between acquisition and the tenure, finance, support and governance needed to make transferred land a durable livelihood asset.**[3][9][1][29][35]

For the 2018–22 cultivation period, non-commercial maize yields of about 1.8 tonnes per hectare were 70.0% below the approximately 6 tonnes per hectare reported for commercial farms.[2]

The 2010 North West study’s ten projects with no farming activity represented 27.0% of its 37 visited projects.[33]

South African agricultural outcomes include sector yields as well as project evidence. Government estimates put non-commercial maize cultivation at an average of 332,000 hectares and output at about 600,000 tonnes in 2018–22; the reported non-commercial yield was about 1.8 tonnes per hectare, compared with 6 tonnes per hectare on commercial farms. The non-commercial yield was therefore 70.0% below the commercial-farm yield.[2] This is a sector comparison, not a measure solely of land-reform beneficiaries.

A South African modelling study found that a hypothetical 50% total-factor-productivity drought shock would reduce agricultural output by 19.5%, raise agricultural prices by 43.3% and increase imports by 84.8%; these are modelled effects, not observations from a particular drought.[92]

The constitutional route to expropriation is more flexible than a market-value rule but remains legally bounded. Section 25(2) permits expropriation only under a law of general application, for a public purpose or in the public interest, with compensation agreed by those affected or decided or approved by a court. Section 25(3) requires compensation that is just and equitable, considering current use; acquisition and use history; market value; direct state investment and subsidy; and the purpose of expropriation. The Constitution expressly includes land reform and equitable access to natural resources within the public interest.[38]

The Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development’s 2020–25 Strategic Plan describes the predominant national approach over the preceding 25 years as government-mediated willing-buyer–willing-seller acquisition coupled with legal restitution. It records that Settlement/Land Acquisition Grants operated from 1995 to 1999, offering poor households grants of up to R16,000 for market purchases. The department found that grants below market land prices and community pooling to purchase farms did not substantially change ownership patterns and produced social tensions and inefficiencies. LRAD, established in 2000, then focused principally on agricultural land transfers to individuals intending commercial farming.[39]

The same strategic plan records the scale of the inherited ownership problem and the shortfall against policy targets. It estimates that white capital owned 83% of agricultural land in 1994; government’s original target was to redistribute 30%, or 24.7 million hectares, by 2014, later reduced in the National Development Plan to 16.5 million hectares, approximately 20%.[39]

Restitution and tenure reform also faced legal and administrative constraints. The Restitution of Land Rights Act created the Commission on Restitution of Land Rights in 1995; a 1997 amendment allowed claimants to approach the Land Claims Court or Minister directly after slow Commission progress. In 2011, 76,506 claims had been lodged and 76,023 settled, with 82% urban and 18% rural; repeated shifts in the claims deadline contributed to backlog. The Constitutional Court’s 2016 Land Access Movement judgment invalidated the 2014 reopening of claims, so the department stated that claims lodged before 31 December 1998 should be finalised pending new legislation for reopened claims.[39]

South Africa harvested approximately 16.4 million tonnes of maize in 2023, demonstrating continued large national agricultural output during the post-1994 reform period. National output is not, however, a measure of productivity on redistributed farms.

- **Double Drift community restoration project:** In 2017, 1,300 hectares across 21 farms were restored to 1,500 community members, who operated a community-run ecotourism and game-farming venture combining biodiversity conservation, job creation and income generation. [40]

This allocation concentrated commercial infrastructure and better-resourced farming on white-owned land, while production in the reserves was chiefly subsistence production constrained by low public expenditure.[41]

The Agricultural Research Council’s 2019 National PLAS Report provides a portfolio-wide test of the acquisition-and-lease model. Its detailed evaluation of 1,956 PLAS farms found that 59.1% had potential for commercial production and 24.2% for medium-scale viability, but in the preceding financial year only 7% were commercially productive, 8% medium-scale productive, 17.9% livelihood-sustaining and 67.2% non-viable. Thus only 15% produced at a financially sustainable medium or commercial scale.[42] The report recorded 305 farms producing no commodities and found that only 45 of 1,631 projects with medium-scale or commercial potential achieved at least 80% of assessed potential net income; 1,586, or 97%, achieved less than 80%.[42] It defined farm performance as net income—total revenue minus total costs—and estimated portfolio potential net income at roughly R2.9 billion, with actual performance below one quarter of that amount.[42]

The Agricultural Research Council found that poor performance was principally an implementation and production-system outcome, not a simple consequence of land quality or farm size. Ninety-eight percent of evaluated PLAS farms had good-to-fair natural resources, 59% were large enough for viable enterprises, and 60% were judged to have commercial potential; yet 88% reported constrained sustainable water access or poor water infrastructure, 47% had some natural-resource degradation, and 51% had poor on-farm infrastructure.[42] Infrastructure condition tracked production: 33% of farms with good infrastructure reached medium or commercial production, compared with 18% with fair infrastructure and 8% with poor infrastructure.[42] The report identified lack of skills and expertise and lack of support, each affecting 92% of farmers, and inadequate infrastructure, affecting 88%, as the largest limitations.[42]

South Africa’s land-reform advisory panel also identified inadequate water rights and basic services, alongside infrastructure and post-settlement investment, as constraints on transferred farms.[3]

Market and support arrangements also separated stronger from weaker PLAS outcomes. The Agricultural Research Council found that 93% of PLAS farmers sold through marketing channels, with about 50% using formal channels only and 24% combining formal and informal channels; only 19% of formal-market participants reached medium-scale or commercially viable income above R350,000.[42] Only 22% had contract-farming engagements, but 30% of those contract farmers reached medium or commercial production, and 91% traded through formal markets.[42] Thirty-two percent of beneficiaries received at least three forms of support; 42% of that group reached medium or commercial production, compared with 11% of recipients of one support form and none among those with no support.[42]

Household-wealth estimates covering 1993–2017 found that the top 10% held more than 85% of South African wealth, the top 1% held 55%, and the bottom 50% had negative average wealth.[43]

### Namibia's ongoing debates

Namibia inherited a dual land system at independence in 1990. About 52% of agriculturally usable land was freehold, mostly held by white landowners, while 48% was communal.[10] Another account records that 36.2 million hectares of the 69.6 million hectares available for agriculture were commercial or freehold land occupied by about 4,200 predominantly white farming households, while 33.4 million hectares of communal land supported about 70% of the population.[12] The central land-reform problem was therefore both racial concentration in commercial freehold land and population pressure in communal areas.

A breakdown of Namibia’s land area records 39,728,364 hectares of freehold commercial agricultural land (48% of total land area), 28,720,443 hectares of communal land (35%) and 13,906,437 hectares of state land (17%).[93]

Namibia adopted a mixed legal approach. First, the state began acquiring freehold farms for subdivision and allocation to previously disadvantaged Namibians from 1990.[44] Second, the Affirmative Action Loan Scheme (AALS) began under the 1991 Agricultural Bank Amendment Act.

It provided subsidised loans to previously disadvantaged farmers to buy freehold commercial farms; full-time borrowers had a three-year repayment exemption on a 20-year bond, then paid 1% interest from year four, rising to market rates by year ten. Part-time farmers became eligible in 1997.[45] Third, the Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act 6 of 1995 gave the state power to acquire agricultural land for reform and to compulsorily acquire certain agricultural land.[46]

Namibia’s income Gini coefficient fell from approximately 0.70 in the early 1990s to approximately 0.56 in 2015–16. This is an income-inequality measure, not a measure of land ownership or of land reform’s effect on net wealth.

Namibia inherited racially concentrated freehold commercial farms at independence in 1990. Its Constitution protected property while permitting expropriation in the public interest subject to just compensation.

The 1995 Act did not create a wholly non-market model. It gave the state a preferential right to buy agricultural land offered for sale: sellers had to offer land to government first, after which government could purchase or waive the offer and allow open-market sale.[47] The state thus used a first-refusal mechanism within a market framework. Expropriation was legally available, but the system retained compensation principles and relied predominantly on negotiated purchase and loan-supported private acquisition.[48][13]

The Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002 added a separate tenure-reform component. It came into force on 1 March 2003 and regulates access to rural land in communal areas.[49] It provides for registration of customary and leasehold rights to residential and arable land, confirms traditional leaders’ powers to allocate and revoke customary rights, and establishes Communal Land Boards.

Communal grazing rights are not registered as individual property rights under the Act.[10] The Act therefore strengthened formal recognition of some communal rights without converting communal grazing land into individually titled farms.

The National Resettlement Programme made measurable transfers but at a limited pace. By June 2018, it had acquired 443 farms covering 3,021,959.91 hectares; 53 farms transferred from the agriculture ministry raised the total number available for resettlement to 496.[47] By February 2018, 5,352 beneficiaries had been recorded, including people in group resettlement schemes, San development projects and cooperatives.[47] A separate 2018 account similarly reports 496 purchased farms allocated to more than 5,000 beneficiaries.[50]

The AALS had a larger footprint in land acquisition but did not necessarily represent direct state redistribution. By 2018, it had financed 648 loans covering 3,407,368.65 hectares, with N$777,453,939.01 granted.[47] Agribank-supported acquisitions since 1992 totalled 882 farms and 6,261,090 hectares when AALS and commercial lending were combined.[47] Commercial lending was available to companies and individuals, unlike AALS, which targeted previously disadvantaged individuals.[47] These are distinct channels: state resettlement transfers land into state-held leasehold arrangements, while AALS finances private freehold acquisition.

The distributional result was real but limited. From 1991 to 2005, 163 farms and 993,841 hectares were acquired for resettlement, while 625 farms and 3.47 million hectares were bought under AALS; roughly 85% of freehold land nevertheless remained in primarily white ownership.[10] By 2018, 8.2 million hectares had been offered to the state since 1992, but government had purchased only about 37%, or slightly over three million hectares.[50] Namibia’s programme therefore redistributed some commercial land without overturning the inherited freehold structure.

Namibia’s reported 15% redistribution by 2005 was measured against its 36 million-hectare freehold area, not all national land.[31][10]

By 2005, 15% of Namibia’s freehold agricultural land had changed hands through channels counted in one source, including state resettlement and Affirmative Action Loan Scheme purchases.[10]

The 2018 tenure distribution shows the gradual shift toward state land: 23% state land, 35% communal land and 42% freehold agricultural land, compared with 20%, 36% and 44% respectively in 2010.[47] This is a modest change in tenure categories. It does not mean that all state land has become secure, productive or equally distributed household wealth.

Tenure security within resettlement was incomplete. Only 172 of 496 resettlement farms had received lease agreements by the time of the 2018 land-statistics booklet, an issuance rate of 35%.[47] Lease issuance matters because beneficiaries who lack documented rights may struggle to make investments, obtain credit or resolve conflicts over farm use. The pattern resembles Zimbabwe and South Africa in one respect: redistribution can change access before it creates secure, usable rights.

Agricultural outcomes were mainly livestock-based, reflecting Namibia’s arid ecology. Individual beneficiaries under the Farm Unit Resettlement Scheme received large allocations because viable ranching requires extensive land: minimum farm sizes were 1,500 hectares in the north and 3,000 hectares in the more arid south, while reported average allocations were 1,200 hectares in the north and 2,138 hectares in the south.[10] Land size in Namibia therefore cannot be compared mechanically with plot size in Zimbabwe or South Africa; a hectare of arid grazing land supports a different production system from a hectare of irrigated or high-rainfall cropland.

Available beneficiary evidence indicates livestock accumulation but constrained farm viability. A survey found that 57% of beneficiary households employed one or two labourers, while one third relied on family labour. The average of about one hired worker per beneficiary household matched the cited freehold-sector average of one worker per 1,000 hectares, suggesting no net additional employment from resettlement.[10] Average wages paid by resettlement farmers were below the statutory minimum wage.[10]

In Namibian beneficiary surveys conducted in 2003–04, 71% of resettlement-beneficiary households had off-farm main incomes, 31% regarded farming as their main occupation, and 45% lived permanently on their farms.[10]

AALS beneficiaries also faced financial stress. More than two thirds were reported to be in arrears on loan repayments in 2004.[10] AALS enabled acquisition of freehold farms, but freehold ownership did not remove the need for working capital, drought resilience, livestock management, marketing and debt servicing. The scheme’s gender distribution was also unequal: women received 10% of loans, men 60%, and couples married in community of property 30%.[47]

Agricultural productivity cannot be inferred from hectares transferred. The Hartebeesteich-Süd case illustrates why. The Land Reform Ministry bought the 3,900-hectare farm in 2014 for N$17 million; it had 6,000 olive trees and a national marketing and supply system. After the farm was transferred to Namibia Central Intelligence Services and not used, all the olive trees died.[50] This was a loss of productive capital after state acquisition and non-use, though it is a case rather than a national productivity measure.

Namibia’s agricultural contribution to GDP declined from roughly 8% in 2006 to roughly 3.5% in 2020; this sectoral GDP share does not measure productivity on resettled farms.[93]

Namibia’s food-security and poverty indicators show continuing deprivation but should not be assigned automatically to land reform. The country has only about 1% arable land, while nearly half the population was broadly counted as employed in agriculture in 2019; rainfall in northern and north-eastern communal areas was becoming more unreliable with climate change.[50] These ecological limits make livestock, drought risk, water access and non-farm income central to rural welfare.

The broader distributional problem also extends beyond commercial farmland. Communal land has experienced enclosure pressures. During the first decade of the twenty-first century, more than 1,000 fences reportedly enclosed three million hectares of ranches in communal areas. In Kavangoland, commercial ranches occupied 30% of land used as communal grazing; fencing reduced access to water and grazing and produced conflicts with neighbouring communities.[51] Long-term leases of up to 99 years in designated communal agricultural areas were reportedly granted to old rural elites and new urban rich.[52] This is a form of internal differentiation that can reproduce inequality even where land is formally communal.

Namibia’s political conflict around land is primarily institutional and distributive rather than a documented pattern of large-scale land-reform violence. The Second National Land Conference in October 2018 debated uncompensated expropriation, the performance of willing-buyer–willing-seller acquisition, occupation rights for people on peri-urban state land, rent control and ancestral land rights.[12] Participants generally reaffirmed the existing legal tenure regime and the substantive and procedural rights of people whose property was earmarked for expropriation.[12]

The conference adopted more than 160 resolutions, including new attention to urban land, informal settlements, tenure protection and the privatisation of communal land by members of a new elite.[50] It also called for re-evaluation of AALS and proposed reintroducing a 30% government guarantee for commercial-land purchases.[53] These are proposals and resolutions, not observed socioeconomic outcomes.

Namibia announced in 2004 that it would implement expropriation of agricultural land to accelerate reform, arguing that willing-seller–willing-buyer transactions inflated prices and restricted access to productive land.[13] The Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act already provided authority for compulsory acquisition.[46] Yet the reported programme record through 2018 is dominated by purchase, resettlement and loan finance, not by a completed shift to uncompensated or mass expropriation. The distinction matters: current debates over ancestral land, restitution and stronger compulsory acquisition should not be presented as outcomes already produced by those proposals.

At a 2018 Omaheke regional consultation, participants recommended a referendum on amending Namibia’s Constitution to permit uncompensated expropriation; this was a proposal, not an enacted amendment.[94]

**Namibia’s mixed regime has shifted some land into state resettlement and previously disadvantaged freehold ownership, but by 2018 it had not dismantled commercial freehold concentration; farm livelihoods remained constrained by debt, weak lease issuance, ecological limits, low employment creation and the absence of adequate post-settlement support.**[50][47][10]

In the beneficiary survey spanning roughly 2000 to late 2003, average cattle holdings rose from 28 to 51 per household, an 82.1% increase.[10]

The reported US$907 average livestock-sales gross margin in 2003 equalled 45.4% of the cited US$2,000 annual decent-living-income benchmark for a five-person household.[10]

Agricultural outcomes on Namibian resettlement farms were principally livestock outcomes. In a beneficiary survey spanning approximately 2000 to late 2003, average holdings rose from 28 to 51 cattle, from 51 to 75 sheep, and from 67 to 84 goats; the cattle increase was 82.1%.[10] These are beneficiary herd and farm-income measures, not national productivity measures.

In 2003–04 beneficiary surveys in Namibia, 71% of resettlement-beneficiary households relied primarily on off-farm income, 31% identified farming as their main occupation, 45% lived permanently on their farms and 87% were mainly livestock farmers. Only 27% produced crops, mostly in small gardens for their own consumption.[10]

The FAO 2006 Namibia case study identifies four implemented rural rights-restoration programmes: AALS, commercial-farm resettlement, communal registration and communal conservancies. Commercial-farm resettlement was initiated in 1990 and formalised by the 1995 Resettlement Act: the state purchased farms offered to it first and partitioned them for middle-scale farmers. Resettled farmers were to establish viable operations within five years before receiving 99-year leases.[45]

Communal registration under the 2002 Agricultural (Communal) Land Reform Act established Communal Land Boards in 12 of Namibia’s 13 regions; Khomas has no communal land. The boards register claims, maintain regional land registries, plan land use and settle disputes. They can approve existing and new customary rights and leasehold rights; approved grantees receive 99-year leases, while farming plots are limited to 25 hectares. By the end of 2005, the boards had heard almost 200 disputes.[45]

The 1996 Nature Conservation Amendment Act also enabled communal conservancies, extending wildlife and tourism rights previously associated with commercial farm ownership to communal-area communities. By 2004, 29 communal conservancies had been registered; in 2003 they covered almost one quarter of communal land.

Namibia’s agricultural production evidence reinforces the importance of rainfall, irrigation and market access alongside tenure. The Namibia Food and Nutrition Security Strategic Review reported that reliable rain-fed crop production requires average annual rainfall above 400 mm, an area covering 34% of the country. Smallholder irrigation-system maize yields ranged from 7.1 tonnes in drought years to 10.6 tonnes in good-rainfall years, whereas dryland yields ranged from 0.17 tonnes per hectare in drought years to 3.2 tonnes per hectare in good-rainfall years.[54]

The same review estimated that 6,500 hectares were under commercial irrigated crop production against potential irrigable area of 27,000 hectares, and that Green Scheme projects produced half of Namibia’s locally produced maize. Northern Communal Areas held 44% of the national cattle herd, but supplied only 11.40% of formally marketed cattle in 2013 and 7.3% in 2014.[54]

National food-security measures show that food availability and household access remain exposed to climatic shocks and imports. Namibia’s 2014 Food and Nutrition Security Monitoring Plan reported average annual coarse-grain production of 131,000 metric tons during the preceding five years, equal to 42% of estimated cereal requirements of 308,400 metric tons. The Namibia Food and Nutrition Security Strategic Review reported average maize imports of approximately 55% of annual requirements.[55][54]

The 2012/13 drought left more than 780,000 people food insecure, more than 330,000 requiring urgent support, and caused the deaths of more than 4,000 animals. In a food-stock study conducted from November 2014 to March 2015, stocks lasted less than one month in five of six studied regions.[54] In July–September 2025, the IPC Acute Food Insecurity Analysis classified approximately 456,000 people—15% of the analysed population—in IPC Phase 3 or above; Kunene, Kavango West and Zambezi were in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis).

Above-average 2024/25 rainfall, increased crop production, better rangeland and government and development-partner interventions drove the improvement from 1.15 million people in the same period of 2024.[56]

The IPC analysis projected deterioration to 612,000 people, or 20% of the analysed population, in IPC Phase 3 for October 2025–March 2026 after countrywide drought relief ended in August 2025, alongside reduced labour opportunities, 36.9% national unemployment and poor livestock conditions in drought-prone areas.

Communal-land conflict has at times become violent. Illegal fencing restricted poorer farmers’ access to land and water, including through enclosure of government boreholes. A dispute between local Kwangali residents and incoming Oshiwambo-speaking farmers involved sporadic violence in 2003 and again in late 2005; police began forcibly evicting farmers and their cattle in mid-August 2006, arresting those who refused to leave.[45]

The Communal Land Reform Act’s registration component had recorded nearly 4,500 holdings by 2002, roughly three quarters of them approvals of pre-existing customary allocations. The FAO case study found no measured effect of this rights reform on land investment, agricultural production or poverty reduction.[45]

A Permanent Technical Team review conducted from late 2003 to 2004 found that none of the resettled farmers had received the long-term leases promised by policy, although most had government documentation of occupancy rights. The team found that most beneficiaries had increased their livestock herds, but marketing opportunities were almost nonexistent, so the increased asset value produced little return.[45]

Namibia’s 2018 land audit reported that white Namibians still held roughly 70% of privately owned commercial agricultural land. This ownership share is subject to the audit’s definitions of owners and commercial agricultural land.

A 2019 University of Namibia case study of ten small-scale livestock farmers resettled for more than five years in Mariental Rural Constituency found reported asset accumulation, including 43 boreholes, solar systems, water pumps, houses, tractors, vehicles, machinery and animal-handling facilities. The same study found that none had received a title deed and that some held only unit-allocation letters; farmers linked the absence of deeds to weaker investment and bank-loan access.[57]

The Mariental study reported an average allocation of 2,420 hectares per beneficiary, while its conclusion gave 2,500 hectares. Both figures were below the 3,000-hectare minimum recommended by the Land Advisory Commission and the cited local viability range of 5,000–8,000 hectares. Farmers linked small units, low carrying capacity and low rainfall to constrained livestock growth, inability to service loans and deteriorating grazing.[57]

Land-reform-linked conflict in Namibia also included disputes between resettled beneficiaries: the Mariental study found that deficient boundary fences, animal-handling facilities, water infrastructure, roads and accommodation increased spending, livestock losses and cross-boundary grazing conflicts.[57]

Namibia’s purchase-based framework included institutions and fiscal tools intended to manage acquisition. In February 2004, the Ministry of Lands, Resettlement and Rehabilitation launched a Land Tribunal to determine purchase prices where an owner and ministry disagreed after land had been offered to government. The government also completed a provisional commercial-farmland valuation roll listing about 12,509 farms and plots for the Valuation Court; 2,738 people inspected the roll and 285 objections were lodged. The land tax was intended partly to fund state acquisition for resettlement, with rates beginning at 0.75% of unimproved site value per hectare for a Namibian-owned first farm and 1.75% for a foreign-owned first farm, rising by 0.25% for each additional farm or property.[58][59]

Early implementation illustrates the slow pace of market acquisition. In 2001/02, Namibia acquired 15 farms totalling 6,483 hectares for resettlement; by late 2003, cumulative acquisition since reform began in 1995 was 124 farms and more than 700,000 hectares, against a five-year target of 9.5 million hectares. The ministry reported an average resettlement rate of 2,222 people annually and an aspiration to resettle 68,000–70,000 people by 2030.[58]

Food security on early resettlement projects was constrained by weak livelihood capacity. The Legal Assistance Center reported that government food-for-work operated in virtually every resettlement project and prevented rampant starvation and malnutrition; it also reported that beneficiaries sometimes sold produce for cash and then entered food-deficit conditions. In 2004, about 650,000 people in a population of around 1.8 million were reported to need food aid amid drought and flash floods.[58]

The same evidence links low productive returns to the post-settlement package. The Legal Assistance Center identified insufficient land-use training and limited access to modern equipment; it reported that beneficiaries with state leases rather than title had difficulty securing loans because lenders were reluctant to accept uncertain legal title as collateral. Namibia’s arid conditions leave much land suitable principally for pasture, making small allocations insufficient for some farmers to earn a living. Some beneficiaries reportedly leased plots to communal farmers for up to N$200 per month or left for urban areas.[58]

The Revised National Resettlement Policy 2023–2033 was tabled in Parliament in February 2024. It emphasises equity, transparent allocation, tenure security, training, post-settlement support, monitoring and agricultural production, and introduces differentiated models for beneficiaries with different capacities and needs. It also provides a pathway from leasehold to possible freehold ownership.[60]

Ongoing debates include stronger use of expropriation, the compensation standard, anti-speculation measures, limits on foreign ownership, urban land redistribution and the treatment of ancestral-land claims.

By 2024, Namibia had acquired about 590 farms covering roughly 3.5 million hectares for an estimated N$2.5 billion and had resettled close to 5,500 previously disadvantaged Namibians.[60]

Namibia acquired 30,097.6430 hectares for resettlement in the 2024/25 financial year.[63]

In the 2023/24 financial year, seven farms totalling about 33,822 hectares were acquired for N$91.4 million and 23 beneficiaries were resettled.[60]

For resettlement in financial year 2024/25, Namibia acquired eight farms totalling 30,097.6430 hectares.[95][63]

In financial year 2024/25, Namibia also acquired two farms totalling 11,026.8059 hectares for generational farm workers and one irrigation farm of 166.0479 hectares.[95][63]

In 2024/25, the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Water and Land Reform resettled six previously disadvantaged Namibians against a target of 25.[61]

Namibia reported 7,396 ratified communal land-right registrations in financial year 2024/25.[63]

A Namibian land-policy concept paper reported low agricultural production on most National Resettlement Programme farms and attributed it to inadequate farming infrastructure and lack of training and mentorship.[62]

The Ministry reported that Green Scheme projects had been returned to it in a dilapidated condition and required substantial recapitalisation to return to full production.[63]

The three cases show that land transfer changes racial and class access to land more quickly than it changes agricultural capacity, food security or broader wealth. Zimbabwe redistributed on the largest scale and most rapidly, but coercive implementation, disrupted assets, insecure tenure and macroeconomic crisis coincided with a severe maize decline; tobacco recovered when smallholders gained finance, inputs, extension and buyers.[1][2][16] South Africa’s slower, market-led approach reduced disruption but transferred land too slowly and often failed to supply tenure, finance and post-settlement support.[3][1][29] Namibia redistributed modestly through state purchase and subsidised loans while retaining extensive freehold concentration and facing severe ecological and financial constraints.[50][10]

The decisive comparison is therefore not market purchase versus expropriation in the abstract. It is whether transfer is accompanied by secure rights, accountable allocation, capital, infrastructure, extension, market access and protection for workers displaced from the old agrarian order.

Namibia adds a further comparison: food and production outcomes there cannot be explained by the modest transfer of commercial land alone. In 2025, improved rainfall, crop output, rangeland conditions and coordinated relief coincided with lower acute food insecurity, while the end of drought relief, unemployment and seasonal market dependence underpinned the projected lean-season deterioration.[56] Its market-based and compensated-expropriation framework has therefore operated within a farming system where rainfall, irrigation, livestock-market access, debt and communal tenure strongly shape socioeconomic outcomes.[54][45]

## Different legal approaches and socioeconomic outcomes

Land reform is not one legal instrument. It can mean **redistribution**—transferring ownership or use rights from existing holders to others; **restitution**—returning land or giving redress for a specifically documented historical dispossession; and **tenure reform**—changing the rules that secure, limit or administer land rights. These distinctions matter because a farm can be acquired by the state, allocated to a beneficiary, and still leave that beneficiary without ownership, a bankable lease, productive support, or secure residence.[67][5]

The three countries therefore cannot be reduced to Zimbabwe as expropriation, South Africa as markets, and Namibia as debate. Zimbabwe used market purchase before 2000, then compulsory acquisition and constitutional entrenchment. South Africa’s Constitution has always permitted public-interest expropriation, but implementation centred on assisted purchase and, later, state purchase followed by leasing. Namibia has combined state pre-emption and market purchase, subsidised private purchase, limited use of statutory compulsory acquisition, and continuing debate over stronger expropriation and restitution. **The decisive comparison is not simply compensation versus no compensation: it is the combination of acquisition power, speed, tenure after transfer, selection and governance, and the finance, inputs and markets available to new land users.**[8][7][36][46][44][5]

### constitutional amendments

A constitutional amendment changes the highest domestic legal rule. In land reform, this can change three connected questions: who may acquire land; whether the state must compensate for the land and/or improvements; and whether courts can review the acquisition. It does not, by itself, allocate farms, build irrigation, create credit access, or determine whether beneficiaries can farm profitably.

Zimbabwe’s post-2000 programme illustrates constitutional change used to consolidate a redistribution already under way. At independence, the Lancaster House constitutional settlement had protected property rights for ten years. Compulsory acquisition was limited and compensation had to be prompt, adequate and assessed on market principles; politically, protected-rights amendments also faced a high parliamentary threshold. This produced a willing-seller/willing-buyer system in which the state could buy land but did not control the supply, price, quality or location of land offered.[13][5]

After those restrictions expired on 18 April 1990, Zimbabwe amended constitutional property provisions and enacted the Land Acquisition Act in 1992. The amendments made compulsory acquisition for redistribution possible and allowed Parliament to specify compensation principles away from market value. The 1992 Act empowered compulsory acquisition, established a Compensation Committee, and allowed ownership to pass immediately to the state even before compensation was agreed, fixed or paid. The legal shift was real, but it did not itself produce the rapid transfer later associated with Fast Track: the 1992 law faced litigation and, according to one account, had little impact on redistribution.[14][5][69]

The February 2000 referendum rejected a government-sponsored draft constitution, including expanded compulsory-acquisition powers. The government then inserted section 16A into the existing Constitution in April 2000 and amended the Land Acquisition Act in May and November. Section 16A widened the basis for compulsory acquisition, placed the stated responsibility for land compensation on the former colonial power, and left Zimbabwe principally responsible for improvements.[14][13]

This legal sequence matters because it corrects a common simplification. Fast Track was not created by Amendment No. 17 in 2005. Land occupations, gazetting, compulsory acquisition and allocation were already operating from 2000. Amendment No. 17 came later and constitutionalised state title over land already identified in Gazette notices, while giving the state a stronger route for later identified land.[68][14][6]

Amendment No. 17 inserted section 16B, Agricultural land acquired for resettlement and other purposes, on 16 September 2005. It covered agricultural land identified under the Land Acquisition Act before specified dates and listed in Schedule 7, as well as land subsequently identified for purposes including agricultural settlement, land reorganisation, forestry, environmental conservation, wildlife and relocation. Such land vested in the State with full title.[6]

The compensation and judicial-review provisions were especially consequential. Section 16B specified that no compensation was payable for land itself, except for improvements made before acquisition. It also prevented former owners from challenging acquisition in court, while permitting a challenge to the amount payable for improvements. This separated the redistribution decision from the settlement of compensation disputes and sharply strengthened state control over the acquisition process.[6][70]

The state did not thereby avoid every financial or institutional consequence. Zimbabwe’s legal framework retained a Compensation Committee to determine compensation for resettlement land, and the Land Acquisition Act permitted payment in cash, bonds or instalments, with defined payment milestones. Yet implementation lagged: by 2009, 297 of 2,721 farmers transferred onto state land had reportedly been paid compensation, while 900 farms had been valued. The relevant point is not that compensation disappeared, but that compensation for the land itself, compensation for improvements, and actual payment were separate questions.[68][17]

Zimbabwe’s 2013 Constitution retained a constitutional power to acquire agricultural land through Gazette notice for public purposes that include settlement and land reorganisation. It also established the Zimbabwe Land Commission to advise government on equitable access, holding sizes, tenure systems, acquisition and fair compensation. This is continuity of compulsory-acquisition authority after Fast Track, not evidence that the 2013 Constitution itself generated the 2000–05 transfers.[71]

Zimbabwe’s constitutional route also changed property relations after acquisition. The FTLRP expanded state ownership of former commercial farmland and replaced much freehold ownership with permits, offer letters and leases. A1 land was intended for poorer and land-short households and generally used permit tenure; A2 was intended to create a black commercial-farming stratum and used larger, leasehold farms. The programme therefore redistributed access and use rights more decisively than it transferred individual freehold ownership.[16][17]

Zimbabwe’s 2006 Gazetted Land framework made state-issued offer letters, permits and land-settlement leases the basis of lawful occupation of affected farms, rather than private title.[70][96]

That distinction has socioeconomic significance. A 99-year lease can in principle provide a durable right, and the government described long leases as secure enough for commercial production. In practice, many A2 farmers reported that few leases had been issued and questioned whether a lease could serve as collateral; financial institutions were reluctant because government did not grant them transfer rights if a borrower defaulted. The legal acquisition of land was therefore more complete than the legal security and finance arrangements for many new producers.[17][21]

Zimbabwe’s later tenure policy has included bankable and tradable 99-year leases for A2 commercial farms.[97]

**Zimbabwe’s constitutional amendments made compulsory acquisition faster and more secure for the State, but they did not automatically create secure, financeable rights for beneficiaries or resolve compensation claims.** The amendments decided who could resist land acquisition; they did not decide whether the resulting tenure system, allocation process and agricultural support system would sustain productive and equitable use.[6][17]

South Africa began from a different constitutional settlement. Section 25 of the 1996 Constitution is often called the **property clause**. It prohibits arbitrary deprivation, permits expropriation for a public purpose or in the public interest, and expressly treats land reform as part of the public interest. At the same time, it requires the state, within available resources, to foster equitable land access; grants restitution or equitable redress for post-19 June 1913 racial dispossession; and requires legally secure tenure or comparable redress for people whose tenure became insecure through past racial discrimination.[7]

This framework matters because South Africa’s Constitution did not require a willing-buyer/willing-seller programme. The Constitution permitted acquisition at just-and-equitable compensation, which may be below market value. One legal assessment stated that expropriation without compensation would require constitutional amendment, but acquiring farms below market value was already possible under the constitutional standard.[11][32]

The constitutional debate over **expropriation without compensation (EWC)** should therefore be separated from enacted land reform. On 27 February 2018, the National Assembly adopted a motion to amend the Constitution to allow EWC, and an expert Advisory Panel was appointed in September 2018 to investigate it within a wider land-reform strategy. The Constitutional Review Committee later recommended amendment to make explicit what it regarded as implicit, but the amendment bill failed to secure a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly on 7 December 2021. EWC was debated extensively; the proposed constitutional amendment was not enacted.[7][29][1]

The constitutional framework has nonetheless produced tangible legal rights through restitution and tenure protections. The Restitution of Land Rights Act created a claim-based route for people and communities dispossessed after 1913 under racially discriminatory laws or practices. Successful claimants could receive returned land, alternative land or financial compensation. The Labour Tenants Act of 1996 and Extension of Security of Tenure Act of 1997 addressed occupation and eviction rights for people living and working on farms, rather than simply transferring farm ownership.[5][29]

Section 72 of Zimbabwe’s 2013 Constitution distinguishes compensation for improvements on acquired agricultural land from compensation for the land itself, subject to constitutional exceptions.

These rights have not translated uniformly into prompt, secure outcomes. The 2017 High Level Panel reported 19,000 unfinalised and 7,000 unsettled claims from the pre-1998 claims process; at its stated average settlement rate of 560 claims annually, it estimated roughly 35 years to process them. The 2014 attempt to reopen claims was declared unconstitutional in 2016 because affected communities had not been sufficiently consulted. Thus constitutional rights and statutory remedies created enforceable claims, but administration, consultation and institutional capacity determined access in practice.[32]

South Africa’s record also shows that restitution settlement is not equivalent to land return. DALRRD data reported for 2023 record 82,976 settled restitution claims, 4 million hectares secured, R25.6 billion in land costs, R22.3 billion in financial compensation, 456,089 beneficiary households and 2.3 million beneficiaries. Yet more than 90% of restitution claims were settled through cash compensation rather than restored land. Cash may be a chosen and valuable form of redress, especially for urban claimants, but it does not alter rural land ownership or give a household a productive land asset.[29][9]

Namibia’s constitutional position has remained more compensation-centred than Zimbabwe’s. After independence in 1990, the government’s 1991 National Conference on Land Reform and the Land Question adopted redress through national reconciliation and Article 16’s just-compensation principle. It selected willing-seller/willing-buyer as the primary route to acquire commercial farms.[13]

Namibia’s constitutional structure also matters for communal land. At independence, communal lands became state property under Article 124 and Schedule 5, while existing rights, obligations and trusts continued. The Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002, effective from 1 March 2003, formalised registration of customary and leasehold rights over residential and arable land, confirmed a role for traditional authorities, and created Communal Land Boards. It did not register communal grazing rights.[12][49][10]

The omission of grazing rights is not a technical detail. In pastoral and mixed farming systems, access to common grazing and water is a productive asset. Where the law protects or registers household plots but leaves common grazing outside equivalent registration, land formalisation can coexist with enclosure and unequal access to the resources that make livestock production viable. In Namibia, more than 1,000 fences reportedly enclosed 3 million hectares of ranches in communal areas during the first decade of the twenty-first century; the reported result included reduced grazing and water access and conflicts between neighbouring communities.[10][51]

Namibia’s 2018 Second National Land Conference debated uncompensated expropriation, ancestral land rights, informal and illegal occupation of state land, rent control and the effectiveness of willing-buyer/willing-seller. Yet participants generally reaffirmed the existing land-tenure regime and the substantive and procedural rights of people whose property was earmarked for expropriation. The debate therefore opened a path for policy change but did not enact Zimbabwe-style uncompensated acquisition.[12]

The constitutional comparison rules out a simple causal story that constitutional amendment alone determines production or equity. Zimbabwe’s amendments materially altered state authority, compensation and court access; South Africa’s Constitution created enforceable redress and expropriation powers but did not force a market-only implementation; Namibia preserved compensation and procedural protections while leaving stronger reforms contested. **Constitutional design establishes the legal frontier of reform, but implementation determines whether that frontier becomes redistribution, secure tenure, productive investment or continuing conflict.**[6][7][12][5]

In 2020, Zimbabwe’s government and representatives of former farm owners signed a Global Compensation Deed valued at US$3.5 billion for improvements on compulsorily acquired farms. The agreement addressed compensation for improvements and did not reverse the completed land transfers.

In April 2025, Zimbabwe reported 740 farms approved for compensation under the Global Compensation Deed; a first processed batch covered 378 farms, with US$3.1 million in cash payments and US$308 million in Treasury bonds.[96][98]

Article 16 of Namibia’s Constitution protects property and permits expropriation in the public interest subject to just compensation.

In 2020, Zimbabwe gazetted regulations under which former holders could apply for restoration of title to agricultural land compulsorily acquired for resettlement; the regulations applied to both indigenous and foreign farmers whose farms had been appropriated.[72]

The Department reported a 56% national settlement rate for labour-tenant claims under the Land Reform (Labour Tenants) Act 3 of 1996, with KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga holding the largest numbers of outstanding claims.[73]

### market-based

A **market-based** or willing-seller/willing-buyer approach acquires land through voluntary sale, usually at or near market price. It can be state-led: the state may be the buyer, beneficiaries need not buy directly, and the state can subdivide or lease the acquired land. Its central strengths are negotiated transfer, compensation and procedural regularity. Its central constraints are the land offered for sale, price, fiscal capacity, financing, and whether beneficiaries receive workable tenure and post-settlement support.

Zimbabwe’s market-based phase preceded Fast Track. The Lancaster House settlement required willing-seller/willing-buyer acquisition, full market-value compensation for under-utilised land acquired for public purposes, and foreign-currency remittance. The government also received a stated British commitment to assist financing if Zimbabwe matched the contribution, but the funding delivered was far below early expectations. One account reports Britain provided US$47 million of US$630 million pledged in the first ten years; another reports approximately £30 million received by 2000 against an earlier £75 million promise.[5][8]

The first post-independence programme did achieve real transfers. Close to three million hectares were acquired in the first ten years, resettling 52,000 households and affecting more than 400,000 people. The share of national land held by 6,000 white farmers fell from 42% in 1980 to 29% in 1990. A separate account reports 2.8 million hectares transferred from white landowners to Black beneficiaries and associates that transfer with increased agricultural productivity and sales.[5][29]

Its implementation conditions were substantially different from Fast Track. The early programme used already available vacant land, detailed planning, infrastructure, specialist input and support services. Smallholders became the largest suppliers of maize and cotton to formal markets within five years of independence. Yet the market route was slow, fiscally demanding, and often delivered scattered or marginal land because sellers controlled what came to market.[29][15][13]

This earlier experience is important for causal interpretation. It shows that Zimbabwe’s later agricultural disruption was not an inevitable result of Black smallholder farming or subdivision itself. It also shows that market purchase was not a complete answer to a racially concentrated land system: it could transfer land peacefully and support productive resettlement, but it could not reliably transfer sufficient well-located land at a pace matching land demand.[29][13]

Zimbabwe’s post-2000 programme did not eliminate all market mechanisms. Some owners offered farms, some transactions and swaps occurred, and a minority of beneficiaries in the 2005/06 survey reported purchasing land gained during the programme. But compulsory acquisition became the principal instrument, and the state declared an intention to acquire not less than 11 million hectares. The relevant contrast is therefore not market versus state in the abstract: it is a shift from negotiated purchase as the main gatekeeper to state power backed by fast, coercive occupation and acquisition.[14][16][68][17]

South Africa operationalised redistribution much more explicitly through assisted markets. The original Settlement/Land Acquisition Grant, or SLAG, ran from 1994 to 2000 and enabled poorer applicants to pool grants to purchase farms collectively through trusts and Communal Property Associations (CPAs). The programme was largely replaced by Land Redistribution for Agricultural Development, or LRAD, from 2000 to 2006. LRAD used matching grants and increasingly targeted applicants with resources and business plans to become commercial farmers.[1][5]

The purchase model achieved some transfers, but its reach remained below stated goals. By March 1999, roughly 60,000 households had received land-acquisition grants, while approximately 650,000 hectares had been approved for redistribution—less than 1% of commercial farmland. By March 2018, state redistribution and restitution had transferred 8.4 million hectares, 9–10% of agricultural land. The 30% transfer target was repeatedly missed.[13][2][9]

The explanation cannot be reduced to an absence of willing sellers. A North West Province study found that on 56% of sampled farms land was identified and acquired within 12 months, countering a simple claim that owners would not sell. But nationally, acquisition at market prices imposed fiscal costs, the land-reform budget remained below 1% of national spending, and land-acquisition finance competed with the funds required for infrastructure, extension, water, credit, training and governance.[33][3][9][5]

South Africa’s move to the Proactive Land Acquisition Strategy, or PLAS, changed the identity of the buyer and owner. PLAS, introduced in 2006 after approval in principle in 2003, made the state the purchaser and overseer of land. It acquired farms and allocated them on leasehold rather than immediately transferring title. Through PLAS, government acquired 2.5 million hectares recorded in the State Land Holding account, plus 0.6 million hectares acquired by national and provincial government for non-farm use.[74][36][29]

This was not an abandonment of the market. It was a state-led market-purchase model. The state still typically purchased land; it simply retained ownership and selected lessees. The 2013 State Land Lease and Disposal Policy then divided beneficiaries into categories: farm dwellers and small-scale or subsistence farmers in categories 1 and 2 could occupy rent-free, while medium- and large-scale commercial farmers in categories 3 and 4 paid rent and could obtain title only after 50 years of successive leases.[36]

State purchase and leasehold can be useful where government wants to prevent immediate resale or re-concentration. But the South African cases show how delayed or unclear tenure can undermine the benefit of acquisition. At Nangkoos, the government bought a 448-hectare farm in 2009. Thirty-two resident families formed a CPA believing it would own and manage the farm. By 2016 they had neither a caretaking agreement nor lease nor another documented tenure right, while local jobs had disappeared and they reported no support or service delivery.[36]

At Malangskraal, a 5,200-hectare farm was allocated in 2011, then informally divided among three interests. Conflict arose over water, fencing and the informal subdivision. Two beneficiaries received 30-year leases only in 2019, while eventual title was said to depend on formal subdivision although title transfer was not then policy. These are not representative national averages, but they identify mechanisms through which legal access can fail to become a secure productive asset: unclear boundaries, delayed documents, inadequate support and contested governance.[36]

The national pattern reinforces the tenure point. A survey of close to 2,000 PLAS farms, representing 98% of PLAS farms between 2006 and 2018, found all on leasehold; the review concluded this did not provide the tenure security envisaged by the White Paper. The problem was not leasehold as a legal category in itself. It was the way leases were administered, their uncertainty, and the limited assurance that beneficiaries could retain and invest in the holdings.[9][36]

South Africa’s market-centred programme also had productive successes where transfer was paired with assets, management and market access. At Grasslands, 49 Black dairy workers collectively owned a 485-hectare farm through a trust; the state subsidised 35% of the transfer, the operating company supplied cows, inputs, skills, labour and markets, and the trust received 60% of profits. Members retained wages and in recent years received around R150,000 annually, though this is a case-specific outcome.[2]

At Ravele, restitution transferred fruit and macadamia farms to seven CPAs or trusts in 2005. Early private partnerships failed and left communities in debt, but later recapitalisation and an operating arrangement produced profitable years and turnover of R9.7 million in 2016. Around 300 CPA members chose reinvestment rather than distribute an estimated R30,000 each. This demonstrates that collective ownership can preserve a commercial operation, but only where recapitalisation, management, governance and market access are sustained.[2][1]

Namibia’s market route has two main forms. First, the state purchases farms for resettlement. The Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act 6 of 1995 provides a preferential state right to purchase: a seller must first offer agricultural land to the government, which may buy or waive the offer; only after a waiver certificate can the owner sell on the open market. Between 1992 and 2018, the government waived 5,165,974.01 hectares. That figure measures land released to the market, not land redistributed by the state.[46][47]

Second, the Affirmative Action Loan Scheme, established in 1992, subsidises Agribank loans for previously disadvantaged Namibians to buy large-scale commercial farms under freehold title. The terms required a 10% buyer contribution, loans over 25 years and eligibility based on livestock holdings or equivalent cash. It is therefore redistributive in racial and historical terms, but not a grant-based programme for the landless: it requires an applicant with assets, stock or capital.[44][10]

AALS has transferred substantial farm area but also reveals how market finance shapes who can benefit. By 2018, the scheme had financed 648 loans for 3,407,368.65 hectares and N$777,453,939.01. Yet the programme’s loan distribution was unequal by gender, and the available earlier evidence reported over two-thirds of beneficiaries in arrears in 2004. A loan-financed route can create freehold ownership, but it can also expose beneficiaries to debt and favour applicants able to meet entry requirements.[47][10]

The state resettlement route was slower. By 2018 government had acquired 496 farms and allocated them to over 5,000 beneficiaries. Of the 8.2 million hectares offered to the state since 1992, only about 37%, slightly more than 3 million hectares, had been purchased. Earlier targets were repeatedly proposed but not adopted or implemented: a Cabinet recommendation to acquire at least 9,552,072 hectares by 2005 was not implemented, and Cabinet did not adopt the 2005 recommendation to acquire 15 million hectares by 2020.[50][10]

Namibia’s market system therefore has a dual distributional outcome. It has enabled some previously disadvantaged Namibians to obtain commercial freehold farms through subsidised loans, while the state’s purchase-and-resettlement programme has transferred a smaller number of farms and often left beneficiaries on leases. In 2018, Namibia’s tenure distribution was 23% state land, 35% communal land and 42% freehold agricultural land, compared with 20%, 36% and 44% in 2010. The movement toward state land was modest, and it does not by itself show who gained economically from the shift.[47]

**South Africa’s post-1994 willing-seller/willing-buyer programme maintained stability and largely avoided economic disruption, but delivered land slowly; Namibia mainly bought commercial farms and had expropriated only three by July 2007; Zimbabwe’s 2000–05 Fast Track forcibly, sometimes violently, removed white farmers and destabilised agriculture and the wider economy.**[29][10]

South Africa formulated the Recapitalisation and Development Programme (RECAP) in 2009 and launched it in 2010 to provide emerging Black farmers with infrastructure and resources after recognising that many land-reform projects lacked post-settlement support.[42]
The PLAS evaluation found that RECAP did not have a significant impact on productivity: 24% of RECAP-supported farms reached medium-scale or commercial income levels, compared with 11% of farms without RECAP, while 76% of RECAP farms were non-productive or produced only at livelihood level.[42]

In the national PLAS evaluation, 22% of farmers had a contract-farming engagement; 91% of those with contracts traded through formal-market channels and 30% reached medium-scale and commercially viable farm-income levels.[42]
The same evaluation found that 19% of PLAS farmers using formal markets achieved medium-scale and commercially viable annual farm-income levels above R350,000.00.[42]

Namibia’s communal-land expansion initiative acquired farms totalling 31,056.1198 hectares for N$66,909,803.93.[63] It expanded Otjimbingwe by 15,160 hectares, Ovitoto by 9,128.32 hectares and Aminius by 6,767.6098 hectares.[63] Two Khomas-region farms totalling 8,132.9469 hectares were acquired for N$33,859,099.00, converted into communal land and handed to the Khomanin Traditional Authority on 9 December 2024 for administration.[63] The Khomas communal land is intended to allocate land and grazing rights, establish livestock commonage, support food production and income generation, and preserve cultural practices and traditions.[63]

Group resettlement projects constituted 4.6% of land acquired for redistribution in Namibia.[65]
Group resettlement farms were a minor part of Namibia’s land-reform process but included more than half of its total beneficiaries.[65]
Farm Unit Resettlement Scheme (FURS) farms and Group Resettlement Schemes were both implemented resettlement models in Namibia.[75]

### expropriation

**Expropriation** is compulsory state acquisition for a public purpose. It is not identical to expropriation without compensation: many constitutions permit compulsory acquisition with compensation, and compensation can be market-based, just and equitable, delayed, partial, or confined to improvements. Nor is it identical to violence: a state can acquire land lawfully and peacefully, while voluntary transactions can occur amid coercion. The Zimbabwean case nonetheless shows how compulsory acquisition, occupation and political violence can become mutually reinforcing when implementation is rapid and partisan.

Zimbabwe’s FTLRP combined formal state acquisition with occupations. The government formally announced Fast Track in July 2000; between June 2000 and February 2001, 2,706 farms covering more than 6 million hectares were gazetted for compulsory acquisition. By January 2002, after duplicate listings and delistings, 4,874 farms covering 9.23 million hectares remained listed. These were successive administrative listings and targets, not identical to completed transfers or household allocations.[14]

One administrative account reports that Zimbabwe’s Fast Track programme had forcibly acquired 6.4 million hectares by August 2003.[84]

The programme used two main models. A1 was a decongestion scheme for landless and poorer households, including villagised and self-contained variants. A2 aimed to create Black small- to medium-scale commercial farmers. Government figures reproduced for 2009 list 145,775 A1 beneficiaries on 5,759,154 hectares and 16,386 A2 beneficiaries on 2,978,334 hectares in 2000–08; another account reports 168,671 families gaining land by 2009, while yet another gives 180,000 families on roughly 12 million hectares. Different sources use plots, beneficiaries, families, acquired area and allocated area differently, so none is a single definitive count.[14][17][18][8]

The direction of change is nevertheless clear. Fast Track redistributed most of the former large-scale commercial-farm sector. One survey describes the pre-reform structure as about 4,500 farmers holding over 11 million hectares alongside 1 million communal-area households on 16.4 million hectares, and estimates that FTLRP redistributed about 80% of former large-scale commercial farms. The post-reform agrarian structure became more broadly based and tri-modal: communal and A1 smallholders, A2 medium-scale farms, and a smaller residual large-scale sector.[16][8][20]

This was a substantial redistribution of land access and racial control over commercial farmland. It was not an equal redistribution of wealth in the broader sense. A2 was designed to create a Black commercial-farming class and allocated larger holdings to applicants with resources. Some elites enlarged holdings through multiple-farm ownership, while women and former farm workers remained underrepresented. The FTLRP thus changed racialised property relations, but it also generated class differentiation within the beneficiary population.[17][18][16]

The political violence around implementation was direct and land-reform related. Farm occupations began before Fast Track and intensified in 2000. They ranged from protest and disruption to intimidation, beatings and killings. Human Rights Watch reported that war veterans and ZANU-PF militia occupying commercial farms intimidated and assaulted owners, killed at least seven white farm owners, and killed several tens of farm workers. It also gathered testimony that farms served as bases from which militia intimidated alleged opposition supporters in neighbouring communal areas.[20][22]

The violence was not merely an incidental background condition. It affected who could retain land, who could claim it and who could participate safely in allocation. The Supreme Court initially barred further acquisitions in December 2000, describing the programme as violent and haphazard; in November 2001 it overturned the order after accepting that new legislation had retroactively legalised earlier occupations. A cited Supreme Court judgment also held that targeting farmers perceived as opposition supporters and allocating expropriated land principally to ruling-party adherents constituted unfair discrimination.[14][17]

State institutions were implicated in implementation. Human Rights Watch found that police failed to act against alleged violent crimes and in some instances assisted illegal actions, while the army helped organise and facilitate occupations without checking violence. Later decentralised District Land Committees included representatives from the President’s Office, police, army, war veterans, traditional leaders and rural-district structures, and held authority over farm identification, beneficiary selection and disputes.[22][16]

This does not mean every beneficiary entered through violent occupation. In the 2005/06 household survey, 82.6% of beneficiaries reported formal government allocation, 9.7% reported occupation, 4.8% both occupation and formal allocation, and 2.9% purchase. Occupation, state acquisition and administrative allocation were overlapping routes, varying across localities and time. The programme cannot accurately be characterised either as entirely spontaneous seizure or as a uniformly orderly bureaucratic reform.[16]

Former farm workers were particularly exposed to the unequal effects of the transition. They had lived and worked on commercial farms but often lacked secure independent land rights. Human Rights Watch reported that several hundred thousand farm workers were largely excluded, with many losing jobs or being driven out by violence. The later AIAS survey found former workers constituted 8.1% of sampled beneficiaries, while 14.9% of interviewed former workers said they had gained A1 or A2 land; a different official land-audit figure reported only 0.03% of ex-farmworkers receiving land. These measures use different denominators and should not be merged, but all show that farm-worker inclusion was incomplete.[22][16][17]

Zimbabwe’s agricultural and food-security outcomes are examined in the Land reform outcomes section. For the legal comparison, the central finding is that coercive, rapid transfer was accompanied by disruption of production systems, infrastructure, labour relations and input supply, while later recovery in some commodities depended on capital, contract farming, inputs, extension and buyers. The law transferred land; it did not itself replace the commercial infrastructure, finance and market coordination that had supported particular crops.[2][1][17][16]

The programme’s tenure design also affected investment after expropriation. A1 and A2 farmers often initially received offer letters or verbal offers rather than the permits and leases envisaged by policy. In the survey, 78.5% of beneficiaries reported only letters or verbal statements of land offers. The offer letter could be withdrawn without compensation for improvements, and some beneficiaries were reluctant to make substantial investments under that uncertainty.[16]

The political and legal process thus shaped wealth distribution through more than the number of hectares transferred. The FTLRP enabled households to acquire land and natural resources previously under the exclusive control of large white landowners; it also displaced owners and workers, disrupted wage employment, and created different forms of access for A1, A2, informal settlers, farm workers and politically connected beneficiaries. Redistribution of land access was broad, but redistribution of secure, transferable and financeable assets was more uneven.[16][18]

South Africa shows a different relationship between expropriation law and observed outcomes. The Constitution permits expropriation in the public interest, and the Restitution Act permits the state, where feasible, to acquire private land through purchase or expropriation to settle valid claims. But post-1994 redistribution was implemented principally through grants, negotiated purchase and later state purchase. The evidence supplied does not identify EWC as an enacted cause of South African agricultural, food-security, wealth or violence outcomes.[7][5][13][36]

That distinction matters because political debate often treats legal permission and actual use as equivalent. South Africa’s existing constitutional standard already allowed below-market, just-and-equitable compensation. EWC was a proposal for a clearer or broader authority, not an implemented national transfer programme. The failed 2021 amendment vote therefore cannot explain the slow transfer rate, leasehold insecurity or uneven beneficiary outcomes already visible under earlier market-purchase and PLAS systems.[11][1][3][9]

South African political conflict surrounding land reform has generally taken different forms in the supplied evidence. There are disputes over community governance, access, boundaries, water, beneficiary identification and tenure documentation. At Loeriesfontein, a CPA was established in 2008, received title to only the first of three commonage farms in 2014, and still faced unresolved access disputes and beneficiary-identification problems in 2019. At Malangskraal, informal subdivision and conflict over water and fencing accompanied delayed leases. These are land-reform disputes, but they are not evidence of a Zimbabwe-style nationally organised campaign of political violence attached to the acquisition process.[1][36]

South Africa’s wealth effects must likewise be separated into land and broader wealth. Restitution generated both land transfers and cash. As of 2023, it had secured 4 million hectares and paid R22.3 billion in financial compensation. Because cash settlements predominated, restitution could provide immediate monetary redress without changing rural ownership or tenancy. Conversely, land transferred to CPAs may preserve a collective asset but can produce weak household income if governance, finance, management or employment are poor.[29][9][2][1]

National inequality did not simply follow land-reform transfers. A World Inequality Database account reports pretax inequality in 2019 as high as in 1993; inequality and racial disparities rose between 1993 and 2005, then pretax inequality gradually fell back toward its 1993 level. It also reports that government redistribution between racial groups did not increase, even as rapid growth in top Black incomes shifted some tax contributions from Whites to top Black earners. This broader wealth pattern cannot be attributed to land policy alone, but it confirms that limited land transfer did not transform aggregate inequality.[35]

Food insecurity also should not be treated as a direct outcome of the legal acquisition instrument. In 2023, 19.7% of South African households experienced moderate to severe food insecurity and 8.0% severe food insecurity. Traditional areas had the highest moderate-to-severe prevalence, at 26.8%, while farming areas recorded 20.9%. A review reports a 10% reduction in food insecurity among restitution-recipient households, but the period and whether this is a percentage-point or relative reduction are not specified. These measures demonstrate variation in food access, not a national causal effect of market purchase, PLAS leasehold or EWC debate.[34][9]

Namibia has enacted compulsory-acquisition authority but has seldom used it relative to purchase. The Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act 6 of 1995 provides for state acquisition and compulsory acquisition of certain agricultural land. The government announced in 2004 that expropriation would be implemented to accelerate reform, arguing that willing-seller/willing-buyer inflated prices and limited access to productive farms. Yet the main implemented framework remained purchase and loan finance, not large-scale compulsory taking.[46][13][48]

Namibia’s resettlement outcomes show why the distinction between formal power and use matters. By 2018, 496 resettlement farms had been acquired, with 5,352 beneficiaries. The National Resettlement Programme had acquired about 3 million hectares since 1990. These are meaningful outputs, but they remain modest against the inherited concentration of commercial freehold land: one account describes 48% of Namibia’s territory as private freehold land held by fewer than 5,000 mainly white farmers, while over 70% of the population depended directly or indirectly on 35% communal land.[47][12][50]

The available evidence on production and livelihood effects is mixed and limited in scale. A survey reported that 57% of beneficiary households employed one or two labourers and one-third relied on family labour, but estimated no net employment creation because beneficiaries employed about one worker per household, roughly matching the freehold-sector average of one worker per 1,000 hectares. Average wages paid were reportedly below the legal minimum. The legal acquisition route alone did not create a large employment effect.[10]

Nor does land acquisition assure continued productive use. In 2014, the Ministry bought the 3,900-hectare Hartebeesteich-Süd farm for N$17 million. It contained a lodge and 6,000 olive trees with national marketing and supply systems. The Ministry transferred it to the Namibia Central Intelligence Services, which did not use it; all olive trees later died. This is a single case, but it plainly identifies post-transfer management and institutional assignment—not merely compensation or acquisition method—as determinants of productive assets’ survival.[50]

Namibia’s ongoing debate is therefore about more than whether to expropriate. The 2018 Second National Land Conference adopted more than 160 resolutions, many revisiting poorly implemented resolutions from the 1991 conference. It proposed evaluating AALS, reintroducing a 30% government guarantee for commercial land purchase, considering farm-size and ownership controls, addressing communal-land privatisation by elites, improving tenure security, and investigating ancestral land rights and restitution.[50][53][76]

These resolutions are proposals, not observed outcomes. The conference also discussed compensated expropriation of foreign-owned or underutilised commercial land, possible separate expropriation legislation, progressive land tax, one-person/one-farm principles, and bankable resettlement tenure. Their common purpose is to overcome bottlenecks visible under market acquisition: high prices, slow purchase, debt, insecure leases, elite access and weak communal-rights protection. They do not demonstrate that uncompensated expropriation has been adopted or that these reforms have already improved productivity, food security or wealth distribution.[12][10][47]

The 2018 Omaheke regional consultation recommended a constitutional referendum on expropriation without compensation, including for excess, underutilised or foreign-owned commercial agricultural land; it also proposed studies of maximum farm sizes, compulsory sale of excess land and punitive land taxes for absentee owners.[94]

Namibia’s communal-land debate is especially important to political violence and conflict, although the evidence here concerns local resource conflict rather than national political violence. Long leases in communal areas have been granted to old rural elites and new urban rich; communal land transactions can reach US$5,000 for less than two hectares, compared with a reported customary transaction fee of US$40 in northern Namibia. Enclosure has reduced access to grazing and water, causing displacement toward neighbouring commonage and conflicts between communities.[52][51]

Expropriation changes bargaining power and can accelerate transfer, but its socioeconomic effects depend on how the state allocates, secures, supports and governs land afterward; Zimbabwe shows the cost of coercive and partisan implementation, South Africa shows that unused expropriation powers do not solve slow market-led delivery, and Namibia shows that proposals for stronger powers remain distinct from realised outcomes.[22][16][1][2][50][12]

South Africa’s Expropriation Act 13 of 2024 took effect on 23 January 2025, replacing the 1975 market-value-based system with acquisition for public purposes subject to just-and-equitable compensation.[77] The Act identifies circumstances in which nil compensation may be just and equitable for an expropriation in the public interest; it does not prescribe nil compensation for every expropriation.[78]

South Africa’s Expropriation Act 13 of 2024 was assented to on 23 January 2025; it regulates state acquisition without amending section 25 of the Constitution.[99][91]

The Expropriation Act provides for notice, compensation particulars, interest from possession, negotiation and court determination. The earlier Expropriation Bill also made unsuccessful efforts at negotiated acquisition the ordinary precondition to expropriation.[100]

The Expropriation Act 13 of 2024 provides for notices, negotiation, compensation offers, court access and interest on unpaid compensation after possession; ordinary expropriation must be preceded by an unsuccessful attempt to agree on reasonable terms.[91][100][38]

Namibia’s 2025 Land Bill would replace the Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act 6 of 1995, the Communal Land Reform Act 5 of 2002 and 10 other laws and ordinances with one framework.[79]

The Minister of Agriculture, Fisheries, Water and Land Reform tabled the Land Bill in Parliament in 2025, where it awaited debate in the National Assembly.[79]

South Africa’s proposed Equitable Access to Land Bill and Communal Land Tenure and Administration Bill remained at Cabinet or public-consultation stage in the reported 2026 account.[101]

In May 2026, South Africa’s government described the proposed Equitable Access to Land Bill as a future centrepiece of redistribution; it would establish a Land Reform Agency to identify land and beneficiaries, allocate land and provide post-settlement support. A proposed Communal Land Tenure and Administration Bill would seek secure tenure in former homelands and communal areas through joint community–traditional-leader administration.[101]

The Bill proposes state-led redistribution in place of the willing-seller model, with the stated aims of accelerating resettlement and dismantling unequal land access.[79]

The Bill would authorise acquisition and redistribution to landless, inadequately resourced, historically disadvantaged or otherwise vulnerable people, including unemployed people, to address social and economic imbalances and promote equitable access.[79]

The Second National Land Conference adopted 176 resolutions; 25 were reported fully implemented and 134 ongoing, including 16 whose implementation measures predated the conference. Most resolutions were expected to be completed only once the Land Bill was enacted.[63]

The 2018 Second National Land Conference resolved to stop using willing-seller/willing-buyer as the primary acquisition method and to pursue expropriation within the Constitution with just compensation.[80]

The conference specified expropriation with just compensation for foreign-owned farms, underutilised land and absentee landlords.[81]

A 2018 consultation in Namibia’s Omaheke Region recommended a referendum to enable uncompensated expropriation of excess, underutilised or foreign-owned commercial agricultural land; the recommendation was not an enacted constitutional amendment.[94]

Namibia had previously expropriated five farms, and some farm owners challenged the process in court on the ground that criteria for expropriation in the public interest had not been gazetted.[62]

Among PLAS beneficiaries surveyed, 35% sometimes worried about having enough food and 10% sometimes had a household member skip a meal because of insufficient food.[42]

The three cases show overlapping legal repertoires, not three pure national models. Zimbabwe moved from market purchase to compulsory acquisition backed by constitutional changes that protected state title and restricted court challenges; it achieved rapid structural redistribution but did so amid violence, insecure beneficiary tenure and uneven support. South Africa’s Constitution already permits expropriation with just-and-equitable compensation, yet implementation largely used purchase and later state leasing, producing slower transfer and often insecure access rather than immediate ownership. Namibia has enacted purchase, pre-emption, subsidised freehold loans, leasehold resettlement and compulsory-acquisition authority, while its 2018 proposals for stronger expropriation and restitution remain proposals. The comparative direction is clear: law determines the state’s power to transfer land, but tenure, beneficiary selection, compensation administration, finance, support and political restraint determine whether redistribution becomes a durable socioeconomic gain.[6][36][9][46][12]

**Ranked by speed of changing racial ownership of commercial farmland, Zimbabwe ranks first: Fast Track resettled 180,000 families on an estimated 12 million hectares in four years from 2000. South Africa ranks second: state redistribution and restitution had transferred 8.4 million hectares, 9–10% of agricultural land, by March 2018. Namibia ranks third: from 1991 to 2005, state resettlement acquired 993,841 hectares and AALS purchases covered 3,470,000 hectares, while only three farms had been expropriated after the first commercial-farm expropriation was announced in early 2004.**[8][2][10]

That ranking reverses on avoidance of land-related coercion: Zimbabwe ranks behind South Africa and Namibia because Fast Track occupations involved intimidation, assaults, at least seven killings of white farm owners and several tens of farm-worker deaths.[22][36][51]

Comparable transferred-farm yields, household food-security series and land-linked violence counts could change a ranking of the three reforms by socioeconomic performance because current national indicators do not isolate policy effects.[29][2][10]

Five years after Namibia’s 2018 Second National Land Conference, 25 of its 169 resolutions had been fully implemented, 133 were ongoing and one was pending; because 16 resolutions already existed before the conference, only nine new resolutions had been fully implemented.[80]

Comparable, post-reform national productivity, food-security and wealth-distribution measures remain open where countries use different land categories, beneficiary definitions and survey periods. The distribution of Namibia’s future outcomes under any expropriation reform is also open because the contested measures have not yet been implemented at a scale from which outcomes can be observed.
