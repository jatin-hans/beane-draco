# Literature Review of Classical and Contemporary Gift Economies

## Executive summary

Classical gift theory remains useful for digital economies only when gift and commodity are treated as intertwined relations rather than as mutually exclusive kinds of thing. Malinowski, Mauss, and Sahlins show that exchange can create enduring obligation, status, and social distance, while feminist anthropology shows that these relations depend on gendered and often obscured labor of production, maintenance, and social reproduction.

Open-source software most strongly preserves gift-like reciprocity through sharing, recognition, and continuing collaborative obligation, but licenses, corporate appropriation, and unpaid maintenance make it a hybrid commons rather than a pure gift economy.

Social-media attention economies most sharply reverse the gift ideal because users’ apparently voluntary gifts of attention, content, and affect are converted into platform-owned data and advertising value, with gendered aspirational and care labor frequently left unpaid.

Cryptocurrency governance combines reciprocal community-building and collective rule-making with tokenized property, speculation, and unequal control, so it often turns participation into an investment relation rather than a Maussian gift bond.

## The question and the gift–commodity distinction

### What is the gift-versus-commodity distinction used to assess the contemporary

Judgment — The gift–commodity distinction is not a test for deciding whether an object is intrinsically a gift or a commodity. It is a relational comparison: it asks what sort of relationship a transfer makes, what obligations endure after it, how value is measured, who controls circulation, and whose work sustains the exchange. This matters for digital economies because code, data, tokens, visibility, and online participation can be shared without payment while also becoming assets, revenues, or speculative property.

In the classical Maussian account, a gift is a transfer that remains connected to persons. Mauss argues that the thing given establishes a bond because it is part of, or pertains to, the giver; to give is consequently to give part of oneself, and to receive is to receive something of another person’s spiritual essence. His total prestation, meaning an exchange that simultaneously engages legal, moral, religious, political, familial, and economic life, entails obligations to give, receive, and return. The gift is therefore neither simply voluntary nor simply altruistic: it creates a relationship of indebtedness and recognition.[1]

A commodity, by contrast, is ideally a useful and socially desired thing whose defining feature is exchange-value: the quantitative proportion in which it can be exchanged for other things, or price when that value is expressed in money. Christopher Gregory’s influential political-economic formulation describes commodity exchange as transfers of alienable things—things separable from their owner—between reciprocally independent transactors. Once an agreed price or counterpart is delivered, each party can in principle leave the transaction without an outstanding personal debt.[2][3][4]

**The central contrast is therefore continuing reciprocal dependence versus settled reciprocal independence.** Gift exchange generates qualitative relations: persons remain answerable to one another, and the transfer can carry identity, prestige, moral claims, or obligations to respond. Commodity exchange generates quantitative comparability: things are priced or otherwise made equivalent, and the exchange can be closed. This does not mean gifts have no material value or that commodities lack social meaning; it identifies the dominant relation that a transfer organizes.[5][4][6]

The distinction is best treated as an ideal-type contrast. An ideal type is an analytical model that clarifies tendencies without claiming that real cases conform perfectly to one category. Gregory’s sharp contrast between inalienable gifts and alienable commodities remains highly useful, but it cannot classify entire societies or all transfers within them. Gift and commodity forms coexist within the same economy, can be intertwined in one transaction, and may be differently understood by the people involved.[7][4][8]

In his 1986 chapter *The Cultural Biography of Things: Commoditization as Process*, Igor Kopytoff (1986, The Social Life of Things, 'The cultural biography of things: commoditization as process') shifts attention from a fixed classification to a process.[9][5] A commodity is a thing with use value that can be exchanged in a discrete transaction for an immediate counterpart judged equivalent.

Yet commodity status is culturally marked and historically variable: the same thing can be a commodity for one person, at one moment, but singularized—treated as uniquely meaningful and withdrawn from ordinary sale—for another. Commoditization expands when more things become exchangeable for more other things; singularization is the counter-process that protects, or restores, special status.[5]

This processual view is especially important for contemporary cases. A purchased object can be turned into a gift through ritual and relationship; United States Christmas practices, for example, transform bought commodities into expressions of intimacy through shopping, wrapping, giving, and receiving. Conversely, gifts can be absorbed into commercial circuits. In a Chinese ceremonial-gift case, money and purchased consumer goods operate as gifts even though recipients are expected to consume them rather than circulate the same object onward; returning the identical gift would signal rejection. This directly qualifies any claim that a gift must remain materially inalienable.[10][6]

The analytical question is thus not whether money, price, or platforms are present. Money can facilitate market exchange, but monetary objects can also be socially marked and used in ceremonial giving. Likewise, marketplaces are not merely spaces of impersonal commodity transaction: research on Papua New Guinea describes them as sites where people buy, sell, socialize, redistribute wealth, and contest what morally acceptable exchange looks like.[11][12]

There is a live disagreement over how strongly to retain the classical opposition. In *The Gender of the Gift: Problems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia* (1988), Marilyn Strathern (1988, no venue recorded, 'The Gender of the GiftProblems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia') maintains that inalienability makes the gift–commodity distinction indispensable, whereas Kopytoff’s cultural-biography approach emphasizes movement between commodity and singular statuses.[13][4][5] Gregory’s formulation supplies a clear relational baseline, while later work cautions against treating inalienability as an invariant property of things.

The productive conclusion is not to discard the distinction but to use it as a set of questions about each transfer’s terms, relations, and trajectory.[4][2][5]

Contemporary marketing illustrates why this is necessary. Rus argues that branding, customer retention, and relationship marketing can attach producer identity, reputation, emotional value, and mutual expectations to commodity exchange. Branded goods can thus appear less anonymous and more personally connected than the classical commodity ideal predicts. They remain market goods, but the market relation may be intentionally made relational; gift-like effects do not automatically turn them into gifts.[4]

For the later digital cases, the working framework has five linked questions:

- **Alienability:** Can the item, contribution, data trace, or token be detached from its creator or prior holder and freely transferred, priced, or sold? Gregory’s distinction makes alienability a central line between commodity and gift relations. [2][4]

- **Closure:** Does a payment, return, or technical settlement end the relation, or does participation produce ongoing obligation, debt, recognition, trust, or status? Kopytoff contrasts discrete commodity transactions with chains of gifts and obligations that are, in principle, non-terminal. [5][4]

- **Value form:** Is value organized primarily through price and quantitative equivalence, or through prestige, moral standing, recognition, access, and relational value? Sahlins distinguishes exchange-value from price, while Maussian exchange includes moral and symbolic purposes. [3][1][14]

- **Governance and control:** Who sets the terms by which something may circulate, be modified, be monetized, or be withheld? Licenses, platform terms, token rules, intellectual-property claims, and voting systems all determine whether sharing produces commons, dependency, or accumulation. [7]

- **Labor and social reproduction:** Who produces, maintains, moderates, repairs, documents, cares for, or otherwise makes the exchange possible, and who receives recognition or compensation? This is the feminist test developed more fully in the next section, but it must be built into the comparison from the outset because exchange never consists only of the visible moment of transfer. [15][6][5]

This framework rules out two recurrent errors. First, a no-price transfer is not automatically a gift: it may be unpaid labor appropriated by a firm, a governed contribution to a commons, or a strategic investment in reputation. Second, a priced transfer is not automatically socially empty: it may carry moral obligations, sustain long-term relations, or be transformed into a gift. The later sections consequently assess open-source software, attention economies, and cryptocurrency governance by their combined arrangements of reciprocity, property, labor, and capture—not by whether they look noncommercial at first glance.[16][17][18]

This review asks whether open-source software, social-media attention, and cryptocurrency governance organize the continuing obligations and dependencies associated with gifts, the alienable and quantitatively comparable transfers associated with commodities, or hybrid arrangements. Its concise test is relational and temporal: gifts create obligations to give, receive, and return, whereas commodity exchange ideally transfers alienable things between independent parties and settles the account. Feminist analysis adds a necessary question: whose gendered capacity and burden to carry transactions through makes the exchange possible, and who receives the resulting recognition, payment, or power?[2][5][1][19][8]

### What principal scholarship is needed to situate classical gift exchange

The classical canon begins with Bronisław Malinowski’s *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), Marcel Mauss’s *Essai sur le don*—first published in the 1923–24 issue of *L’Année sociologique* and conventionally dated 1925—and Marshall Sahlins’s *Stone Age Economics* (1972). Malinowski supplied a detailed ethnography of Trobriand exchange; Mauss used such ethnographic material comparatively to theorize obligations and social solidarity; and Sahlins developed reciprocity as a framework for relating material transfers to social distance and cultural order.[20][21][22][23][24]

The first classical text, Malinowski’s *Argonauts*, remains necessary because it distinguishes ceremonial transfer from barter while showing that they can coexist within one regional system. His account of kula made visible a rule-governed, inter-tribal network of prestige, partnership, deferred return, and circulation. Its importance for this review is methodological as well as substantive: exchange must be studied in relation to status, magic, partnership, travel, and social obligation rather than reduced to utility-maximizing trade. The section “Classical accounts of gift exchange” develops this argument and its subsequent revisions.[20][25]

Mauss is the indispensable comparative theorist. He did not himself conduct fieldwork; rather, his essay synthesised reports by ethnographers including Franz Boas, Bronislaw Malinowski, and Charles G. Seligman.[26] That comparative method enabled him to make the gift into a theory of social relations, but it also means that later readers must distinguish Mauss’s general model from the specific ethnographies on which he relied.

Jane Guyer further notes that translation and editorial choices have shaped how English-language readers encounter the essay’s terminology, footnotes, and argumentative complexity.[26][24][22]

Jane Guyer’s expanded 2016 English translation of Mauss restored the memorial and extensive review section omitted from the shorter 1954 translation.[101]

Sahlins is required because he resists treating economy as a separate, self-regulating domain. His substantivist approach treats economy as the material life process of society and as an expression of culturally organized values and relations. This bears directly on digital cases: platforms, software communities, and crypto networks are not merely technical markets but arrangements that provision people, allocate authority, organize labor, and encode particular systems of value.[3][27]

The later gift–commodity literature supplies the conceptual tools that make the classical canon usable without treating it as a closed tradition.

- **Christopher A. Gregory, *Gifts and Commodities* (1982; revised edition 2015):** Gregory brought political economy, kinship, colonial history, and Melanesian ethnography together. His theory of alienability, dependence, and reciprocal value provides this review’s basic ideal-type distinction; his book also explicitly addresses the transformation of gifts into commodities and commodities into gifts in colonial Papua New Guinea. [28][2]

- **Arjun Appadurai, ed., *The Social Life of Things* (1986), including “Commodities and the Politics of Value,” and Igor Kopytoff, “The Cultural Biography of Things” (1986):** Appadurai treats commodity status as a situation in a thing’s social life rather than an unchanging property of the thing, and foregrounds value’s political organization. [4][5] Kopytoff explains commoditization and singularization as changing cultural processes through which an object can move into and out of commodity status. [5]

- **James G. Carrier, *Gifts and Commodities: Exchange and Western Capitalism since 1700* (1995):** Carrier’s work is important because it rejects the assignment of whole societies to either the gift or commodity side. Gifts and capitalist exchange can coexist, and market processes can personalize goods without dissolving their commodity character. [4][29]

- **Jonathan Parry and Maurice Bloch, eds., *Money and the Morality of Exchange* (1989):** this intervention challenges the assumption that money necessarily depersonalizes social life. It identifies the recurrent coexistence of different transactional orders, including relatively impersonal and acquisitive activity, within the same society. [30][12]

- **Nicholas Thomas, *Entangled Objects* (1991), and Annette Weiner (1992, Inalienable Possessions, 'Kula: the Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving') [31], *Inalienable Possessions* (1992), including the chapter *Kula: The Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving*:** both are essential for understanding how colonialism, property, historical attachment, and retained wealth complicate the simple image of gifts as endlessly circulating objects. Weiner’s formulation of keeping-while-giving becomes especially important to feminist critique. [31][30][4][16]

These scholarship clusters alter the basic question. Instead of asking whether a society is a gift economy or a commodity economy, they direct analysis toward the particular social networks, moral classifications, property claims, and political conditions through which things become transferable, retain attachment, or enter new spheres of value. Kirsch (2001, Social Anthropology, 'Property effects. Social networks and compensation claims in Melanesia')’s distinction between property regimes—relations between persons and things—and transaction regimes—relations between persons created through circulation—offers a particularly useful supplement for studying digital property, data rights, and governance.[7]

Judgment — The principal literature should be read as a debate, not as a succession in which later authors replace earlier ones. Malinowski establishes the ethnographic problem of ceremonial circulation; Mauss theorizes obligation; Sahlins situates reciprocity in social distance and cultural order; Gregory formalizes an alienability-based contrast; Appadurai and Kopytoff historicize and destabilize object categories; and Carrier, Parry, Bloch, and Kirsch show why market and relational logics must be studied together.

The digital applications require a second, cross-disciplinary bibliography. Christopher Kelty’s *Two Bits: The Cultural Significance of Free Software* (2008) treats free software as collaborative source-code production made openly available through an unconventional use of copyright law. Bergquist and Ljungberg’s 2001 article, *The Power of Gifts: Organizing Social Relationships in Open Source Communities*, is an early direct link between gift theory and open-source communities, while Lerner and Tirole (2002, The Journal of Industrial Economics, 'Some Simple Economics of Open Source')’s 2002 economic analysis explains features of open source through labor economics and industrial organization rather than through gift exchange alone.[32][33][34][4]

For social media, Tiziana Terranova’s account of free labor is foundational because it identifies unpaid yet willingly contributed cultural and affective activity as a possible source of value within capitalism. Dallas Smythe’s audience-commodity framework, developed for advertising-supported media and extended by Christian Fuchs to commercial internet platforms, is necessary for tracing how attention and user activity become saleable audiences and data. Brooke Erin Duffy’s *Not Getting Paid to Do What You Love* (2017) adds a gendered account of aspirational social-media labor based on fifty-six in-depth interviews with bloggers, vloggers, designers, photographers, and other producers.[18][35][36][37]

For cryptocurrency, the core literature should separate research on money, communities, and governance. Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz’s 2013 study addresses Bitcoin’s practical materiality; Nigel Dodd’s 2018 article examines Bitcoin’s social life; Lana Swartz’s work addresses Bitcoin as a money technology; and Koray Çalışkan’s 2022 ethnography concerns a global cryptocurrency community. Governance-specific analysis includes Primavera De Filippi and Benjamin Loveluck’s 2016 *Internet Policy Review* article *The Invisible Politics of Bitcoin: Governance Crisis of a Decentralised Infrastructure*, which studies Bitcoin’s block-size dispute, and Samer Hassan and De Filippi’s 2021 *Internet Policy Review* article *Decentralized Autonomous Organization*, on decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs), alongside studies of on-chain and off-chain governance.[38][39][16][40][41]

The literature does not support one generic category of digital gift economy. Free-software research investigates code-sharing, licensing, maintenance, and collaborative governance; social-media research investigates visibility, affective and aspirational labor, data, advertising, and platform power; cryptocurrency research investigates money, speculation, trust, technical protocols, and contested collective authority. Their common question is whether apparently voluntary contribution creates reciprocity among participants, accumulation for owners, or both.[42][43][44] (Society; Fourcade and Kluttz, 2020)[44][45]

This bibliography makes the forthcoming comparisons possible while preventing anachronism: the review can use Mauss and Sahlins to ask about obligation and relation, while relying on digital labor, platform, and governance research to identify the actual technical institutions through which contemporary exchange operates.[46][16][43][40]

Gayle Rubin’s 1975 essay *The Traffic in Women: Notes on the “Political Economy” of Sex* is also important to feminist critique because it exposes theories in which women figure primarily as objects exchanged among men. It sharpens the question of who is recognized as an exchanging subject and whose constrained labor, sexuality, and kinship position underwrite exchange.

Thomas Widlok’s *Sharing* (2013) is also necessary to the conceptual bibliography because it refuses to subsume sharing under either gift reciprocity or commercial exchange. Widlok argues that market exchange, reciprocal gift exchange, and sharing coexist with distinct moralities and values, and identifies the development of a general theory connecting forms of transfer with values in social practice as an outstanding theoretical task.[47]

Widlok’s intervention also revises the canon’s empirical emphasis. He argues that hxaro, the celebrated reciprocal-exchange case among the !Kung and a small number of southern African forager groups, accounts for very few transfers even among the !Kung; the substantial remainder is sharing. This makes sharing scholarship necessary when assessing whether a visible and prestigious form of reciprocal exchange represents everyday provision.[47]

A 30 April 2016 SOAS conference on Jane Guyer’s expanded translation of Mauss’s *The Gift* brought together Guyer, Marshall Sahlins, Marilyn Strathern, Maurice Bloch, Jonathan Parry, Keith Hart, David Graeber, Jacob Copeman, Kostas Retsikas, and Giovanni da Col. The event situated Mauss’s 1925 essay as a continuing point of debate over obligations, reciprocity, competition, and market economies.[48]

- **Thomas Widlok, “Sharing: Allowing others to take what is valued” (2013):** Widlok argues that sharing cannot be reduced either to reciprocal gift exchange or commercial exchange: market exchange, reciprocal gift exchange, and sharing coexist with distinct moralities and values. [47]

### The anthropology of gift economies developed through accounts of Trobriand

Gift exchange has long been a central topic in anthropology because it reveals that value is not exhausted by price and that transfers help make persons, groups, obligations, and hierarchies. The field’s basic insight is that circulation is social: an object’s movement can create friendship, rivalry, debt, rank, trust, dependence, and moral judgment. The classical literature and its feminist revisions make this insight available for digital cases, but also warn against equating visible exchange with all the labor that sustains it.[49][6]

Malinowski’s Trobriand research, Mauss’s comparative synthesis, and Sahlins’s theorization of reciprocity are connected but not identical. Malinowski focused on the organization and effects of particular exchange practices among Trobriand Islanders. Mauss generalized from cases including kula and potlatch to argue that gift obligations create group solidarity. Sahlins built on this tradition by placing reciprocity on a continuum associated with social distance, making the relation between exchange partners central to the kind of return expected.[24][50][51][52]

The next section, “Classical accounts of gift exchange,” compares their mechanisms in detail. For the present purpose, three shared propositions establish the baseline for digital comparison:

- **First, exchange is not reducible to material transfer.** For Mauss, an accepted gift binds giver and receiver; for Sahlins, a material transaction is commonly an episode in a continuing social relation; and for Malinowski, exchange categories correspond approximately to different social relationships and duties. [1][53][25]

- **Second, return need not mean immediate equivalence.** Kula is organized as a gift followed later by a counter-gift rather than haggled barter; Mauss’s obligations extend beyond repayment to the duties to give and receive; and Sahlins distinguishes generalized, balanced, and negative reciprocity. [25][1][51][50]

- **Third, economic calculation and social meaning are not opposites.** Ceremonial exchange can involve equivalence, strategy, prestige, and political gain without becoming simple barter. Sahlins further argues that capitalist pecuniary calculation itself depends on wider systems of meaningful attributes and human relationships. [20][24][27]

This baseline supports a relational and temporal account of gifts. Commodity exchange is ideally discrete: a counterpart settles the transaction. Gift exchange is temporally open: a transfer can initiate a deferred return, continuing indebtedness, or a relation whose value exceeds the item’s price. Yet neither form excludes calculation, hierarchy, conflict, or self-interest. Gifts can impose unwelcome obligations, and market exchanges can become durable, morally evaluated relationships.[5][52][10][24]

Feminist anthropology shifts attention from the public exchange partner to the gendered production and reproduction that enable exchange. Annette Weiner’s *Women of Value, Men of Renown* (1976) is the key intervention for the Trobriands. It compares women, men, and wealth exchanges in the same region where Malinowski worked from 1915 to 1918, challenging male-centered accounts that had dismissed women’s work as unimportant. Weiner documented women’s production of banana-leaf bundles and skirts for elaborate mortuary transactions and demonstrated that women’s giving was vital to Trobriand social life.[19][54][55]

Weiner’s critique does more than add women to an established kula narrative. It changes the analytical object from prominent male exchange to the life cycles, rights, wealth production, mortuary obligations, and social reproduction through which women and men establish claims on one another. Her later concept of inalienable possessions—valuables whose significance lies partly in being retained—further counters a model in which value arises only from circulation and reciprocal return.[56][57][4][23]

Marilyn Strathern’s *The Gender of the Gift* (1988) extends this rethinking across Melanesia. Strathern treats gift exchange as an anthropological heuristic, a tool for making comparisons rather than a literal description of a self-contained gift society. She also insists that gift/commodity is a Western analytical opposition rooted in Western metaphysics, so it cannot simply be imposed as a universal division. Her work connects gender, personhood, ownership, and relationality: for some Melanesian groups, exchange is not merely something already formed persons do but part of what makes persons.[8][50][58]

The feminist implication is direct: a theory of exchange that recognizes only the visible giver, recipient, or prestige holder risks missing the labor that creates valuables, maintains relations, organizes ceremonial obligations, and reproduces the people and households on which exchange depends. Later Pacific scholarship explicitly frames gendered production, consumption, and the value assigned to women’s work as a critique of anthropological theories of value and exchange. It also notes that women’s position in producing wealth items has been more central than Gregory or Strathern acknowledged in some accounts.[15][29]

Strathern’s cited discussion of women’s wok meri savings-and-credit groups in Papua New Guinea shows why gendered exchange should not be presumed to be only domestic or subordinate. Women collectively protected savings from husbands, used capital for business and lending, and represented loans through maternal idioms of nurture and growth. Warry’s account of comparable Chuave practices argues that women’s created relations could link strangers and disrupt interpersonal networks maintained by men.[8]

There are unresolved debates within feminist and Melanesian anthropology. Strathern’s dividual model—a person understood as constituted through relations rather than as a bounded individual—has been highly influential, but Gillison (2013, Oceania, 'The ‘Dividual Androgyne’ and Me: a Personal Affair')’s 2013 *Oceania* article *The ‘Dividual Androgyne’ and Me: A Personal Affair* contests whether the dividual is a valid Melanesian or broader premodern model of personhood.[59] The point for this review is not to resolve that debate in advance; it is to avoid treating relational personhood, gender, and reciprocity as culturally uniform or theoretically settled.[59][60]

Judgment — Feminist scrutiny provides the necessary test for digital gift claims: identify the visible contribution, then trace the maintenance, emotional, reproductive, and administrative labor that permits it to circulate; ask who bears that labor, who can refuse it, who receives status or payment, and who captures the value it creates. A digital practice can be gift-like among peers and exploitative in its wider organization at the same time.

This test already has support in research on digital labor. Terranova emphasizes that unpaid online labor can be willingly contributed for the pleasures of communication and exchange, and that unpaid labor is not automatically exploited. But platform political economy shows how audience activity, user content, and data can enter commodity production, while Duffy documents women whose social-media aspirations frequently become unpaid work for corporate brands rather than paid careers.[18][61][35][37]

The same issue appears in collaborative software. Geiger, Howard, and Irani’s 2021 interview study of thirty-seven free and open-source software maintainers found that code contributions may be experienced by contributors as donations of time, effort, and intellectual property, while maintainers experience those contributions as obligations involving review and indefinite maintenance. The gift of code is therefore not costless: it redistributes work, authority, and responsibility.[42]

The remaining sections apply this feminist test separately. Open-source software examines how licenses, maintenance, funding, and paid work complicate the image of code as a pure gift. Social-media attention economies examine how relational interaction and visibility become data and advertising commodities. Cryptocurrency governance structures examine how tokens, protocol rules, voluntary work, and voting claims organize obligations and power. The analytical result is an account of which relations are reciprocal, which are priced, which are enforced technically, and whose labor remains hidden.[62][43][45][63]

### Synthesis

The literature establishes an ideal-type contrast between gifts, which create potentially continuing obligations, recognition, and dependence, and commodities, which ideally circulate as alienable and quantitatively comparable things among independent parties. Kopytoff, Carrier, and later exchange scholarship show that things move between commodity and gift spheres, while markets can be moral, personal, and identity-bearing.[2][5][4]

Malinowski, Mauss, and Sahlins provide questions rather than a template for calling every online contribution a gift. Weiner and Strathern make visible the labor that produces wealth and reproduces exchange relations; digital analysis therefore examines sharing, attention, and participation together with ownership, licensing, data extraction, maintenance, governance, and gendered work.[19][42][43][44]

## Classical accounts of gift exchange

Classical scholarship on gift exchange asks why transfers that appear voluntary so often create durable duties, status differences, and political ties. Malinowski, Mauss, and Sahlins agree that exchange cannot be understood simply as goods moving between self-interested individuals, but they explain its binding force in different ways.

### How do Malinowski’s Trobriand kula rings work as gift exchange?

Bronisław Malinowski’s *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) describes the **kula** as an extensive inter-island ceremonial exchange system in the Massim region of what is now Papua New Guinea. Two kinds of shell valuables circulate in opposed directions around the circuit: red-shell necklaces, *soulava*, move clockwise, while white-shell armbands, *mwali*, move in the opposite direction. Each type is exchanged for the other rather than sold for money.[64][20]

Malinowski’s *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) drew on Trobriand fieldwork conducted in 1914–18.[64]

Malinowski described fixed kula routes and predetermined places and dates for exchanges, supported by myth, magic, law, taboos, canoe voyages, and mortuary ceremonies.[64]

Kula transactions occur only between recognized partners. Malinowski described partnership as formally established and lifelong, with the number of partners varying by a man’s rank and importance. A recipient receives a valuable, holds it temporarily, and is expected to pass it onward to another partner; valuables normally complete a circuit in approximately two to ten years. The holder does not bargain directly over the equivalence of the counter-gift. Instead, the opening gift, *vaga*, is followed after an interval by a return gift, *yotile*, whose adequacy is judged through custom, dignity, and the relationship.[64][25][20]

Malinowski reported that a commoner might have a few kula partners, while a chief could have hundreds; partners also owed one another protection, preferential access to valuables, and exchange in a permanently fixed direction.[64]

Participants usually held a kula valuable for one or two years, and only a very small number of valuables became hereditary heirlooms outside circulation.[64]

The valuables were not valued for practical utility. Malinowski wrote that they were not money, were not a general medium of exchange or measure of value, and were possessed for renown, display, and their associations with former holders and historical events. The most prestigious objects had names and known biographies. This made their circulation socially productive: a participant gained prestige not by permanently accumulating a shell, but by attracting, displaying, and properly passing it on.[64][20][57][65][25]

Kula was also a relationship, not merely a sequence of transfers. Partners behaved as friends and owed each other forms of assistance, service, generosity, and hospitality. Malinowski therefore explained the binding force of exchange through **reciprocity**—the expectation that a return would occur and the sanction that a partner could cease giving and end the relationship.[64][6] **Kula thus works as delayed, rule-governed reciprocal circulation: valuables create durable inter-island relationships and prestige precisely because they must be passed on rather than permanently accumulated.**[64]

Malinowski sharply distinguished kula from *gimwali*, or ordinary barter. Gimwali involved useful goods, haggling, negotiated equivalence, and mutual material advantage; kula involved public ceremonial giving, magic, fixed partnerships, and deferred counter-gifts. Yet the distinction did not mean that kula voyages were outside material economy. Trade in useful and sometimes indispensable goods occurred alongside ceremonial exchange, and kula expeditions depended on canoe construction, mortuary observances, ritual restrictions, and collective work.[25][64][20] **Malinowski’s contrast between kula and barter therefore distinguishes forms of social organization, not a socially pure gift sphere from an economically relevant world.**[64][20]

Malinowski described kula counter-gifts as arriving after delays ranging from minutes to more than a year, whereas a gimwali barter transaction could end immediately.[64][25]

Later Massim scholarship substantially qualified the classic image of a uniformly closed, male, lifelong kula ring. Roger Keesing’s review reports that Malinowski extrapolated too broadly from the northern sector of the network: elsewhere, paths could be more variable, and southern exchanges could include useful goods such as pigs, canoes, and axe blades.[57] Later research also identified *kitoma*: a valuable free from a debt obligation and available to its holder for retention, gift, further kula transfer, or exchange for pigs, canoes, land, mortuary payments, magic, or services.[57]

The candidate account calls these transferable valuables *kitomwa* and also lists marriage, compensation, healing, and sorcery services among the transactions they could enter.[57]

Wendy Smith treats kula and gimwali as coexisting systems with distinct exchange rates and reproductive logics.[102]

Later ethnographers also found partnership less fixed than Malinowski’s lifelong model. Links could form, cycle, end, and be replaced, with some paths durable and politically important and others temporary. Women’s roles also require attention: Annette Weiner (1992, Inalienable Possessions, 'Kula: the Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving')’s *Women of Value, Men of Renown* (1976) documented women’s autonomous ceremonial wealth and mortuary distributions, a domain given little coverage in Malinowski’s account.[31][57][6][55] **The classical kula model remains foundational, but later research shows that circulation, ownership, participation, and regional variation were more complex than its closed-ring image suggests.**[57][6]

Although Malinowski recorded women carrying valuables to canoes and acknowledged distinct marriage and matrilineal economic relations, he did not develop an account of women’s exchange labour.[25]

Rubin argues that kinship transactions are simultaneously economic and political transactions and transfers of rights of domicile and land use. This directs analysis of kula’s prestige relations to the material objects, labor, rights, and power that make ceremonial circulation possible.[66]

### What does Mauss’s essay on the gift argue about gift

Marcel Mauss’s *Essai sur le don*, first published in *L’Année sociologique* in 1923–24 and conventionally dated 1925, transforms cases such as kula into a general theory of social obligation. Mauss’s central object is the **total prestation**—an exchange that simultaneously involves economic, legal, political, religious, moral, familial, and symbolic life. In this sense, the transfer of objects is only one part of a much wider relation among persons and groups.[21][14][22]

Mauss included aesthetic relations among the dimensions of the total prestation.[103][104]

Mauss identifies three linked obligations: to give, to receive, and to return. A present is therefore not a free, final transfer. To accept it is to enter a relationship and incur a debt whose return may involve goods, services, recognition, alliance, honor, or prestige. In Mauss’s formulation, a gift is at once apparently voluntary and materially interested, morally obligatory and socially productive.[1][24][6][67]

A key claim is that the object retains a connection to its giver. In Mauss’s account of Māori *hau*, or spiritual force, the gifted *taonga* remains animated by an aspect of the giver and seeks a return toward its source. Giving is therefore also a giving of part of oneself; receiving incorporates something of another person and establishes an enduring bond.[1][4] Mauss’s account of Samoan exchange likewise links reciprocal giving to honor, prestige, and *mana*, with failure to reciprocate threatening authority and standing.[22][68]

This account does not depict gift exchange as peaceful altruism. Mauss also examines competitive or **agonistic** giving, in which people and groups seek honor by outgiving rivals, making others debtors, or returning more than they received. Gifts can produce friendship and alliance, but they can also establish inequality: an unreciprocated gift may make the recipient inferior.[22][68][52] **Mauss overturns the image of the gift as voluntary one-way generosity: giving, receiving, and returning form a continuing cycle of mutual dependence that can produce solidarity, competition, and hierarchy.**[1][69][68][22]

In the potlatch material Mauss examined, chiefs could give away or destroy wealth to impose debt and assert hierarchy and authority.[104]

For Mauss, the relevant contrast is not exchange versus no exchange. It is between a relational prestation, which keeps persons connected across time, and a discrete transaction in which a quantified counterpart settles the parties’ obligations. Commodity transactions ideally render objects alienable and parties independent once the exchange is complete; gifts make relations, debts, and social identities persist.[5][4][14] This is why Mauss could treat ceremonial exchange as morally consequential even when it involved calculation, equivalence, or strategic competition.

Mauss’s explanation through *hau* remains disputed. Marshall Sahlins argued that *hau* logically addresses only why gifts are repaid, not why people must give or receive in the first place. Raymond Firth criticized Mauss’s interpretation of Māori thought, Claude Lévi-Strauss called the spirit-of-the-gift explanation a mystification, and Jonathan Parry argued that the models of spiritual attachment and reciprocity do not consistently cohere.[3][6][53] **What survives these disputes is Mauss’s stronger insight that gifts bind social relations; what remains unsettled is whether spiritual attachment, social sanction, hierarchy, or another mechanism best explains the obligation to return.**[6]

Firth’s specific objection was that Mauss confused a gift’s *hau* with its giver’s *hau* and attributed agency to a social construction.[6][105]

Stephanie Frank locates Mauss’s model centrally in Roman legal institutions, in which ritual enacts obligations, some things belong to families, and people repeatedly “sell” and “redeem” themselves through exchange.[6][105]

Mauss also contrasted collective total prestations conducted by group representatives with modern commodity exchange among independent individuals in an evolutionary account.[103][6]

Jonathan Parry’s 1986 critique argues that conventional readings understate the evolutionary dimensions of Mauss’s essay and its engagement with Hindu law and comparative religion.[103]

### How does Sahlins’s analysis of reciprocity distinguish forms of exchange?

Marshall Sahlins’s *Stone Age Economics* (1972) shifts the focus from one institution, such as kula, to a comparative model of **reciprocity**: back-and-forth movement between parties. He treats reciprocity as a continuum shaped by kinship distance, sociability, generosity, and the social distance between exchange partners. A material transaction is usually an episode within an ongoing social relation, but transactions can also initiate relations: “gifts make friends.”[6][3][53][52]

- **Generalized reciprocity:** This is the solidarity-oriented end of the continuum, most associated with close kin and intimate social relations. A person gives without stipulating the exact amount, timing, or form of return; no precise ledger is maintained, although the relationship is expected to balance over time. [24][51]

In Sahlins’s Pilagá example, people who were unproductive received food from more people than they supplied.[53]

- **Balanced reciprocity:** This is more direct and more explicit. The recipient is expected to return something understood as equivalent in value within a specified period. The relationship persists, but obligations are more clearly measured than in generalized reciprocity. [24][51]

Examples of balanced reciprocity discussed in this literature include sister exchange and reciprocal obligations arising from blood feuds.[106][27]

- **Negative reciprocity:** This is the unsociable extreme. One party attempts to secure advantage at another’s expense—getting something for nothing, haggling, or extracting more value than one gives. Sahlins associates it especially with socially distant actors and strangers. [24][51][70]

Sahlins’s account includes appropriation and theft as forms of negative reciprocity where obligations weaken beyond a circle of sociability.[106][107]

Sahlins’s main analytic contribution is to separate the existence of a return obligation from its form. Generalized reciprocity has an open-ended and unmeasured expectation of balance; balanced reciprocity specifies equivalence and timing; negative reciprocity aims at unilateral gain rather than mutual balance.[24][51] **The closer and more enduring the relationship, the more exchange tends toward generalized reciprocity; greater social distance tends toward balanced return and, at the outer limit, negative reciprocity.**[6][27][52]

The social distance relevant to reciprocity includes familiarity, information, kinship, and moral inclusion; different forms of reciprocity can coexist, and an actor’s position affects the relevant social universe.[107][27][106]

This framework also distinguishes reciprocity from **pooling** or redistribution. Reciprocity is a relation between two parties, while pooling is a within-group arrangement in which resources are collected at a social center and redistributed among members. Households may pool food while their members also enter reciprocal relations with one another or with outsiders.[53] This distinction makes it possible to analyze household provisioning, chiefly redistribution, and intergroup exchange without treating them as identical.

Sahlins presented the model cautiously. He said its generalizations emerged from dialogue with ethnographic materials and had not undergone rigorous tests; subsequent summaries note broad cross-cultural variation in institutional arrangements. One disagreement concerns generalized reciprocity: some accounts define it as eventually balancing over time, while others describe it as giving without expectation of return.[71][52][51][50] **Sahlins provides a powerful relational map of exchange, but it is best used as a comparative heuristic rather than a universal law assigning every transfer to a fixed social category.**[71][52]

Danby argues that affluent capitalism also relies heavily on forward commitments, debt, and relationships structured by power, rather than only on detached spot transactions.[107]

### How do Malinowski’s Trobriand kula rings

The three approaches share a rejection of the idea that exchange is simply a utilitarian swap. Malinowski provides the detailed ethnography of a ceremonial system: kula transfers are delayed, rule-governed, prestige-bearing, and embedded in partnerships. Mauss generalizes from such cases to a theory of total prestation, in which gifts bind people, groups, objects, status, and obligations. Sahlins then classifies different forms of return according to the closeness and structure of social relationships.[64][22][6]

Their central difference is explanatory. Malinowski grounds return in reciprocal expectation, social sanction, and the risk that a partner will stop giving. Mauss grounds it in a broader moral and spiritual relation between giver, recipient, and thing. Sahlins treats neither mechanism as universal; instead, he asks how the social distance and relationship between parties shape the degree of expected return, equivalence, calculation, and self-interest.[6][22][1][24]

Kula is especially important because it shows why equivalent counter-giving is not automatically commodity exchange. Kula partners were expected to give an appropriate counter-gift, but they could not bargain item by item. Equivalence had to be realized through customary judgment, personal dignity, delayed repayment, and the continuing relationship; the valuables’ histories and prestige were inseparable from their movement through persons and places.[64][20][57] **The comparison establishes that the decisive issue is not whether a transfer has a return, but whether it closes a relationship or sustains one.**[5][4][64][1]

All three accounts also leave room for calculation and material interest. Kula voyages combined ceremonial valuables with ordinary barter; Mauss included trade among the occasions of contractual gifts; and Sahlins’s continuum includes exchanges ranging from generalized solidarity to adversarial acquisition. Gift and trade are therefore not separate worlds but different social forms that may coexist in the same institutions and journeys.[64][22][24]

Kula’s expected, delayed equivalent counter-gift has features of Sahlins’s balanced reciprocity. Its enduring partnerships and prohibition on item-by-item bargaining distinguish it from a settled sale: equivalence is owed, but it is not determined through a single negotiated transaction that closes the parties’ relationship.[64][20][51][25]

### Synthesis

Malinowski, Mauss, and Sahlins establish a common relational premise: exchange creates, maintains, and can stratify social relationships rather than merely reallocating things. Malinowski’s kula demonstrates delayed, ceremonial counter-giving among named partners; Mauss explains such relations as total prestations structured by giving, receiving, and returning; Sahlins differentiates reciprocal forms according to social distance and the specificity of return.[64][1][6][24]

They disagree over what compels return. Malinowski emphasizes enforceable reciprocity, Mauss emphasizes moral and spiritual attachment, and Sahlins offers a relational typology rather than a single causal mechanism. Later work also shows that the classic kula picture was more variable in ownership, partnership, gendered participation, and regional practice than Malinowski’s model implied.[6][57]

## Feminist critiques of gendered labor

Judgment — Feminist critiques matter because gift exchange is never only the visible passing of valued objects. It also depends on who produces wealth, performs care and ritual, bears the duty to give, receives public credit, and can refuse an obligation. These questions revise classical exchange theory from inside: rather than merely adding women to a pre-existing model, they ask whether the model’s apparent subjects, valuables, and returns were already gendered.

### Which feminist anthropologists’ critiques are relevant to gift exchange?

Annette Weiner (1992, Inalienable Possessions, 'Kula: the Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving')’s *Women of Value, Men of Renown* (1976) is the foundational feminist-informed correction to male-centred accounts of Trobriand exchange.[31] Malinowski’s work gave women’s roles little coverage, whereas Weiner examined women’s and men’s differential relations through life and death, internal exchanges of female and male wealth, annual yam transfers from men to women, and women’s wealth in mortuary ceremonies. Her fieldwork took place in Kwaibwaga, Kiriwina Island, in June–October 1971 and May–November 1972.[55][19]

Weiner’s 1971–72 Kwaibwaga fieldwork also examined father–son relations, land tenure, and wealth at the hamlet level alongside life cycles, yam distributions, and mortuary exchange.[55]

Weiner’s central intervention was to shift attention from publicly prominent male exchange to women’s autonomous ceremonial activity and matrilineal claims. Women were not simply things exchanged by men: in mortuary exchange they publicly gave wealth and reaffirmed matrilineal solidarity. Her analysis therefore treats rights between women and men as established through object-giving, and asks how cultural conceptions of time and space organize gendered spheres of exchange.[6][56] **Weiner changes the unit of analysis from the male exchange event to the gendered production, circulation, and retention of wealth through which women and men establish social rights, matrilineal identity, and power.**[51][30][56]

Marilyn Strathern (1988, no venue recorded, 'The Gender of the GiftProblems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia')’s *The Gender of the Gift* (1988) extends this critique beyond the demand to make women visible.[13] For Strathern, gender means classifications of persons, objects, events, and sequences that use sexual imagery to express social relations; it does not automatically mean an individual’s gender identity. A gift is consequently not inherently male or female. Its gendered significance depends on how it is transacted and toward what social end.[8]

This position directly challenges the notion of exchange as a neutral transaction in which power belongs only to the person controlling assets or the public event. Strathern argues that the action of transacting is itself gendered: gender structures both people’s ability to transact particular items and the unequal necessity and burden of carrying transactions through. Asking about the gender of the gift is therefore asking how exchange relates to domination in a particular society.[8] This makes it possible to analyze power without presuming that either objects or persons possess fixed gendered meanings before a transaction occurs.[8]

Gayle Rubin’s 1975 account of the traffic in women poses a different, sharper problem. Drawing on Lévi-Strauss and political economy, Rubin describes patriarchal alliance as operating through rules that require groups to exchange women for reproduction and to form bonds especially among men. In this formulation, power travels through women without being wielded by them.[72] Rubin thus foregrounds women’s objectification in alliance-making; Strathern cautions that even when women appear to be gifted, analysis must still determine what the transfer means locally, how it is organized, and to whose ends.[8]

Rubin developed her traffic-in-women argument partly through Sahlins’s teaching on tribal economies as well as Lévi-Strauss’s kinship theory.[108]

Feminist anthropology is not one settled theory. Strathern herself noted disagreement over whether men are universally prominent and warned against treating Western feminist premises as directly transferable to Melanesian cases. Her account identifies Weiner and Daryl Feil as early feminist-informed exceptions, while also naming Brenda Clay, Deborah Gewertz, Lisette Josephides, Miriam Kahn, Rena Lederman, Nancy Munn, and Lorraine Sexton in the expansion of Melanesian gender scholarship; not all identified their work as feminist.[8] The relevant lesson is methodological: accounts of society and power must specify whose standpoint they describe, because apparently general accounts may promote the interests of a narrower group.[8]

A live disagreement concerns Strathern’s influential account of the *dividual*: a person understood as composite and constituted through relationships rather than wholly self-contained. Gillison (2013, Oceania, 'The ‘Dividual Androgyne’ and Me: a Personal Affair') argues that Strathern mistook a strongly anti-female Gimi ideology for benign accommodation between the sexes; Lipset’s cited criticism adds that the dividual model can erase affect, agency, and identity.[59][8] This dispute does not make gender irrelevant to exchange. It establishes that feminist interpretation must distinguish gendered relationality from an ethnographic account that inadvertently softens domination.

The literature also divides between accounts of complementarity and domination. A review of New Guinea ethnography groups Weiner, Errington, and Gewertz with a Lowlands account of male–female complementarity rooted in equal but distinct interests and powers, while Josephides and Godelier are grouped with neo-Marxist reconstructions of domination.[73] Josephides’ specific criticism is that theories of gifts to men can depend on wealth produced by women being alienated from them, while women’s role in producing circulating wealth items is more central than Strathern or Gregory acknowledge.[29] These disagreements make labor, control, and recognition—not participation alone—the decisive questions.

In *Inalienable Possessions* (1992), Weiner argues that control over valued possessions retained while other goods circulate reproduces authority and social ties.[70][74]

Weiner’s keeping-while-giving account includes retaining names, narratives, and claims that anchor group identity while other things are given.[109]

Rubin’s *sex/gender system* directs analysis to the socially organized arrangements through which biological sexuality is transformed into social activity and sexual needs are met. It separates gender hierarchy from biological inevitability and from an explanation based only on economic forces: sex/gender systems are historical products, and gendered oppression also occurs in non-capitalist societies.[66] Her political economy of sexual systems therefore requires analysis of the particular mechanisms through which conventions of sexuality are produced and maintained.[66]

Rubin argues that Marxism raises questions about class, housework, production, and reproduction but does not adequately explain gender oppression, sexual difference, or sexuality.[108]

For Rubin, kinship does not exchange women alone: it organizes sexual access, genealogical statuses, lineage names and ancestors, rights, and people, while also structuring production, distribution, goods and services, solidarity, ritual, and ceremony.[66] Calling women gifts identifies a distinction between gift and giver: men may be positioned as the exchange partners linked by women’s circulation, while women’s rights are more residual.[66] Rubin nevertheless rejects exchange of women as a universal cultural necessity or a sufficient analytic key; it must be examined as one element in specific systems.[66]

Rubin later distinguished compulsory marriage and descent systems that generate political structure from voluntary support networks in complex societies.[108]

Rubin consequently rejects an account of marriage exchange framed only as immediate or deferred reciprocity. Kinship obligations are simultaneously economic and political transactions and charters to rights of domicile and land use; analysis must examine the objects transferred, the rights they establish, and their material and political consequences.[66] Her proposed reworking of anthropology makes the conversion of female labor into male wealth, female lives into marriage alliances, and marriage into political power central to accounts of stratification, land tenure, commodity forms, and subsistence technology.[66]

Jolly challenges a sharp gift–commodity divide, characterizing the apparent separation of these economies as “discursive rather than the geography of the real world.”[29]

### How does gendered labor bear on accounts of gift exchange?

Gendered labor means tracing the productive, ritual, domestic, and relational work that makes gifts possible, as well as the allocation of credit and return. In the Trobriands, men cultivate yams largely for married-out daughters and sisters, who display them publicly in yam houses. These gifts confer prestige on women by demonstrating matrilineal support and recognizing a woman’s position as owner of the matrilineal group.[6] Women, meanwhile, give banana-leaf bundles and skirts in mortuary rituals, mourn the deceased, and contribute ritual labor; the woman who distributes the most is recognized as a wealthy woman.[6]

The two streams of work and wealth are connected rather than separable into a male public sphere and a female domestic sphere. Yam giving obliges a brother-in-law to make a return in women’s banana-leaf wealth, and yam and banana-leaf production keep both women and men busy throughout the year for gifts to others and anticipated returns rather than simply for personal consumption.[6] Malinowski likewise described harvest payments from a wife’s brothers as creating a lifelong duty for men to work for female kin: a boy gardens for his mother and later for married sisters; yet return gifts never equal the full value of the ongoing food gifts.[20] **A labor-centred account therefore recasts gift exchange as social reproduction: the continuing production and renewal of people, kinship, authority, and ritual obligations, not an equal bargain between isolated givers.**[6][20]

This revision also corrects the tendency to identify prestige exclusively with male ceremonial activity. Campbell’s interpretation of Vakuta kula describes men’s preparations, gifts, travel, and persuasive speech as resembling courtship, but with male exchange partners entering a symbolic marriage. Her interpretation contrasts women’s actual regeneration of society with men’s reproduction of male wealth and renown through male-only relations.[57] Keesing further argues that Massim leaders could manipulate exchange rules and valuable flows to preserve regional power and prestige in ways that conflicted with the interests of ordinary men and, especially, women.[57] Relationship-making exchange can thus distribute status and power unequally even where many people contribute to its material conditions.

In the Vakuta interpretation, men’s pursuit of kula renown has also been described as a pursuit of immortality through reputation.[57]

Strathern’s comparative cases show why neither domination nor complementarity should be presumed. In Hagen, men’s public prestige rests on collective exchange and political activity while women are prominent in domestic production; public rhetoric can devalue production even as men acknowledge dependence on women’s work.[8] Yet Strathern also reports that husbands’ and wives’ labor may be understood as mutually dependent, with men’s garden preparation not valued above women’s tending of crops or cooking.[8] Gendered labor can therefore create real authority and mutual dependence while still being differently visible, publicly valued, and convertible into prestige.

Women’s collective financial practices provide another corrective. Sexton’s *wok meri* case concerns women’s savings-and-credit groups in the Daulo area of Papua New Guinea’s Eastern Highlands. Co-resident lineage wives collectively bank savings, make loans, sponsor daughter groups, and use capital for businesses; their transactions are accompanied by birth and marriage rituals and described through nurture and mother–daughter relations.[8] Warry’s account of related Chuave practices argues that women’s fictive ties can link strangers and disrupt networks that men sustain.[8] Such cases show women as makers of financial and social-reproductive capacity, not simply beneficiaries of male-controlled exchange.

Gendered reciprocity also concerns whether labor is recognized as a gift at all. Ashwin, Tartakovskaya, Ilyina, and Lytkina’s 2013 *Gender & Society* study defines a gift cycle as giving, recognition, reciprocation, and the giver’s response to reciprocation or its absence. In its Russian qualitative sample, women provided unpaid task assistance in 30 relationships and men in 13; women’s help was more regular and intensive, and women reported non-reciprocation in 10 of their 30 relationships, whereas no man reported it.[75] The authors argue that unpaid care is often treated as women’s natural obligation rather than a gift deserving return; gender norms thereby make labor invisible and undermine reciprocity.[75]

Social reproduction encompasses daily and generational work sustaining people, households, kin, bodies, and reputations; its paid or unpaid tasks include cleaning, cooking, child socialization, care for sick and older people, planning, and emotional labour.[104]

Marxist-feminist criticism also identifies childcare and the labour sustaining intellectual and professional careers as potentially invisible work behind apparently generous gifts.[101]

This framework has direct value for digital exchange. In social media, gendered visibility, self-presentation, sexualized and connective labour, and unequal commercialization show why apparently voluntary attention-giving cannot be treated as a disinterested gift. In cryptocurrency governance, as in F/OSS, the feminist question is whether the labour of care, moderation, visibility, and coordination is recognized as a contribution and who can afford to bear it.[76][77]

In the Russian qualitative sample, women supplied 30 of 43 unpaid task-assistance relationships, or **69.8%**; in **33.3%** of the relationships in which women gave such assistance, they reported non-reciprocation. This quantifies how care and assistance can be naturalized as women’s obligation rather than recognized as a gift requiring return.[75]

### Synthesis

Taken together, feminist critiques show that the classical language of gifts, obligation, and reciprocity remains useful only if it follows labor and power through the whole exchange process. Weiner demonstrates women’s public wealth, authority, and matrilineal claims; Strathern shows that gender lies in transactions and their purposes, including unequal burdens; Rubin identifies patriarchal alliance-making that positions women as exchangeable; and later work shows that care labor can be rendered invisible precisely when it is construed as obligation rather than gift.[6][8][72][75]

The strongest shared conclusion is not that all gendered exchange is exploitation. Women may acquire status, build autonomous networks, and reshape financial relations through giving. But complementarity does not settle the question of power, and Gillison’s critique warns against translating unequal gender relations into benign accommodation.[6][8][73][59]

## Open-source software

### How does open-source software challenge or reinforce classical anthropological models

Judgment — Open-source software is an unusually revealing test of the gift–commodity distinction because people can copy code at near-zero marginal cost, yet producing, reviewing, maintaining, documenting, and governing it consumes time, skill, and infrastructure. It reinforces classical gift theory when contributors place usable code in a shared pool and receive continuing relations, recognition, and access in return. It challenges any simple gift/commodity binary because those relations are organized through copyright licences, formal authority, paid employment, and commercial extraction.

Free and/or open-source software (F/OSS) is a set of production practices and organizations around making and distributing software; practitioners distinguish free as no-price access from free as the licensed freedom to inspect and modify source code. Developers in different locations and organizations share code to develop and refine programs. In Kelty’s gift-economy account, contributors give products of their labour to a pool available to all and, in return, draw on the accumulated surplus created by others. This resembles generalized reciprocity: return is indirect, delayed, and collective rather than a bilateral payment for a discrete object. Contributors may also receive usable fixes, peer credit, reputation, skills, professional networks, enjoyment, and belonging; Lerner and Tirole (2002, The Journal of Industrial Economics, 'Some Simple Economics of Open Source') group career concerns and peer recognition as signaling incentives.[32][42][78][79]

Software code is non-rival: one person can copy or use it without preventing another from doing so, and a contributor can share it without losing their own use of it.[110][42]

This pattern supports the relational side of the classical models discussed in the preceding section. Software transactions do not merely move an alienable thing between independent parties: credit records, review, support, and ongoing collaboration make and sustain relations. Visible source code also lets outsiders assess an individual’s contribution and its difficulty, while author credit is treated as essential in the Open Source Definition. Yet scholarship differs on what explains cooperation. Gift-oriented analysis emphasizes relations, norms, and meaning; political-economic and economic accounts emphasize collective-action problems, modular work, indirect rewards, and coordination mechanisms. These are not mutually exclusive descriptions: a code contribution can help its author solve a personal problem, acquire standing, and bind the author into a collaborative public at once.[3][53][79][78]

Licensing makes the return obligation unusually explicit. F/OSS code remains copyrighted rather than falling into the public domain, but licences grant permissions to use, copy, alter, and redistribute it. Under copyleft arrangements such as the GNU General Public License, distributing a covered derivative work triggers a requirement that it be licensed and distributed on the same terms, keeping its source available to recipients; Kelty characterizes this as an immediate repayment of labour’s fruits to the commons.[79][78][62] BSD/MIT-style permissive licences, by contrast, can allow a derivative to be copyrighted and sold as proprietary software without specifying how users must give back.

Mauss describes gift exchange as socially obligatory: people must give, receive, and repay. Copyleft differs by attaching legal conditions to distribution: a distributor of a covered derivative work must license and distribute it on the same terms, returning the derivative’s source to subsequent recipients. Unlike a conventional commodity sale, however, access to the original code need not require payment to its author.[1][79][78][62]

Open availability also does not mean equal authority. Contributors typically copy a codebase, modify it, and submit a change for review, but maintainers decide whether to merge it into the canonical, or official, version. Authority to merge code can entail authority to set the project’s direction; Linux, for example, has used a cascading chain of trust in which subsystem maintainers select changes before they pass to the lead maintainer. Projects range from centralized leaders to committees using voting or consensus, and informal conventions discourage forks, unauthorized distribution of changes, and removal of contributors’ names. Openness therefore creates participation rights and reputational claims, not an absence of hierarchy.[42][79][78]

Reported Linux kernel review and signoff shares differed from patch-production shares: Red Hat accounted for 18.4%, the Linux Foundation for 13.5%, and Intel for 12.1%; the candidate source does not specify the measurement period.[111]

The commodity side is not outside this system but intertwined with it. Firms can pay programmers to contribute, bundle open and proprietary components, or sell complementary support, customization, integration, hardware, and enhanced services. Lerner and Tirole’s Sendmail case combined open development with a venture-backed firm selling related software and services while employing two engineers to contribute almost full time to the open program. Geiger, Howard, and Irani’s 2021 interview study of 37 F/OSS maintainers in 2019–20 likewise finds that projects initially reliant on volunteers can become commercial technology infrastructure. The same shared code may therefore support public use, paid labour, and commodity sales simultaneously.[62][78][42]

A Linux Foundation report published in 2017 stated that more than 85% of Linux kernel development was paid work; the quoted figure does not specify the period measured.[112]

Reported unpaid contributions to Linux kernel development fell from 14.6% in the 2012 report to 7.7% in the 2016-report period.[111]

Reported Linux kernel patch-production shares included Intel at 12.9%, Red Hat at 8.0%, Linaro at 4.0%, Samsung at 3.9%, SUSE at 3.2%, and IBM at 2.7%; the candidate source does not specify the measurement period.[111]

That hybridity has distributive consequences. Maintainers reported that well-resourced companies sometimes incorporated F/OSS into products they sold without returning money or labour, which they described as free riding and inequitable extraction; corporate reliance could bring users, prestige, connections, and some donations, but also more support demands and moral responsibility for downstream products. Terranova’s free-labour analysis similarly treats voluntary, unwaged digital work as potentially pleasurable and socially meaningful while still central to capitalist value production. The relevant question is therefore not whether code is free, but who gains from its circulation and who bears its recurring costs.[42][18]

A feminist reading shifts attention from the conspicuous code gift to the reproductive infrastructure of collaboration: the work that keeps collective production viable. This follows Annette Weiner (1992, Inalienable Possessions, 'Kula: the Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving')’s correction to male-centred accounts of Trobriand exchange, which had dismissed women’s work as unimportant, by asking whose less-visible work makes exchange possible and who receives credit or commercial returns.[31] Maintainers perform not only bug fixes and security patches but also review, conflict resolution, emotional management, documentation, moderation, fundraising, governance, and community building.

A patch can establish collaborative expectations for its contributor, yet impose review and continuing maintenance obligations on the maintainer who receives it. Maintenance and infrastructure work are often undervalued through gendered and classed assumptions; prior work cited by Geiger, Howard, and Irani describes women’s event-organizing labour as unacknowledged when technical contributions receive greater value, while visible evangelizer-maintainers can accumulate disproportionate personal credit.[19][42]

In a sample of software projects, the minimum number of contributors whose departure could derail a project—its “truck factor”—was one for 57% of projects and two for 25%; the source excerpt does not specify the sample period.[113][114]

Maintainers can receive demands for features, fixes, and documentation from users with whom they have no contract; hostile requests and scrutiny on GitHub can add to stress and burnout.[113][114]

**Open-source software is therefore neither simply a Maussian gift nor simply a commodity transaction.** It preserves gift-like pooled contribution, indirect return, and relationship-making, but licences can turn reciprocity into enforceable obligation, maintainers retain consequential gatekeeping power, and commercial actors can convert shared infrastructure into private revenue. The feminist critique makes the decisive correction: counting only commits, public credit, or free access conceals the unevenly recognized maintenance, organizational, and emotional labour through which this trans-local commons survives.[79][42]

## Social media attention economies

Social media attention economies turn recognition—likes, follows, comments, mentions, shares, and visibility—into a resource that users offer one another. They matter to gift theory because the same interaction can sustain a relationship between users while also producing data, advertising audiences, and revenue for a platform.

Judgment — The useful analytical unit is therefore not an isolated Like or gift purchase, but a *three-sided exchange*: user-to-user recognition, user-to-platform data provision, and platform-to-advertiser commodification. This extends the gift–commodity distinction developed in the preceding section: a gift establishes or renews a social relation and a claim on recognition or future response; a commodity is produced or organized for market exchange. Social media commonly joins both processes rather than replacing one with the other.

### How do social media attention economies challenge or reinforce classical

Sahlins’s proposition that material transactions both express and create social relations applies to attention-based interaction even when no material object moves. Likes, comments, retweets, follows, and other engagement with community-created Dogecoin content have been described as reciprocal modes of giving and baseline stakeholder support; meme-making, sharing, and promotion also amount to cultural labor offered to the community. In this limited but important sense, attention can work as a gift: it acknowledges another person, creates status, and helps make a network of obligation and belonging.[80][3][53]

Studies synthesized by CEPR found that badges, reactions, likes, and comments moderately increased short-run content production, whereas sharing advertising revenue strongly increased creation.[115]

Facebook birthday-gift exchange supplies evidence about digitally mediated birthday gifts, rather than Likes or attention: *specific reciprocity* is returning something to the original giver, whereas *generalized reciprocity* is passing generosity onwards.[81] Kizilcec, Bakshy, Eckles, and Burke’s 2018 CHI study of birthday gifts exchanged by U.S. adults on Facebook in 2013 estimated that recipients gave 1.56 times as many gifts per day in the 30 days after their birthdays as in the 30 days before, excluding the birthday and adjacent days. The authors also report that one third of observed gift exchanges was caused by the sender first receiving a gift.[81]

The difference between the 10.9% behavioral measure and the 73% survey measure is substantive rather than contradictory: the latter includes offline and cross-channel exchange, while only 11% reported direct reciprocity specifically through Facebook. Online gifts thus crossed the online–in-person boundary and could be both direct returns and onward giving.[81]

Yet social-media attention differs from a classical dyadic gift relation because platforms organize who becomes visible. Bucher’s 2012 *New Media & Society* analysis of Facebook’s EdgeRank argues that News Feed software structures the flow of communication and creates a perceived threat of invisibility. Participation is therefore shaped not simply by peers’ voluntary recognition, but by algorithmic power that can make users fear disappearance or obsolescence.[82] **Attention is consequently not just given; it is ranked, distributed, and made scarce by platform infrastructure.**

In a three-month experiment conducted in 2020, reverse-chronological feeds reduced time spent on Facebook by 26% and on Instagram by 13%.[116]

Platform political economy adds a second break with a clean gift–commodity opposition. Fourcade and Kluttz (2020, *Big Data & Society*) call the resulting arrangement *accumulation by gift*: invitations to sign up for a free service, accept a friend request, or share content appear benign and voluntary, but the relation masks an asymmetry through which personal data become a privately appropriated commodity.[44] Nachtwey and Schaupp (2024) similarly call user interactions *asymmetrical gift exchange*: users exchange information with friends while simultaneously supplying the platform with individual and relational data.

The platform first uses these data as assets to optimize its service, then processes and sells the capacity to influence users to third parties—a sequential process they term *secondary commodification*.[17]

This approach differs from Christian Fuchs’s audience-commodity account, which treats users’ content, behavior, and attention as unpaid labor generating data commodities sold to advertisers. Fuchs argues that platform access is not a wage, because it cannot be converted into a universal means of exchange, and that targeted advertising turns user activity into a commodity for sale.[35] Nachtwey and Schaupp contest the automatic classification of all participation as labor: for users, ordinary communication may be social practice rather than activity undertaken for an external productive purpose.

Their account instead locates exploitation in the unequal appropriation of relations among users.[43][17] The live disagreement is therefore whether commercial platforms exploit unpaid labor, extract rent from the social field they control, or do both in different moments of the exchange.[17]

Monetized livestreaming makes this overlap especially visible. On Chinese live-streaming platforms, viewers convert money into platform currency, purchase virtual gifts, and send them to streamers; streamers and platforms split and cash out the proceeds. Virtual gifts can also generate screen effects, attract other viewers’ attention, express emotion, display status, and prompt acknowledgement from streamers.[83] Xu’s 2022 study of 392 prior virtual-gift senders found that pleasure, streamer interaction, group interaction, and support for streamers predicted attitudes toward gifting, while family, friends, streamers, and other viewers shaped perceived norms.[83] This is not a market transaction stripped of social meaning, but neither is it a Maussian gift outside commerce: platforms set the gift values and convert relational display into revenue.[83]

Money’s effect on sociality remains contested. Xu notes arguments that monetary incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation, but interprets the study as evidence that tangible incentives and gratitude-based social incentives coexist. By contrast, Wang, Lee (2026, Cogent Arts and Humanities, 'From influencer donations to streaming drama: digital gift economies and the limits of reciprocity in Mauss, Bataille and Derrida'), and Fu’s qualitative study of 13 streamers found that gift-seeking shaped content and interaction strategies and that gifting could make streamer–viewer relationships feel unequal or one-sided.[84][83][85] (Wang et al., 2019) The stronger conclusion is that payment reorganizes reciprocity: it can fund interaction and recognition, but can also turn ongoing responsiveness into a revenue-seeking performance.[85]

Feminist political economy changes what counts as the labor of attention. Brooke Erin Duffy’s 2017 interview-based study of 56 fashion and lifestyle creators identifies *aspirational labor*: mostly uncompensated independent work undertaken in hope of future income, visibility, or social capital. Creators timed posts, monitored feedback, maintained networks, curated feeds, and kept a coherent personal brand; only a minority attained major success, while many received deferred promises of exposure.[36] Duffy connects this work to historically gendered expectations of community, affect, commodity-based self-expression, domestic labor, reproductive labor, and care labor.[36]

An estimate attributed 90% of YouTube viewership to the top 3% of channels. Leaked Twitch data suggested that reaching median U.S. household income required a top-0.015% earnings rank, and only three of the top 100 Twitch earners were women; the excerpt does not specify the dates of these estimates.[117]

Interviews with 30 social-media creators described extensive time spent interpreting opaque algorithms through peer discussion and experimentation, then adjusting content in response to perceived rewards and penalties.[117]

This feminist critique sharpens the gift comparison: a visible Like, comment, or donation depends on less visible work of self-presentation, relational maintenance, emotional responsiveness, and content production. It also guards against privileging technically coded contribution over communicative labor; Terranova identifies a masculine bias in accounts that celebrate open-source programming while neglecting unpaid chatting, messaging, and website participation.[18] Attention economies thus distribute prestige unequally while making gendered affective work appear as personal passion, authentic self-expression, or a freely given gift.[36]

1. Treat social-media attention as a mixed exchange: analyze peer recognition, platform data capture, and commercial monetization together.
2. Test claims about reciprocity against platform design, algorithmic visibility, and the gendered labor needed to remain visible.
3. Do not infer that voluntary, enjoyable, or socially meaningful participation lies outside commodity production; social relations may be gifts to peers while becoming assets and commodities for platforms. [36][43][17]

Douyu’s reported virtual-gift menu ranged from RMB 0.1 to RMB 500, a **5,000-fold** price range. Platform-set denominations therefore do not merely facilitate interpersonal recognition: they prestructure the monetary scale on which viewers can display support and status.[83]

Among Douyu viewers in the reported figures, the 2.7% classified as high-value paid viewers contributed 80.2% of total virtual-gift value—an average contribution **146.0 times** that of all other paid viewers combined on a per-viewer basis. Monetized recognition is thus highly concentrated even within a practice presented as social gifting.[83]

## Cryptocurrency governance structures

Cryptocurrency governance matters to gift-economy analysis because it makes social coordination appear to be automatic while distributing money, voting rights, and technical authority through protocols. The key question is not whether a blockchain is decentralised in design, but who contributes what, who is rewarded, who can alter the rules, and whose necessary work is not counted.

### How do cryptocurrency governance structures challenge or reinforce classical anthropological

Judgment — A useful application of the gift–commodity distinction treats a gift as a transfer that creates or sustains a social relation and an obligation, whereas a commodity is alienable property exchanged through price, contract, or equivalent calculation. Cryptocurrency governance is neither category in pure form. It joins commodity-like tokens, monetary rewards, and speculation to open-source contribution, shared infrastructure, reputation, and collective obligations to coordinate.

Bitcoin shows why governance cannot be reduced to code. De Filippi and Loveluck (2016, Internet Policy Review, 'The invisible politics of Bitcoin: governance crisis of a decentralised infrastructure')’s 2016 *Internet Policy Review* analysis distinguishes governance *by* the infrastructure—the protocol rules that constrain transactions—from governance *of* the infrastructure—the decisions of developers and other stakeholders who maintain and modify it.[38] Bitcoin is therefore a two-layered socio-technical arrangement: a peer-to-peer payment network and a comparatively small developer community that designs its software.[45] This distinction rules out the claim that a self-executing protocol abolishes human authority; it relocates authority into software maintenance, adoption, and dispute resolution.

Bitcoin accounts can be created without charge, centralized vetting, or real names.[118]

Proof-of-work, the mechanism through which miners compete to validate blocks, reinforces the commodity side of the contrast. A miner that first solves the required computational problem receives bitcoins; Bitcoin’s initial block reward was 50 bitcoins and was designed to halve every four years. The arrangement pays a measurable resource input—computing power—with a tradeable asset, and it encourages competitive investment in hardware and energy rather than an open-ended obligation to reciprocate.[45] De Filippi and Loveluck characterize this as a market-driven system of coordination based on public information, consensus rules, and economic incentives.[45]

That incentive design also changes who can govern. As mining became more difficult and rewards fell, hashing power concentrated in pools; De Filippi and Loveluck reported in 2016 that a few pools, mostly from China, controlled over 75% of the network. They consequently described a network designed for distributed consensus as having become highly centralized through an oligopolistic market structure.[45] Barinaga similarly argues that remunerating validation in bitcoins made specialist infrastructure increasingly costly and concentrated validation and coin ownership among a small number of investors.[86] **Cryptocurrency governance therefore reinforces commodity exchange where contribution, influence, and access to reward track control of scarce capital, computing equipment, or tokens.**

Yet Bitcoin’s monetary incentives do not exhaust its governance. Developers propose changes, full nodes adopt and validate rules, miners enforce rules by producing blocks and selecting transactions, and users exercise choice through wallets, nodes, and networks. A 2023 empirical study of 34 Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash consensus forks describes these as distinct but sometimes overlapping roles.[87] Developers can include a change in a code repository, but cannot compel deployment: activation depends on wider network coordination and user adoption.[87] In Sahlins’s terms, the material transaction is an episode within continuing social relations, while transactions can also establish the relationships through which later exchange becomes possible.[3][53]

Forking makes that relational dimension unusually visible. A hard fork is a non-backwards-compatible software change: node operators must update or leave, and a sufficiently large refusal can preserve the older chain as the main network.[88] Bitcoin’s block-size conflict produced Bitcoin XT in August 2015, with a proposed increase from the one-megabyte limit to eight megabytes if a specified miner-adoption threshold were met.[45] Later conflicts over SegWit and Bitcoin Cash demonstrated that rule changes require coordination among miners, nodes, wallet providers, exchanges, and users, and can impose direct economic costs.

During the 12-hour-and-20-minute interval between BIP148 activation and Bitcoin Cash activation, blocks produced by Bitcoin Cash-compliant miners were rejected by the majority chain, wasting the energy used to produce them.[87]

This is not Maussian reciprocity in the narrow sense of a binding obligation to give, receive, and return a gift. Bitcoin’s protocol instead creates coordinated dependence among participants with divergent interests: miners need user adoption for a coin to retain value, while users depend on miners for network security.[87] Barinaga argues more directly that Bitcoin has no built-in Maussian obligation to give, take, and reciprocate, and that its scarcity-oriented design induces holders to retain assets rather than circulate them.[86] The consequence is a hybrid: coordination and mutual dependence persist, but they are organized around the defence and appreciation of a commodity-like asset rather than around an obligation to pass value onward.

The literature disagrees on whether this arrangement is centrally governed in practice. De Filippi and Loveluck emphasize that anyone may submit code, but only a small number of core developers decide what enters the main branch; they call Bitcoin’s technological development highly centralized and undemocratic despite its open-source form.[45] They also argue that the 2015 block-size conflict revealed a fragile model that relied on the goodwill and aligned interests of a handful of people.[45] By contrast, the 2023 consensus-evolution study finds that miners, full nodes, users, and the economic majority act as decentralising factors because their adoption or refusal determines whether changes persist.[87] The disagreement is substantive: the first account foregrounds agenda-setting and technical expertise; the second foregrounds the distributed capacity to withhold adoption.

A gift-economy reading must therefore distinguish the ability to propose, to implement, and to make a rule socially effective. The Bitcoin Improvement Proposal process opens source code and documentation to public discussion, but core developers assess support and determine whether a change is implemented.[45][40] Technical knowledge is unequally distributed: developers can assess technical soundness, while miners, full nodes, and users may act on uncertain technical and economic expectations.[87] This hierarchy resembles neither equal communal reciprocity nor a simple market sale. It is a relationship of interdependence structured by specialised expertise, reputational authority, and the ability to exit into an alternative implementation.

In Ethereum protocol governance, authors submit Ethereum Improvement Proposals (EIPs) with rationales and technical specifications; stakeholders deliberate in forums, conferences, and developer meetings, client teams implement rough consensus, and nodes and application developers decide whether to adopt changes.[119]

Decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) extend this hybrid arrangement. Hassan and De Filippi (2021, Internet Policy Review, 'Decentralized Autonomous Organization') define a DAO as a blockchain-based system in which people coordinate and govern themselves through self-executing rules on a public blockchain without central control.[39][89] In practice, however, DAO is not a single organisational form: it can refer to crowdfunding platforms, ride-sharing systems, automated companies, financial protocols, or collective decision systems.[89][90] The relevant analytical question is therefore always which rights, assets, labour obligations, and decision powers a particular DAO attaches to its tokens.

Token-weighted governance moves DAOs strongly toward commodity logic. In the common one-token-one-vote model, purchasing or accumulating tokens converts financial ownership into decision-making power.[41][91] Earlier decentralized autonomous corporation proposals made this connection explicit: people could acquire tokenised shares by purchasing them or receiving them for services, and shareholders could receive profits and influence organisational direction.[89] DAO treasuries may receive income from services, token sales, or investment returns, while many DeFi DAOs govern exchanges, lending, interest-bearing liquidity protocols, and collectively held assets.[41] These are not marginal commercial features; they place property, investment, and return at the centre of many DAO governance structures.

DAO membership need not be based on freely transferable governance tokens: share-based membership uses non-freely tradable shares that provide proportionate treasury ownership and voting rights, earned or granted under specified conditions. Open or reputation-based membership permits broad participation while non-transferable standing is earned through peer recognition.[120]

The distributive consequences are clear in the critical literature. Augustin, Eckhardt, and de Jong’s 2023 *Electronic Markets* study reports participant critiques of Compound-style token weighting as plutocratic because wealthier holders possess more voting power; one discussed participation threshold was approximately US$60,000.[41] A 2026 analysis argues that token governance substitutes market principles for public deliberation and can privilege early adopters, venture-capital funds, exchanges, and other large holders.[91] Token voting thus converts capital into political authority, complicating any claim that technical openness produces equal membership.

Some DAO designs try to detach governance from pure token ownership. Colony, for example, uses contribution-based reputation: contributors can be paid in the DAO’s native token and earn domain-specific reputation, which decays over time so newer contributors can gain influence.[41] Optimism separates a Token House, responsible for protocol upgrades and treasury management, from a Citizen House that allocates grants by ecosystem impact.[92] Its Citizen House uses one-person-one-vote and can veto certain proposals, providing a distinct channel beyond token weight.[92] These designs do not restore classical gift exchange, but they make reciprocity, contribution, and public benefit more visible than a pure ownership vote does.

Voluntary contribution nevertheless remains central to cryptocurrency communities. DAOs have been described as relying on voluntary contributions from internal stakeholders to operate, manage, and evolve through collective consultation.[89][90] Dogecoin offers a vivid case: its creators offered an open-source meme-based currency without crowdfunding or an intention to profit, and tipping bots enabled Reddit and Twitter users to send tokens as appreciation for shared content.[80] Participants invest unpaid time in creating, promoting, teaching, and engaging with Dogecoin information; this produces relationships and cultural meaning as well as market attention.[80]

Dogecoin also shows why gift and commodity must not be opposed too sharply. Community discourse values personable interaction, collaboration, trust, and welcome, but Brichta finds an ongoing orientation toward self-interested wealth gain.[80] The practice of “hodling” combines an expectation of future price appreciation with a performance of allegiance through downturns, while repeated social-media exchanges teach newcomers how to manage volatility and reinforce holders’ confidence.[80] The same practice is therefore speculative asset holding and collective moral work. It preserves a Sahlins-like connection between material circulation and social relationship, but it does so around a tradeable token whose value is publicly priced.

A feminist-informed account changes what counts as a contribution. Bitcoin’s protocol can reward verifiable hashing power but cannot reward other forms of contribution.[45][40] In both cryptocurrency and DAO settings, those omitted forms include onboarding, moderation, documentation, conflict resolution, care for newcomers, translation of technical knowledge, and the relational work that enables collective deliberation.

A 2025 qualitative report on DAOs finds that such tasks are often unpaid or underrecognized, while reputation systems privilege visible and readily measured activity over relational and emotional labour.[93] This is the digital counterpart to feminist critiques of exchange accounts that record prestigious, public transfers while overlooking the labour that reproduces the social conditions of exchange.

The distribution of that hidden labour is not socially neutral. The same report describes DAO participation as requiring time to track proposals, calls, and messages, making sustained presence harder for people with care responsibilities, full-time employment, or poor connectivity.[93] It reports male-dominated Web3 spaces, especially in DeFi, where competitiveness, hyper-rationality, and visibility-based status performance can exclude people who do not share those norms.[93] Merk, Lotti, Houde, and Mannan’s 2026 *Internet Policy Review* workers’ inquiry similarly finds that DAO contributors described opaque governance as producing invisible coordination work, with some spending around 12 hours weekly on coordination calls; participants also reported unstable, undercompensated work and self-exploitation.[94]

This labour evidence qualifies the language of voluntary contribution. DAO contributors may join for community, conviviality, values, autonomy, and collaboration rather than money alone.[94] Yet the same study found that 66.7% of its contributors worked as contractors or through informal arrangements, only 4.8% held formal employment contracts, and all reported regulatory uncertainty about their status.[94] All interviewees preferred stablecoin payment; some treated accepting native DAO tokens as volunteering.[94] Community obligation can thus become a means through which organisations obtain low-paid or unpaid work while token holders, core teams, or investors retain disproportionate control.

The strongest conclusion is not that cryptocurrency governance is a new gift economy. It is that digital tokens, protocols, and governance forums combine several exchange logics at once: monetary payment for infrastructure, speculative ownership, unpaid communal contribution, reputational reward, and reciprocal support. Classical models remain useful when they direct attention to obligations and relationship-making; feminist critique is essential because it reveals which forms of contribution protocols measure, compensate, and authorize, and which they treat as freely available social background.[45][89][93][94]

1. Assess each cryptocurrency or DAO separately: identify whether authority follows hashing power, token ownership, reputation, equal membership, delegated voting, or off-chain influence.
2. Count governance, care, moderation, and coordination as labour alongside mining, coding, and financial investment.
3. Treat decentralisation as an empirical distribution of power and participation, not as a property guaranteed by a blockchain’s architecture.

In the DAO worker study, the reported 66.7% contractor-or-informal share was **13.9 times** the 4.8% formal-employment share. This sharp imbalance supports the feminist concern that community participation and governance work may be organized through precarious rather than secure employment relations.[94]

Alternative voting architectures modify, but do not remove, this conversion of assets into authority. One-person-one-vote assigns equal voting power but is rarely used in DAOs; quadratic voting makes additional votes progressively more costly; liquid democracy permits direct voting or changeable, issue-specific delegation; weighted voting can assign influence through reputation or knowledge; rage quitting lets dissatisfied members withdraw their funds during a grace period; and Holographic Consensus couples proposals to a GEN-token prediction market.[95] Cong, Rabetti, Wang, and Yan (2020, Open Encyclopedia of Anthropology, 'Gifts') find that delegation and quadratic voting reduce proposal managers’ abnormal trading returns to levels statistically indistinguishable from zero.[49][96] Reputation-weighted designs make contribution and expertise more visible, whereas prediction markets explicitly make a financial stake part of collective decision-making.[95]

Cong, Rabetti, Wang, and Yan found in 2,988 Snapshot proposals from 216 DAOs during 2020–2024 that mean participation was 6.3% of eligible votes, the mean voting-power Gini coefficient was 0.801, the top decile controlled 76.2% of voting power, the largest voter held 37.5%, and blockvoters holding more than 5% of a proposal’s votes collectively held 75.7%.[96] Their evidence also shows that concentration grew across DAOs’ first 30 proposals and links initial concentration to allocations of approximately 28.8% of eligible-voter tokens to early-stage investors and 20.6% to core-team members.[96] The relevant powerful actors include core-team members, institutional investors, third-party service providers, and key opinion leaders, not simply an undifferentiated community of token holders.[96]

## Comparisons, conflicts and open problems

### Where does each contemporary practice preserve

The comparison treats gift and commodity as intersecting relations within each infrastructure, and identifies the obligations, conversion of value, authority, and labour that each relation organizes.[7][5] A gift relation, in its strong classical sense, joins people through an obligation, recognition, or continuing claim; a commodity relation makes things transferable through price and exchange. Digital systems repeatedly combine both logics, while also adding platform control, intellectual-property rules, data extraction, token ownership, and unpaid maintenance work.[4][7][5]

**The decisive question is not whether a digital object is inherently a gift or a commodity, but what relations, obligations, rights, and unequal labour its circulation creates.** Kirsch (2001, Social Anthropology, 'Property effects. Social networks and compensation claims in Melanesia')’s distinction between a property regime—relations between people and things—and a transaction regime—relations made between people through circulating things—offers a better starting point than a fixed binary.[7] Kopytoff (1986, The Social Life of Things, 'The cultural biography of things: commoditization as process')’s cultural-biography approach similarly treats commoditization as a process: an object can be bought, withdrawn from sale, recirculated as a gift, or retain potential exchange value.[9][7][5]

This approach also corrects a misleading contrast between supposedly gift-based and commodity-based societies. Gregory’s strong ideal type defines non-commodity exchange as the circulation of inalienable things among reciprocally dependent people, whereas commodities are ideally alienable and exchanged by independent parties. Yet critics argue that gifts and commodities coexist within societies, that things can move between statuses, and that even branded commodities can carry producer identity, create loyalty, and invite continuing obligations.[4][7]

#### Open-source software: commons provision with conditional return and uneven maintenance

Open-source software most closely preserves the collaborative side of classical gift exchange. Developers in different locations and organizations share code in order to develop and refine programs, and Free and Open-Source Software, or F/OSS, encompasses the working processes, movements, and organizations built around producing and distributing such software.[32][42] Contributors can place code in a common pool from which they and others receive usable software, resembling generalized reciprocity: a person need not receive a direct return from the particular user who benefits from their contribution.[79]

The analogy to the gift is strongest where contribution establishes affiliation, mutual recognition, and a claim on a shared future. Kelty describes free-software exchange as global in reach but governed through trans-local norms: contributors may receive software made by others, credit may distribute reputational standing, and project conventions regulate forking, attribution, and unauthorized removal of names from a project history.[79] Those norms resemble the reputational and relational dimensions of kula more than a one-time spot-market sale, although they are not identical to the delayed counter-gift described in the section on classical accounts.[64][79]

Open-source licensing makes the comparison more exact and more difficult. Copyright remains in force, but the license grants rights to use, copy, alter, and distribute code. Under the GPL, users who modify GNU software must make source code available and may not impose further licensing restrictions; BSD-style licenses can permit modified software to be redistributed for a fee without requiring its source code to remain free.[79][78] Copyleft therefore creates a legally specified onward-sharing obligation, while permissive licensing allows commercial appropriation without the same required return to the commons.[79][97]

This is not a Maussian obligation in the strict sense. It does not arise because a recipient is personally bound to the original contributor by the spirit of an object or a socially enforceable duty to reciprocate that person. Rather, it is a condition attached to a legal right of reuse. **Open-source licenses convert some desired forms of reciprocity into enforceable property rules, while leaving other forms—maintenance, support, mentoring, documentation, and funding—to voluntary or organizational arrangements.**[86][78][42]

Open-source contribution also complicates the opposition because contributors frequently gain private benefits. Lerner and Tirole (2002, The Journal of Industrial Economics, 'Some Simple Economics of Open Source') identify direct user benefits, career concerns, peer recognition, and the prospect of improved human capital and social networks as important motivations.[32] Their examples show contributors fixing problems that affect their own work, while skilled Apache programmers could gain employment and corporate contributors might work on Apache as part of paid assignments.[32][78] A contribution can thus be useful to its author, valuable to a prospective employer, and freely reusable by a wider public at the same time.

Commercial firms do not simply stand outside this commons. Companies contribute paid programmer time and in-house code to open-source communities; firms may sell support, customization, integration, hardware, or fuller-featured products around freely downloadable code.[62] Open-source definitions also permit proprietary code to be bundled with open-source programs under some licenses.[78] The result is a hybrid arrangement: shared code can be a common resource, an input into paid work, and infrastructure from which firms derive private revenue.

The feminist test makes visible the labour hidden by the language of voluntary contribution. Geiger, Howard, and Irani’s 2021 interview study of 37 F/OSS maintainers found that code review, coordination, conflict resolution, documentation, promotion, infrastructure management, and governance are necessary to sustain projects.[42] Contributors may experience a pull request as a generous donation of time and intellectual property, but maintainers can experience it as an obligation to review code and bear indefinite maintenance costs.[42] Automation shifts rather than removes labour because tests, continuous-integration systems, and self-hosted infrastructure themselves require building and upkeep.[42]

Gendered labour appears particularly clearly in event organization and community work. Geiger and colleagues report prior research showing women’s organizational labour in F/OSS events often goes unacknowledged because technical contributions receive greater value; their participants also reflected on this pattern.[42] Their respondent pool was predominantly male among those completing the demographic survey—81% men/male and 19% women/female—which describes that study’s respondents rather than F/OSS as a whole.[42] The comparison with feminist anthropology is therefore not that open-source reproduces any particular Melanesian gender order, but that the prestige attached to visible technical gifts can obscure the relational and reproductive work enabling them.

Corporate dependence intensifies this problem. Maintainers reported that well-resourced organizations could benefit from their software without returning labour or resources, creating what the authors call free riding and inequitable extraction when company-derived wealth is not shared with contributors.[42] Projects that become widely used infrastructure may gain status, users, donations, and connections, but they also acquire moral responsibility for downstream products, demanding users, and growing backlogs.[42] Funding itself creates governance questions: one maintainer reported receiving corporate contributions through OpenCollective without a settled mechanism for deciding who should receive funds or how they should be spent.[42]

Open-source governance is consequently neither a pure commons nor a flat gift circle. Contributors can fork and propose changes, but maintainers decide whether patches enter a canonical version, and authority to merge code can become authority to define a project’s vision.[42] Funding may also buy formal influence: Geiger and colleagues report that the Linux Foundation’s Platinum membership cost US$500,000 annually and entitled Platinum members to choose about 80% of its board.[42] **Open-source software preserves shared provisioning and indirect reciprocity, but its sustainability turns on who absorbs maintenance, who receives recognition, and whether commercial beneficiaries return resources.**[79][42]

#### Social-media attention: recognition circulates, while platforms commodify its traces

Social-media attention preserves a recognizable gift-like dimension when users give one another visibility, acknowledgement, endorsement, or support. Romele and Severo treat Facebook Likes and Twitter Mentions as forms of online gift circulation and define gift exchange primarily through mutual recognition rather than through the movement of goods.[46] In this sense, a Like can be a small relational act: it says that another person’s post has been noticed and can make the recipient more visible to others.

Yet attention is not a freely circulating equivalent of a shell valuable. Facebook’s News Feed was structured through EdgeRank, and Bucher’s analysis argues that algorithmic processes construct a regime of visibility that produces a perceived threat of invisibility among users.[82] Attention is therefore allocated through both social action and platform architecture. A user can give a Like, but the platform decides which interactions are rendered prominent, aggregated, ranked, recommended, or obscured.

The phrase Like economy names this overlap of relational and market exchange. Romele and Severo argue that online gift exchange and market economy interfere with one another daily, rather than inhabiting separate domains.[46] The 2026 account of digital gifting similarly treats donations, subscriptions, and symbolic attention as practices through which platform capitalism retains gift-exchange dynamics, while influencer culture turns Maussian relations toward visibility and prestige.[84] Recognition can create relational bonds, but it can also be converted into follower counts, influencer status, advertising opportunities, and platform revenue.

A political-economy account makes the commodity side explicit. Fuchs argues that advertising-funded social-media platforms provide communication access without selling access or content directly as commodities, while commodifying users’ data and selling access to advertising audiences.[35] Users’ activity generates behavioral data, content, and attention that platforms can process into commercially valuable audience categories.

Projected worldwide social-media advertising sales were US$338.75 billion in 2026; this is an advertising-revenue estimate, not a measure of creator earnings or the value of attention itself.[121]

In Fuchs’s account, watching, reading, listening, posting, and using social media produce the Internet prosumer commodity: users help create the audience and user profiles sold to advertisers.[35]

Nachtwey and Schaupp offer a more directly anthropological synthesis. Their 2024 article treats platform interactions as asymmetrical gift exchange among users while locating those interactions within a field of structural inequality between users and platform companies.[17][43] Platform terms require users to share data both with other users and with the company; the company first uses data to optimize services, then conducts secondary commodification, and finally sells the capacity to influence users to third parties.[43] Their central claim is that the user-to-user gift relation is not abolished by commercialization, but is made a condition of platform accumulation.

This differs from a direct commodity sale. A person does not normally receive money for a Like, comment, or photograph; the platform’s free service is also not easily treated as a wage because users cannot convert access into general purchasing power.[35] Nor is it simply accurate to call every interaction intentional value-producing labour.

Nachtwey and Schaupp caution that participants may not understand ordinary interaction as work undertaken to create utility outside the activity itself.[43] The dispute is therefore not whether platforms profit from data and attention—they do under advertising models—but whether users’ everyday sociality should analytically be equated with wage labour.

The issue is sharpened by feminist scholarship on digital self-presentation. Duffy argues that ordinary acts such as liking a brand post, reviewing a product, or updating a profile can create value for media and marketing institutions.[36] Her work on women’s social-media projects finds that a small number obtain lucrative careers while many others’ passion projects become unpaid work for corporate brands; postfeminist promises of visibility and individual expression present self-promotion as financial empowerment while preserving a close association between consumption and femininity.[37][36] This is a contemporary instance of the question raised by feminist critiques of exchange: who performs the work through which relations and value are reproduced, and who receives the prestige or revenue?

Paid virtual gifting in live streaming makes the hybrid structure unusually visible. On Chinese platforms, viewers exchange money for platform currency, purchase virtual gifts, and send them to streamers; the streamer and platform divide and cash out the proceeds.[83] The gift can communicate gratitude, intimacy, fandom, or public recognition, while also constituting a monetized revenue stream.

On Douyu, only 4% of monthly active viewers had previously sent paid virtual gifts, while 2.7% of high-value paid viewers contributed 80.2% of total virtual-gift value.[83] A small group of spenders thus disproportionately produces both monetary value and publicly visible status.

The social mechanics remain consequential. Xu’s 2022 study finds that offline and online subjective norms together explained 23% of variance in live-streaming gift intention, compared with 16% for attitudes and 13% for perceived behavioral control.[83] The authors argue that virtual gifting can combine tangible and intangible incentives rather than displacing intrinsic motivation.[83] **Social-media attention complicates rather than breaks the gift–commodity distinction: users give recognition and sometimes reciprocate, but algorithms, monetization, and data extraction turn that circulation into a managed commercial infrastructure.**[46][82][43][83]

#### Cryptocurrency governance: collective contribution inside monetary and political systems

Cryptocurrency systems contain gift-like practices, but their central operations are usually not gifts. Bitcoin mining rewards computational contribution with newly created bitcoins, and proof-of-work makes participation a competitive, protocol-defined incentive system.[45][40] Barinaga therefore characterizes Bitcoin as money imagined, designed, and governed as a commodity: an asset without a corresponding liability and without an internal Maussian obligation to give, receive, and return.[86] In this respect Bitcoin breaks most sharply with the classical gift model: holding is permitted, exchange is mediated by a tradable asset, and no partner-specific obligation requires the holder to pass value onward.

The system nevertheless depends on community, trust, and contribution. Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz describe Bitcoin as a promise underwritten by algorithm and peer-to-peer network, while emphasizing that trust in code does not erase the community that bestows such trust.[98] Bitcoin’s promise of trustlessness thus relocates trust to code, developers, validators, users, and the institutions that support their coordination. Barinaga similarly argues that algorithmic money shifts political decisions and trust from bankers to developers and code rather than eliminating them.[86]

Dogecoin illustrates the overlap most vividly. Brichta’s 2023 study describes early Dogecoin tipping as gift-like because the coin then had only a fraction-of-a-cent value and could convey more social than economic value.[80] Memes, advice, teaching, remixes, promotion, and engagement with other users’ content are treated as cultural labour that can amount to gifting.[80] Likes, comments, retweets, and follows provide reciprocal support, while they also publicize a financial asset and help circulate its cultural meaning.[80]

Dogecoin does not therefore form an alternative outside markets. Participants value personable interaction, collaboration, and trust over profit-driven economics, but Brichta finds an enduring orientation toward self-interested wealth gain.[80] Hodling means both holding through price declines in expectation of appreciation and performing allegiance to the community; spending may be praised because it demonstrates currency legitimacy, while selling for profit can be treated as betrayal.[80] The same act can thus be economically speculative, morally charged, and socially performative.

The charity cases show this double articulation especially well. The Dogecoin Foundation’s partnership with #TEAMSEAS designated Dogecoin an official donation payment method, presenting donations as both altruistic action and proof of the community’s transactional capacity.[80] Its 2014 Charity Water sponsorship raised more than US$30,000 for clean-water infrastructure.[80] Such cases preserve giving for collective purposes, but they also publicize adoption and support the asset’s legitimacy.

Influence is uneven despite the decentralized imagery. Brichta describes influential figures as validating nodes or filters who shape participants’ economic rationality; these include both highly active community members and public figures with pre-existing influence.[80] A decentralized network of users can therefore co-create a cryptocurrency’s meaning while a smaller set of actors disproportionately directs its public valuation.[80] This resembles neither egalitarian generalized reciprocity nor a neutral market of independent individuals.

Cryptocurrency governance adds a further complication: contributions have different forms and are recognized differently. In Bitcoin, developers propose changes, full nodes validate blocks and adopt rules, miners enforce rules through block production, and users choose wallets and networks.[87] Participation is distributed, but technical competence is not: the 2023 consensus-evolution study notes that developers are best placed to assess a proposal’s technical soundness, while other participants must judge its implications under greater uncertainty.[87] Governance is thus an exchange of code, computational resources, adoption, legitimacy, and economic commitment—not merely an exchange of coins.

Bitcoin’s governance also shows why open-source access does not guarantee equal power. Anyone may submit a code change, but core developers decide which changes enter the main branch.[45] The protocol governs transactions, but governance of the protocol requires developers, miners, node operators, wallet providers, exchanges, and users to coordinate around changes.[45][88] The 2015 block-size conflict demonstrated that technical choices distribute social power: arguments over a 1-megabyte limit concerned scaling, security, inclusion, and decentralization, while disagreement could produce incompatible chains.[45]

The evidence on Bitcoin’s decentralization is contested rather than uniform. De Filippi and Loveluck (2016, Internet Policy Review, 'The invisible politics of Bitcoin: governance crisis of a decentralised infrastructure')’s 2016 account described mining pools controlling over 75% of network hashing power at the time and concluded that Bitcoin had become highly centralized through an oligopolistic market structure.[38][45] By contrast, the later consensus-evolution study argues that miners, full nodes, and users can act as decentralizing factors because activation depends on coordinated adoption and user support, not merely developer or pool preference.[87] These are not directly contradictory claims: one concerns concentration of important operational resources; the other concerns the distributed vetoes and adoption choices involved in permanent rule change.

Decentralized autonomous organizations, or DAOs, sharpen the same tension. A DAO is a blockchain-based system in which people coordinate and govern themselves through self-executing rules on a public blockchain, independently of central control.[89][90] They are often presented as leaderless organizations in which members vote through governance tokens, creating community ownership, transparency, and global coordination.[93] Yet token-weighted voting assigns political power according to financial holdings under a one-token, one-vote principle.[91]

This makes DAO governance a direct fusion of market and political authority. Token ownership can give wealthy holders greater voting power, and an Electronic Markets study records community criticism of such arrangements as plutocratic.[41] A 2026 analysis argues that token governance substitutes market principles for public deliberation and can privilege early adopters, venture-capital funds, exchanges, developers, validators, and large token holders.[91] This is a commodity logic of stake and asset ownership operating inside a rhetoric of collective governance.

DAO work also requires a feminist labour analysis. Contributors in one workers’ inquiry often joined for community, collaboration, values, and experimental forms of organization rather than primarily for money.[94] However, the same study found no strong evidence of better labour conditions: participants described DAO work as unstable, undercompensated, and unpredictable, sometimes amounting to self-exploitation.[94] Only 4.8% of contributors in that study held formal employment contracts, while 66.7% typically worked as contractors or through informal arrangements.[94]

The often-unpaid tasks are precisely those a narrow focus on code or token holdings fails to value. DAO contributors report that moderation, onboarding, documentation, conflict resolution, and ongoing presence are necessary but underrecognized; newcomers may be expected to work for weeks or months before receiving recognition or compensation.[93] Reputation systems tend to reward public and measurable activity while neglecting relational and emotional labour, and care responsibilities, full-time employment, or poor connectivity make continual participation harder.[93] The gendered consequences are not incidental: DAO spaces remain male-dominated and often reward competitiveness, hyper-rationality, and visibility-based status performance.[93]

Cryptocurrency governance therefore makes the overlap of gift, commodity, and political power most visible: solidarity and unpaid contribution can sustain communities, while tokens, mining rewards, speculative assets, and unequal access determine whose contributions become authoritative.[80][40][91][93]

### Where do accounts of gift versus commodity exchange conflict?

The first conflict concerns what makes a return obligatory. Mauss’s formulation links giving, receiving, and returning to the gift’s continuing attachment to the giver; his controversial account of hau treats the gift as carrying a spiritual force that tends toward return.[6][67] Malinowski rejected this explanation for Trobriand exchange and instead emphasized reciprocal expectation and sanction: a person returns because the other party can stop giving and sever the relationship.[6] Both accounts reject the image of a gift as merely free and unilateral, but they disagree about whether obligation resides in a spiritually attached thing or in the social consequences of non-reciprocation.

Weiner (1992, Inalienable Possessions, 'Kula: the Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving') provides a third account.[31] Her principle of keeping-while-giving holds that reciprocity is motivated not only by circulation but also by the desire to retain identity-bearing possessions that mark the distinction of a person or group.[6] This directs attention to what does not circulate: access to land, reputation, political authority, proprietary code, token holdings, or the capacity to decide whose contribution counts. In digital settings, the same question becomes: what assets or powers are retained while apparently open contributions circulate?

The hau explanation itself remains disputed. Firth argued that Mauss misread hau, Lévi-Strauss called the spirit-of-the-gift explanation a mystification, and Parry argued that spirit and reciprocity do not cohere as a single model.[6] Hénaff adds that return in the Māori account can move through third parties in a chain rather than requiring the original object to return materially to its first giver.[14] The dispute matters for digital research because generalized reciprocity—giving onward rather than back—may be central in commons, social networks, and community currencies.

The second conflict concerns whether calculation disqualifies a relation from being a gift. Bourdieu’s analysis emphasizes the temporal interval between gift and counter-gift, while Appadurai-oriented critiques argue that gift exchange can involve self-interested calculation.[4] Mauss himself treats gifts as simultaneously obligatory and free, interested and disinterested.[6][67] It is therefore misleading to identify gifts with pure generosity and commodities with calculation alone. Open-source contributors may seek reputation and jobs; stream viewers may seek recognition; token holders may seek price appreciation while also sustaining a community.

The third conflict concerns whether gifts and commodities are opposite categories or changing states. Gregory’s ideal types make gift exchange relational and inalienable, and commodity exchange impersonal and alienable.[29][4] Kopytoff, Appadurai-oriented approaches, and Kirsch’s property/transaction distinction instead focus on conversion, changing exchange spheres, and the relations formed through circulation.[7][5] A commodity can become a meaningful gift, as with consumer goods transformed through ritual; a gift can be commoditized; and some things are exchanged in restricted spheres but not sold.[10][5]

This processual view fits digital cases better than a fixed binary. A freely shared code contribution may be incorporated into proprietary services; a Like can be an interpersonal acknowledgement and a data point; a virtual gift can be a public display of gratitude and platform revenue; a token can be a community badge, voting right, payment method, and speculative asset.[62][43][83][80] The classification depends on the surrounding rights, forms of recognition, constraints on circulation, and pathways through which value is captured.

Feminist anthropology introduces a fourth conflict: whether exchange analysis describes relations neutrally or obscures domination. Rubin’s traffic-in-women argument shows that exchange can forge bonds among men by positioning women as objects of exchange.[72] Strathern (1988, no venue recorded, 'The Gender of the GiftProblems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia') rejects the assumption that exchange is gender-neutral and argues that the gender of a gift lies in how an item is transacted and toward what ends, including the burdens of carrying transactions through.[13][8] Josephides further argues that theories of gifts can overlook women’s role in producing circulating wealth and the alienation of women’s domestic production into gifts controlled by men.[29]

There are disagreements within feminist anthropology as well. Strathern’s dividual account has been praised as a non-unitary model of embodiment, but Gillison (2013, Oceania, 'The ‘Dividual Androgyne’ and Me: a Personal Affair') and Lipset criticize it for erasing affect, agency, and identity.[59] Gillison argues that Strathern misread Gimi exchange material rooted in anti-female ideology as benign accommodation between the sexes.[59] This disagreement cautions against treating any account of gendered reciprocity as automatically emancipatory merely because it rejects Western individualism.

The digital cases make this feminist challenge practical. F/OSS can celebrate technical contribution while obscuring gendered organizing work; social media can turn feminized self-expression into commercial value; DAOs can praise voluntary participation while leaving moderation, onboarding, and care-intensive community work unpaid or invisible.[42][36][93] The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not only the visible transfer—code, attention, token, or virtual gift—but the labour that maintains the relation and the authority that allocates recognition and return.

**The principal conflict is not gift against commodity as two sealed worlds, but whether exchange should be explained by reciprocal obligation, strategic calculation, retained property, changing classifications, or gendered relations of power.** The contemporary cases require all of these questions because they join relational circulation to commercial accumulation rather than choosing one logic over the other.[6][4][5][8][43]

A further distinction is needed alongside gift and commodity: **sharing**. Widlok (2013) defines sharing as a culturally instituted mode of transfer in its own right, neither a preliminary gift nor a market transaction. It extends access to a valued good and can occur without a received or expected return; its intrinsic good takes precedence over the future-obligation goals that characterize gift-giving. Market exchange, reciprocal gift exchange, and sharing coexist, each with its own morality and values.[47]

This distinction changes the digital comparison. Open access to code can be sharing where it enlarges the circle able to use a resource, rather than a gift intended to establish a personal debt. Social-media and crypto participation can also include demands for provision, visibility, or support that are neither voluntary charity nor bilateral return. Widlok identifies demand sharing, initiated by the taker, as sharing’s prototype: it rests on moral role relations or co-presence, decouples giving from receiving, and need not involve charity or even an explicit request.[47]

Maussian scholarship also supplies a less purified account of gifts than the conventional opposition suggests. Vandenberghe’s account of Mauss identifies obligation and freedom, egoism and altruism, and peace and war as intertwined polarities; Godbout and Caillé define a gift as provision without a guaranteed return intended to create, strengthen, or recreate a social bond. On this approach, interest and generosity coexist, and goods circulate in the service of relationships rather than solely for use value or price.[99]

Widlok’s distinction makes the open-source case more precise: broad access to usable code can be analyzed as sharing, while contribution, attribution, and copyleft obligations can establish reciprocal gift-like and legally enforceable relations. Neither form is reducible to a commodity sale. Sharing also depends on common ground in relatedness, communication, and presence; it breaks down as that common ground disappears, although the critical threshold is produced by a changing combination of conditions.[47]

### What open problems remain in applying gift-economy scholarship to these

Open-source research must distinguish between reciprocal commons provision and extraction from unpaid infrastructure labour. The key empirical question is whether the people and firms that benefit from a project return code, money, review labour, documentation, governance capacity, or support proportionate to the burdens they create. Geiger, Howard, and Irani show that rapid growth can create scalar debt: a project’s user base expands faster than its ability to maintain itself, leaving maintainers with backlogs, overwork, and burnout.[42] Corporate users can create prestige and donations, but also extract value without contributing resources or labour in return.[42]

What would settle this question is comparative observation across project types rather than treating F/OSS as one homogeneous field. The relevant design would compare volunteer infrastructure projects, small single-user public projects, foundation-governed projects, and fully corporate-developed open source; track commits, code review, documentation, issue triage, donations, paid employment, corporate use, maintainer workload, and withdrawal from projects over time. Geiger and colleagues specifically propose supplementing interviews with participant observation, diary studies, and trace-data analysis.[42] Such work would show whether recognition and indirect benefit sustain reciprocity or merely rationalize a persistent transfer of maintenance costs downward.

A second open problem is how to separate social-media reciprocity, social learning, platform affordances, and commercial appropriation. Kizilcec, Bakshy, Eckles, and Burke’s CHI 2018 study of Facebook birthday gifts offers evidence that receiving a gift changes later giving: recipients gave 1.56 times as many gifts per day in the 30-day period after their birthday as in the 30-day period before it, excluding the birthday and adjacent days.[81] Yet direct return in Facebook records accounted for only 10.9% of gifts given by prior recipients, while 73% of surveyed givers reported having previously received a gift from the recipient when offline exchanges were included.[81] The result suggests both direct and generalized reciprocity, but it does not reduce one to the other.

The same study found that 87% of surveyed Facebook gift givers expected a future gift from the recipient, indicating an expectation of continuing reciprocity.[81] It also found that gifts could be substitutes for offline giving and incremental exchanges enabled by platform convenience: 58% said they would likely have given the same person a gift without Facebook, while 46% said non-Facebook giving would have been at least somewhat difficult.[81] Online infrastructure can therefore expand the occasions and ease of exchange rather than merely digitizing a pre-existing practice.

But the mechanisms remain unresolved because reciprocity and social learning could not be disentangled.[81] Platform architecture also matters: Facebook’s reciprocally endorsed friend network differs from Twitter’s follower structure, and the authors caution that relational inducements and social learning may therefore operate differently.[81] A stronger design would compare platforms with mutual-friend, follower, livestream, and private-messaging structures; record price, visibility, algorithmic ranking, audience size, and off-platform ties; and separately measure receiving, observing, and being prompted to give. This would identify whether later action follows indebtedness, imitation, public norm enforcement, algorithmic exposure, or a combination.

The feminist version of this problem asks who can afford to remain visible. Duffy’s analysis of gendered aspirational work shows that the promise of monetizing self-expression is distributed unevenly.[37][36] The relevant research must therefore connect gender, race, class, care responsibilities, disability, platform visibility, and monetization rather than treating attention as a universally available gift.

**A Like economy cannot be understood only by counting attention exchanges; it must track who produces visibility, whose visibility is algorithmically rewarded, and who captures the resulting value.**[82][36][43][35]

Cryptocurrency governance raises a third open problem: whether decentralization distributes effective authority or merely disperses technical participation around concentrated power. Bitcoin governance includes multiple roles and possible veto points, but code development, mining capacity, infrastructure ownership, technical expertise, and market adoption do not carry equal weight.[87][88] TheDAO and Ethereum’s subsequent fork further demonstrate that code cannot fully settle political disputes: participants disagreed over whether code should be treated as law or overridden through collective judgment after a major exploit.[41][91]

The question must be investigated separately at technical, protocol, and social-institutional levels. Technical governance concerns validator selection, block production, and security; protocol governance concerns upgrades and parameter changes; social governance concerns who decides, how dissent is handled, and how legitimacy is produced.[91] Collapsing these levels into a single claim that a network is decentralized conceals the difference between open access to read or submit transactions, formal voting rights, control over repositories, ownership of tokens, and practical ability to coordinate a fork.[88][91]

Token-based systems require particular scrutiny because they directly join property and political voice. The 2026 democratic-innovation analysis reports that participation rates in DAO voting are often below 10% of eligible wallets and that a small minority of token holders accounts for most votes, while token distribution can be concentrated among early adopters, venture-capital funds, and exchanges.[91] These reported patterns vary across ecosystems, but they show why formal accessibility is not equivalent to equal decision-making power.[91] The comparison to gift exchange should consequently ask whether token holders owe continuing responsibilities to non-holding contributors, users, and affected communities—or whether governance rights follow ownership alone.

The labour evidence gives this question material force. DAO contributors can join for autonomy, mission, community, and collaboration, but actual influence often depends on proximity to core teams or early token holders.[94] Contributors may be underpaid or expected to prove loyalty through unpaid work, while care responsibilities and limited time make sustained participation harder.[94][93] The common language of community can thus describe a genuine moral commitment and conceal a transfer of risk from asset owners to precarious contributors.

What would settle decentralization claims is cross-chain, longitudinal research linking distributions of token holdings, hash power, validator control, developer authority, treasury access, proposal authorship, voter turnout, forum participation, fork outcomes, and contributor compensation. It should distinguish Bitcoin-like off-chain governance, token-voting DAOs, foundation-coordinated networks, and systems with emergency controls rather than treating all blockchain governance as one model. Bitcoin differs from Ethereum in governance history and institutional organization, and DAOs vary between algorithmic and participatory arrangements.[88][100] The outcome of interest is not simply whether a vote occurs, but whether people who perform indispensable work can propose, understand, contest, refuse, and alter collective rules.

Across all three practices, the open methodological problem is how to connect circulation to production and reproduction. A code contribution, a post, a comment, a token vote, or an act of moderation is never only a transfer; it depends on prior time, training, care, infrastructure, legal rights, and the capacity to remain present. Strathern’s insight is especially useful here: gender is not read directly from an object, because the relevant question is how an item or activity is transacted and to what ends.[8] Feminist analysis therefore does not add gender as a demographic variable after classifying an exchange; it changes the classification by identifying whose labour makes it possible and whose obligations remain unrecognized.

### Synthesis

Open-source software most closely resembles shared provisioning, but licenses, career rewards, corporate dependence, and invisible maintenance prevent its reduction to a Maussian gift. Social-media attention preserves recognition and reciprocal circulation, yet platforms algorithmically allocate visibility and commodify data generated through interaction. Cryptocurrency communities can sustain gifting, solidarity, and unpaid cultural work, while mining, tokens, speculation, and unequal governance power embed those practices in monetary markets.[79][42][43][83][80][91]

The strongest conclusion is that gift and commodity are not rival labels for a whole digital system. They are intersecting relations within the same infrastructure. Feminist critique identifies the empirical test that matters across cases: trace who gives, who must return, who may refuse, who performs maintenance and care, and who converts collective participation into income, property, prestige, or governance power.[8][42][93][94]

### Open questions

The relative distribution of unpaid maintenance, moderation, care, and governance labor by gender, class, race, region, and technical status remains open across open-source projects, platforms, and cryptocurrency communities. So does the extent to which particular licensing regimes, platform designs, and token-voting rules create durable reciprocal obligations rather than merely extractible participation. Comparative ethnography that follows contributions, ownership claims, compensation, decision-making power, and value capture across specific digital communities would settle these questions more precisely.
