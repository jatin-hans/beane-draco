# Voice-First Medication Adherence in Older Adults with Mild Cognitive Impairment

## Executive summary

For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment, conversational medication support is most defensible when it gives a timely cue, accepts several meaningful responses, records unresolved status, and provides a simple path to retry or involve a caregiver; this can support confidence and reminder-service engagement, but it has not been shown to cause sustained medication adherence.

The closest direct study, the 20-week MATCHA deployment with seven MCI member–caregiver dyads, found 84 responses to 476 medication check-ins, about 18%, alongside qualitative reports of greater confidence, so a conversational check-in should not be treated as proof that a dose was taken.[1][2] Amazon Alexa documents scheduled medication reminders and medication-due queries, Google Assistant-related work supports health-information and tracking conversations, and Catalia Health’s Mabu is a specialized relational care-support system, but they do not supply comparable longitudinal adherence, task-completion, or confidence metrics for the specified 65+ MCI population.[3][4][5][6] The practical design choice is therefore an adaptive, brief default prompt with explicit options such as taken, taking now, remind later, do not remember, and check pillbox, followed by a clear repair or escalation route rather than a single binary confirmation.[1] Voice, visual, and haptic feedback should be treated as complementary accessibility supports, not as a demonstrated package for maintaining engagement beyond 90 days in this population.

## The question and its population

Patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) are the target population for this review. MCI is not normal ageing, and it is not dementia: it is measurable cognitive decline with minimal impairment of instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), such as managing medicines, although those tasks may require more effort or compensatory support. The distinction matters because voice interaction can be accessible and burdensome at the same time: it removes typing and screen-navigation demands, but asks the user to remember a conversational goal, interpret a response, and repair misunderstandings.[7][1]

Judgment — The core interpretive rule for this review is the **outcome-separation rule**: receiving or answering a reminder, using a health-tracking service, and taking a medication as prescribed are related events but distinct outcomes. A voice-first system can support all three, but a result for one cannot stand in for a result for another.

### Which findings concern patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment

MCI describes cognitive impairment above expected age-related change while independence is mostly retained. The American Academy of Neurology guideline defines MCI as cognitive impairment with minimal impairment of IADLs, and it distinguishes MCI from dementia by whether functional impairment limits independent daily life. The American Academy of Neurology guideline notes that studies used varying definitions of MCI and related terms, including cognitively impaired, no dementia (CIND), because ascertainment methods, neuropsychological measures, thresholds, and required cognitive deficits differed; accordingly, a group labelled cognitively impaired, older, or MoCA-low is not automatically the same clinical population as patients aged 65+ with diagnosed MCI.[7]

MCI also has varied cognitive profiles. Amnestic MCI primarily affects learning and remembering new information, whereas non-amnestic MCI may primarily affect attention, planning, language, or visuospatial ability. The MATCHA medication-management study describes MCI as an intermediate stage between expected ageing and more substantial dementia-related change, with possible memory loss, language problems, and attentional difficulty. These differences matter for voice interaction because a user may have trouble remembering a dose, formulating a request, maintaining conversational context, or judging whether a repair attempt worked.[8][9][1]

The closest medication-management deployment is Mathur et al.’s 2022 ASSETS paper, *A Collaborative Approach to Support Medication Management in Older Adults with Mild Cognitive Impairment Using Conversational Assistants*. The study recruited seven older-adult–caregiver dyads through a US clinical programme requiring the member participant to have received a neurologist’s MCI diagnosis within the previous year. The member participants’ mean age was 76.4 years; caregivers averaged 65.4 years; and the listed member ages were 62.6, 70.8, 74.3, 75.4, 75.4, 78, and 85.3 years, with six of seven aged 65 or older.[2][1]

That deployment is directly relevant to MCI and home medication routines, but its enrolled group was not exclusively 65+. It also involved co-resident caregivers: MATCHA ran on a shared Google Home device, caregivers were often present, and some contributed to conversations even though the system had no separate caregiver dialogue flow. Thus, its findings concern medication management within a dyadic household arrangement rather than unassisted, individual patient use.[1]

A separate set of Alexa studies contains age-eligible MCI subgroups but investigates cognitive assessment rather than healthcare application use. Liang and colleagues’ *Evaluating Voice-Assistant Commands for Dementia Detection* examined 40 adults aged 65 or older: 18 healthy controls and 22 participants classified as MCI by a MoCA score below 26. Its purpose was to assess whether acoustic and transcript features of Alexa commands could distinguish cognitive groups, not whether medication reminders or health-tracking conversations changed medication-taking.[10]

Kurtz and colleagues’ *Early Detection of Cognitive Decline Using Voice Assistant Commands* likewise recruited 90 older adults to interact with an Alexa Echo: 30 healthy controls, 30 people classified as MCI, and 30 people with dementia. Its MCI group included 9 people aged 65 to under 70, 14 aged 70 to under 75, 6 aged 75 to under 80, and 1 aged 80 or older. For distinguishing MCI from healthy controls, the Random Forest model trained on HuBERT acoustic features achieved the study’s highest accuracy, 62.8%. The study gives useful information about the demands of producing spoken commands, but its outcomes are cognitive-status classification rather than medication adherence, reminder completion, or sustained use of a healthcare feature.[11]

The 18-month voice-assistant speech-collection study by Ezzine and colleagues also included age-eligible participants with MCI. It involved 35 participants aged over 65, including 20 identified as MCI and 15 healthy controls; their mean age was 72 years (SD 5.1). They completed seven virtual speech sessions at roughly three-month intervals from 2022 to 2024, producing 243 sessions; one participant withdrew from the final two sessions because of health problems. Those sessions addressed 18 cognitive-task questions across naming, memory, attention, sentence repetition, verbal fluency, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation, so repeat participation beyond 90 days measures research-session completion rather than continuing use of a medication application.[12]

The strongest age-and-MCI match outside voice assistants is Kamimura, Ishiwata, and Inoue’s 2012 *Medication Reminder Device for the Elderly Patients With Mild Cognitive Impairment*. Eligibility required home-dwelling adults aged 65 or older who had missed doses, overdosed, or required verbal reminders at least once in the preceding week. The 18 participants had a mean age of 81.2 years (SD 6.2), ranging from 70 to 93; 10 had Clinical Dementia Rating scores of 0.5 and 8 had scores of 1; Mini Mental State Examination scores were 21–26 for 13 participants and 14–21 for 5. Seventeen participants (94.4%) lived alone; family members living separately filled the device for 10 participants (55.6%), and a caregiver or visiting nurse filled it for 8 (44.4%).[13]

This reminder-device study shows the practical circumstances surrounding medication management in a cognitively impaired, older sample: device use often occurred alongside setup by relatives or formal caregivers. Kamimura, Ishiwata, and Inoue reported that people with old age, little prior experience of similar aids, and Clinical Dementia Rating scores of 0.5 or 1 could become good users of medication reminder devices; they attributed this pattern to external programming and preparation requiring minimal cognitive resources. Professional support and caregiver monitoring at the onset of use are contextual features of a medication-management arrangement, not merely characteristics of a reminder interface.[13]

Broader older-adult voice-assistant studies have value because they show how spoken interaction works in real homes. Pradhan, Lazar, and Findlater’s 2020 three-week Amazon Echo Dot deployment recruited seven adults aged 65 or older with no prior voice-assistant experience and infrequent digital-device use; three reported memory challenges affecting daily activities. Their sample was deliberately older and digitally inexperienced, but memory difficulty was self-reported rather than an MCI diagnosis.[20]

Similarly, the 2026 month-long Alexa in-home study deployed Echo Dots in 15 older adults’ homes for four weeks, including participants aged 66 and 94. Participants had no disclosed memory problems, and the study focused on conversational breakdowns, errors, and repair strategies. In the MCI breakdown study, participant quotations identify the older adult with MCI as M and the caregiver as CG; participants were already familiar with the AI system through non-medication use over a period of time, which the authors said influenced willingness to repair breakdowns.[21] It therefore provides practical evidence on older adults’ interactions with a commercial assistant, while the MCI-specific implications arise from comparison with the MCI-focused work rather than from this sample itself.[22][23][21]

Several reviews show why population labels need close reading. A systematic review of 72 older-adult health conversational-agent studies found that participant minimum ages ranged from 50 to 77 years among studies reporting ranges; reported mean ages ranged from 55.58 to 81.90 years; and 65 of 78 studies had fewer than 50 older-adult participants. A review of longitudinal coadaptation with voice assistants and wearables found only five eligible papers, with populations mostly aged 73–83 years and generally relatively healthy.[24][25]

A 2026 review of medication-adherence interventions for older people with cognitive impairment included seven randomized trials and 350 participants, whose average ages were in the mid-70s; most trials focused on mild impairment, and outcomes were generally positive without statistical significance.[108]

A separate review of digital-health interventions for adults aged 65 and over included 35 articles and found improved medication adherence in 17 studies; electronic reminders and social assistive robots were among the intervention categories.[108][109]

A chatbot meta-analysis included 5,676 participants across studies whose mean participant ages ranged from 39.5 to 64.5 years, below the specified 65+ population.[30]

**The population pattern is clear: direct medication-support evidence includes MATCHA’s mostly age-eligible MCI dyads and a telephone-reminder trial of people aged 65 or older with MCI or AD, while age-defined voice studies commonly lack an MCI diagnosis and the telephone trial was predominantly dementia.** MATCHA remains the closest conversational medication-management deployment; the telephone trial supplies age-eligible reminder evidence, and broader older-adult studies supply interaction context rather than interchangeable patient-outcome evidence.[1][19][17][15][20]

This population separation decides how subsequent findings should be read: a result from diagnosed MCI dyads can inform medication-management design, whereas a result from broader older adults informs likely accessibility, onboarding, and conversational demands.

### How do the circumstances of patients aged 65+ with mild

Medication adherence is broader than taking a dose after a cue. The medication-adherence literature defines it as treatment initiation, implementation of the prescribed regimen, and discontinuation of pharmacotherapy. It is therefore a longitudinal pattern of medication use, not a single spoken answer, a single reminder status, or an isolated opening of a container.[26]

An **acknowledged reminder** is an interaction event: a patient has heard, received, dismissed, answered, or otherwise responded to a prompt. An Alexa reminder record can have the statuses `ON` or `COMPLETED`, but those are states of the reminder record. Alexa defines reminders as prearranged messages spoken at a specified time, and its medication-management flow allows a customer to review prescriptions, choose preferred medication times, and ask which medication is due when the alert sounds.[27][28][3]

Medication-taking evidence is different. An electronic medication-event monitoring system (MEMS) cap records the time a medicine bottle is opened, and the pain-medication voice-reminder study describes that opening as an indirect measure of medication dispensing. Electronic monitors can show timing and patterns of container use, but opening a container does not prove that the correct medicine or dose was ingested.[16][15][29][26]

Self-report also measures something different from device-detected dispensing. One chatbot study counted daily self-reported `TAKEN` confirmations and reported mean adherence of 71.8%; that is a patient-reported adherence measure, not independently verified ingestion. The methodological review of adherence measures advises combining complementary measures because there is no gold-standard single measure, while noting that patient diaries may overestimate adherence relative to MEMS data.[30][26]

The South Korean automated telephone-reminder trial makes the outcome separation explicit. It enrolled patients aged 65 or older with MCI or Alzheimer’s disease who lived alone or with a cognitively impaired spouse; 29 participants were analysed, with a mean age of 79.6 years, and 20.7% had MCI while 79.3% had dementia. Researchers collected monthly medication-adherence data and call-response rates separately, using pill counts where possible; among the 29 analysed participants, mean age was 79.6 years (SD 6.0), 20.7% had MCI, and 79.3% had dementia. The authors concluded that reminders might improve adherence when a patient’s compliance with the reminder service is good.[31][19]

That distinction matters especially for MCI. Prospective memory is the ability to remember and carry out an intended action in the future, and it contributes to medication nonadherence among people with MCI. A cited study found that 49% of people with MCI reported difficulty remembering to take medicines; the difficulty is thus not merely lack of exposure to a reminder but remembering, deciding, and carrying out the planned action in a complex routine.[32]

MCI does not imply that a person cannot self-manage. The DSM-5 formulation cited in the supplied material says that cognitive deficits do not interfere with independence in everyday activities, including managing medications, although greater effort, compensatory strategies, or accommodation may be necessary. The clinically important design implication is to preserve agency while reducing avoidable memory, setup, and decision burdens.[33][7]

In a qualitative study of 11 people with MCI, most managed their medicines themselves and generally used and filled pill organizers, with only occasional caregiver checks or help obtaining medicines.[110]

The circumstances of use may determine whether a reminder is even encountered. In the telephone-reminder trial, home telephones were familiar and accessible for rural participants who often lacked internet service or mobile phones, but a telephone reminder could be missed if the person was away from home. In the voice-reminder study of older adults with pain, the authors suggested that latency between reminder and medication use might also reflect pain characteristics and beliefs about pain medicines.[31][15]

Caregiver and clinician participation can change the meaning of independent-looking use. In the reminder-device study, every participant’s medication was filled into the device by either a family member, a caregiver, or a visiting nurse. In MATCHA, six of the seven neurologist-diagnosed MCI members were aged 65 or older (85.7%); the older adult and caregiver shared a device and caregivers sometimes joined conversations. The older adults had already used the Google Home Hub for non-medication tasks for more than three months, while the intervention deployment lasted 20 weeks.[13][1]

A qualitative study of people with MCI found that medication-management responsibility commonly shifted to caregivers only after repeated errors, observed cognitive decline, or an adverse health event.[110]

The first outcome family is therefore **medication-reminder engagement**: whether the service successfully delivers a scheduled cue and whether the person responds. In MATCHA, whose neurologist-diagnosed MCI member group included six people aged 65 or older out of seven (85.7%), Phase 1 initiated 476 medication check-ins over four weeks and received 84 responses, which the authors reported as an engagement rate of around 18%. Four of the five members who continued to Phase 2 were aged 65 or older. That numerator is a response count and its denominator is system initiations, so it measures participation in a check-in service rather than doses taken.[1][2][34]

The second outcome family is **medication adherence**: whether the prescribed regimen is initiated, implemented, and persisted with. In the Alexa pain-reminder feasibility study, the five MEMS-monitored participants had adherence percentages from 82% to 100%; after 168 reminders, 25 cap openings occurred within five minutes and 107 within 30 minutes. These measures describe bottle-opening timing around prompts, not conversational task completion or a spoken confirmation.[16]

The third outcome family is **continued use**, also called engagement, adoption, or acceptability depending on the measure. Continued use may mean repeatedly answering a system, daily device operation, remaining in an intervention, or saying one intends to use it again; those indicators differ in time horizon and behavioural meaning. The 2023 integrative review of healthcare conversational agents found that trust was identified as an adoption factor in 36 of 73 patient studies, facilitating conditions in 18 of 73, and social influence in 8 of 73.[35]

A long duration of use does not turn a non-medication system into an adherence intervention. In the 2025 five-case series of a conversational companion robot, five community-dwelling women aged 85–90 used the robot for psychological support in daily life over 18 months, with intermittent light-touch assistance; three participants had MCI—Parkinson’s disease-associated MCI, MCI with Lewy bodies, and MCI due to Alzheimer’s disease—and two had late-onset psychosis. Four of five said they wanted to continue after the study.[36][37]

Likewise, health tracking is distinct from medication prompting. Dialogue systems can collect and track health information from older people and offer comments or advice, while medication reminders cue a scheduled medicine action. The Google Home healthy-coping application for elderly patients with type 2 diabetes received positive reactions to personalization and natural conversational flow, but users also reported difficulty learning commands, restricted response options, slow processing, and problematic responses; that application addressed diabetes coping and health information rather than a documented Google Assistant medication-adherence feature.[38][4]

Specialized medical conversational agents can also have aims that differ from either reminders or tracking. A relational agent is an embodied conversational agent that builds and maintains socioemotional relationships across multiple interactions. The hospital Care Coach study of 95 adults over 65 aimed to affect loneliness, depression, delirium, and falls during the hospital stay; those outcomes belong to inpatient psychosocial support and safety, not medication adherence.[18]

Catalia Health’s Mabu belongs conceptually to this specialized medical-assistant category because it is presented as a social-robot conversational experience that offers patient-centric support and adjusts to each patient and circumstance over time. Product descriptions also report that interaction data return to a backend system and that patients receive a monthly summary report to discuss with their doctor. These features describe a relationship- and care-support service, and the relevant outcome may be interaction continuity, reporting, confidence, or clinical management rather than a reminder acknowledgement alone.[5][6]

Medication reminders, health tracking, and specialized medical voice assistants can therefore be compared by purpose without treating their outputs as common metrics:

- **Medication reminders:** These systems cue a dose or help a person retrieve what medicine is due. Their immediate interaction outcome is alert delivery or response; medication adherence requires a separately defined medication-use measure. [3][26]

- **Health tracking:** These systems gather, present, or discuss health information, symptoms, or behaviours. Their immediate outcomes include successful reporting, access to information, and perceived usefulness; they do not inherently show that a medicine was taken. [38][4]

- **Specialized medical voice assistants:** These systems may deliver condition-specific support, relationship-building, psychological support, or clinician-facing summaries. Their continued use can be a meaningful exposure or acceptability outcome even when medication-taking is outside the intervention’s purpose. [18][36][5][6]

The MATCHA study, whose neurologist-diagnosed MCI member group included six people aged 65 or older out of seven (85.7%), provides a concrete example of how engagement and perceived medication support can coexist without becoming verified adherence. Its medication check-in offered branches for a user who said they had already taken medication, wanted a later reminder, were taking it now, did not remember, or wanted to check a pillbox. Members reported feeling more confident and assured about their routines, including less pressure to remember a pill because the assistant would ask; these are qualitative reports of confidence and support.[1][2]

The same study also illustrates why MCI medication management needs more than a binary yes/no confirmation. Participants expected the assistant to remember whether a medicine had been taken earlier, but the assistant could not answer historical questions; one caregiver proposed a smart pillbox that could tell the assistant whether a pill had been removed. Such integration would improve the evidence chain from prompt to medication-related action, though even pill removal remains an indirect measure of ingestion.[1][26]

**For patients aged 65+ with MCI, an acknowledgement is best interpreted as engagement with a reminder service, while adherence requires medication-specific evidence gathered independently or alongside that interaction.** This interpretation preserves the value of conversational check-ins, tracking, companionship, and relational support while keeping their purposes and outcome measures analytically distinct.[31][26][36]

This outcome separation decides the rest of the review: interface patterns can be linked to the interaction outcome they actually measure, but claims about medication adherence require an adherence measure rather than a proxy for use.

Medication nonadherence can result from misunderstanding instructions in a complex medication regimen; not knowing a medication’s purpose; unwillingness arising from side effects or a perception that medication is ineffective or unnecessary; forgetting; inability to swallow or self-administer a medicine such as an inhaler, eye drops, or injection; packaging difficulties; and medication cost or availability.[39]

An acknowledged reminder can be recorded before a dose is taken, or may reflect a caregiver rather than the patient responding. The clinically relevant sequence is a timely, comprehensible prompt; correct recognition of the requested action; access to the appropriate medicine; safe execution; and valid recording or independent verification of that action.

After a user links a Fitbit account, Google Assistant can retrieve steps, calories burned, heart rate, and workout metrics and display them on a Nest Hub or another Google Assistant-powered smart display.[40]

### Synthesis

The available research divides into two complementary strands. MCI-focused work most closely resembles the target population when it follows diagnosed older adults and caregivers through medication routines, especially MATCHA’s 20-week deployment, but its seven members were not all aged 65 or older and its shared-device context makes caregiver participation part of the intervention. Age-defined voice-assistant studies more often show real-world interaction barriers and use patterns among people aged 65+, but usually do not identify diagnosed MCI.[1][22][20]

Read together, these findings support an outcome-separation rule: reminder response shows service engagement; medication adherence is the longitudinal implementation of a prescribed regimen; and continued use can reflect acceptability, psychological support, convenience, or care integration. MCI increases the relevance of prospective-memory support and low-burden routines, while preserved independence makes externally prepared tools and caregiver-supported arrangements preferable to assumptions of incapacity.[32][33][13][31][26]

## Medication adherence: what is established

Medication adherence in patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a clinical behavior: taking the prescribed medicine, in the prescribed amount and timing, across initiation, implementation, and discontinuation. Conversational user-interface (UI) patterns matter because MCI can make prospective remembering—remembering to carry out an intended action later—medication-status recall, attention, and multi-step decisions more effortful. A voice reminder may provide a useful cue, but a response to that cue is not the same outcome as taking the medicine.[15][32][1]

### How do conversational UI patterns in voice-first healthcare applications affect

**The direct finding is that no particular conversational voice-UI sequence has yet demonstrated improved medication adherence specifically in a fully age-65+ MCI population.** The most relevant conversational deployment, MATCHA—Medication Action To Check-In for Health Application—studied older adults diagnosed with MCI and their caregivers, but one listed member was aged 62.6; it therefore cannot be treated as a strictly 65+ sample. Its outcome was interaction and acceptance, rather than independently verified medication ingestion.[1]

MCI is nevertheless directly relevant to medication-taking. MCI is characterized by cognitive decline beyond normal ageing that does not substantially interfere with activities of daily living, yet it is associated with poorer everyday decision making. In one adjusted analysis, participants classified with MCI missed 2.17 times as many everyday-decision-making points as cognitively normal participants (95% CI 1.02–4.61; p=0.044).[41] The medication-management literature also identifies prospective-memory deficits as a contributor to medication nonadherence among people with MCI, and 49% of people with MCI in one cited study reported difficulty remembering to take medicines.[32]

The first implication for conversational design is to support *status reflection*, not simply to issue an imperative. In the MATCHA design rationale, an alarm that says to take medicine can be unsafe for an individual with MCI who has already taken the dose but cannot remember doing so; the person may take a duplicate dose or take the next day’s dose. MATCHA instead asked whether the person had taken the medication and made checking a pillbox or calendar a legitimate part of the interaction.[2]

The resulting sequence was specific: first, MATCHA issued a scheduled and named check-in—such as, “it’s time to take your medication… Have you taken it yet?”—then the user could say that they had taken it, request a later reminder, say that they were taking it now, say that they did not remember, or say that they were checking the pillbox. The assistant acknowledged a reported dose and ended the exchange; for uncertainty, it suggested a pillbox check and waited for a follow-up response. The Google Home Hub also displayed the initial question and accepted a touch response.[1]

This is a medication-*status* interaction, not medication verification. A routine exchange of “Yes” followed by “Ok, thanks for letting me know” records a user’s report of the dose. The same distinction applies when an assistant stops an alert after a command: it records that the alert was stopped, not that the correct medicine and dose were ingested.[1][22]

MATCHA’s 20-week, two-phase in-home deployment with seven older-adult–caregiver dyads provides the longest MCI-focused conversational result in the supplied material. Phase 1 ran for four weeks. MATCHA issued 476 initiations and received 84 responses, an engagement rate of about 18%, calculated by the authors as interactions divided by system initiations.[2] Phase 2 then ran for 16 weeks—112 days, exceeding 90 days—and produced 760 responses from 1,120 initiations, or about 67%. The authors attributed the increase to revised user experience design together with growing comfort and familiarity as MATCHA became part of daily routine.[2]

In MATCHA’s first four-week phase, members gave the system a modified System Usability Scale (SUS) score of 84.66 points, while carepartners gave it 86.16 points; these measured perceived usability, not confidence or medication adherence.[2]

The design changes in Phase 2 formed a package rather than a single manipulated UI feature:

- **Lower-intensity acknowledgement:** The original response included four seconds of loud cheering, four seconds of clapping, and verbal praise. Some users found this overwhelming, especially when they took several medications per day, so Phase 2 used a two-second moderate cheer while retaining the verbal praise. [2]

- **Alternative response path:** Members could press a matching touch-screen button instead of replying aloud. This gave a route around speech recognition, word-finding, hearing, or speech-production difficulties. [2]

- **More time and a distinct immediate-action response:** MATCHA increased its response time-out and added a “Taking Now” scenario for a person who took the dose in response to the check-in. [2]

- **Personalized routines and check-ins:** The system scheduled prompts around each person’s medication routine. In Phase 1, the seven dyads received an average of two check-ins per day, ranging from one to five. [2]

The observed result supports a usable conclusion: personalized, contextual check-ins with a way to express uncertainty and an alternative touch response can sustain *response-based engagement* during a 16-week second phase for MCI dyads. The result does not convert the 67% interaction rate into an adherence rate, because all the Phase 2 changes and accumulated familiarity occurred together.[2]

The participants’ accounts describe why the check-in design may be better suited to MCI than a narrow alarm. Members said MATCHA reduced the pressure to remember their routine because it would ask if they had forgotten a pill. Some reported checking the pillbox before answering when they could not remember whether they had taken the medication.[9] That behavior is clinically meaningful as a safeguard against an accidental duplicate dose, even though the interaction log itself does not prove ingestion.[9]

Context in the prompt also matters. MATCHA participants characterized alarms without labels as unreliable because they could not recollect what the alarm was for; the design goal became specific, unambiguous medication messaging. Older adults in a separate month-long Alexa study similarly sometimes asked what they were supposed to do when a reminder rang without a message; its authors recommend obtaining more detail when the reminder is set.[2][22] The practical consequence is that a reminder should identify the medication task and, where clinically appropriate, the routine it refers to, rather than rely on a generic alert tone.

Judgment — For medication safety, the most appropriate conversational sequence is: first, a contextual cue; second, a low-burden status question; third, explicit choices for taken, taking now, later, and uncertain; fourth, a cue to inspect an external medication aid when uncertain; and fifth, an option to hand off unresolved status to a caregiver or clinician. This sequence minimizes the demand to reconstruct medication history from memory while avoiding a system instruction that could trigger duplicate dosing.

A different MCI-relevant intervention tested automated telephone reminders rather than a voice-first assistant. The six-month randomized trial enrolled patients aged 65+ with MCI or Alzheimer’s disease who lived alone or with a cognitively impaired spouse. Of 29 analyzed patients, the mean age was 79.6 years, 20.7% had MCI, and 79.3% had dementia.[31] Patients or caregivers set the reminder times; answering the home telephone played a recorded instruction to take medication.[31]

In the six-month South Korean automated telephone-reminder trial, a researcher contacted participants after more than three days of unanswered reminder calls.[19]

The South Korean trial delivered its telephone medication reminder once daily; if reminder calls went unanswered for more than three days, a researcher contacted the participant.[19]

The telephone trial measured reminder response and medication adherence separately. The intervention group’s mean response rate was 51.3%; six participants responded to more than 70% of reminder calls, with a mean response rate of 86.6%.[31] Across all participants, mean medication adherence was 92.6% in the reminder group and 90.2% in controls, a difference reported as not statistically significant (p=0.295).[31] In the six high-response participants, adherence was 94.6%, versus 90.2% in controls (p=0.048).[31]

The trial’s result answers an important design question: frequent answering and higher adherence occurred together in a six-person subgroup, while simply offering automated reminder calls did not raise adherence for the full group. The authors consequently concluded that telephone reminders might improve adherence when compliance with the reminder service is good and that reminder tools need to improve such compliance for cognitively impaired patients.[31] Pill counts supplied the adherence measure, and the researchers noted that participant- or caregiver-counted pills could be inaccurate, although researchers checked remaining medication at more than 90% of visits.[31]

This nonconversational study evaluated a medication reminder device in older adults aged 65+ spanning Clinical Dementia Rating scores of 0.5 and 1.[13][14] A three-month before-and-after study of a medication reminder device recruited 18 home-dwelling adults aged 65+ with Clinical Dementia Rating scores of 0.5 or 1 and a history of missed doses, overdosing, or needing verbal medication reminders.[13][14] At three months, the study reported improvement in the self-administration medication rate—independently taken doses divided by all prescribed doses during one week—for 13 of 18 users (72.2%).[13][14] Examiners trained users and caregivers in their homes at initiation, and caregivers monitored the device during the first week.[13][14]

A later synthesis described the three-month medication-reminder-device intervention as increasing the proportion of participants who achieved a self-administered medication rate above 80% at both one month and three months.[109]

Kamimura, Ishiwata, and Inoue reported that the proportion of participants whose self-administered medication rate exceeded 80% increased at both one month and three months relative to baseline.[13][109]

A separate two-year study of 131 people with MCI, whose mean age was 82.5 years, found improvement on the eight-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale: the age-controlled odds ratio for moving one adherence level higher from Time 1 to Time 4 was 3.316. Its interventions were verbal advice, an educational leaflet, and comic books, not conversational prompts.[111]

That study supports reducing setup and memory burden around an aid. The authors proposed that external programming and preparation reduced the cognitive resources required for use, and that professional support and early caregiver monitoring may have aided use.[13] This aligns with the MCI-focused MEDSReM redesign, where heuristic evaluation identified visibility, user control and freedom, error prevention, and flexibility of use as improvement needs; cognitive walkthroughs recommended simpler interfaces and direct, prescriptive instructions to reduce decision-making demands.[42][32] Subject-matter experts also recommended minimizing decision-heavy options, such as skipping medication, and improving visual layout to reduce errors and ease navigation.[32]

MEDSReM-M stands for Medication Education, Decision Support, Reminding, and Monitoring–Memory; its intended functions include medication education, decision support, reminders, monitoring, and progress tracking for people with MCI.[32]

Judgment — The design direction is therefore low decision burden, not maximal dialogue. For a patient with MCI, a short, explicit prompt with predefined responses and an external reference—pillbox, medication list, or caregiver-supported record—is more likely to support safe status checking than a broad request that requires the patient to formulate the right command and remember prior interactions.

Voice reminders can still cue medication activity in adjacent populations. In a four-week Google Home Mini feasibility study of 15 adults aged 55+ without cognitive impairment who used scheduled pain medication, each participant created a Google Assistant profile and a spoken reminder. Five participants used Medication Event Monitoring System (MEMS) caps, which record bottle openings as an indirect measure of dispensing.[16][15] In that five-person subsample, implementation-adherence percentages ranged from 82% to 100%; of 168 reminders, 25 (14.6%) were followed by a bottle opening within five minutes and 107 (63.7%) within 30 minutes.[15]

In the Google Home Mini medication-reminder feasibility study, participants were instructed to place the MEMS-equipped bottle where they normally kept their routine medicines.[15]

The study reports a mean absolute reminder-to-cap-opening latency of 55 minutes (SD 100), with participants’ average latencies ranging from 17 minutes to 4.5 hours.[15] The paper concluded that voice reminders may cue scheduled medication use while timing varies.

Some cap openings occurred before the reminders, however, and the study’s absolute-latency measure treated pre-reminder and post-reminder events alike.[15] The implication is that reminder-timed bottle openings show temporal proximity, not a simple causal pathway from spoken prompt to ingestion.

Medication-monitoring technology has the same measurement boundary. A MEMS cap records an opening event, but a patient may open the container without taking a dose, take an incorrect dose, remove several doses at once, or transfer medication to another container.[29] An acknowledged voice prompt is still further upstream: it is evidence of an interaction, not of dispensing or ingestion. This distinction decides which outcome a voice-health intervention can legitimately claim.

The broader medication-adherence literature also cautions against treating forgetfulness as the only mechanism. Patients have cited forgetfulness, competing priorities, deliberate omission, lack of information, and emotional factors as reasons for not taking medication.[29] Medication schedules themselves matter: a review of 76 electronic-monitor trials found adherence decreased as daily dosing frequency rose, with average adherence around 50% for four-times-daily schedules and a 31%–71% range.[29] A conversational UI can reduce cueing and status-recall burdens, but medication regimen complexity, side effects, beliefs, access to care, and caregiver support remain part of the clinical setting.

A scoping review estimated that 20%–55% of older adults do not follow medication-use instructions; an older-adult medication-app study cited 41% non-adherence.[112]

This makes a reminder most useful when embedded in a wider medication-management arrangement. MATCHA participants did not want to abandon pillboxes, sticky notes, calendars, or caregiver practices; they wanted a new digital tool to work alongside them.[2] Medication management was likewise described in the later MATCHA breakdown study as collaborative, involving spouses, family members, caregivers, digital aids, nondigital aids, clinicians, and pharmacy staff.[1] The medication-assistant design decision is therefore integration with established routines and people, rather than replacement of those routines.

The relevant HCI consequence is that a conversational pattern must keep uncertainty actionable. In MATCHA, users sometimes asked whether they had taken medication earlier, asked about pillbox status, requested later reminders, or tried to change a medication time.[1] Commercial-assistant limitations in speech recognition, short-term context, long-term conversational memory, and sustained verbal engagement often required users to repeat, rephrase, or simplify speech.[1] Such repair and memory issues are examined in detail in **Error recovery**; for adherence, their central implication is that an assistant should never treat a failed or ambiguous response as proof of medication status.

Medication-taking ability and medication adherence are separate clinical constructs. Medication-taking ability means accurately following a prescribed regimen—knowing which medicines to take and when, and administering them correctly—whereas medication adherence is the extent to which medication-taking behaviour corresponds with treatment recommendations agreed with a healthcare provider. Non-adherence includes under-use, over-use, and incorrect use; it can be unintentional, through forgetfulness, lack of understanding, physical problems, or regimen complexity, or intentional, when a person decides not to take treatment as instructed.[43]

Adherence should be measured independently of the conversation. Objective measures include refill or pharmaceutical-claims data, electronic monitoring, biological assays, and used or unused medication such as pill counts; subjective measures include self-reported doses and validated questionnaires. The older-adult medication-intervention review preferentially extracted objective measures when studies reported both kinds.[43]

Across 35 digital-health studies involving adults aged 65 or older, 18 used objective medication-adherence indicators, such as pill counts, electronic monitoring or refill patterns, while 17 used questionnaires or self-report.[109]

A site-specific six-month analysis at the SAS site found a 0.13 absolute difference in adherence to both medicines (nominal P=.02).[44]

A cross-sectional study of 436 patients aged 60 years and older with chronic diseases provides additional, adjacent evidence that MCI is a medication-management risk factor. Using education-specific Mini-Mental State Examination cutoffs, the authors classified 181 participants (41.5%) as having MCI; medication adherence was measured with the self-reported eight-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8). The mean MMAS-8 score was 5.90 (SD 1.61), 212 participants (48.6%) scored below 6 and were classified as having poor adherence, and MCI was independently associated with non-adherence after adjustment (OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.64–4.24; p<0.001).[45] The authors identify memory impairment, executive dysfunction, forgetfulness, misinterpretation of medication instructions, and difficulty coordinating complex dosing schedules as medication-use challenges; adverse drug reactions and poor evaluation of medication effects were also independently associated with non-adherence.[45] This supports designing a reminder as one component of medication management rather than treating a missed cue as the sole explanation for non-adherence.[45]

### How does medication adherence differ from sustained engagement with a

Medication adherence and sustained engagement answer different questions. Medication adherence is whether the patient takes medicines according to agreed decisions with the prescribing health professional, including whether actual dosing corresponds to the prescribed regimen.[46][15] Sustained engagement is continued or repeated interaction with the application. In conversational-agent research, user engagement is the depth and frequency of interactions with an intervention.[47]

An application log therefore measures use of the application, not the medicine. In the telephone-reminder trial, researchers collected monthly medication-adherence and reminder-response rates separately. The intervention group had 92.6% adherence despite a 51.3% mean call-response rate, and the full-group adherence result did not differ significantly from the 90.2% control rate.[31] A person can take a dose without answering a call, and can answer a call without taking the dose.

MATCHA makes the distinction especially visible. Its Phase 1 engagement rate of about 18% meant 84 recorded responses from 476 system check-ins over four weeks. Its Phase 2 engagement rate of about 67% meant 760 responses from 1,120 check-ins over 16 weeks.[2] These are response-to-initiation ratios. They quantify whether dyads interacted with scheduled conversational prompts, not the proportion of prescribed doses taken or the correctness of medication timing.

The 16-week second phase is sustained engagement beyond 90 days in the ordinary temporal sense: 16 weeks equals 112 days. The result shows that the revised check-in system continued receiving responses throughout that phase, and the authors describe increased engagement over time.[2] It does not mean 67% medication adherence, and it does not show that a particular one of the simultaneous revisions—less exuberant praise, touch input, longer time-out, or the “Taking Now” branch—produced the increase.[2]

A related distinction is between *engagement*, *acceptance*, and *confidence*. MATCHA members described feeling confident and assured about medication routines because the assistant would ask about a forgotten pill.[2] Those are qualitative reports of perceived support, not numerical confidence scores and not measures of verified medication-taking. The next sections address confidence, confirmation design, and error patterns as usability outcomes rather than converting them into clinical adherence outcomes.

Application use can also decline while the reminder function remains available. In a 60-day MedBuddy medication-reminder skill study, conducted with 25 undergraduate women rather than older MCI patients, the mean response rate fell from 70.5% (SD 0.23) in the first two weeks to 59.2% (SD 0.27) in the last two weeks (t23=2.4; p=.03).[46] Participants reported fewer missed medications at the end of the study, but there was no significant change in reported consistency of taking medication within the same two-hour daily window; the authors also noted that their adherence questions had not been psychometrically tested and that self-report can overestimate adherence.[46]

The 60-day MedBuddy feasibility cohort comprised 25 undergraduate women taking oral contraceptives, with a mean age of 21.8 years (SD 6.2); participants responded on a mean 50.97% of study days (SD 29.5).[113]

This adjacent study illustrates the two-outcome rule: declining prompt response is a change in engagement, while self-reported missed doses and dose timing are adherence-related measures. They may move together, move in opposite directions, or be affected by different mechanisms. The distinction is essential when interpreting a system’s dashboard, because a high response rate can reflect willingness to converse and a low response rate can reflect successful routine formation, alert fatigue, illness, absence, or technical failure.

The Alexa field studies likewise show that reminder use is not medication adherence. In a three-week deployment with seven adults aged 65+ who used computing devices less than daily and had no prior voice-assistant experience, reminder-setting represented 26 of 1,049 primary commands (2.5%); 18 of those 26 commands occurred in the first week.[20] Participants initially viewed reminders and timers as useful but used them less than expected because of concerns about reliability, forgetting to set the reminder, electricity, and internet connectivity.[20][48]

One participant explicitly used multiple backups rather than relying solely on Alexa for important medication reminders: Alexa, other alarms, and family or friends.[20] Another said she could have benefited from reminders but forgot to set one, and welcomed the idea of daughters or a doctor setting reminders remotely.[20] These accounts show that the application’s engagement burden starts before a reminder fires: the person or support network must create, maintain, and trust the reminder routine.

General conversational-agent research retains the same separation. A review of cardiometabolic self-management defines engagement as interaction depth and frequency, while defining self-management as monitoring symptoms and circumstances and understanding their implications for health.[47] Another review defines chatbot “adherence loss” as stopping use of the chatbot during a trial—also called non-usage attrition—which is an application-use outcome rather than medication adherence.[49] The two measures should be reported side by side, not substituted for one another.

Judgment — A clinically useful evaluation has an outcome ladder. First, measure whether the prompt arrived and was noticed. Second, measure whether the person completed the conversational sequence, including an uncertainty or deferral route. Third, measure medication implementation with a prespecified method such as verified dispensing, pill count, pharmacy refill, or an appropriately validated self-report measure. Fourth, record safety events, particularly duplicate-dose risk and unresolved uncertainty. Each rung answers a different question; combining them hides where a system is helping or failing.

Measurement choice changes interpretation. Electronic packages offer time-stamped records of openings, but they remain indirect; diary entries can overestimate adherence, with one review reporting diary entries averaging 30% more than MEMS data.[29][26] Some adherence scales classify at least 80% of objectively measured doses as adherent, but thresholds differ across instruments and should be stated with the measure rather than assumed.[26] A verbal “taken” confirmation is valuable as a self-report and as a trigger for follow-up, but it belongs lower on the verification hierarchy than independently observed ingestion.

The distinction also protects against overstating favorable experience measures. Health conversational-agent users often value reminders, routine support, accountability, and ease of use.[50] These attributes can make a system worth deploying and can support continued use, but they are not evidence that medicines were taken as prescribed.

Similarly, technical problems affected performance expectancy, acceptability, acceptance, and adoption in 21 of 73 patient studies (29%), with some patients ending use early.[35] The immediate design target is therefore reliable interaction and recoverable uncertainty, while the clinical target remains medication-taking behavior.

For each outcome, report its measure, denominator, follow-up window, and rule for handling missing observations separately; otherwise retention, response, and medication-taking results cannot be interpreted as comparable.

### Synthesis

The MCI-specific conversational pattern with the strongest longitudinal result is the scheduled, personalized medication check-in: a contextual prompt asks about status, permits uncertainty, and can direct the person to inspect a pillbox before reporting back. In MATCHA, that approach, together with touch alternatives, longer response time, reduced celebratory feedback, and accumulated familiarity, accompanied engagement rising from about 18% over four weeks to about 67% over the next 16 weeks.[2] The telephone trial points in the same practical direction—higher reminder response coincided with higher adherence in six high responders—while its full randomized comparison showed no significant adherence difference.[31] The direction is thus toward low-burden, contextual, uncertainty-aware assistance embedded in established medication routines; the clinical outcome remains adherence measured separately from application engagement.[2][29]

## Amazon Alexa’s medication reminders

### What interaction design strategies are documented as deployed in Amazon

Judgment — For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a medication reminder is useful only if the person can set it, understand it when it arrives, and act on it. Amazon Alexa’s deployed design centres on scheduling and delivering prompts, with medication information protected by authentication; it is not a closed-loop medication-adherence system that verifies ingestion.

Amazon’s deployed pharmacy-linked medication experience for Giant Eagle Pharmacy customers is a prescription workflow: the customer enables the Giant Eagle Pharmacy skill, links the pharmacy account, creates an Alexa voice profile if needed, and creates a personal passcode.[3] The customer then says, “Alexa, manage my medication”; Alexa guides review of current prescriptions and schedules reminders at the customer’s preferred medication times.

At the medication-management launch, Amazon described Giant Eagle pharmacies as operating in more than 200 supermarket locations.[3]

At alert time, the customer can ask which medication is due, and can request a prescription refill by voice. Voice recognition plus the passcode controls access to prescription information, and a mismatch prevents Alexa from relaying that information.[3] **This is a prescription-oriented setup and due-time retrieval sequence: it supports prospective memory—remembering to do something later—rather than recording that the medicine was swallowed.**[46][3]

Separately, Amazon’s general Alexa reminder capability offers two setup routes: a person can speak a reminder request or create and edit a reminder in the Alexa app.[51] A person may speak a reminder request or enter and edit it in the Alexa app; the app flow is to open Reminders, select Add Reminder, enter details, and choose the Alexa device that will announce the alert.

Alexa+ also lets users request a consolidated list of reminders and tasks and set daily or weekly recurring reminders.[51]

Alexa+ also allows users to request follow-up reminders until they confirm that a task is complete; its task list is accessible by voice and through iOS and Android apps.[51] [114]

The platform supports one-time schedules, relative delays, and recurring absolute-time schedules, with the device time zone used unless another is specified.[51][27] In Amazon’s general reminder capability, the reminder is announced on the Alexa device used to make the request.[51] For third-party custom skills using Amazon’s Reminders API, content can use default text for both display and speech or separate Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML) for spoken content, and the skill can enable an Alexa-app push notification to a phone.[27] Amazon’s third-party-skill tutorial documents a due-reminder sequence in which an Alexa-enabled device chimes and announces the reminder, with an Alexa-app push notification when the app is installed and push notifications are enabled; the API also supports display text.[51][27][52]

Amazon’s custom-skill guidance specifies a setup confirmation dialogue. A skill must obtain informed consent each time it creates a reminder and must accurately describe the action; after successful creation it should restate the reminder’s time and nature. The example pattern first proposes frequency, time, and purpose, receives agreement, and then confirms the recurring schedule.[28][53] This confirmation verifies authorization and schedule creation, not dose-taking.

The guidance also says reminder labels should be concise and lead with the critical information, while lengthy text may be visually truncated to approximately 25 characters on an Echo Spot.[28]

The documented recovery path handles permission and service failure. If reminder permission is absent, a skill gives spoken instructions to enable it in the Alexa app and may present a home card linking to the permissions page. If creating, modifying, or deleting a reminder fails, the skill tells the customer that the relevant action failed; Amazon’s tutorial example says that there was an error creating the reminder and directs the user to the skill publisher.[28][53] This is explicit error notification, not a conversational repair loop in which Alexa asks the person to correct or retry a misunderstood medication response.

The difference between a reminder being stopped and a dose being taken matters in this population. In a month-long in-home Alexa study of older adults, 88 of 251 reminder interactions failed; 69 failures (78.41% of erroneous reminder interactions) occurred when participants tried to stop a ringing reminder. Participants used natural acknowledgements such as “Thank you,” “I got it,” and “I took my medicine,” whereas Alexa generally recognised scripted commands such as “Stop,” “Cancel,” or “I’m done.” The study participant who used nightly medication reminders also struggled to tell Alexa that medication had been taken and to query whether it had been taken that day.[22][23] Consequently, alert dismissal and a completed-reminder record are interaction events, not medication adherence.

Amazon’s general medication reminder was described separately from MedBuddy, a research team’s Echo skill. The Amazon service provided one reminder and did not let users record whether medicine was taken as prescribed, whereas MedBuddy accepted spoken reports of taken or missed medication and used up to three alerts around the chosen dose time.[46] The latter sequence should not be attributed to Amazon’s deployed medication feature.

For general tasks, Alexa+ can send hourly or daily follow-up notifications until the user confirms completion; this is not a medication-specific dose-confirmation protocol.[114]

MedBuddy’s three-alert sequence began 15 minutes before the chosen medication time, repeated at that time if the user had not responded, and issued a third alert 15 minutes later; users could verbally report taking or forgetting the medication, after which the skill recorded the response and, when reported, the time.[115]

MedBuddy scheduled three spoken alerts for each dose, at 15 minutes before, at, and 15 minutes after the chosen time; the alerts ended when the user verbally reported taking or forgetting the medicine. Its published protocol describes voice responses and logging, but not a visual fallback or caregiver escalation path.[115]

For Amazon Alexa itself, the documented strategy is scheduled prompting, protected medication-information retrieval, optional app-mediated setup and notification, and setup-focused confirmation; it lacks a built-in ingestion-confirmation loop.[46][3][27]

For voice setup, Alexa repeats the reminder details and asks the user to confirm them.[54]

Amazon’s initial medication-management launch repeatedly requested a passcode as a privacy and security measure, adding access friction to the service.[55] Users are instructed to check reminders whenever medication or dosage times change and to contact McKay Pharmacy before changing a reminder when uncertain when to take a medicine.[54]

In a pilot deployment with homebound older adults, participants received an Echo Show and training in voice commands, including setting reminders. Participants or caregivers manually set medication reminders during training or follow-up visits because Alexa required user-initiated commands to set up routines.[56]

In a three-week study of older adults with low technology use, most participants used Alexa reminders at least once, except P5, but reminder setting accounted for 26 logged commands (2.5%), including 18 in Week 1.[20][48] Reminder and alarm use decreased over the study, although grocery-list use increased: 23 of 47 grocery-list commands occurred in Week 3.[20][48] Participants were initially enthusiastic about memory-support functions such as reminders, but used them much less than expected, citing technology failure and forgetting to set the reminder through the voice assistant.[20]

Five participants had difficulty remembering keywords needed to invoke or navigate features. P5’s request, “Alexa, I would like you to tell me when it's 6:00”, was not recognised as a reminder because it omitted the required “remind/reminder” keyword.[20] Five of seven participants did not consider reminders or alarms reliable.[20] Two participants worried about remembering to set a reminder or alarm; P5 had forgotten to set a reminder and welcomed the prospect of daughters or a doctor setting reminders remotely as collaborative remembering.[20] P1 would not rely on Alexa alone for important medication reminders and would use backups.[48]

Doteveryone reported that patients with dementia could be “deeply distressed” by medication reminders from Alexa-type devices.[57]

In the MedBuddy feasibility study, poststudy interviews after at least 60 days found that participants who wanted to continue cited an external alert separate from the phone, audibility from another room, motivating human-sounding verbal prompts, and more than one reminder.[46] Participants also enjoyed the external prompt, hearing it from a separate room, multiple reminders, and verbal responses to prompts.[58] One participant said, “I really liked that I got three reminders because I almost always ignored the first two, but it was enough reminders to force me to do it.” The authors concluded that MedBuddy may be useful in promoting medication adherence and could benefit from further usability enhancements.[46][58]

In the research-built MedBuddy Alexa skill, the first medication alert occurred 15 minutes before the participant-selected dose time, a second occurred at that time if unanswered, and a third followed 15 minutes later; users verbally reported taking or forgetting the dose, and the skill logged the response and reported medication time.[115]

In co-design with people living with early-stage dementia or MCI and care partners, medication reminders were identified as a “Must have” function, and participants highlighted self-monitoring to compensate for short-term memory loss.[59] Requested medication support comprised verbal alarm reminders with personalised prompts, including the number of tablets, and visual images that could be stock or personal.[59] The proposed digital voice-assistant programme could be activated by the user or by an automatically broadcast audio conversational reminder; one proposed broadcast says, “Hi [name] it's time to take your tablets… Have you thought of it?”[59]

Co-design participants also proposed showing a picture of the person’s Webster medication pack alongside a personalised reminder.[116]

Professionals in the same co-design study identified adjustable speaking rate, as well as volume, as an accommodation for slow processing speed and hearing impairment.[116]

Visual feedback depends on the device; it is not inherent to an audio-only Echo. On screen-equipped devices, reminder content can use display text, and lengthy text may be truncated to approximately 25 characters on an Echo Spot; the Echo Show 10 has a 10-inch touch display that provides visual feedback.[27][28][60]

No established longitudinal study of Amazon Alexa’s deployed medication-reminder feature reports verified medication adherence specifically among patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment.

Household acoustics and account configuration can therefore affect whether a scheduled prompt is usable.

Alexa medication reminders can be turned off or snoozed.[55]

The Giant Eagle Pharmacy Skill’s interactions are redacted in the Alexa app, and customers can review and delete voice recordings in the Alexa app, on the Privacy Settings page, or by voice.[61][62][63]

## Google Assistant’s health tracking features

### What interaction design strategies are documented as deployed in Google

Google Assistant’s documented health-tracking design is principally a sensor-to-feedback loop, rather than a medication-adherence conversation. On the second-generation Nest Hub, Sleep Sensing is an opt-in bedside wellness feature: it combines low-energy radar motion sensing for movement and breathing with microphone, light, and temperature sensors, after the user positions and calibrates the device for the sleeping area. Google states that it is not intended to diagnose, treat, mitigate, prevent, or cure disease.[64]

First, the system passively detects sleep-related signals overnight; second, it produces a sleep summary on the Nest Hub display the following morning; third, the user can touch the display for detail, ask Google Assistant about their sleep, or inspect longer-term history in Google Fit. Voice access to personal sleep results requires both Voice Match and personal results to be enabled.[65][66] This sequence minimizes spoken data entry, but it depends on correct placement, calibration, account settings, and an environment without interfering people, pets, or movement.[67][68]

The feedback is progressively personalized. Sleep tips begin after one week of daily tracking, while a recommended bedtime and wake-up schedule becomes available after 14 consecutive days of tracking. Users may opt to save processed cough and snore outputs—such as cough counts and snore minutes—to Google Fit for later review.[64][66][69] **Thus, the deployed Nest strategy is recurring passive sensing followed by visual, app-based, and optional voice-accessible feedback, not a spoken confirmation dialogue.**

Google’s activity-tracking design similarly emphasizes linked data and display presentation. A Nest display can show steps and calories from Google Fit or Fitbit once the user connects the account in Google Home settings. Turning on personal results and selecting proactive display makes these metrics appear without a spoken request.[70] This creates an at-a-glance health-information channel, although anyone with access to the display can review the most recent activity metrics when personal results are enabled.[70] Neither the Sleep Sensing nor activity-display documentation describes haptic feedback, health-data confirmation dialogs, medication reminders, or a medication-adherence outcome.[64][66][70]

For older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI)—a condition involving measurable cognitive decline that does not necessarily prevent independent living—the Google-based medication design is MATCHA, an independently developed custom Google Conversational Action rather than a native Google health-tracking feature.[1][2] MATCHA was installed on Google Home devices and prompted medication-status reports at pre-scheduled times.

Its opening prompt addressed the person by name, reminded them that it was time for medication, and asked whether they had taken it; the Home display simultaneously showed the prompt and allowed a touch response.[1]

MATCHA used status-specific conversational paths: a reported dose taken received acknowledgement; a request to defer prompted setting a later check-in; an uncertain user was directed to check the pillbox; and the system paused while a user checked the pillbox. Responses outside its predefined scenarios fell to Google Assistant’s general dialog behaviour.[1] The design also displayed spoken commands and responses, reducing the need for participants to remember what they had said, and accommodated more than 200 anticipated dyad responses.[2] An affirmative answer is a self-report of medication status, not independent verification that medication was ingested.[1]

**Conclusion:** Google’s deployed Assistant health-tracking features are Nest Hub Sleep Sensing and linked Google Fit or Fitbit activity display: passive sensor capture followed by next-morning and proactive visual presentation, touch-accessible detail, Google Fit history, and optional voice queries.[64][66][70] MATCHA is an independently developed medication-check-in application built on Google Assistant infrastructure for the MCI population: its voice-plus-screen prompt, touch fallback, and status-specific routes record self-reported medication status rather than verified ingestion.[1]

Google Assistant is a general-purpose voice assistant, not a single standardized medication-adherence intervention; health and fitness interactions in its ecosystem are distinct from prompting and verifying medication administration.

Google Assistant’s linked Fitbit/Google Fit functions add two health-data presentation routes. On supported Nest displays, the user links one service, enables personal results and proactive health and fitness results, and can then have steps and calories displayed without asking.[71][40] Google Home Personal Routines can instead read selected Fitbit or Google Fit measures aloud on compatible Google Home devices: step count, distance travelled, calories burned, days exercised in the week, hours slept, and sleep start time.[72] The routine separates editable activity data from sleep data.[72]

After linking either Google Fit or Fitbit in Assistant’s Wellness > Activity settings, Google Assistant could answer questions about steps, heart rate, exercise zone, calories burned, and related fitness measures; Nest Hub smart displays and the Assistant mobile app could present the result as a data card with an icon and its information source.[71]

## Catalia Health’s Mabu robot

### What interaction design strategies are documented as deployed in specialized

For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Mabu is relevant as a specialized **social robot**—a physically embodied conversational agent—rather than a general-purpose smart speaker. Its design combines medication support with disease-specific check-ins and links the patient’s conversation to human care teams.[73][6]

In September 2019, Pfizer and Catalia announced a 12-month Mabu pilot to explore patient behaviors outside clinical environments and test the impact of regular AI engagement on treatment journeys; the announcement identifies symptom-management and medication-adherence trends as areas for which Mabu would gather insights.[74] First, setup confirms the patient’s identity and asks when they usually take a medicine; the patient can then configure a reminder for that time.

Second, daily check-ins begin with how the patient feels and branch according to the answer. Third, concerning symptom information can trigger immediate advice, a next-day check-in, or transmission to a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, or caregiver.[6][73] Mabu’s deployed conversation is deliberately focused on a particular disease or treatment rather than open-ended assistant dialogue.[6]

Descriptions of Mabu’s daily check-ins also identify mood and activity as topics alongside symptoms and medication adherence, and describe escalation of repeated missed doses to care teams.[75][117]

Mabu delivers spoken, tailored conversations through a tablet integrated into a sculpted robot body. Its documented visual relational cues include user tracking, eye contact, and blinking; interaction data also feed clinician outreach and a monthly patient report.[75][74][6] Mabu also displays its spoken content on its screen, accepts either spoken replies or touchscreen selections, and can send text-message reminders when a patient is away from home.[76] Catalia describes daily conversations as varying in length with the patient’s answers, supporting adaptation to behavior, preferences, symptoms, and treatment routines.[73][77][78] The documented multimodal design is therefore voice plus screen-based visual and touch interaction, supplemented by text messaging; its physical face and head are intended to support connection with the robot.[76][79]

The Mabu Care Insights Platform is described as sending patient data and insights specifically to specialty-pharmacy clinicians to support human outreach.[74]

Pfizer–Catalia announced a 12-month pilot on symptom-management and medication-adherence trends, while a separate home-use trial involved 45 patients for two months.[74][77] These deployments do not identify participants as having MCI; one application was described as serving mostly men over 70.[80] Consequently, Mabu’s documented design supports individualized reminders and response-contingent care, but not an MCI-specific medication-adherence or 90-day engagement result.[74][77][80]

The patients in the Pfizer–Catalia pilot announced in September 2019 were described as “select patients”; the announcement did not identify them as aged 65 years or older or as having MCI.[74]

In a separate four-month, single-group study, 30 adults aged 65 or older used Hyodol, a sensor- and AI-enabled robot combining medication reminders, cognitive stimulation, and companionship; their mean age was 82.6 years (SD 6.36). Medication Adherence Rating Scale scores changed significantly from baseline to post-intervention, with a reported Cohen’s d of 0.82; this was not a Mabu study or a study identified as enrolling people with diagnosed MCI.[109]

The available Mabu materials do not report quantitative task-completion rates, speech-recognition errors, usability scores, or adherence effects attributable to its confirmation sequence.[2][74]

In chronic-condition programs, Mabu has also been used for patient education, patient-reported-outcome collection, and coaching. Its longitudinal relational-conversation strategy carries prior responses into repeated encounters, rather than treating each interaction as a standalone request.

Mabu product descriptions say its tailored conversations draw on prior health history, preferences, family details, and interactions.[74]

Mabu’s documented patient-facing sequence is: greeting and small talk; a patient-relevant health topic; then either a check on whether medication has been taken or discussion of anticipated treatment side effects.[76] The system largely directs this conversation while allowing the patient to influence its direction, and its platform adapts the dialogue in real time as it learns the patient’s personality and treatment issues.[76] A symptom-check-in sequence can begin with how the patient feels, proceed to condition-specific questions such as shortness of breath and weight for heart-failure patients, and then lead to immediate feedback plus one of three next steps: advice to call a doctor, transmission to the care team, or a next-day check-in.[81]

A Mabu product description gives this medication sequence: daily check-in, medication reminder, request for confirmation, adherence tracking, and escalation to the care team after repeated missed doses.[117]

## Where the deployed systems differ

Judgment — For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the crucial comparison is not which device is “best,” but what each interaction is designed to accomplish. A scheduled alert, passive health monitoring, and a response-contingent care conversation place different memory, speech, and follow-through demands on the patient; an acknowledged prompt or a recorded medication habit is not the same outcome as medication adherence.

### How do interaction design strategies compare across Amazon Alexa’s medication

Amazon Alexa’s documented medication-reminder pattern is configurable, scheduled prompting. In November 2019, Amazon, Omnicell and Giant Eagle deployed a pharmacy-linked Alexa service through which Giant Eagle Pharmacy customers could set medication reminders and request prescription refills. Enrollment required enabling the Giant Eagle Pharmacy Skill in the Alexa app, creating and linking an account, verifying prescription information, setting a four-digit passcode, and creating an Alexa voice profile; the user then said, “Alexa, manage my medication,” reviewed current prescriptions, and scheduled preferred reminder times.[61][62] A person can also create or edit general reminders by voice or in the Alexa app, select the announcing device, and assign a reminder only to a profile on the same Amazon account.

The device announces the reminder on the device where it was requested. An Alexa Reminders API design tutorial advises that a skill state the frequency, time, and purpose before the user agrees, and the due reminder chimes and announces its message; enabled mobile push notifications can add a phone channel.[51][52]

This is primarily a reminder-delivery interaction rather than a medication-management dialogue. A mixed-methods evaluation distinguishes the research team’s MedBuddy skill from Amazon’s general medication-reminder skill: the latter provided one reminder, did not let the user record whether medication was taken as prescribed, and lacked support for as-needed medicines. Medication-specific setup guidance describes Alexa repeating the entered details for confirmation, separate reminders for each dosing time, optional generic wording to avoid speaking drug names aloud, and app-based support from a friend or relative.[46][54] **Thus, Alexa’s documented sequence centres on creating, confirming, and announcing an alert, not on verifying ingestion or adapting care to a response.**

Google Assistant’s cited native health-tracking example has a different purpose: Nest Hub second-generation Sleep Sensing is opt-in wellness monitoring, not a medication-reminder workflow. It passively uses motion and sound to track sleep-related patterns, breathing, coughing and snoring, and environmental disturbances; Google states that the feature is not intended for medical purposes. Nest Hub displays sleep stages and sound disturbances in a timeline, and processed measures such as cough counts or snore minutes may be saved to Google Fit. Users can view a morning summary on the display or ask, “Hey Google, how did I sleep last night?”[64][69][82]

The Google workflow is therefore sensor-to-summary-to-insight: setup and calibration occur through the app and Hub display, data are passively collected, and feedback is supplied visually, through voice access, and through Google Fit. It does not include a documented spoken medication-taking confirmation, a user-facing correction sequence for health data, or haptic feedback.[83][82] **Google’s cited feature supports retrospective wellness awareness rather than time-critical medication action.**

For MCI specifically, the more relevant Google-based example is MATCHA, a research Google Action rather than a native Google health-tracking feature. MATCHA ran on a Google Home Hub with an 8 × 6 inch touchscreen that displayed spoken commands and responses so participants did not need to remember what they had said. It scheduled medication-status check-ins to each dyad’s routine—averaging twice daily, ranging from once to five times daily—and greeted the older person by name.[2]

MATCHA also has a medication-specific response structure: a reported dose taken receives cheering and affirmation; a dose not taken leads to an offer to repeat the check-in later; uncertainty leads to checking the pillbox before the conversation continues; and timeout sends a notification to both the member’s and care partner’s phones. When a response was not understood, it told the user that feedback had been sent to the research team, rather than documenting a spoken correction dialogue.[2] This sequence illustrates voice-plus-visual memory support and care-partner notification, while retaining established tools such as pillboxes and notes.[2]

Mabu differs from both commercial-assistant patterns because it is a dedicated, in-home robotic care companion. It uses tailored daily voice conversations about symptoms, treatment, and medication habits; medication reminders are embedded in this ongoing care-management relationship. Conversation length varies with the patient’s answers, and symptom responses can trigger advice to contact a doctor, a plan to check in the next day, or transmission of information to a clinician, nurse, or pharmacist.[74][73][78]

Mabu’s onboarding reportedly confirms the patient’s identity, asks about medication and usual dosing time, and then supports either a requested reminder or an everyday check-in beginning with how the person feels. Its focused disease- or treatment-specific conversations are explicitly distinguished from open-ended general-assistant dialogue.[6] **Mabu therefore combines prompting with response-contingent support and human-care-team escalation, whereas Alexa documents configurable prompting and Nest Hub documents passive sensing with feedback.**

The practical implication is to avoid treating the three systems as equivalent medication services. Alexa’s general medication skill does not record whether medication was taken; Mabu is described as tracking medication habits and relaying information to clinicians; neither description demonstrates medication adherence. Google Nest Hub’s cited tracking function concerns sleep and wellness, while MATCHA shows how Google Assistant infrastructure can support a distinct medication-status workflow for older adults with MCI.[46][64][74][78][2]

## Confirmation dialogs

### What do geriatric HCI studies find about confirmation dialog patterns?

Confirmation dialogs are exchanges in which a conversational agent checks what a user meant or intended to do. In geriatric HCI, this includes **system-initiated repair**—the system reconfirms a response—and **user-initiated repair**—the user asks for clarification or changes their answer. They matter for people aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), for whom remembering a medication event or producing the expected wording can be difficult.[1][85]

The MCI-focused MATCHA medication-check-in design illustrates a confirmation sequence that does not force a potentially inaccurate immediate answer. First, the assistant addresses the person by name, states that it is time for medication, and asks whether it has been taken; the initial prompt is also shown on the Google Home display, with touch response available.[1] Second, a reported Yes or Taking Now is acknowledged and ends the exchange.

Third, a No opens a deferral route in which the assistant helps schedule a later check-in. Fourth, Don’t Remember or Checking Pillbox prompts the person to inspect the pillbox; MATCHA then waits and continues along the taken or not-taken path.[1][9] Participants reported that these check-ins sometimes made them think about or confirm their status and, when unsure, check their pillbox before reporting completion.[2]

**This pattern confirms a self-report and supports uncertainty management; it does not verify ingestion or demonstrate improved medication adherence.** The deployment defined an interaction as one system utterance and the participant’s subsequent response, so even a response to Have you taken your medication? was not evidence that a dose was actually taken.[1] The design implication is specific: medication confirmation for people with MCI should include acknowledgement, deferral, and an external-memory route such as pillbox checking, rather than treating every prompt response as a reliable yes/no claim.[1][9]

Confirmation also has a burden trade-off. In the CAMMIA spoken-dialogue comparison, explicit, final, and implicit confirmation styles were implemented; users preferred a final confirmation with fewer confirmation turns when no error occurred, but preferred explicit confirmation when an error occurred.[86] A separate appointment-booking experiment with 48 users and nine dialogue systems likewise found that presenting more options per turn while avoiding explicit confirmation subdialogues made both older and younger users faster without reducing task success.[87] These are general spoken-dialogue results rather than medication or MCI findings, but together they support conditional confirmation: ask for an explicit check when recognition is doubtful or consequences are material, not after every uncomplicated exchange.[86]

Response format is part of the same design decision. Predetermined answers can communicate how to respond, improve input validity, and reduce speech-recognition ambiguity in health conversations.[35] Yet patients in other health-agent studies sometimes wanted free-text input to explain problems accurately; some valued its contextual detail, whereas others did not know how to phrase an answer the agent would understand.[88][35] In the Sofia questionnaire study, discrete numerical responses agreed more closely with paper WHO-5 answers than open-ended responses, whose phrases sometimes could not be mapped directly to a standard scale; open responses nevertheless supplied richer contextual information.[89] Thus, closed choices suit status reporting when the action paths are clear, while an available clarification route is needed when a person’s answer does not fit the offered categories.

Several older-adult studies propose ways to make confirmation less cognitively demanding. They recommend concise, memorable wording; an assistant that infers a likely request when an exact keyword is missed and verifies that interpretation; and visual display plus spoken repetition of the interpreted request.[90][20][91] Older adults also preferred step-by-step dialogue and simple sentence structures, while safety prompts can prevent unintended actions and preserve user control.[60] These are design recommendations, not comparative medication-adherence results.

Judgment — The practical confirmation rule is *confirm selectively, then preserve agency*: confirm uncertain or consequential interpretations, offer a visible and spoken account of what was understood, and give a low-pressure route to defer, inspect a pillbox, or correct the record. The evidence favors this balance over either relentless confirmation or unrestricted free-form reporting: it reduces the risk of an erroneous health record without turning a routine medication prompt into an exhausting interrogation.[1][9][86][35]

MATCHA’s Phase 1 logs also exposed a consequential response-category failure: a participant who said Taking Now initially received I don’t understand what you just said. The Phase 2 revision made Taking Now a separate scenario, distinct from an unknown response; reported medication taking then triggered cheering and positive affirmation. The original feedback comprised four seconds of loud cheering, four seconds of clapping, and verbal praise; after some participants found it overwhelming, Phase 2 used a two-second moderate cheer with the same verbal praise.[34][2]

Explicit confirmation asks the person to verify the system’s interpretation with a yes/no response; implicit confirmation repeats or incorporates the understood information while proceeding unless the person corrects it. In medication interactions, the item being confirmed may be the medicine, dose, scheduled time, or the person’s report that a dose was taken.

The controlled appointment-booking evidence supplies quantitative detail on the burden of routine explicit confirmation. Across 48 users and nine dialogue systems, 92% of tasks were completed successfully, and confirmation strategy and cognitive abilities did not affect task success.[92] Avoiding explicit-confirmation subdialogues reduced median dialogue length from 22 to below 13 turns for older users and from 17 to 9 turns for younger users; the one-option-per-turn strategy with a separate confirmation for each choice produced the longest dialogues, at 27 turns for older users and 23 for younger users.[93] Explicit confirmation did not improve appointment recall, including for users with lower working-memory span; lower information-processing speed, rather than working-memory span, was associated with poorer recall of appointment details.[93]

The appointment-booking experiment varied one, two or four options per turn alongside explicit, implicit or no confirmation, yielding nine dialogue strategies. It used a Wizard-of-Oz simulation: a human interpreted participants’ speech and controlled the dialogue rather than automatic speech recognition doing so.[92]

The same appointment study defines the alternatives precisely: explicit confirmation repeats a choice and asks whether it is correct; implicit confirmation repeats the choice within the next question; and no confirmation does not repeat it.[93] Its final appointment confirmation summarized the health professional, day, time, and location.[93] Older users produced longer dialogues and reported lower satisfaction than younger users on the study’s 1–5 satisfaction scale; shorter dialogues and higher cognitive-test scores were associated with higher satisfaction.[92]

The appointment-booking comparison involved older and younger adults rather than patients aged 65+ with MCI; its older-adult group began at age 50, and 26 older participants were recruited.[93]

## Error recovery

### What do geriatric HCI studies find about error recovery mechanisms?

Error recovery is the sequence used after a conversational system fails to hear, understand, act on, or adequately explain a user’s request. For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), this matters because memory, attention, language, and executive-function changes can make it harder to notice a failure, formulate a different request, and judge whether a later response solved the problem.[1] Medication-management systems therefore need to repair a breakdown without making the patient repeatedly remember a precise command or carry the whole burden of troubleshooting.

Judgment — In medication support, the recovery goal is not merely to end an annoying dialogue. It is to restore a safe path to recording, postponing, checking, or escalating a medication-related action while preserving the distinction between a self-reported dose and verified ingestion.

The most directly relevant deployment is MATCHA, a medication-management conversational assistant installed on Google Home devices for 20 weeks with seven older-adult–care-partner dyads with MCI. Researchers analysed 844 interactions and identified 324 breakdown instances.[94][1] The supplied participant description identifies MCI but does not give all participants’ ages, so these results should not be read as a result exclusively for the requested 65+ population.

The study is nevertheless valuable because it observes repair in the home over a period long enough for routines, care partners, and recurring failures to matter.

MATCHA used several explicit recovery paths. First, when a member said that they had not taken medication, MATCHA asked whether they wanted another check-in later and guided them to set a time.[2] Second, when a member did not remember whether medication had been taken, or needed to inspect a pillbox, it asked them to check and waited before continuing down the taken or not-taken path.[2] These sequences avoid treating uncertainty as non-adherence and make the existing pillbox part of the repair process.

Third, for an unknown spoken response, MATCHA told the person that it had sent feedback to the research team; the system description also states that it asked the user to repeat the last sentence.[2] This is a limited conversational repair: it acknowledges the failed input and requests another attempt, while routing the unfamiliar response for later system improvement.

Fourth, after a timeout without any response, MATCHA sent a phone notification to both the member and care partner that the routine had started.[2] The resulting model is distributed recovery: speech, a medication aid, a care partner, and a phone notification jointly support the task.

The Spanish Assistant on Care and Health Offline (ACHO) combines voice and text medication reminders with daily consumption records; when medication is forgotten, it places telephone alarms to family members or caregivers.[112]

MATCHA’s most concrete fallback was a **voice-plus-touch alternative**. Its second phase added four touchscreen responses—Yes I did, No I did not, I don’t remember, and Taking Now—on the Google Home Hub.[2] Touch responses accounted for 20–35% of all interactions for each dyad during the study, and participants described using a button when Google did not catch their speech.[2] **A visible response alternative can preserve medication-status interaction when speech recognition fails, because the MCI dyads used it routinely rather than only exceptionally.** The mechanism is touchscreen input and visual buttons, not haptic feedback.

The repair work was not confined to speech recognition. The 20-week MCI study classified four overlapping breakdown types: Language and Semantics, or wording, phrasing, and tone mismatches; Flow, or failures in turn-taking and in handling departures from a structured script; Historical Understanding, or inability to retrieve prior relevant information; and Explanations, or inability to answer context-sensitive why questions.[1] Historical Understanding appeared in 220 of 324 flagged instances (68%), Explanations in 139 (43%), Language and Semantics in 126 (39%), and Flow in 68 (21%); categories overlapped.[1] This broader taxonomy matters because a transcript can be captured accurately while the medication-support conversation still fails.

Participants’ repairs changed with the failure. For language and semantics problems they adjusted pronunciation, code-switched, repeated or reformulated speech, and sought clarification. For flow problems they restarted commands, interrupted, persisted, or waited. For historical-understanding problems they recalled prior details, adjusted information based on memory, or involved caregivers. Explanation-related failures most often prompted emotional disengagement or quitting.[1] Caregivers sometimes reformulated requests and supplied contextual information, making recovery collaborative rather than solely the patient’s responsibility.[1] This means an error-recovery design must support both a patient’s immediate next action and a care partner’s legitimate role in restoring context.

The study also found that simple medication-status exchanges worked more smoothly than personalized follow-up requests. Participants described the system as adequate when it asked whether medicine had been taken and received a yes-or-no response, but as failing when asked to schedule a later reminder or check another household member’s medication.[1] Participants also expected reminder changes to persist across days; one reported that a requested daily later reminder occurred once but did not continue.[1] A medication assistant should therefore recover not only the current utterance but also the ongoing routine that gave the utterance its meaning.

Evidence from a four-week Amazon Echo Dot in-home study of 15 older adults aged 66–94 shows why retry-and-rephrase alone is a weak recovery design. Researchers coded 2,552 Alexa query–response turns and classified 632 as errors (24.76%); 98.10% of those errors became conversational breakdowns.[23] The study did not characterize this sample as having MCI, so it is adjacent older-adult evidence rather than an outcome for the requested population.

The Alexa researchers separated user mistakes, speech-capture errors, and failures after accurate speech capture. Human errors included wrong wake word (49) and incomplete query (23). Speech errors included not listened (69), unintended triggering (15), partially listened (29), interruption (1), and inaccurate transcription (40). Voice-assistant errors comprised system failures (15), no response after accurate capture (57), inability to fulfil a request (130), and intent-recognition failures (204).[22] Intent recognition accounted for 32.3% of all errors and request-handling limitations for 20.6%, so the dominant problem was often not pronunciation but the assistant’s failure to infer or fulfil what the person meant.[23]

Reminder interactions illustrate this mismatch. The study reported that 35% of 251 reminder interactions failed, and 69 errors—78.41% of erroneous reminder interactions—arose when participants tried to stop a ringing reminder. Users often said natural phrases such as Thank you or I got it, whereas Alexa recognized more scripted commands such as stop, cancel, or I’m done.[23] In one example, Alexa did not understand I took my medicine or Yes I took my medicine, but it stopped the alert after Cancel the alert.[23] The practical consequence is that stopping an alert, acknowledging a reminder, and reporting medication taking can become separate actions even when the user treats them as one.

Most Alexa recovery was user initiated: moving on or giving up; repeating or reformulating; offering clarification; correcting the wake word; or restarting the task or conversation. Alexa sometimes prompted a person to complete or clarify an incomplete query.[23] Only 160 of 632 errors, or 25.47%, were resolved in the immediate next attempt.[23] When repeated retries were grouped with the original failed attempt, the study identified 346 distinct compound errors: 160 resolved and 186 unresolved, a 46.24% eventual resolution rate; 107 of 346 (30.92%) resolved with one retry.[23][22] Most recoveries occurred within the first few attempts, while additional retries produced fewer resolutions.[22]

The researchers found no clear improvement in error-resolution rates, recovery strategies, or overall error rates across the four weeks; errors remained between 20% and 30% of turns.[22] Participants commonly attributed failures to the assistant not understanding their request, insufficient time to respond, interruption, uncertainty about the next action, unclear system capabilities, and forgetting how to phrase a command.[22] **Repeated exposure did not turn repeated reformulation into a reliable recovery mechanism for these older Alexa users.** That result supports recovery prompts that explain what happened and offer a concrete next option instead of a generic failure message.

The same study points to a system-led alternative. Participants’ immediate verbal reactions, such as negation or comments, and their recovery attempts identified about 80% of errors, yet commercial assistants used these cues in fewer than 5% of cases.[22] Participants explicitly wanted Alexa to seek clarification when it was unclear about a request.[22] The authors recommend proactive, context-sensitive recovery: briefly explain the misstep, suggest a rephrasing, or ask for clarification.[22] This makes the assistant responsible for detecting the breakdown rather than requiring an older person to infer the hidden cause.

Other geriatric HCI work reinforces the importance of specific feedback. In a three-week Echo Dot deployment with seven older adults with low technology use, participants often repeated commands two or three times before giving up; four reported timeouts after the wake word, five had difficulty remembering feature keywords, and the logs contained 25 complex commands that Alexa did not understand.[20] Four of seven participants initially used Alexis rather than Alexa, and two continued doing so in weeks two and three.[20] The authors recommend feedback that distinguishes lack of system knowledge from failure to hear the user or background-noise interference.[20] Specific diagnosis makes it possible to choose a relevant repair rather than blindly repeat.

The field also distinguishes **system-initiated repair**, such as reconfirming a response, from **user-initiated repair**, such as clarification or adjusting one’s response.[1] A technical study of dementia-friendly interruption recovery further supports interactive clarification over system guessing: when a mid-sentence pause truncates an utterance, an incremental clarification request asks the person to supply the missing continuation rather than predicts it.[95] On the SLUICE test set, the interactive pipeline answered 45.63% of questions correctly, only 0.77 percentage points below the 46.40% upper baseline given complete questions; prediction-based pipelines performed worse because they made arbitrary guesses.[95] This is language-processing evidence, not a medication-management usability outcome, but it supplies a useful recovery principle: ask for the missing information rather than fabricate it.

Judgment — For medication reporting, recovery should be ordered from least demanding to most escalated: first make the system’s interpretation visible or audible; second offer a short clarification or repeat; third offer a bounded non-speech response; fourth allow a later check-in or care-partner escalation. The MATCHA sequence implements much of this order, while the Alexa findings show the cost of leaving repair primarily to repeated user reformulation.

Finally, recovery design faces a real trade-off between restricted and open input. Predetermined responses can improve input validity, clarify how to answer, and reduce speech-recognition ambiguity in health conversations.[35] Yet many patients prefer free-text input because it lets them express health complaints more precisely, and users have reported frustration and early disengagement with multiple-choice-only dialogue.[35][47] The most workable pattern is therefore a safe, simple set of status responses for routine medication check-ins plus an available route for clarification, contextual information, or human support when the routine does not fit.

Measured findings are that MATCHA dyads used touchscreen responses for 20–35% of each dyad’s interactions, including when Google did not catch speech, and that 186 of 346 distinct Alexa compound errors remained unresolved after retries.[2][22]

Across the 20-week MATCHA deployment, the 324 flagged breakdown instances amount to 38.4 breakdown instances per 100 analysed interactions (324 of 844).[1]

Medication adherence means taking medication as prescribed, not merely responding to or engaging with a reminder. When recovery could change a medication record, the assistant should confirm its interpreted intent and provide a way to cancel or undo the action.

MATCHA also supported recovery from an uncertain medication status: if a participant had not taken the medication, it offered a later check-in and guided selection of a time period; if the participant was unsure, it asked them to check and then respond again.[34] A care partner reported the recurrent recognition failure that this design addressed: “Sometimes Google doesn’t understand what I am saying, she keeps asking me to repeat.” The Phase 2 revisions extended the response timeout as well as adding touch buttons, transmission of unknown responses, and a request to repeat the last sentence.[34]

The recovery literature distinguishes an explicit nonunderstanding from a misunderstanding: a misunderstanding occurs when a system acts on an erroneous interpretation and is more detrimental to task success than nonunderstanding, where both parties recognize the trouble.[96] A system correction turn is a turn primarily devoted to resolving trouble caused by recognition or understanding failures or by illogical, contradictory, or undefined input; a user correction turn includes repeating information the system failed to extract.[96] Useful recovery measures therefore include the correction rate—the percentage of system or user turns primarily rectifying a problem—and the implicit-recovery measure (IR), the capacity to regain utterances only partly recognized or understood.[96] Dialogue-quality measures can also record time-out prompts, ASR rejections, help requests, cancel attempts, and barge-ins, normalized by total turns where appropriate.[96]

## Voice, visual, and haptic feedback

### What do geriatric HCI studies find about multi-modal feedback (voice

Multimodal feedback combines more than one sensory channel—for example, spoken prompts, an on-screen message, and vibration—to make an interaction easier to notice, understand, or complete. For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), this matters because a voice prompt can be missed or misrecognised, while recalling the exact prior exchange can itself be difficult. Voice can also reduce barriers from impaired vision, dexterity, and typing, provided that it is audible and has adjustable volume.[97]

In the MCI-specific MATCHA medication-management deployment, the Google Home Hub spoke personalised medication check-ins, addressed the member by name, and had pitch, volume, and timing adjusted for household layout and preferences. Its screen also displayed recognised commands and system responses, explicitly reducing the need for participants to remember what they had said.[9][2] **This establishes voice-plus-visual feedback as a memory-support and accessibility design, not as a demonstrated medication-adherence effect.**

The most concrete multimodal sequence was a fallback route. First, MATCHA asked a spoken medication-status question and displayed the same initial check-in; second, the user could answer by speech or select a visible touchscreen button: Yes I did, No I did not, I don't remember, or Taking Now.[1][2] The buttons supported recovery when speech was muffled, unrecognised, or otherwise difficult; touch accounted for 20–35% of each dyad’s interactions during the study.[9] The authors also caution that options must be adequately visible so that multiple modes do not prompt duplicate responses and confusion.[9] This sequence makes response completion less dependent on speech recognition while preserving speech as the principal interaction channel.

That pattern agrees with a 40-day real-world deployment with older adults: participants appreciated visual outputs but preferred replying by speech rather than touch.[17] An Echo Show 10 field study likewise found visual feedback advantageous, particularly for people with hearing impairments, although some participants were unfamiliar with touch interfaces.[60] Screen feedback is therefore complementary rather than interchangeable: it can preserve what was said, signal system state, and supply an alternative response route, while speech remains preferable for many routine replies.

Feedback also needs cognitive calibration. In MATCHA, four seconds of loud cheering, four seconds of clapping, and verbal praise after a reported dose overwhelmed some members with multiple daily medicines; the later design reduced this to a two-second moderate cheer with the same verbal praise.[9] In another older-adult conversational system, real-time visual icons and end-of-session charts were judged cognitively demanding, so the version for older users provided feedback through speech and text instead.[38] More feedback is not automatically better: concise, timely, and interpretable feedback is the useful form.

Haptic feedback—vibration or tactile alerts—has not been evaluated as part of the MATCHA voice-and-screen medication sequence. Wearables are proposed as a potentially more noticeable notification route than phones because they can provide wrist-based visual or haptic cues, but that is a design opportunity rather than a tested MCI medication intervention.[98] In a 2026 discrete-choice experiment among older adults, participants preferred prototype voice-plus-haptic reminders over screen-only reminders (preference coefficient 0.671, *p* < 0.001), and also preferred haptic-plus-screen reminders (0.265, *p* < 0.01). These were stated preferences, not observed medication adherence, and neither condition combined voice, screen, and haptics in one tested sequence.[99]

Evidence on voice itself is mixed: one cardiometabolic conversational-agent study found no effect of voice functionality, whereas users in another said hearing communication added depth relative to reading it.[47] **Taken together, the strongest result for older adults with MCI is the recovery sequence of spoken check-in, persistent on-screen prompt, and visible touch response when speech fails; haptic prompting remains a plausible but untested addition to that workflow.**[1][2][9][98]

Visual persistence addresses a specific working-memory property of voice dialogue: visual option lists can be scanned repeatedly, whereas auditory lists normally require the user to request replay. A prior interactive-voice-response study found that a graphical aid explaining the menu structure improved older users’ performance.[93] The 2026 review of AI-powered voice assistants for older adults likewise reports that auditory-plus-visual feedback is generally more effective than one feedback mode, recommends parallel visual and auditory methods for users with hearing loss, and distinguishes visual information consumption from input: users preferred a touchscreen during setup but voice-only interaction for routine use when screen placement made touch inaccessible.[100]

The documented Google Assistant/Nest Hub health-tracking pattern is also voice-plus-visual, rather than haptic. Sleep Sensing provides a morning on-screen sleep summary, weekly trends and personalised tips, access through the display’s Wellness page and Google Fit, and spoken access through Hey Google commands; the display also shows an indicator while tracking is active, while a hardware switch deactivates microphones.[84][101] Google Fit and Fitbit integrations add activity data to the Wellness display.[102]

Cross-device escalation is another multimodal reminder sequence. In the 90-day MedSentry intervention, blue lights around the medication bin and an audio alarm first alerted the participant; if no dose was taken within 30 minutes, a monitoring-centre advisor called; after three contact attempts over 45 minutes, the system left a voice message and called an optional caregiver.[43] The MedMinder ReMinder design similarly combined remotely programmed visual and/or aural prompts with uploaded medication-cup removal and refill times, caregiver alerts, and follow-up interventions.[43]

MAMS was designed as a companion-robot and wearable medication loop: it issues a reminder, asks conversationally for confirmation, recognizes medicine type and dose from images captured by the robot or wearable, and records the event for caregiver review in a shared mobile interface.[118]

MAMS’s 35-person evaluation lasted roughly 25 minutes and included 11 participants aged 51–80; convenience, usefulness, and overall ratings were 8.17, 8.49, and 8.23 out of 10, respectively. These are short-session usability ratings, not longitudinal medication-adherence or engagement measures.[118]

The MAMS companion-robot and wearable system issues medication reminders, asks for conversational confirmation, uses images captured by the robot or wearable to recognise medicine type and dose, and records the event. Caregivers can create, modify, and review reminders through a shared mobile interface, while care recipients can set reminders by voice.[118]

Haptic cues can provide a private attention signal when hearing difficulty or environmental noise makes an audio alert less reliable. Redundant cues can also create alert burden or confusion when the channels give conflicting indications of what action is required or what status was recorded.

A separate 2026 medication-adherence interaction survey, with 42 valid modality-preference responses, ranked visual-plus-auditory-plus-haptic feedback first for time-based reminders (31.0%) and safety-critical warnings (46.8%); visual-plus-auditory ranked second for these functions (22.2% and 19.8%, respectively).[103] For medication instructions, visual-only feedback ranked first (27.8%), followed closely by visual-plus-auditory feedback (26.2%).[103] This task-specific ordering supports matching feedback richness to the consequence of the message: the triple modality for reminders and warnings, and readily reviewable visual information for instructions.[103]

## Engagement beyond 90 days

Sustained engagement means continued use or response to the system; it is not proof that a medication was swallowed. For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the relevant question is whether a repeatable conversational sequence becomes tolerable and useful enough to fit daily medication routines.

### Which specific interaction sequences correlate with sustained engagement beyond 90

Judgment — No confirmation-dialog, error-recovery, or voice–visual–haptic sequence has a reported statistical association with post-90-day engagement specifically among patients aged 65+ with MCI. The closest result is a 20-week, seven-dyad deployment of MATCHA, a medication-management conversational assistant used through Google Home devices.[2]

MATCHA’s Phase-2 revised check-in bundle asked about medication action; accepted spoken or touchscreen responses—“Yes I did,” “No I did not,” “I don’t remember,” and “Taking Now”; recognized “Taking Now” as a distinct scenario; increased the response timeout; and replaced the original four-second loud cheer plus four-second clapping with a two-second moderate cheer while retaining verbal praise.[9] Across the full 16-week Phase 2, the revised bundle received 760 responses to 1,120 initiations, a reported response-based engagement rate of around 67%; this is a phase-wide ratio, not an engagement rate calculated only after day 90.[9][2]

The authors reported that weekly engagement rose at the beginning of Phase 2, remained sustained through that phase, and rose again near its end; they attributed this pattern both to the revisions and to growing comfort and familiarity with MATCHA in daily life.[9] All Phase-2 dyads expressed a desire to continue, and use reportedly continued to around 55 weeks.[9] **The strongest MCI-specific longitudinal signal therefore favors personalized, reflective check-ins over narrow reminders or alarms, while the combined revision prevents attribution of the increase to any one confirmation option, recovery feature, or feedback change.**[2][9]

A separate MCI caregiving-network study, summarized in a longitudinal scoping review, followed 26 people with neurologist-validated MCI and 26 care partners across three 10-week deployments. Use increased consistently, particularly in the third deployment, although recovery and personalization difficulties persisted.[25]

Recovery mattered within that sequence. In Phase 1, a person saying they would take medication immediately elicited “I don’t understand what you just said”; Phase 2 made “Taking Now” a dedicated scenario.[9] The later breakdown analysis characterized individual repair attempts rather than multi-turn repair trajectories, so it does not tie a particular repair path to retention after day 90.[1]

Prompt burden cut in the opposite direction: one dyad with five medication times daily withdrew before Phase 2 because frequent check-ins were tiresome.[9] Likewise, some participants found the original four-second loud cheer plus four-second clapping overwhelming, prompting replacement with a two-second moderate cheer and the same verbal praise.[9] These results make individualized cadence and feedback intensity part of the sustained-engagement sequence, not decoration.

For broader older-adult Alexa use, residents adopted alarms and other skills into routines for over a year and used recovery tactics such as rephrasing or waiting before repeating a request.[104] Those findings concern general Alexa use, not medication adherence.[104] MATCHA documents voice plus touchscreen feedback; no equivalent voice–visual–haptic sequence with a beyond-90-day engagement result is documented here for Amazon Alexa medication reminders, Google Assistant health tracking, or Mabu.

Using the reported response-to-initiation counts, MATCHA’s response-based engagement increased by 50.21 percentage points, from 84/476 in Phase 1 to 760/1,120 in Phase 2; the Phase 2 rate was 3.85 times the Phase 1 rate.[2]

Engagement can be measured as retention, number of conversations, reminder acknowledgements, continued weekly use, or completed medication check-ins; these measures are not interchangeable. A patient may take medication correctly without interacting often with the application, so neither engagement nor acknowledgement establishes medication adherence.

No post-90-day MCI study compares direct acknowledgement, yes/no confirmation, or repeated confirmation against continued use, response rate, retention, or completed check-ins. Nor does such a study compare repeat requests, clarification menus, undo functions, deferred reminders, or caregiver escalation as error-recovery paths linked to sustained use.

The MATCHA authors identify the clear, unambiguous, conversational check-in—integrated with a familiar Google Home routine—as the pivotal feature for long-term engagement. When unsure, members sometimes checked their pillboxes before reporting completion; this makes the sequence a prompt for reflection and verification rather than an acknowledgement of an alarm.[34][2] The authors report that contextual, conversational, personalized and gentle check-ins throughout the day increased confidence in maintaining medication routines and supported integration into existing Google Home use.[21]

The 20-week MATCHA logs contained 844 recorded interactions, defining an interaction as one medication check-in and the user’s response. Participants also asked MATCHA about earlier medication intake to track adherence over time.[21] The breakdown analysis identified four primary categories of conversational breakdown and found that older adults attempted to repair or realign the conversation after every breakdown by drawing on knowledge situated in their environment.[21]

Continuation extended beyond the formal 20-week deployment: all Phase-2 dyads expressed a desire to continue using MATCHA in their Google Home Hubs, and their use was continuing at around 55 weeks when the paper was written.[34][2]

A separate five-case, 18-month home-use series of a conversational companion robot included older people with MCI or late-onset psychosis. Daily operation persisted through brief, predictable sequences of greetings, date or weather information, and simple small talk despite slow, off-topic, or repetitive replies; four of five participants wanted to continue using the robot after the study.[37]

## Longitudinal task performance, errors, and confidence

Longitudinal usability measures matter because an acknowledged reminder, a completed dialogue task, and confidence in using a system are different outcomes. For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the available medication-management study measures response engagement and conversational breakdowns rather than verified medication-taking task completion.Judgment — Prompt verbosity means how much spoken information a system gives in each turn; confirmation strategy means how the system checks that it heard, understood, or correctly inferred a user’s intended response.

### How do voice-input errors differ across prompt verbosity levels in

For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), no longitudinal usability study reports medication-task completion separately for concise versus verbose prompts.[9] In the 20-week MATCHA medication-management deployment, Phase 1 recorded 84 responses to 476 system initiations over four weeks (about 18% engagement); Phase 2 recorded 760 responses to 1,120 initiations over 16 weeks (about 67% engagement). The authors describe this as an interaction-to-initiation engagement ratio, and Phase 2 combined revised design with greater user familiarity.[9]

**Adjacent evidence—other healthcare dialogue tasks.** Diabetes telemonitoring studies reported overall successful task completion of 90.4% at eight weeks and 92.2% at 16 weeks. These were different systems and samples, not prompt-verbosity conditions and not medication-management studies in people with MCI.[105]

Adjacent evidence—response format, not verbosity. In a WHO-5 wellbeing-questionnaire study, open-ended spoken responses took 57.62 seconds (SD 10.99), versus 68.31 seconds (SD 19.28) for discrete numeric responses. The discrete design had significantly higher habitability, meaning a better match between the user’s conceptual model and the system, and its responses correlated more strongly with paper WHO-5 responses (ρ=.91, 95% CI .81–.96) than open-ended responses (ρ=.77, 95% CI .51–.90).[89]

No longitudinal result identifies a prompt-verbosity level that improves medication-task completion in MCI. The response-format comparison shows faster open-ended completion but greater habitability and closer agreement with a structured paper measure for discrete responses.[9][89]

In an adjacent medication-reminder recall experiment, perfect recall fell by 70.3 percentage points when spoken reminders listed four medicines rather than one (4.5% versus 74.8% of reminder trials).[106]

### How do voice-input errors differ across confirmation strategies in longitudinal

MATCHA’s 16-week MCI deployment achieved about 67% response-based engagement, not a medication-task completion rate by confirmation-dialog condition.[9] In other health dialogue studies, one scripted diabetes scenario study reported 97% successful task completion with a 25% confirmation rate; a hypertension telemonitoring study reported 80% completion with confirmation questions in 35% of dialogues.[105]

**Adjacent evidence—appointment booking.** In a comparison involving 26 older and 24 younger adults using nine spoken dialogue systems, systems that presented more options per turn and avoided explicit confirmation subdialogues enabled faster appointment booking without reducing task success.[87]

This makes a practical distinction: explicit confirmation adds turns that may slow a simple, low-risk task, whereas confirmation remains potentially valuable when an inferred medication status, dose, or timing is safety-critical.Judgment — Medication interfaces should reserve confirmation for consequential or ambiguous input, rather than require it after every routine acknowledgement.

### What patient-reported confidence scores do longitudinal usability studies reveal across

MATCHA participants repaired Language and Semantics problems through repetition, reformulation, clearer pronunciation, and clarification. They addressed Flow failures by restarting, interrupting, or waiting; they involved caregivers or supplied remembered context when the system did not retain history. Disengagement or quitting was most prevalent during Explanations failures.[1]

Disengagement or quitting was most prevalent when explanation failures occurred.[1] These results show that voice failure in MCI is not merely automatic speech recognition: it includes loss of dialogue context and inability to explain system behaviour.

A four-week in-home Alexa study of 15 older adults, not identified as having MCI, found 632 erroneous turns among 2,552 query–response pairs (24.76%); 98.10% became conversational breakdowns. The coded errors included wrong wake word (49), partial query (23), not listened (69), unintended trigger (15), partially listened (29), interruption (1), transcription (40), system failure (15), not handled (57), limitation (130), and intent-recognition failure (204).[23][22] Intent-recognition failures comprised 32.3% of errors, and unsupported-request limitations comprised 20.6%.[23]

The same study found that most recoveries happened in the first few retries. Across 346 distinct compound errors—an original error plus its repair attempts—160 were resolved and 186 remained unresolved; partial-listening errors had no successful resolutions in the reported ChatGPT-skill examples.[22] Participants described uncertainty about whether the assistant was processing, what had failed, and how to proceed when the system gave little feedback.[22]

**Adjacent evidence—12-week study of 20 senior households.** The overall command-error rate was 4.9%, with 13 households below 5%. Logs recorded answer-not-known responses in 7% of commands and incorrect responses attributed to command wording in 5%; participants repaired occasional speech-recognition problems by repeating or reformulating commands. These results were not stratified by prompt verbosity.[60]

MedBuddy use illustrates the medication-specific consequence of these failures. Users reported difficulty opening the Alexa skill, Alexa opening a similarly named skill, repeated commands needed to stop prompts, logging errors, and spoken taken/missed reports that were not recognized or passed to the intended skill; the latter could produce one or two additional reminders after a user had reported taking medication.[46] **A medication system should therefore distinguish “speech not heard,” “request understood but unsupported,” and “medication status ambiguous,” because each needs a different recovery path.**[23][22][46]

MATCHA members with MCI described feeling confident or assured about their medication routines after the assistant became part of their Google Home Hub routine. One member said the check-in reduced the pressure to remember pills. These are qualitative interview reports rather than numerical confidence scores.[2]

The closest repeated-use numerical confidence result came from nine LISSA users after ten sessions. On a five-point agreement scale, they rated “I felt very confident using the program” at a mean of 4.25 (SD 0.43).[38] In a separate five-person pain-reminder subsample, the mean PROMIS Self-Efficacy for Managing Symptoms T-score was 55.5, ranging from 48 to 64; this measures confidence in managing pain symptoms, not confidence in operating a conversational interface.[15]

On the PROMIS Self-Efficacy for Managing Symptoms instrument used in the four-week Google Assistant study, a T-score above 50 indicates higher-than-average symptom-management self-efficacy.[15]

These measures answer different questions: MATCHA captured perceived assurance about a medication routine, LISSA captured confidence using a program, and PROMIS captured symptom self-management efficacy. Healthcare conversational-agent studies have also used inconsistent technical and user-experience measures, often with non-validated or custom questionnaires.[2][38][15][105][107]

No longitudinal MCI study reports patient confidence separately for short versus detailed voice prompts or for alternative confirmation strategies. Confidence in operating the conversational interface, confidence in managing medication, and confidence that a dose record is correct are distinct outcomes.

### Synthesis

The longitudinal pattern is clear. MCI-specific work shows sustained response engagement over 16 weeks after MATCHA’s redesign, alongside breakdowns involving language, dialogue flow, memory, and explanations.[9][1] Broader older-adult studies show that many failures arise after speech is captured—especially intent recognition and unsupported requests—and that repeated retries rapidly lose value.[23][22] Response-format evidence points in two directions: open-ended responses can be faster, while constrained responses can be easier to understand and align more closely with structured health data.[89]

1. Use short, structured prompts for routine medication check-ins, with an immediately available alternative response mode when speaking fails.
2. Confirm ambiguous medication-status input, but avoid mandatory explicit confirmation after every unambiguous routine response.
3. Give recovery feedback that states whether the problem was hearing, interpretation, capability, or system processing, and offer a next action rather than a generic failure message.

## What remains unresolved

For patients aged 65+ with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the requested comparison cannot currently be made as a like-for-like longitudinal comparison of medication adherence, application engagement, task completion, voice-input errors, and patient-reported confidence. The important distinction is between *medication adherence*—taking a medicine according to the prescribed regimen—and *engagement*—continuing to respond to or use the application. A reminder response, an opened pill bottle, and an actual dose taken are different events.

### Can longitudinal findings on adherence

The closest long-duration medication-management evidence for MCI is not a general Google Assistant health-tracking feature. It is MATCHA, a custom Medication Action To Check-In for Health Application deployed on Google Home Hubs through the Google Assistant backend for older-adult–caregiver dyads. In its second phase, five dyads used the revised system for 16 weeks, which exceeds 90 days; MATCHA generated 1,120 initiations and recorded 760 responses, reported as response-based engagement of about 67%. All participating Phase 2 dyads expressed a wish to continue after the study, and use was reported at around 55 weeks. These outcomes document interaction with the assistant and intended or reported continued use, not medication-taking adherence.[2]

MATCHA’s 67% response rate is meaningfully higher than its Phase 1 rate of about 18%, calculated from 84 responses to 476 initiations over four weeks. The authors linked the change to both a bundle of design revisions and increasing comfort and familiarity as MATCHA became part of daily life. The revisions included reduced positive feedback, a touch-screen alternative to verbal responding, a longer response timeout, and a distinct Taking Now response following a medication check-in. **Thus, the observable longitudinal result is higher response-based engagement after a redesigned check-in service, not proof that one confirmation, verbosity, feedback, or recovery sequence caused either engagement or medication adherence.**[2]

A subsequent 20-week MATCHA deployment provides the most detailed MCI-focused account of breakdowns during long-term voice interaction. Researchers analysed 844 interactions from seven older-adult–caregiver dyads and flagged 324 breakdown instances: language and semantics, flow, historical understanding, and explanations. They characterized repair attempts within those instances rather than multi-turn repair trajectories. This identifies the types of interaction trouble that a long-term medication assistant must handle, but does not yield rates of voice-recognition errors by prompt verbosity or confirmation strategy, nor identify a repair sequence associated with use after 90 days.[1]

The Google-based MCI studies are also not representative of all patients aged 65+ with MCI. Participants belonged to a cognitive program, most had already interacted with a conversational assistant for more than a month, and every study unit was a member-carepartner pair. These circumstances matter: a carepartner can assist with interpreting, responding to, or recovering from a voice interaction, so dyadic response rates cannot be assumed to describe independent patient use.[2]

Amazon Alexa supplies evidence of long-term routine incorporation, but not the requested medication-and-MCI outcome set. Ten residents in one care community who had used Alexa for at least a year described incorporating learned skills such as alarms, music, and games into everyday routines. The study did not collect health or disability information, so it cannot identify an MCI subgroup; it also reported no medication adherence outcome and did not isolate a causal conversational sequence.[104] A purpose-built Alexa-compatible medication skill, MedBuddy, was evaluated over 60 days, below the requested 90-day threshold, without a longitudinal comparison of prompt verbosity or confirmation strategies.[46]

Google Assistant reminder research outside MCI demonstrates why reminder timing must not be equated with ingestion. In a four-week feasibility study of cognitively unimpaired adults aged 55+, five women aged 56–80 had medication-event monitoring system (MEMS) caps, which record bottle opening rather than confirmed ingestion. Their adherence percentages ranged from 82% to 100%; among 168 reminders, 14.6% preceded an opening within five minutes and 63.7% within 30 minutes. The mean absolute reminder-to-opening latency was 55 minutes (SD 100 minutes), and the study counted medication opened before a reminder in the same absolute-latency measure. These descriptive results are neither specific to patients aged 65+ with MCI nor evidence that Google Assistant health tracking, confirmation design, or reminder acknowledgment improved adherence.[16]

No Mabu-specific longitudinal outcome series in patients aged 65+ with MCI is reported here for medication adherence, task completion, voice-input errors, or confidence. Broader long-term companion-robot evidence cannot substitute for Mabu: an 18-month case series involved five women aged 85–90 with MCI or late-onset psychosis using RoBoHoN, a different conversational robot, and did not separate diagnoses. All used the robot in daily life, four wanted to continue, and usability assessments found high satisfaction and ease of use; these are acceptability findings rather than medication outcomes or a tested interaction sequence.[36]

Across healthcare conversational-agent studies, the reported measures do not resolve the missing comparison. A systematic review found task-completion proportions of 80%–90% for three agents, but these were technical performance measures, not medication-adherence outcomes and not results stratified by prompt length or confirmation style. Satisfaction was high wherever measured, but only one study used a validated questionnaire; two studies described moderate ease of use with unreported or nonvalidated instruments. Consequently, these measures cannot be converted into comparable patient-reported confidence scores for Alexa, Google Assistant, and Mabu.[105]

At the aggregate level, the direction for trial retention is also mixed rather than a platform effect. A meta-analysis of 14 health-chatbot trials found no statistically significant difference in loss to follow-up between chatbot and comparator arms, despite an overall reported effect size of 0.99 (95% CI 0.95–1.03) and substantial heterogeneity. One included trial had higher chatbot-arm retention (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.03–1.67), whereas another had lower retention (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.92). Loss to follow-up means failure to return trial questionnaires; it is not medication adherence and may not indicate whether a person stopped using an application.[49][107]

Judgment — A defensible cross-system study would hold the medication regimen, MCI diagnostic criteria, caregiver role, reminder schedule, and adherence measure constant; randomly vary concise versus detailed prompts and explicit versus implicit confirmation; and continue observation through day 91 and thereafter. It would report, for each assigned interaction sequence and at each follow-up point after day 90, five separate denominators: medication implementation as implemented doses out of prescribed dose opportunities; delivered prompts out of scheduled prompts; answered prompts out of delivered prompts; completed dialogues out of answered prompts; and unresolved or wrongly recorded medication responses out of completed dialogues. Medication adherence includes treatment initiation, implementation of the prescribed regimen, and discontinuation, so implementation must remain distinct from a reminder response or dialogue completion.[26][31] Family members living separately or a caregiver or visiting nurse would prepare the medication records and fill the medication device at setup; the designated caregiver or visiting nurse would check remaining medication and reconcile unresolved or wrongly recorded medication responses, as caregiver-supported MCI arrangements have documented medication filling and medication checks.[13][31][1] The study would also measure repair type and a validated confidence measure. That design would separate a patient’s medicine-taking from their interaction with the device.

1. Treat MATCHA’s 18% and 67% figures as response-based engagement, not medication adherence.
2. Do not transfer Alexa routine-use findings or general chatbot retention findings to MCI medication management.
3. Do not rank Alexa, Google Assistant, and Mabu on confirmation, verbosity, error recovery, multimodal feedback, task completion, or confidence until they are tested with common longitudinal measures in the same 65+ MCI population.

MATCHA’s 20-week deployment was an MCI dyad study rather than a sample consisting exclusively of patients aged 65+: member ages ranged from 62.6 to 85.3 years, six of seven members were at least 65, and four of the five members continuing into Phase 2 were at least 65.[34]

### Open questions

No identified comparison establishes which Alexa, Google Assistant, or Mabu interaction sequence sustains engagement beyond 90 days among patients aged 65+ with diagnosed MCI, or whether prompt verbosity, confirmation strategy, and voice-plus-visual-plus-haptic feedback change longitudinal task completion, voice-input errors, patient confidence, or verified medication adherence. This would be settled by a common, at-least-90-day study in that population that randomizes or otherwise compares these sequences, records repairable voice-recognition and user-input errors, separates reminder response from electronically or otherwise independently measured medication implementation, and reports confidence and retention using the same measures across systems.
