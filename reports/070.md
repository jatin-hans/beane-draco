# Hayes’s Remote-Work Promise and Summary Judgment

## Executive summary

Hayes is unlikely to obtain summary judgment compelling an ongoing full-remote arrangement, although the April promise, June confirmation, and relocation evidence could support a trial issue and a narrower reliance remedy.

The signed May 1 letter’s hybrid-schedule term and bold integration clause strongly undermine enforcement of an earlier oral full-remote assurance, particularly because the manager lacked authority to alter standard terms.

Hayes has substantial evidence of detriment in the May 15 move, including the $40,000 home-sale loss and Texas lease, but those expenditures must have been actually and reasonably induced by the pre-May promise rather than the written hybrid offer.

The unobjected-to remote work through June and the two written approvals given to other engineers support Hayes’s factual case, but neither conclusively establishes that he had an authorized individual exception or that the July 1 three-day office policy could not apply.

The stronger likely outcome is denial of Hayes’s request for full-remote specific enforcement, with a factfinder potentially considering reliance-based relief if Hayes proves an authorized or reasonably relied-on recruiting promise and causation of his relocation losses.

## Applicable law and written terms

This dispute turns first on what law governs and what the signed offer letter did to the earlier recruiting discussion. Those questions matter because promissory estoppel is a reliance-based doctrine: it may protect a serious promise that induced a detrimental change of position, but it does not ordinarily permit a court to disregard a final written agreement merely because an earlier oral assurance was more favorable.

The immediate written-document issue is stark. During the April 2024 negotiations, the manager allegedly promised Hayes full remote work to induce a Seattle-to-Austin relocation; the May 1, 2024 letter instead stated hybrid schedule per company policy and expressly superseded prior verbal representations.[1] Whether that wording is an unambiguous rejection of full remote work, or an incomplete description of a schedule that may be clarified by context, is consequential to both contract interpretation and the later reasonable-reliance analysis.

### What state law potentially applies to Hayes’s claim?

No stated term selects Texas, Washington, or any other state’s law. Hayes’s move from Seattle to Austin is therefore a factual contact in a choice-of-law analysis, not an express contractual selection of Texas law. Under the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws, contract issues are governed first by a valid law chosen by the parties under § 187 and otherwise by the state with the most significant relationship to the particular issue under § 188. Relevant contacts include the places of contracting, negotiation, and performance; the location of the subject matter; and the parties’ residences, domiciles, places of incorporation, and places of business. Those contacts are weighed by their importance to the particular issue, rather than simply counted.[2][3][4][5]

**Texas is the leading candidate for the remote-work dispute because the alleged arrangement concerned performance of employment from Austin, but Washington remains a genuine competing candidate because Hayes lived in Seattle and the April recruitment negotiations occurred before the move.** The Restatement gives the state where contractual performance is to occur an obvious interest in the performance and in the party who performs there. It also provides that the law of the place of performance ordinarily governs questions concerning performance details. A dispute over whether a software engineer could work remotely or had to attend an Austin office concerns precisely such a performance detail.[3]

If Hayes was expected, at the time of contracting, to perform the substantial part of the job from his Austin home, Texas has a strong substantive connection: the claimed full-remote term would determine where his work was performed, and the later three-days-per-week office rule would determine the required Austin attendance. The Restatement’s services-contract rule likewise treats the place where the major portion of services is rendered as an important contact.[3][5]

Washington’s connection is not erased by the move. Hayes was located in Seattle during the April negotiations, the alleged inducement was directed at persuading him to relocate from Washington, and the loss on the Seattle home sale arose there. The place of negotiation is a significant contact because the state has an interest in negotiations and the agreement resulting from them. It is less important if negotiations were conducted from separate states by telephone, email, or other remote means, as may often occur in hiring, but that qualification does not make the pre-move Washington connection disappear.[3]

The place of contracting alone is relatively insignificant under the Restatement, particularly if negotiations were geographically dispersed. The record as stated does not identify where Hayes accepted the May 1 offer, where the manager was located, the company’s headquarters or principal place of business, the company’s state of incorporation, or whether the parties understood that Hayes would perform primarily from Austin at the moment the offer was accepted. Those facts matter because the significance of party residence, domicile, incorporation, and business location depends on the particular issue and on how those contacts combine with performance and negotiation contacts.[3]

If the action is filed in Texas state court, Texas choice-of-law principles are the likely procedural starting point. Texas courts use the Restatement’s most-significant-relationship approach absent a statutory directive. Restatement § 6(2) directs consideration of the needs of interstate and international systems; forum policy; other interested states’ policies and relative interests; justified expectations; the field’s basic policies; certainty, predictability, and uniformity; and ease of determining and applying the law. In the absence of an effective choice, § 188(2) directs consideration of the places of contracting, negotiation, and performance; the subject matter’s location; and the parties’ domiciles, residences, nationalities, incorporation, and places of business.[6][2][7][8]

If the action is instead brought in federal court on diversity jurisdiction, the federal court applies the forum state’s choice-of-law rules to identify substantive law, while federal procedural law governs the summary-judgment process. Thus, a federal court sitting in Texas would use Texas choice-of-law rules; a federal court sitting elsewhere would begin with that forum’s rules.[4][9][10]

The Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187(1) recognizes party autonomy: the law of the state chosen by the parties governs a contractual-rights-and-duties issue that they could have resolved by an explicit contractual provision. Texas has adopted § 187.[3][6] Even then, the choice can be displaced in defined circumstances, including where the selected state lacks a substantial relationship or applying its law would violate a fundamental policy of a materially more interested state whose law otherwise would apply.[11][4]

There is no stated governing-law clause in Hayes’s offer letter. The Seattle-to-Austin relocation therefore cannot be treated as an implied choice-of-law term, and no conclusion that Texas law governs can rest solely on the fact that Hayes moved to Texas. The relocation is important as a performance and injury contact, but it is not the same thing as an agreed contractual choice.[2][3][11]

Texas and Washington supply materially similar but differently stated frameworks. The Texas Supreme Court in English v. Fischer states three requisites: a promise, foreseeability of reliance by the promisor, and substantial reliance by the promisee to the promisee’s detriment. Washington requires a promise, reasonable expectation of a change of position, an actual change of position, justifiable reliance, and injustice avoidable only by enforcement.[12][13][14]

That difference matters mainly in how directly each jurisdiction expresses the final equitable inquiry. Texas cases commonly list three elements, while the Restatement formulation adopted in Wheeler v. White includes enforcement only where injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. Washington makes justifiable reliance and the injustice requirement express parts of its stated five-part test.[15][16][17][18]

Texas and Washington are both plausible potentially applicable laws. The governing law should be selected only after the forum applies its conflicts rules to established contacts, including the forum, the place and circumstances of negotiation and contracting, the employer’s relevant contacts, and the intended place of performance when the offer was accepted. If Hayes was expected to perform substantially from an Austin home, Texas has a strong performance connection; Washington has a substantial connection through Hayes’s Seattle residence during the April negotiations and the alleged pre-move inducement.[3][5]

The Texas Supreme Court’s 1990 decision in DeSantis gives additional weight to the Austin-performance contact. DeSantis concerned enforcement of a noncompetition agreement, and the court held that Texas had a materially greater interest than Florida in that agreement’s enforceability and that Florida law conflicted with Texas fundamental policy.[19] In that employment case, although negotiations had partly occurred in Florida, the employee signed in Houston and both parties performed in Texas; the Court described the agreement’s gist as personal services in Texas and stated that this factor was, as a rule, conclusive on the governing law in that case.

If Hayes’s contemplated services were principally to be rendered from Austin, DeSantis strengthens the conclusion that Texas has the strongest disclosed connection to the work-location issue.[20][19]

Agency authority is predominantly a matter of state law. The unresolved Texas–Washington choice-of-law question therefore matters to whether the manager had authority to promise a full-remote arrangement. Apparent authority depends on what the company communicated to Hayes that reasonably conveyed its consent to the manager’s action; the manager’s own assertion of authority does not create apparent authority.[21][22]

Agency authority is predominantly a matter of state law.

### What are the relevant promissory estoppel rules?

Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine that may make a promise enforceable even though ordinary contract formation is incomplete or traditional consideration is absent. Consideration means a bargained-for exchange of value. Promissory estoppel instead focuses on a promise that reasonably induces reliance: conduct taken because the recipient trusted the promise.[23][24][25]

The Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 provides the general formulation: a promise is binding when the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce action or forbearance, it does induce that action or forbearance, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The same provision states that the remedy may be limited as justice requires.[26][27]

Texas follows the Restatement approach. In Wheeler v. White, 398 S.W.2d 93 (Tex. 1965), the Supreme Court of Texas stated that Texas follows Restatement of Contracts § 90 and quoted the then-current formulation requiring a promise expected to induce definite and substantial action or forbearance, actual inducement of that action or forbearance, and enforcement necessary to avoid injustice.[15][28]

Texas cases often express the doctrine in a shorter three-part form: a promise; foreseeability that the promisee would rely; and substantial reliance by the promisee to the promisee’s detriment. A Texas appellate court has also held that promissory estoppel may be an affirmative cause of action for a person who reasonably relied to their detriment on an otherwise unenforceable promise, though it is commonly described as defensive in origin.[17][18]

The doctrine’s defensive character needs explanation. In Wheeler, the Texas Supreme Court said promissory estoppel does not create a contract where none existed before. Rather, it prevents a promisor from insisting on strict legal rights where doing so would be unjust. In ordinary language, the doctrine can stop a party from using a formal defect or legal entitlement as a shield after inducing another person to take a harmful, foreseeable step in reliance on a serious assurance.[29][28][30][15]

That equitable function does not eliminate the need for a real promise. A promise is a manifestation of an intention to act or refrain from acting in a specified way that reasonably justifies the recipient in understanding that a commitment has been made. Predictions, opinions, preliminary negotiations, and statements that the speaker will merely consider a future course usually do not qualify.[31][23][32]

The clear-promise factor is not met merely because an employee changed position. The asserted words must amount to a serious commitment. Scholarly treatment of § 90 identifies the central question as the quality of the promisor’s commitment, because every assurance may influence conduct but not every relied-on assurance is enforceable.[23]

Foreseeability is assessed when the promise was made. The question is whether the promisor had reason to know that the promise would cause action or forbearance of the sort the claimant took, not whether the promisor privately intended to be legally bound. Objective foreseeability governs.[15][31][23]

Actual inducement is also necessary in the ordinary case. The reliance must result from the promise, rather than from a decision already made for independent reasons. Courts distinguish a promise that shaped the recipient’s decision from one made after the recipient had already committed to the action.[38][39]

The phrase substantial detriment refers to a definite, reliance-caused worsening of the promisee’s position. Economic loss, expenses, relinquished opportunities, and resource expenditures can qualify. A claimant must connect the loss to the promise; a change of position that would have occurred anyway does not establish the causal reliance needed for estoppel.[31][38]

The fourth factor, injustice without enforcement, is a distinct equitable inquiry rather than a label automatically attached to any loss. Restatement § 90 comment b identifies relevant considerations: the reasonableness of the reliance; its definite and substantial character in relation to the relief requested; the formality of the promise; whether the setting supplied the evidentiary, cautionary, deterrent, and channeling functions of form; and policies favoring enforcement of bargains and prevention of unjust enrichment.[26][38][32]

“Formality” in this context means the extent to which the promise was recorded, carefully expressed, and given in a setting that tends to confirm it was serious. A signed offer letter and an individual written remote-work approval have high formality; a manager’s undocumented recruiting conversation has much less. Formality is not an absolute writing requirement, but it affects both the reliability of proof and the equitable case for enforcement.[23][26][38][40]

The Restatement (Second) differs from the earlier Restatement on the wording of reliance. The earlier formulation expressly required action or forbearance of a definite and substantial character. The later Restatement omitted those words from the black-letter text, though comment b still treats the definiteness and substantiality of reliance in relation to the remedy sought as important. Texas independently uses the phrase substantial reliance to the promisee’s detriment.[23][41][26][17]

The difference means that substantial loss remains important under Texas law and in the requested analysis even if one does not treat the older Restatement’s wording as an independent universal element. It is especially important where, as here, the claimant seeks a substantial ongoing remedy—full remote work—rather than a narrower reimbursement of reliance costs.[26][42][32]

Texas authority also distinguishes promissory-estoppel damages from ordinary contract expectation damages. Expectation damages give the claimant the benefit of the promised bargain. Reliance damages instead seek to restore the claimant to the position occupied before acting in reliance. In David McDavid Nissan Inc. v. Subaru of America Inc., the Texas Court of Appeals stated that promissory-estoppel damages are limited to the amount necessary to restore the injured party to the position they would have occupied had they not relied, rather than expected profits.[18]

The possible remedy matters to the requested full-remote arrangement. Section 90 expressly allows a court to limit relief as justice requires. Thus, even a claimant who proves a promissory-estoppel violation does not automatically receive the entire promised performance or an injunction requiring ongoing performance. The appropriate remedy can be narrower and tailored to the detrimental reliance.[26][42][38]

Texas authority is not entirely uniform in the abstract on the measure of relief. Wheeler is described as allowing reliance damages where an underlying loan agreement was too indefinite for contract enforcement. Other Texas appellate decisions have sometimes awarded expectation-style relief.[23][18][25] Scholarly accounts conflict: Yorio reports that courts routinely award specific performance or, when feasible, expectation damages in § 90 cases, that awards below expectation are relatively rare and attributable to a defect in the promise or proof, and that courts enforce promises rather than protect reliance in practice.[41] A different scholarly account states that reliance damages are not awarded in § 90 cases even when readily calculable and that expectancy is the routine measure of relief.[23] Section 90 nevertheless expressly permits a remedy limited as justice requires; for this relocation claim, the claimant must prove a concrete reliance loss and any relief should be tailored to avoid injustice rather than automatically grant an indefinite continuing workplace entitlement.[26][18]

At-will employment is a related limitation, not a complete answer. Texas generally presumes employment at will, meaning either side may end the relationship at any time absent a valid limitation. Texas appellate authority has been cautious about using promissory estoppel to convert an at-will employment promise into guaranteed employment.[30]

A promise about work location differs from a promise of permanent employment. It may be a specific condition of employment rather than a promise of continued employment for a definite duration. Still, an order requiring full remote work indefinitely can function as a substantial restriction on an employer’s ongoing management of an at-will workplace. That concern is central to the later remedy analysis in Summary judgment and relief.

**The governing framework therefore requires more than Hayes’s assertion that he moved and lost money: he must show a serious, foreseeable, and actually inducing promise; objectively reasonable reliance; substantial reliance-caused detriment; and an equitable need for relief, with the remedy limited to what justice requires.**[15][17][26]

Texas intermediate appellate authority is divided on whether promissory estoppel supplies affirmative relief in employment cases. The concurrence instead stated the familiar elements—promise, foreseeable reliance, and substantial detrimental reliance—and added that reliance must be reasonable and justified. This conflict makes Wheeler’s § 90 rule and the remedy question especially important if Hayes seeks an affirmative order requiring continuing full remote work.[43][44]

The Texas Workforce Commission’s employment-litigation guidance identifies promise or offer, detrimental reliance, reasonable reliance under the circumstances, employer knowledge that the offeree would rely, and damages beyond mere disappointment as the elements of promissory estoppel. That formulation reinforces that Hayes must establish an actual compensable loss, not merely disappointment at losing a preferred work arrangement.[45]

If Washington law governs, Clipse confirms that the five-element test requires proof of a promise, expected and actual change of position, justifiable reliance, and injustice avoidable only by enforcement. In its at-will permanent-employment setting, Clipse required a clear and definite promise of permanent employment subject only to discharge for just cause, and held that shared hopes for a successful employment relationship were insufficient. That heightened permanent-employment rule does not convert Hayes’s alleged work-location promise into a permanent-employment claim, but it confirms Washington’s insistence on a clear and definite commitment rather than aspirational recruiting language.[46]

Washington’s pattern instruction places the burden on Hayes to prove each promissory-estoppel requirement by a preponderance of the evidence.[47]

Wheeler also emphasizes that the induced conduct need not be performance of the promised arrangement itself: it can be a step the promisee must take before beginning performance, if the promise was designed to influence that conduct and the promisor knew of it. Relocation and the pre-employment transactions necessary to begin work can therefore be legally relevant reliance conduct, rather than merely acts performed after employment begins.[44][48]

Promissory estoppel does not require consideration or an independently enforceable offer or contract. The Restatement-based formulation instead requires that the promise reasonably be expected to induce action or forbearance, actually induce it, and cause a detrimental change of position; action taken before the promise cannot supply the required reliance.[49]

Moving, spending money, giving up employment, beginning performance, and forgoing a legal right are recognized forms of reliance that may cause detriment; the promisor need not receive a benefit. Thus, Hayes’s relocation-related conduct is the kind of conduct capable of constituting reliance if it was caused by the alleged April promise.[49]

A broken promise is distinct from promissory misrepresentation in tort. Tort liability for a promise requires that the promisor had no intention to perform when making it; promissory estoppel instead concerns a commitment that foreseeably and actually induced detrimental reliance.[50][49]

The 1987 article Beyond Reliance explains that courts considering an implicit bargain tend to treat reliance as sufficient when the promisee acted consistently with the bargain’s terms, and that reliance is a condition of liability in that setting. Hayes’s sale, lease, and move would therefore support the claimed arrangement only if they were conduct consistent with, and caused by, the alleged full-remote commitment.[50][49]

The same article identifies pre-employment cases in which an applicant resigned after receiving an apparently firm offer that the employer withdrew before the start date; those courts awarded reliance loss, including loss from leaving the former job. That treatment supports reliance-based compensation rather than automatic enforcement of the promised employment term.[50][49]

Whether injustice requires relief is a discretionary policy judgment that considers the seriousness of reliance, the promisor’s conduct, the promise’s clarity, and whether another remedy adequately addresses the harm. Section 90 permits reliance damages, partial enforcement, or another limited remedy rather than automatic full expectation relief.[50][49]

Agency authority is governed primarily by state common law and the Restatements of Agency. The manager’s authority to make a full-remote commitment is therefore governed by the applicable state agency law.[21]

Washington recognizes that a promise supporting a justifiable-reliance claim need not be written: the governing concept of promise includes written and oral promises, and the doctrine extends to oral promises.[14]

On summary judgment, the moving party must show no genuine dispute of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law; the court may not choose between competing credible evidence or draw reasonable inferences against the nonmoving party. A genuine dispute over whether the April assurance was made or unconditional, whether the manager had actual or apparent authority, whether Hayes knew of authority limits, or whether the sale and lease were caused by the assurance ordinarily cannot be resolved by crediting one side’s account.

### What is the effect of the May 1

The May 1, 2024 offer letter states hybrid schedule per company policy and leaves the division between remote and office work undefined. The term does not itself grant full remote work.[36][37]

In ordinary employment usage, hybrid work combines in-office and remote work, with employees splitting their workweek between the workplace and a remote location. A hybrid schedule ordinarily identifies the split: which days are remote and which are office days. It does not carry a universal number of office days.[51]

That ordinary meaning gives the company a substantial contractual argument. A written hybrid schedule does not say fully remote. Full remote work ordinarily means no regular office attendance; hybrid work ordinarily means a mix of office and remote attendance. The company can therefore argue that the offer letter affirmatively chose a mixed arrangement and omitted the very full-remote guarantee Hayes now seeks.[36][51][52]

The fact that the letter did not state a number of remote or office days is material, but it does not itself establish a right to work fully remote. Hybrid arrangements vary widely. General drafting guidance emphasizes that hybrid terms should specify the expected number of office days, designated in-office days, reporting location, and whether the schedule is fixed or flexible because hybrid otherwise can mean different things at different companies.[53]

The offer’s phrase per company policy is equally important. It links Hayes’s work schedule to an external policy rather than stating a fixed personal schedule in the letter itself. Under general contract principles, parties determine contractual obligations by express terms; when they leave an issue unaddressed, the governing contract law supplies the applicable rule. Here, the letter addressed the category of arrangement—hybrid—but did not state the frequency.[3][36]

The company can characterize the policy reference as an incorporation of the company’s work-location rules, including policy changes permitted by the employment relationship. Hayes can characterize it more narrowly: as incorporating the policy in effect on May 1, not authorizing the company to replace an individually promised full-remote arrangement through a later policy change. The offer itself does not state whether future amendments to company policy automatically alter the schedule, whether a manager could approve exceptions, or whether individual remote approvals override general policy.[36][3][54]

The undefined frequency creates a potential interpretation question, but not necessarily a legal ambiguity. Under Texas contract law, a court first decides whether the disputed provisions are ambiguous by examining the contract as a whole in light of the circumstances existing when it was signed. A contract is ambiguous only if, after that examination and application of construction rules, its meaning is uncertain and doubtful or reasonably susceptible to more than one meaning. A disagreement between the parties about its meaning does not itself create ambiguity; both proposed meanings must be reasonable.[9][8]

There are two plausible readings:

- **Company reading:** Hybrid schedule has an ordinary, definite meaning—some office work and some remote work. Per company policy means the employer’s policy governs the schedule details. On this reading, the term is unambiguous and incompatible with a promise of permanent full remote work. [51][36][37]

- **Hayes reading:** The letter specifies only that the company generally uses a hybrid model and leaves the actual frequency or personal exception unresolved. The alleged April assurance and subsequent conduct might then be relevant to clarify whether Hayes’s individual schedule was fully remote within a broader hybrid-policy environment. This reading depends on the court finding that the wording reasonably permits a personal exception rather than treating full remote as contradictory to hybrid. [36][9][54]

The company’s interpretation is stronger at the contract-text level. The word hybrid is not a blank term; it generally denotes a combination of office and remote work. The offer’s failure to identify the number of office days makes the arrangement less precise, but it does not naturally mean that Hayes was promised zero office days.[51][36]

Hayes’s interpretation remains legally significant because the letter also does not expressly state any minimum office attendance, any fixed schedule, any right to change the schedule by policy revision, or any exclusion of individual exceptions. An undefined hybrid term can leave factual questions about the parties’ intended frequency if the surrounding circumstances demonstrate that the parties attached an objectively reasonable special meaning to it.[36][55][9]

If the court finds the wording ambiguous, extrinsic evidence may be admitted to explain or clarify the essential terms. The factfinder then determines the parties’ intended meaning, and summary judgment on interpretation is generally improper because ambiguity creates a fact issue regarding intent.[9]

Extrinsic evidence used for clarification is different from evidence used to contradict a complete written term. Texas courts may consider objectively determinable circumstances surrounding a negotiated contract to help interpret language, but may not use those circumstances to change or contradict unambiguous terms.[9]

That distinction controls the use of the April assurance. If hybrid schedule per company policy has only one reasonable meaning—an arrangement involving both office and remote work—the alleged prior full-remote promise contradicts the written term. The April statement then cannot be used to make the written contract say what it unambiguously does not say.[9][27][56]

If the phrase is reasonably susceptible to a meaning that permits an individualized full-remote exception within a hybrid policy, the April discussion may be relevant to interpretation. It would not automatically become a separate enforceable oral agreement; rather, it could help establish what the parties meant by the incomplete schedule reference.[9][55]

The effect of the hybrid term also intersects with promissory estoppel. Texas authority described in the findings treats reliance on an oral statement as unreasonable as a matter of law if the statement is contradicted by the plain language of a binding written contract. The Employment Law Restatement commentary similarly reports that courts generally regard reliance on a prior oral representation as unreasonable where a later writing conflicts with it.[27][39]

This is a serious obstacle to Hayes, but it turns on the threshold interpretation question. If hybrid plainly excludes permanent full remote, the written term directly weakens both the substantive claim and the reasonableness of relying on the earlier oral assurance after Hayes signed the offer. If hybrid frequency is genuinely open to a full-remote exception, the conflict is less direct and reliance cannot be rejected solely by labeling the phrase hybrid.[9][27]

The offer letter is also relevant because promissory estoppel is generally unavailable under the stated Texas rule where an express contract covers the dispute’s subject matter. If the offer letter is a valid express contract that covers work location and scheduling, Hayes may not use promissory estoppel to rewrite that term. If, however, the letter leaves the individual remote-work commitment outside its scope or merely provides an incomplete policy reference, Hayes may argue that his reliance claim concerns a separate promise.[57][36][58]

That argument has a structural weakness. Work location and frequency are subjects that reasonable parties ordinarily would include in an offer letter if they intended a personal, permanent exception to a company’s hybrid policy. The existence of written remote approvals for other engineers, addressed in Promissory estoppel, reinforces the practical distinction between an individual written remote arrangement and an oral recruiting assurance.[59][54]

**The May 1 term gives the company the stronger written-terms position: it establishes a hybrid, policy-linked arrangement rather than a guaranteed full-remote position, although its failure to state frequency leaves a genuine interpretive question only if Hayes can show that full remote was a reasonable individualized meaning rather than a contradiction of hybrid work.**[36][51][9]

If Washington law governs, Washington’s context rule permits the court to consider the contract’s subject matter, the circumstances of formation, the parties’ later conduct, and the reasonableness of the competing interpretations when discerning mutual intent. But that evidence may illuminate the words used; it may not vary, contradict, or modify the written terms or establish Hayes’s unilateral understanding.[60]

Hayes sold the Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signed the Texas lease on May 15. The June email occurred after both May 15 transactions and can corroborate the alleged April promise, but cannot have induced either transaction.[36][37]

### What is the effect of the bolded “Integration and Merger”

The May 1 offer letter included a bolded Integration and Merger clause: “This agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations.” That is a merger clause, also called an integration or zipper clause. Such clauses state that the parties’ promises and agreements are those contained in the writing, rather than prior negotiations or outside representations.[36][1][59]

The clause directly addresses the April promise because it expressly supersedes prior verbal representations. Full remote work was allegedly promised in April; the offer letter was issued May 1. The timing therefore puts the asserted assurance squarely within the category the clause identifies.[36][1]

The legal doctrine associated with that clause is the parol evidence rule. Despite its name, it is a substantive contract-law rule, not merely an evidentiary rule. When parties assent to a writing as the complete and accurate expression of their agreement, the rule prevents either party from using earlier or contemporaneous outside agreements and negotiations to vary or contradict the writing.[59][13][61]

An integrated agreement is a writing intended as the final expression of one or more agreement terms. Under Restatement principles, whether an agreement is integrated is a preliminary legal question for the court. A writing that appears complete and specific is treated as integrated unless other evidence establishes that it was not intended as a final expression.[55][61][13]

A completely integrated agreement has a stronger effect. A binding completely integrated agreement discharges prior agreements within its scope. Even a binding integrated agreement discharges prior agreements to the extent that they are inconsistent with it.[55]

The bold merger clause strongly supports the company’s contention that the letter was intended as the final agreement regarding work arrangement. It states that the written agreement supersedes earlier verbal representations. If Hayes received and assented to the letter, the company can argue that the hybrid-policy term displaced the April oral full-remote representation.[36][1][55][59]

The company has an additional argument based on contradiction. If fully remote and hybrid per company policy are inconsistent terms, the parol evidence rule excludes the April assurance regardless of whether the writing is merely partially or completely integrated. A prior oral agreement that contradicts a later written contract is generally inadmissible to vary the writing.[59][9][62]

The question is not merely semantic. A promised permanent full-remote right would be a major employment term affecting where the engineer performs work and whether the company may require office attendance. It is the sort of term reasonable parties would naturally include in a signed employment offer, particularly where the employee says it induced relocation. The omission of such a term from a writing that instead says hybrid schedule per company policy supports the company’s integration argument.[53][59][63]

Hayes has two responses. First, the term may be a clarification rather than a contradiction. If hybrid schedule per company policy does not establish a particular office frequency, Hayes can argue that the April assurance supplies the missing frequency or shows the intended individualized exception. Prior negotiations may be admissible to establish whether a writing is integrated, whether it is completely or partially integrated, and what it means.[55][9]

Under the Texas collateral-agreement rule, a prior agreement may survive an integrated writing only if it addresses a different subject, is consistent with the writing, has separate consideration, and is the kind of agreement the parties would naturally make separately.[102][103]

Second, an ordinary merger clause is not necessarily an express no-reliance clause. A merger clause says the writing is the agreement; a no-reliance clause says that the party did not rely on statements outside the writing. Texas law, as described in the findings, distinguishes the two. An unambiguous, negotiated no-reliance waiver may conclusively negate reliance in an arm’s-length transaction, but an ordinary merger clause is not automatically equivalent to such a waiver.[56][59]

That distinction prevents overstatement of the clause’s effect. Hayes’s clause does not say that Hayes did not rely on pre-contract representations, does not name remote work, and does not state that the company disclaims any responsibility for recruiting statements. It says only that the agreement supersedes prior verbal representations.[36][1][56]

Still, the distinction does not materially improve Hayes’s contract-enforcement position if the court finds direct contradiction. Under the Texas direct-contradiction rule described in the findings, reliance on an oral promise directly conflicting with an express, unambiguous contractual term is unjustifiable as a matter of law. A merger clause is not itself a no-reliance clause, but the written hybrid term may independently make continued reliance on an earlier full-remote assurance unreasonable.[56][27][39]

The bold formatting makes the supersession warning conspicuous. If Hayes had received the letter before the May 15 transactions, its conspicuous warning supports the company’s argument that continued reliance on an omitted prior verbal assurance was not objectively reasonable.[36][1][56]

Bold formatting does not, however, resolve integration in every jurisdiction. Merger clauses are conclusive or generally conclusive of complete integration in some jurisdictions. In others, they are significant but fact-dependent evidence of integration. The governing law determines which approach applies.[59][32]

Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 140 provides that the local law selected under §§ 187–188 determines whether a contract is integrated in a writing and the effects of integration. That means the question cannot be answered by assuming that all states give merger clauses identical force.[2]

Texas contract law generally gives substantial effect to unambiguous final writings. The Texas summary-judgment authority provided states that extrinsic oral statements cannot be used to alter or contradict a valid, complete contract that is unambiguous on its face. Affidavits contradicting the plain meaning of a contract and barred by the parol evidence rule are not competent summary-judgment evidence.[9]

The Texas authority also recognizes the limits of this principle. Courts may consider objectively determinable circumstances surrounding a negotiated contract to aid interpretation; when the contract is ambiguous, extraneous evidence may be admitted to determine its true meaning. The court, not the jury, initially decides whether there is ambiguity.[9]

Hayes therefore cannot avoid the merger clause simply by recasting the April promise as promissory estoppel. The parol-evidence issue and the reliance issue are analytically distinct:

- **Integration/parol evidence:** This asks whether the April oral assurance may be used as a contractual term or as interpretive evidence in light of the final written offer. The merger clause is central here. [55][61][59]

- **Reasonable reliance:** This asks whether, after receiving and signing an offer that says hybrid schedule per company policy and supersedes prior verbal representations, Hayes could objectively continue to rely on the earlier assurance as a binding full-remote commitment. The written terms are central here too, but the inquiry is equitable rather than merely evidentiary. [27][39][26]

- **Remedial estoppel:** This asks whether the company’s reliance on the formal written term should yield because Hayes reasonably and foreseeably made a detrimental change of position. That question does not make the merger clause irrelevant, but it may affect whether strict enforcement of the written terms would be unjust. [29][32]

The evidence favoring Hayes is not nonexistent. General promissory-estoppel analysis recognizes that a merger clause does not always end the matter if credible proof shows an actual oral agreement, reliance corroborates its existence, the writing does not specifically negate the assurance, and the other party knew the claimant continued to regard the promise as binding.[32]

But these considerations are weaker here than in a case involving merely omitted or collateral terms. The offer did not simply fail to mention work location. It supplied a work-arrangement term—hybrid schedule per company policy—and expressly superseded prior verbal representations. The company can thus argue not only omission but affirmative replacement.[36][1][59][37]

The record’s unresolved facts remain important. If the company issued the same boilerplate clause to every employee, did not explain it, and knew that Hayes had accepted only because he believed full remote work remained promised, a court could treat the surrounding circumstances as relevant to whether the clause fully integrated the particular remote-work commitment. If Hayes negotiated the offer with sophistication and had the opportunity to request a written remote exception, the clause is correspondingly stronger. These are legal inferences from the integration framework, not established facts on the stated record.[32][59]

**The Integration and Merger clause is a strong, independent barrier to treating the April assurance as an enforceable contract term, and it substantially weakens reasonable reliance; it is not an express no-reliance waiver, but that distinction helps Hayes only if the hybrid term is genuinely ambiguous or the alleged full-remote commitment is shown to be separate from and consistent with the signed offer.**[36][1][55][56][9]

The offer’s clause is an integration clause, not language in which Hayes states that he did not rely on outside representations or that he relied solely on his own judgment. Under Italian Cowboy, that textual difference is material to whether the clause itself negates reliance. The company nevertheless retains its separate argument that the signed hybrid term directly contradicts the alleged oral full-remote promise and thereby makes post-signing reliance unjustifiable.[64][65][56][27]

The clause reaches only prior verbal representations by its terms. Hayes’s June email followed the May 1 offer letter and therefore is not itself a prior verbal representation superseded by that clause. A merger clause does not automatically defeat a later agreement or modification; whether the June email created or confirmed a later enforceable commitment is a separate question.[44][66]

Wheeler illustrates that distinction. Its written agreement declared that it voided and took precedence over prior agreements, yet the Texas Supreme Court held that estoppel pleadings stated a cause of action based on alleged assurances given after the parties signed the writing and urging the promisee to undertake preparatory work.[44][48]

If Texas law applies, Italian Cowboy Partners v. Prudential Insurance Co., 341 S.W.3d 323 (Tex. 2011), supplies direct Texas Supreme Court support for the distinction between a merger clause and a disclaimer of reliance. The court held that a merger clause alone does not bar a fraudulent-inducement claim and that an effective disclaimer of reliance requires clear and unequivocal language; the word rely does not appear in Hayes’s stated clause.[67]

Italian Cowboy does not make the clause immaterial. The Texas Supreme Court explained that merger language serves to supersede prior agreements and, when it shows an intent fully to integrate the agreement, invokes the parol-evidence rule that renders prior agreements on the same subject matter unenforceable. Hayes’s clause expressly says that the agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations, which directly supports that contractual consequence for the April assurance.[67][65]

The court also explained that whether an adequate reliance disclaimer exists, and whether clear contract language has a definite legal meaning, are questions of law for the court. If Hayes’s clause is evaluated as a reliance disclaimer, its wording does not expressly disclaim reliance; if it is evaluated as merger language, the court determines its integration and parol-evidence effect.[65][67]

A 2016 article states that courts have uniformly disallowed promissory estoppel from overcoming the parol-evidence rule.[27]

The company updated its policy on July 1, 2024, to require three office days each week. Hayes refused the requirement on July 8. The timing establishes that the employer imposed the policy requirement before Hayes’s refusal, not after it.[36]

The company’s position is that, if Hayes assented to the May 1 term, the July 1 policy identified the policy-linked hybrid schedule as three office days weekly, making his July 8 refusal noncompliance with that arrangement. Hayes’s position is that an enforceable individual full-remote exception would prevail over the general policy.[36][54]

That conclusion depends on the scope of the incorporation. The stated facts do not identify the policy in force on May 1, whether that policy then prescribed a particular office schedule, whether it reserved a right to change hybrid schedules, or whether the offer incorporated future revisions as well as existing policy. The phrase per company policy strongly points to policy governance, but it does not itself expressly state that all future policy changes automatically modify every employee’s individual work arrangement.[36][3]

The distinction is important because a general company policy and an individual work-location agreement are different legal instruments. A hybrid policy typically states the general eligibility, schedule, office-day, and business-need rules that govern a workforce. An individual agreement records a particular employee’s specific arrangement. General policy materials describe hybrid policies as documents explaining how employees divide work time between workplace and remote locations and note that policies may be paired with per-employee agreements recording each person’s arrangement.[54]

The existence of written remote approvals for two other engineers, addressed in Promissory estoppel, may support the inference that the company used individualized approvals when it intended to grant a personal remote exception. It does not itself establish the terms of Hayes’s arrangement, but it makes the absence of a comparable written approval legally significant when interpreting the policy reference and the claimed oral exception.[54][68]

The company may ordinarily modify workplace policies in an at-will employment setting, particularly where the offer expressly ties the employee’s schedule to company policy. General remote-work analysis describes return-to-office requirements as often treated as policy changes, and notes uncertainty over whether courts will treat them as more than policy changes for employees hired remotely without a commuting expectation.[69][63]

A proven individual full-remote commitment would make the July policy non-dispositive, but the policy alone cannot resolve the commitment’s scope, duration, or amendability. The policy reference may fill a gap in the offer letter, while the terms of any individual commitment determine whether and to what extent the July 1 policy affects Hayes’s arrangement.[3][36]

The disputed question is therefore not whether a company may ever change hybrid policy. It is whether Hayes had a separate, enforceable full-remote exception before the July 1 revision. If the answer is no, the policy is the contractual mechanism to which the May offer refers. If the answer is yes, the company’s general policy may be subordinate to Hayes’s individualized arrangement.

Hayes’s full remote work through June without objection is relevant context, but it does not itself establish a permanent entitlement. Course of performance—the parties’ repeated conduct while performing an agreement—can help clarify meaning and can be relevant to whether reliance was reasonable. Yet prior performance of a promise does not automatically prove the promise’s terms or create reliance sufficient for promissory estoppel.[39][38]

Here, June conduct supports the narrower proposition that the company allowed Hayes to work fully remotely before the July policy took effect. It may corroborate Hayes’s account that a full-remote arrangement was understood in practice, or it may show only that the company had not yet implemented its July office-attendance policy. The practice predates the July 1 change and is therefore not evidence that the company agreed never to alter remote-work requirements.[36][39][38]

The distinction between permission and contractual commitment matters. An employer’s temporary acquiescence in remote work can be a relevant circumstance, particularly if the employer knew that the employee relocated in reliance on a full-remote assurance. But silence or non-objection, without more, does not necessarily communicate a permanent waiver of a written hybrid-policy term.[70][39][38]

The July rule is prospective in the stated chronology. Hayes worked fully remotely through June; the policy changed July 1; he refused July 8. The company did not apply the three-office-day requirement retroactively to make Hayes’s June remote work a breach. That timing favors the company’s characterization of the rule as a new policy requirement, while leaving open whether its adoption breached an existing individual commitment.[36]

The policy’s effect also depends on its treatment of exceptions. The stated facts do not say whether the July 1 rule applied to engineers with written remote approvals, whether it allowed management to grant exceptions, or whether it expressly superseded individual arrangements. That unresolved factual issue is material to whether the policy uniformly governed Hayes and to the comparative significance of the two engineers’ written approvals.[36][54]

**The July 1 policy gives the company the stronger operational position because it supplies a specific three-days-in-office requirement for the hybrid, policy-linked arrangement in the offer letter; it does not conclusively defeat Hayes’s claim if he proves a separately enforceable full-remote exception, but June remote work alone does not establish that permanent exception.**[36][39][38]

The July policy’s legal force remains distinct from an agreement to alter at-will termination rights. Texas employment is presumed at will absent a specific agreement to the contrary, and handbook language changes that status only when it specifically and expressly curtails the employer’s right to terminate. Hayes seeks enforcement of a work-location term, not a limitation on termination; the at-will presumption therefore does not itself answer whether the company could alter a promised remote-work arrangement.[71]

Flower involved a signed acknowledgment that the employer could change handbook policies, practices, or benefits at any time. Hayes’s stated offer refers generally to company policy and does not contain that express acknowledgment that future policy updates would alter his schedule.[36][72]

## Promissory estoppel

Judgment — Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine. It can protect a person who changed position because of a serious promise even when ordinary contract formation is disputed. The critical distinction is between proof that a manager made a full-remote assurance and proof that the company became bound to maintain full remote work despite the May 1 hybrid-policy offer.[36][37]

### Was the hiring manager’s April 2024 verbal promise of full

A promissory-estoppel claim begins with a promise: a communication that reasonably conveys a commitment to act or refrain from acting in a specified way, rather than a prediction, opinion, preliminary negotiation, or discussion of a possible opportunity. The governing frameworks identified in Applicable law and written terms require a promise, foreseeable and actual reliance, and substantial detriment; the Restatement formulation separately requires enforcement to avoid injustice.[16][17][73][28]

Hayes’s account describes a concrete employment condition: full remote work. The manager allegedly made that assurance during April 2024 negotiations specifically to persuade Hayes to relocate from Seattle to Austin. That is materially more definite than saying an employee will be “taken care of,” which leaves the promised benefit unspecified, or discussing an executive role as a possible future opportunity.[37][38][74] Full remote work identifies the central promised performance—work without a required office schedule—and is therefore capable of being a clear promise if the factfinder credits Hayes’s account.[39][31][75]

The company can argue that “full remote” still lacks a duration, a policy-exception process, and terms addressing later company-wide changes. Those omissions bear on the scope of any enforceable obligation and on remedy. They do not convert an alleged assurance of full remote work into a mere prediction in the way that a statement about hoped-for advancement or anticipated approval might be. Courts have treated testimony describing a definite oral commitment as sufficient to create a fact issue on definiteness, even where the defendant characterized the alleged term as too vague.[18][76][32]

The May 1 offer letter sharply limits a contractual reading of the April assurance. It provided for a hybrid schedule under company policy and included the bolded provision stating, “This agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations.” A prior full-remote assurance conflicts with, or at least would ordinarily be expected to appear alongside, an employment term specifying hybrid work subject to policy. Merger clauses are strong evidence that the writing is intended as the final statement of the agreement, and the parol-evidence rule generally bars prior negotiations offered to contradict an integrated written agreement.[37][1][59][61]

**The April assurance is sufficiently definite to present a promise question, but the signed May 1 terms strongly oppose treating it as an additional enforceable contract term.** The clear-promise issue therefore remains distinct from the issues of integration, authority, reasonable reliance, and the remedy sought.[76][23][61]

Washington defines a promise as a manifestation of intent to act or refrain from acting in a specified way, expressed so as to justify the promisee in understanding that a commitment was made. Washington also recognizes that an oral promise may support promissory estoppel; a writing is not required.[47]

Employment Law Restatement § 2.02 comment c defines a promise as an assurance that something will or will not be done, intended to assure a future event; it must be definite enough reasonably to induce the action taken and must actually induce that reliance.[39] Decker v.

HSBC Bank USA, N.A. treated an alleged assurance that an employee could work remotely from Minnesota for at least part of the time as clear and definite enough to require continued remote work from Minnesota during employment.[75]

The bolded clause is specifically captioned “Integration and Merger” and states, “This agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations.” In Texas, a merger clause may both supersede earlier agreements and negate an agent’s apparent authority later to vary the written terms orally.[37][1][65]

### What significance does the manager’s June confirmation email have for

The June email is the only identified documentation in which the manager confirmed the alleged remote-work promise. It corroborates Hayes’s account that the April conversation involved a commitment rather than a vague recruiting impression. The manager’s retirement in June makes that email particularly important evidence of what the manager said and intended.[36][1][77]

The email is not contemporaneous proof of the April negotiations. It is later evidence from which a factfinder could infer that the April assurance occurred and was serious. Promissory estoppel permits reliance on oral promises; a writing is not categorically required.[16][14][39] The June email therefore supports the promise element even though it does not itself establish an authorized contractual modification.

Timing sharply narrows what the email can do. Hayes sold the Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signed the Texas lease on May 15. The June confirmation followed those actions. An action must be induced by the promise relied upon, so the June email could not have caused the May 15 transactions.[36][38][32] Its relevance to those expenditures is corroborative: it may make Hayes’s account of an earlier April inducement more credible.

The merger clause does not automatically bar a post-formation modification. Merger clauses and the parol-evidence rule generally govern matters before or at formation; parties may later modify an agreement.[59] But the June email’s effect as a modification depends on whether the manager could bind the company to a departure from the standard hybrid-policy term. HR records say the manager lacked authority to modify standard terms.[37]

**The June email supports a factfinder’s conclusion that an April full-remote assurance was made, but it neither caused Hayes’s May 15 reliance nor conclusively created an authorized remote-work modification.** It strengthens the proof of the alleged promise while leaving attribution and remedy contested.[36][37][38]

The Texas Supreme Court’s Wheeler decision shows why the post-May 1 timing matters. Wheeler involved a written agreement that voided and took precedence over previous agreements, followed by alleged assurances after signing that induced the promisee to proceed; the court held that the estoppel pleadings stated a cause of action. Thus, the May 1 merger clause does not itself eliminate the legal significance of a genuinely post-signing assurance, although Hayes must still establish company attribution and the email’s operative effect.[44][37]

The stated facts do not say that the offer letter required later modifications to be written and signed.[78]

### How does the manager’s lack of authority to modify standard

The HR records provide the company a substantial defense: the hiring manager lacked actual authority to modify standard terms. Actual authority is authority the principal—the company—communicates to the agent. If the manager had no authority to grant a special remote arrangement, the June confirmation does not itself bind the company as a modification.[37][79][80]

Agency is not presumed under Texas law; Hayes bears the burden of proving that the manager had authority to act for the company.[104]

Actual authority is not the only route by which a company can be bound. Apparent authority exists when a third party reasonably believes the agent has authority and that belief is traceable to the principal’s manifestations, such as the employee’s assigned position and responsibilities. The agent’s own claim of authority cannot create apparent authority.[79][81][80][22] A company also can ratify an unauthorized act by manifesting assent or engaging in conduct justifiable only on the assumption that it chose to be bound.[80][22]

Ratification requires the company to adopt the unauthorized act with knowledge of material facts; adoption may be express or inferred from clear conduct.[105][106][107]

Hayes’s strongest argument is that the company placed the manager in the role of hiring manager, permitted the manager to negotiate employment conditions, allowed Hayes to work remotely throughout June without objection, and thereby manifested authority or ratified the arrangement. A business manager may hold implied authority over ordinary hiring and operational matters, although the extent of that authority depends on the company’s practices and whether the action was ordinary or extraordinary.[22] The June email and the period of unobjected-to remote work are conduct Hayes can invoke in support of that theory.[36][22]

The company’s stronger response is that a permanent or individualized exception to standard work-location policy is not an ordinary hiring representation once the written offer places scheduling under company policy. The contemporaneous written remote approvals issued to two other engineers support an inference that the company used a formal method for individual remote exceptions.[37][54] If Hayes knew or had reason to know that an exception required written approval, reliance on the manager’s contrary oral assurance would not be reasonable under apparent-authority principles.[80][81]

The manager’s June retirement does not retroactively eliminate authority that existed in April or June. Apparent authority can continue after actual authority ends only while it remains reasonable for the third party to believe the actor still acts with authority.[80] Retirement principally affects the available proof: it does not retroactively eliminate authority that existed in April or June, and the June email remains the only identified documentation of the alleged verbal promise.[36][37][80]

**The authority limitation does not make the alleged words unclear, but it makes full-remote enforcement turn on apparent authority or ratification rather than the manager’s words alone.** Whether the manager’s role and the company’s conduct reasonably conveyed authority is a factual issue where the competing inferences matter.[79][80][22]

Restatement (Third) of Agency § 2.03 defines apparent authority as power to affect a principal’s legal relations when a third party reasonably believes the actor has authority on the principal’s behalf and that belief is traceable to the principal’s manifestations; the agent alone cannot create apparent authority.[81] Constructive knowledge that the agent lacks authority can defeat the reasonableness of that belief.[81]

Ratification requires that the manager purported to act for the company.[82] A principal may also be liable if it believes or has reason to believe its agent will make statements that others will rely on, even after notifying those dealing with the agent that the agent lacks authority to make them.[32]

Under Restatement (Third) of Agency § 3.11(2), apparent authority ends when it is no longer reasonable for the third party to believe the agent continues to act with actual authority.[80]

### Was Hayes’s reliance on full remote work reasonable?

As addressed in Applicable law and written terms, Texas is a plausible substantive-law candidate because the contemplated relocation was to Austin, but the Seattle-to-Austin move is not itself an express choice-of-law provision. Under the Texas authority in the materials, reliance on an oral statement contradicted by the plain language of a binding written contract is unreasonable as a matter of law.[27][56] Texas also recognizes promissory estoppel as a claim requiring a promise, foreseeable reliance, and substantial detrimental reliance.[17][13]

Before May 1, Hayes has a substantial argument for reasonable reliance. The alleged April promise was made to induce the very relocation Hayes later undertook. Promises deliberately designed to influence conduct known to the promisor are the core setting for promissory estoppel, and employment-related relocation is a recognized form of potentially foreseeable reliance.[15][29][83] A promise of full remote work would be especially material to a move from Seattle to Austin because it could affect whether Austin office attendance would be required.

The May 1 letter changes the analysis if Hayes received or accepted it before undertaking the May 15 transactions.[36][37][1] It says hybrid schedule per company policy, does not define the remote-work frequency, and expressly supersedes all prior verbal representations.[37][1] The company can reasonably characterize full remote and hybrid work as directly inconsistent: full remote means no routine office obligation, while hybrid ordinarily means a mixture of remote and office work. The written link to company policy further signals that the schedule may change under policy.[37][84]

The undefined frequency in “hybrid” gives Hayes a narrower counterargument. The letter did not specify three office days, one office day, or any stated office-day requirement as of May 1. Hayes can argue that hybrid left room for a predominantly or temporarily remote arrangement and that the April promise supplied the intended meaning.[37][55] If Hayes received or accepted the letter before May 15, the integration clause expressly notified him that prior verbal representations were superseded.[36][37][1] A merger clause is not necessarily the same as an express no-reliance clause, but it makes reliance on a prior oral term substantially harder, especially where the claimed term concerns the same subject as the written one.[56][59][39]

Hayes undertook the claimed reliance on May 15, 2024, after the letter’s May 1 date; whether he had received or accepted the letter by then requires proof.[36][37][1] The $40,000 home-sale loss and Texas lease therefore occurred after the written hybrid and integration terms appeared.[36] If the company proves that Hayes received or accepted the May 1 letter before May 15, the Texas direct-contradiction approach favors the company: a party ordinarily cannot reasonably rely on an earlier oral assurance when a later signed writing directly states a contrary arrangement.[36][37][1][27]

**For the May 15 home sale and lease, the company has the stronger reasonable-reliance argument because Hayes acted after signing a letter that made scheduling hybrid, policy-based, and exclusive of earlier verbal representations.** Hayes retains an argument from the undefined frequency of “hybrid,” but it is weaker than a claim based on a written full-remote guarantee.[37][1][84][56]

The company can also argue that a party signing an offer must exercise ordinary care rather than blindly rely on an oral representation directly contrary to its written terms.[108][109][110]

Hayes’s May 15 home sale and lease occurred 14 calendar days after the May 1 offer letter. That two-week interval places the claimed reliance after—not before—Hayes had received the hybrid-schedule term and the clause superseding prior verbal representations.[36]

### What is the significance of Hayes working fully remote through

Hayes worked fully remotely through June without company objection. This is post-formation conduct, known in contract law as course of performance: the parties’ conduct in the current transaction after the agreement was made. Course of performance can inform the meaning of an agreement and cannot be erased simply by an integration clause directed at prior statements.[36][59][39]

That conduct supports Hayes’s view that the company treated his arrangement as remote and that the manager’s June email reflected an actual understanding. An employer’s silence can sometimes support estoppel where the employer had an equitable duty or ordinary reason to speak while another person was acting to their detriment.[16][70] The company’s lack of objection is thus corroborative of Hayes’s account.

The June remote-work period does not establish a permanent full-remote term. It occurred before the July 1 policy update requiring three office days weekly.[37] It is also consistent with a temporary or flexible application of the written hybrid policy before that policy changed. The offer letter tied the schedule to company policy, and the manager lacked authority to modify standard terms.[37]

Ratification by conduct requires conduct that is justifiable only on the assumption that the principal chose to be bound by the agent’s act.[80] Permitting remote work for a limited period is not justifiable only as assent to perpetual full remote work when the written agreement itself refers to policy-based hybrid scheduling. The July 1 policy supports the company’s account that June was a policy period, not an irrevocable individual exception.[37]

Waiver requires an intentional relinquishment of a known right or conduct unequivocally inconsistent with asserting that right; allowing remote work through June does not conclusively establish that the company waived the integration clause or its policy-governed hybrid term.[105][106][107]

For Hayes to establish ratification or waiver from the unobjected-to June remote work, an authorized company actor would need to know the material facts and clearly adopt the arrangement.[105][106][111]

**June’s unobjected-to remote work is useful corroboration for Hayes, but it is weak evidence of ratification of a permanent full-remote exception.** It can support a disputed inference of temporary assent without overcoming the written hybrid-policy framework as a matter of law.[36][37][80]

Washington permits relevant context and later conduct to help interpret a writing even when its terms are unambiguous, including the contract’s subject matter, the circumstances of formation, the parties’ conduct thereafter, and the reasonableness of the competing interpretations.[60] But Washington permits that extrinsic evidence only to illuminate the words used and mutual intent; it may not vary, contradict, or modify the writing or establish an independent or unilateral meaning.[60]

### What is the significance of two other engineers hired simultaneously

Two engineers hired at the same time received written remote-work approvals. Their approvals establish that the company could formally document individualized remote arrangements.[37][54] This fact has opposite implications for the parties.

For the company, the written approvals reinforce the HR record that a hiring manager could not unilaterally alter standard terms. The company can argue that remote exceptions required formal approval and that Hayes never obtained one. The comparison makes an oral manager assurance less persuasive as evidence of an authorized exception.[37] It also supports the company’s argument that reasonable reliance required Hayes to secure the same written protection before selling a home and signing a lease.

For Hayes, the approvals show that full remote work was not impossible or categorically incompatible with company operations. The company knew how to grant and honor remote arrangements for engineers.[37] If the other engineers were similarly situated in material respects, different treatment may be relevant to an argument that refusing Hayes an exception was arbitrary or in bad faith. Comparator analysis generally asks whether employees were similarly situated in material respects, including applicable standards and comparable conduct.[85]

The written approvals do not transfer their terms to Hayes. Their existence neither changes Hayes’s integrated offer letter nor proves that the company approved the April promise. The comparison instead bears on authorization, the company’s formal exception practice, and—if Hayes knew of the approvals or approval process before May 15—the reasonableness of Hayes’s reliance.[36][37]

**The written approvals favor the company more strongly on authorization and formal process than they favor Hayes on entitlement to equivalent treatment.** They show that a written exception mechanism existed while leaving open whether Hayes’s circumstances warranted or received an exception.[37][1][85]

Their approvals therefore cannot by themselves make Hayes’s May 15 reliance reasonable.

### Did Hayes suffer substantial detriment?

Hayes sold a Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signed a Texas lease on May 15, 2024. These are concrete economic changes of position, not merely disappointment at having to commute.[36][69] Change of residence and change of job are traditional sources of reliance-related monetary loss and personal inconvenience in employment-promissory-estoppel cases.[86][83]

The $40,000 loss and lease can satisfy the substantial-detriment factor only if they were induced by the alleged full-remote promise. Promissory estoppel requires a causal link: the promise must cause the action or forbearance. A loss that would have occurred for reasons independent of the promise does not constitute induced reliance.[38][32] Hayes’s claimed April promise preceded the May 15 move-related actions, which supports an inference of inducement.[37][36]

The May 1 offer complicates causation. Hayes may have moved because he accepted the job in Austin under the written hybrid offer, if he had received or accepted that offer before May 15, rather than because he expected never to attend an Austin office.[36][37] The hybrid term and integration clause therefore affect not only reasonable reliance but also the question whether the full-remote assurance caused the sale and lease.[37][1][39]

The June email cannot have induced the May 15 sale or lease because it came afterward. It may corroborate Hayes’s testimony that the April promise caused those actions, but the relevant promise for causation remains the alleged April promise.[36][38] The June remote-work practice similarly supports the credibility of Hayes’s account but does not alter the May chronology.[36][37]

The claimed losses also require proof of amount and connection. A $40,000 sale loss is stated, but reliance damages compensate detriment caused by reliance, not every economic consequence of a relocation. Texas’s Wheeler rule limits no-contract promissory-estoppel recovery to foreseeable, definite, and substantial reliance damages measured by the detriment sustained.[28][15] Hayes would therefore need to show the relevant net loss from the house sale and lease, accounting for costs avoided or benefits received through the move.

**Hayes has shown a substantial type of alleged detriment—the $40,000 home-sale loss and a new lease obligation—but recoverability depends on proving that full remote work, not the written hybrid job offer, induced those specific losses.** That distinction supports possible reliance damages while limiting any claim to the proven change in position.[36][38][28]

The relocation acts are substantial in the same concrete sense as the move-related acts considered in Flower: after accepting employment, that employee sold a home, prepared to move family, and signed a six-month apartment lease. Flower reversed summary dismissal because material factual issues existed, rather than treating relocation expenses as legally immaterial.[72]

The Texas Workforce Commission lists these promissory-estoppel elements in employment-related litigation: a promise or offer, detrimental reliance, reasonable reliance, employer knowledge that the offeree would rely, and damages beyond mere disappointment.[45] Schley v. Microsoft Corp. allowed a promissory-estoppel claim to survive dismissal where the employer actively encouraged the employee to sell his home and relocate.[87]

Those facts bear on whether the home-sale loss and lease were caused by the alleged remote promise rather than by accepting an Austin-based hybrid position.

### Would injustice result without enforcement of the remote-work promise?

“Injustice without enforcement” is a separate promissory-estoppel factor. It does not automatically follow from a promise, reliance, or economic loss. The inquiry weighs the reasonableness of reliance, its definite and substantial character in relation to the remedy sought, the formality of the promise, the commercial setting’s safeguards, enforcement of bargains, and prevention of unjust enrichment.[16][26][32]

Hayes’s equitable case is real. On his version of events, the company used a full-remote promise to induce a major relocation, and Hayes then suffered a $40,000 home-sale loss and assumed a Texas lease.[37][36] The manager’s June email and accepted remote work through June can support the inference that the company gained the benefit of Hayes’s relocation and work while allowing him to believe the remote arrangement would continue. Where a promisor induces the action it seeks, justice can favor performance or compensation for nonperformance.[36][32]

The company has a powerful equitable answer. The May 1 written offer called the role hybrid under company policy and superseded earlier verbal representations; its equitable significance for the May 15 transactions depends on proof that Hayes received or accepted it before those transactions.[36][37][1] Courts view a failure to incorporate an important assurance in a negotiated writing as weighing against equitable enforcement, particularly where the writing points in the other direction.[76] The company also had a stated policy interest in requiring three office days weekly from July 1, and HR records limit the manager’s authority to override standard terms.[37]

Those competing equities make an order requiring ongoing full remote work disproportionate to the established reliance harm. Specific enforcement would override the company’s later office-attendance policy and would convert an alleged oral recruitment assurance into a continuing individualized policy exception. The July policy, integrated offer, and authority limitation all make that result difficult to characterize as the only means of preventing injustice.[37][1][31]

An indefinite remote-work order would also require terms governing performance standards and future policy changes that the alleged April promise did not supply.[15][112]

A narrower remedy can address any proven inequity. Restatement (Second) § 90 provides that the remedy may be limited as justice requires.[27][26][14] Texas’s Wheeler decision provides that, where no legally sufficient contract exists, a claimant may recover foreseeable, definite, and substantial reliance damages measured by the detriment sustained, rather than expected profits or the value of promised future performance.[15][28] That framework better fits claimed relocation losses than a permanent injunction requiring full remote work.

The company identifies transitional relief, as well as monetary relief, as a possible alternative to permanent full-remote work for addressing proven reliance.[15][112]

**Denying all relief could be unjust if Hayes proves the April promise induced the relocation, but the present facts do not show that permanent full remote work is the only remedy capable of avoiding that injustice.** The more proportionate potential relief is reimbursement of qualifying reliance loss, not enforcement of an indefinite ongoing remote arrangement.[42][14][28]

The remedy question differs materially by potentially applicable law. Washington follows Restatement § 90’s rule that the remedy may be limited as justice requires; Washington’s Supreme Court considered but did not resolve whether promissory-estoppel recovery is limited to reliance damages in Farm Crop Energy.[47] Texas’s Wheeler rule permits no more than reliance damages measured by the detriment sustained when the promisee did not bind the promisor to a legally sufficient contract, excluding expected profits.[44]

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 139(2) directs the court to consider the availability and adequacy of other remedies, particularly cancellation and restitution; the definite and substantial character of reliance relative to the remedy sought; whether reliance corroborates the promise and its terms or those are otherwise shown by clear and convincing evidence; the reasonableness of reliance; and the extent to which reliance was foreseeable.[42]

In Bouton v. Byers, the Kansas Court of Appeals held that, viewing the record and reasonable inferences for the employee, evidence that she lost a lucrative job that could not be resumed and received far less compensation could constitute substantial detriment and precluded summary judgment on the other promissory-estoppel components.[62]

### Jordan Hayes’s April 2024 recruitment involved an alleged full-remote promise

The chronology controls the analysis. First, in April 2024, the manager allegedly promised full remote work to persuade Hayes to relocate. Second, on May 1, the offer letter stated that the schedule was hybrid under company policy and included a bold clause superseding prior verbal representations.[37][1] Third, on May 15, Hayes sold the Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signed a Texas lease.

Fourth, in June, the manager confirmed the promise by email, Hayes worked remotely without objection, and the manager retired. Fifth, on July 1, company policy required three office days weekly; Hayes refused on July 8.[37][1][36]

That sequence gives Hayes meaningful evidence that an April promise was made and that relocation losses followed it. The allegation that the promise was designed to induce a Seattle-to-Austin move supports foreseeability.

The same sequence gives the company the stronger legal position on enforcing full remote work. The company can prevail on its written-terms defense if it proves that Hayes received or accepted before May 15 a writing that described the position as hybrid, tied it to company policy, and superseded prior oral representations.[36][37][1] The written approvals issued to contemporaneous engineers support the company’s position that any remote exception required formal documentation.[37] The July 1 policy then supplied a uniform, stated basis for office attendance.[37]

**The combined factors support a disputed claim for narrowly measured reliance relief, not summary enforcement of a permanent full-remote arrangement.** The alleged April assurance is clear enough to be litigated as a promise; reasonable reliance, company attribution, causation, and the necessity of full performance remain contested and favor the company on the written terms.[18][37][1][28]

Under Texas’s traditional summary-judgment standard, the movant must establish both that no genuine issue of material fact exists and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.[43]

In an equitable action, summary judgment is inappropriate when the record does not fully develop the facts needed for equitable discretion and even the undisputed facts permit more than one reasonable inference. The competing inferences about the April promise, apparent authority, the meaning of hybrid scheduling, receipt and effect of the integration clause, inducement, and ratification preclude summary enforcement of full remote work and can require factfinding on any narrower equitable relief.[9][37][1][36]

### Synthesis —

The April alleged full-remote assurance is a concrete promise capable of inducing relocation, and the $40,000 home-sale loss plus the Texas lease are substantial types of claimed detriment. The June email and unobjected-to remote work corroborate Hayes’s account of an earlier commitment.[37][36] The decisive counterweight, if the company proves pre-May 15 receipt or acceptance, is that Hayes acted after receiving or accepting a hybrid, policy-based offer that superseded prior verbal representations; the manager also lacked authority to modify standard terms, and other engineers received formal written remote approvals.[36][37][1] Taken together, the facts support litigation over whether Hayes should receive reliance-based compensation if he proves inducement and justified reliance.

They do not support compelling the company, as a matter of law, to continue a full-remote arrangement despite the July 1 three-office-days policy.[37][14][28]

Under Lotito’s employment-context rule, proof of Texas’s stated promissory-estoppel elements—a promise, foreseeable reliance, and substantial detrimental reliance—would not itself permit Hayes to obtain affirmative relief on that claim. Lotito affirmed summary judgment on that employment claim without reaching whether evidence supported the elements.[43][35] Texas appellate authority described in the materials is divided: Collins holds that a promise of at-will employment under a valid at-will contract supplies no basis for detrimental reliance and bars reliance damages, while Roberts allowed an employee who detrimentally relied on a promise of at-will employment to sustain a promissory-estoppel claim.[30]

The competing inferences about receipt and effect of the integration clause, the April promise, apparent authority, the meaning of hybrid scheduling, inducement, and ratification require factfinding on any narrower equitable relief.[9][37][1][36]

Under Texas’s traditional summary-judgment rule, the movant must establish both that no genuine issue of material fact exists and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.[43] Even apart from the Lotito defense, disputed evidence remains as to whether the manager made the April assurance, whether the company manifested authority or ratified it through June conduct, and whether the asserted promise rather than the integrated hybrid offer induced the May 15 sale and lease.[36][37][22][38]

## Summary judgment and relief

This part addresses a narrower question than whether the company made a troubling recruitment assurance. Summary judgment asks whether the admissible record permits only one legal result, without a trial. The requested remedy also matters: compelling an employer to maintain a full-remote work arrangement is materially different from compensating an employee for a proven loss caused by reliance.

### What summary-judgment standard applies?

Summary judgment resolves a claim without trial only when there is no genuine dispute or issue of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Federal court applies Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a), Washington state court applies CR 56(c), and Texas state court applies the traditional-summary-judgment standard of Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 166a.[88][89][18][9] A material fact is one that could affect the outcome under the substantive law governing promissory estoppel.

A dispute is genuine when the evidence would permit a reasonable factfinder to decide that fact for the nonmovant—the party opposing the motion.[88][9][90][91]

The procedural rule depends on the court in which Hayes sues, rather than on the state law selected to decide the employment claim. Courts ordinarily use their own procedural rules to conduct litigation even when another jurisdiction’s substantive law supplies the contract or estoppel rules.[2][3]

- **Federal court:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(a) requires judgment if the movant shows no genuine dispute of any material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. The parties must support factual assertions with particular record material, including documents, emails, depositions, declarations, admissions, and interrogatory answers. A federal affidavit or declaration must be based on personal knowledge, state facts admissible in evidence, and show that its declarant is competent to testify to those facts. [88]

Affidavits must provide particularized, admissible facts based on personal knowledge rather than conclusions.[113]

A party offering the June email or other electronic documents must provide evidence sufficient to establish that each document is what the party claims; contents, distinctive features, and surrounding circumstances can establish authenticity, while only qualifying materials are self-authenticating.[114]

- **Washington state court:** Civil Rule 56(c) uses the same core threshold: the pleadings, discovery materials, admissions, and affidavits must show no genuine issue of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Washington frames the test as requiring that all reasonable persons could reach only one conclusion after the evidence and reasonable inferences are viewed for the nonmovant. Supporting affidavits must be based on personal knowledge and set out evidence admissible at trial. [89][72]

- **Texas state court:** A traditional motion under Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 166a requires the movant to show no genuine issue of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. The Texas Supreme Court’s final amendments to Rule 166a took effect March 1, 2026, but apply only to motions filed on or after that date. [92][18] If the company instead brings a Texas no-evidence motion after an adequate time for discovery, it may identify an essential element that Hayes must prove and need not initially offer its own evidence. Hayes must then produce more than a scintilla—enough evidence that reasonable and fair-minded people could differ—on each challenged element. [9]

Review of a Texas summary-judgment ruling is de novo.[115][116]

Under each formulation, the court credits competent evidence and reasonable inferences favoring the nonmovant and does not weigh evidence or decide witness credibility. A genuine dispute over a material historical fact or a reasonable inference from it requires trial; undisputed facts that leave only a legal question may be decided on summary judgment.[76][88][10][9] The court takes competent evidence favorable to the nonmovant as true, gives that party the benefit of reasonable inferences, and does not weigh the competing evidence.

It cannot decide that one witness is more credible than another on a paper record. When a material issue requires assessing demeanor or choosing between competing credible accounts, summary judgment is inappropriate.[76][88][10][9]

That constraint is particularly important here. The key questions include what the hiring manager said in April 2024, whether full remote work was promised as a firm employment term or discussed more generally, whether the manager had actual or apparent authority, and whether the company’s conduct in June confirmed or merely tolerated remote work while policy remained unsettled. Those are historical-fact and inference questions, not merely questions of interpreting an undisputed document. The Federal Judicial Center observes that summary judgment is generally inappropriate where the dispute concerns the terms or conditions of employment, although a court may decide the legal significance of undisputed facts.[10]

Hayes, as the claimant seeking affirmative summary judgment, must establish every required promissory-estoppel element as a matter of law. The company, as a traditional-summary-judgment movant, may prevail by conclusively negating an essential element or conclusively establishing an affirmative defense.[9][18] The company, as a traditional-movant defendant, can prevail by conclusively negating one essential element or conclusively establishing an affirmative defense.[18][9] A party opposing a properly supported motion cannot simply repeat allegations; it must identify competent evidence creating a triable issue.[89][88][91]

Cross-motions for summary judgment retain separate burdens, and both may be denied if the record reveals a material factual dispute.[115][117][118][119]

Pleadings alone are not evidence on summary judgment, although an opponent’s unequivocal assertion in live pleadings may constitute a judicial admission.[118]

**The controlling point is that summary judgment decides law on an evidentiary record; it does not authorize a judge to select between reasonable accounts of an April promise, the company’s authority structure, or the meaning reasonably drawn from June conduct.**[76][88][10][91]

Thus, the forum’s procedural rule governs the motion, while the applicable substantive law identifies which facts matter. The federal, Washington, and Texas traditional standards converge on the same practical result: a party wins only if the record leaves no reasonable factfinder basis for the opposing party on an outcome-determinative fact or inference.[2][89][88][9]

Washington appellate decisions independently review summary-judgment rulings and reverse summary dismissal of employment promissory-estoppel claims when material factual issues remain. In *Flower*, decided March 29, 2005, the Washington Court of Appeals reversed summary dismissal because material issues existed concerning an employer’s recruitment assurance and the employee’s relocation.[72]

In Washington, promissory estoppel is generally an equitable issue for the court once the underlying facts are established; if factual questions go to a jury, whether those facts create an estoppel remains for the court. That allocation does not permit the court to resolve disputed historical facts at summary judgment.[47][72]

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Vastoler v. American Can Co.* illustrates the point: after a district court granted the employer summary judgment for lack of detrimental reliance, the appellate court reversed because a jury could reasonably find detrimental reliance from the pressures and stress of supervisory work and the employee’s acceptance of a promotion in exchange for recognition of past service.[50]

Under Washington CR 56(c), the court may enter an interlocutory summary judgment on liability even when the amount of damages remains genuinely disputed.[89] If summary judgment does not resolve the entire case or all requested relief and trial remains necessary, CR 56(d) directs the court, if practicable, to identify facts without substantial controversy and facts genuinely disputed; facts identified as without substantial controversy are established for trial.[89]

Washington contract interpretation may be resolved as a legal question only when it does not depend on extrinsic evidence or when the extrinsic evidence permits only one reasonable inference. Thus, under Washington law, the offer-letter interpretation can be resolved on summary judgment only if it does not depend on extrinsic evidence or that evidence permits just one reasonable inference; otherwise summary judgment is rarely appropriate.[60][93]

Potentially material record questions also include the June email’s exact wording and recipients, whether authorized company personnel knew of and accepted Hayes’s fully remote June work, and what Hayes knew about the manager’s authority and any required formal remote-work approval process. Those facts bear on both the claimed promise and whether Hayes could reasonably treat it as company-authorized.

### Can Hayes enforce the full remote work arrangement on summary

The choice of substantive law remains addressed in **Applicable law and written terms**. The Seattle-to-Austin move is not itself an express choice-of-law clause. If Texas law governs, promissory estoppel generally requires a promise, foreseeable reliance, and substantial detrimental reliance; the requested four-factor analysis also asks whether enforcement is necessary to avoid injustice. Washington describes closely related requirements: a promise; foreseeable change of position; actual change; justifiable reliance; and injustice avoidable only through enforcement.[17][18][14] Under either formulation, Hayes must establish more than disappointment with a subsequent return-to-office policy.

The company has the stronger legal position against an order compelling continuing full remote work. The May 1 offer letter stated a hybrid schedule under company policy and included a bold integration and merger clause declaring that the agreement superseded prior verbal representations. As discussed in **Applicable law and written terms**, those provisions bear directly on the alleged April assurance rather than merely addressing a collateral subject. A merger clause can make a writing completely integrated—intended as the complete agreement—and thereby exclude even additional terms consistent with the writing. Jurisdictions differ, however, on whether such clauses are conclusive or a powerful but nonconclusive integration factor.[1][59]

**Hayes cannot obtain affirmative summary judgment ordering full remote work because the signed hybrid-schedule and integration provisions materially undermine the proposition that full remote work became an enforceable, unqualified employment term.**[1][27][26][9]

The timing reinforces that conclusion. Hayes’s claimed reliance acts—selling the Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signing a Texas lease—occurred on May 15, after receipt of the May 1 writing. The June email did not induce those completed expenditures. At most, it may corroborate Hayes’s assertion that the manager made the April promise, or it may be offered to show the manager’s own continued belief in such an arrangement. The causal issue for the home sale and lease is therefore whether the April assurance caused the May 15 decisions despite the intervening written hybrid term and superseding clause.[1][76]

The company’s best argument is not merely that the word hybrid lacks an exact office-day count. It is that a signed agreement placing scheduling under company policy is incompatible with Hayes’s asserted right to work fully remote, indefinitely, and despite policy changes. A full-remote term removes or sharply limits the company’s ability to require routine office attendance; a hybrid-per-policy term preserves the company’s control over the division between office and remote work. The July 1 policy requiring three office days weekly therefore fits the company’s reading of the signed arrangement. Hayes’s July 8 refusal, by itself, establishes neither a preexisting remote-work contract nor a right to disregard policy.[1][94]

Judgment — On these facts, full remote work and hybrid scheduling per company policy are not naturally harmonized as two equally operative work-location terms. The former is an individual entitlement to no regular office attendance; the latter is a work arrangement governed by company policy. That direct conflict gives the May 1 writing unusual force when assessing both reasonable reliance and the availability of specific performance.

Hayes nevertheless has a substantial argument against disposing of every promissory-estoppel theory on summary judgment. His evidence, if offered in admissible form, would include his own testimony about an April recruitment assurance intended to induce relocation, the manager’s June confirmation email, the absence of objection while Hayes worked fully remote through June, and the fact that two engineers hired at the same time received written remote approvals. Those facts can support an inference that full remote work was a real recruitment commitment, that remote exceptions were available, and that the company knew how to document them when it approved them. The reported Decker decision also illustrates that an assurance permitting remote work can be sufficiently clear and definite to create an employment obligation, although Decker was decided at the pleading stage rather than on a developed summary-judgment record.[75]

In *McDavid*, deposition testimony and an affidavit describing a definite promise to approve a dealership relocation raised a fact issue on whether the promise was sufficiently definite to enforce.[18] McDavid’s affidavit further stated that it would not have relocated its Oldsmobile dealership or incurred more than $350,000 in construction expenses had it known the promised dealership relocation would not be allowed; that evidence raised a genuine issue of material fact on detrimental-reliance damage despite evidence that McDavid may have benefited from the move.[18] The court therefore reversed summary judgment on McDavid’s promissory-estoppel claim and remanded it for further proceedings.[18] That analogy assists Hayes only on the existence and definiteness issue; it does not overcome an integrated writing as a matter of law or establish the propriety of ordering continued performance of an employment condition.

The manager’s authority is a further material issue. HR records reportedly show that the manager lacked authority to modify standard terms. That fact supports the company’s position that the alleged April statement was unauthorized and that the June email could not amend the May 1 offer. The manager’s retirement in June, and the lack of documentation other than the email, make the proof problem more acute because the factfinder must assess the terms, setting, and authority of an oral discussion without the manager’s live testimony. The June email, the company’s lack of objection to fully remote work through June, and written remote approvals for two simultaneously hired engineers support competing inferences about whether the manager’s assurance was confirmed, ratified, or merely tolerated. HR records stating that the manager lacked authority to modify standard terms, the manager’s June retirement, and the absence of other documentation support the opposite inference. A court may not choose between those inferences or resolve credibility on summary judgment.[36][37][76][88][10]

The competing inferences should be kept distinct:

- **Company inference:** The manager could recruit but could not alter standard terms; the May 1 offer supplied the authoritative work-schedule term; the integration clause eliminated earlier verbal representations; and June remote work reflected temporary tolerance before the July 1 policy, not ratification of an individual remote agreement. The written remote approvals for other engineers show that the company required writing for exceptions and that Hayes never obtained it. [1][94][84][68]

- **Hayes inference:** The manager made a specific April assurance to induce a cross-state move; the June email confirmed rather than created that assurance; company acceptance of fully remote work through June ratified or at least substantiated the promised arrangement; and written approvals for the two other engineers show both the feasibility of full remote work and a company practice of granting it. [18][75][62]

- **Legal consequence:** The court may not resolve these competing factual inferences by selecting the company’s account simply because the manager is retired or Hayes’s account is substantially supported by his own testimony. A credibility-dependent dispute is for trial if the testimony and documentary evidence could support a reasonable finding. [88][10][95][91]

The June work period matters, but it has bounded significance. Working fully remote through June without objection may corroborate Hayes’s claimed April understanding and may suggest the company accepted remote performance for that month. It does not, without more, prove a continuing entitlement beyond the company’s July 1 policy change, especially where the written offer assigned scheduling to company policy. The two written approvals carry a similar double inference: they may show full remote work was a genuine available arrangement, but their written form may also support the company’s contention that individualized approvals had to be formally documented.[94][84][68]

The remedy issue independently prevents Hayes from obtaining the requested judgment. Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine: it prevents a promisor from invoking strict legal rights where doing so would be unjust after foreseeable detrimental reliance. It does not necessarily create a contract on the asserted terms. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 makes a promise binding only to the extent justice requires and permits reliance damages, partial enforcement, or another limited remedy; full expectation damages are not automatic.[15][17][49] Employment contracts generally are not specifically enforceable against the employer, a rule that strongly disfavors judicial supervision of an ongoing work-location arrangement.[15][17][27][26][38][23]

Specific performance requires a separate showing of necessity, expediency, and propriety; even an express contractual right to seek it does not establish entitlement by itself.[112]

The authorities are not uniform on remedies. Scholarship reviewing Section 90 cases reports frequent awards of specific performance or expectation relief when a serious commitment is established; by contrast, Texas’s Wheeler decision limited relief for an insufficiently definite promise to reliance damages, and Texas authority describes promissory estoppel as restoring a claimant to the pre-reliance position rather than awarding expected profits.[23][18] The difference matters because Hayes asks for ongoing full-remote performance, not merely compensation for a completed reliance loss.

In *Moore Burger*, the court left both specific performance and damages open as possible remedies rather than treating performance of the promise as automatic.[120]

Judgment — Even if a factfinder ultimately credits the April promise, the better remedy analysis is backward-looking compensation rather than an injunction requiring a private employer indefinitely to maintain an individualized remote-work schedule. The offer letter’s hybrid term, the absence of a written remote approval for Hayes, the uncertain managerial authority, and the need for continuing court supervision all weigh heavily against specific performance.

The July 1 policy change cannot itself extinguish a valid contractual remote-work guarantee; an employer ordinarily cannot unilaterally revoke a term guaranteed by an employment agreement.[96] But that proposition assumes the term has first been established.

Hayes’s refusal on July 8 therefore turns on the threshold issue: if the May 1 hybrid-and-policy terms legally control, the three-office-days policy applies; if Hayes proves an enforceable individualized remote promise, the later policy cannot simply override it.[1][96][94]

Hayes’s motion for summary judgment compelling continuing full remote work should be denied. The company may obtain summary judgment on that specific-performance request if the court holds that the May 1 hybrid-and-policy term and integration clause control work location, but disputed evidence on the April promise, authority, inducement, reasonable reliance, and causation may leave a narrower reliance-damages claim for trial.[36][1][37][76][88][10] The company’s motion must be assessed separately: it has the stronger basis for judgment on specific performance if the May 1 integrated hybrid-and-policy term controls work location, but not necessarily for judgment eliminating reliance damages, because the April promise, the manager’s perceived authority, the June email and remote practice, and the causal connection to the May 15 expenditures present disputed evidence and competing inferences.[59][1][76][10][9][62]

Hayes refused the office requirement 7 calendar days after the July 1 policy update. The one-week interval confirms that the refusal followed the announced three-office-days-per-week requirement rather than preceded it.[36]

If Washington law governs and the employment was at will, the Washington Court of Appeals in *Clipse v. Commercial Driver Services, Inc.* held that the promise for promissory estoppel must be a clear and definite promise of permanent employment subject only to dismissal for just cause; it reversed the employee’s judgment because he presented no evidence of such a promise.[46] *Clipse* concerned an alleged promise of permanent employment subject only to dismissal for just cause, not a work-location promise; Hayes’s claimed April assurance and June confirmation email are therefore evidence a factfinder must assess for definiteness rather than mere hopeful statements.[46][47]

*Flower* supplies a closer relocation comparison than a generalized recruitment case: its employee expressly resisted relocating without a secure long-term future, and the employer’s president gave a specific assurance about discharge. Hayes can analogize the claimed April full-remote assurance as a specific inducement to relocate; the company can distinguish *Flower* because its assurance concerned discharge protection rather than a continuing remote-work schedule.[72][47]

Texas authority adds a potentially dispositive rule if Texas law governs and Hayes seeks affirmative promissory-estoppel relief as an employee. In *Lotito v. Knife River Corp.*, decided by the Court of Appeals of Texas, Waco, on November 8, 2012, the majority affirmed traditional summary judgment without reaching the evidence on the estoppel elements; its concurrence separately concluded that reliance on an oral eight-year employment promise was unreasonable where the employer remained free to terminate employment.[43]

Contract construction begins with the parties’ intentions as expressed in the writing itself. The May 1 offer letter therefore supplies the starting point for deciding whether its hybrid-schedule language and integration clause leave room for the asserted individual full-remote term.[67][1]

Wheeler demonstrates the remedial distinction: Texas permitted reliance damages under a formal written agreement that was unenforceable because it omitted, among other matters, how annual interest would be compounded; the ambiguity did not matter to the reliance measure sought. Wheeler arose from special exceptions to the sufficiency of the pleadings, and the Texas Supreme Court reversed and remanded for trial on the merits rather than deciding the claim on a summary-judgment evidentiary record.[50][44]

The May 1 offer letter's bolded Integration and Merger clause states: “This agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations.” That express language places the alleged April verbal full-remote assurance directly at issue.[1]

In Texas, whether contract language is ambiguous is a question of law for the court. If the offer's hybrid-schedule wording is unambiguous, its interpretation may be decided on summary judgment; if it is ambiguous, the parties' intent is a fact issue for the factfinder and summary judgment on interpretation is improper.[9]

The remote-work-specific equitable analysis points the same way on the requested injunction: courts are unlikely to intervene absent extraordinary harm to an at-will employee and are unlikely to interfere where the employee merely prefers working from home to commuting.[69]

The parol evidence rule concerns a written-contract party’s evidence of a prior or contemporaneous extrinsic agreement.[32] The traditional view limits that rule to extrinsic agreements or statements made before or simultaneously with the signing of an integrated writing.[32]

Because the June email postdates the alleged April assurance, the traditional parol-evidence rule’s treatment of prior or contemporaneous extrinsic statements does not itself determine the email’s evidentiary significance.[32]

### Is reliance-based relief available even if Hayes cannot enforce full

Yes, but it is contingent relief, not automatic reimbursement. Reliance damages are monetary compensation designed to restore the promisee to the position occupied before acting on the promise. They differ from expectation damages, which seek the benefit the claimant would have received had the asserted agreement been performed.[97]

Under *Wheeler*, Texas permits reliance damages where a promisee acted to his detriment without forming a legally sufficient contract. The measure is no more than the foreseeable, definite, and substantial detriment sustained—restoring the promisee to the pre-reliance position, not providing expected profits or the benefit of the promised arrangement.[15][17][44][18] Texas appellate authority states the measure similarly: recoverable damages are the amount necessary to restore the injured party to the position they would have occupied absent reliance.[15][17][18][28]

The proposed remedy is therefore the proven net loss caused by reasonable reliance on the April assurance, not an order preserving full remote work, an automatic $40,000 award, all lease payments, or the value of continuing remote work.[15][17][36][18] If Hayes proves the April promise, reasonable reliance, causation, and injustice, he may seek the loss caused by changing position in reliance on it.

Relocation and housing-related costs are recognized examples of the category of expenditures a reliance remedy can address.[27][83][31] However, the claim must connect each item of loss to the promise and to reasonable reliance, not simply to the decision to accept employment in Austin.

The May 15 dates are decisive to that causation inquiry. The Seattle home sale, its stated $40,000 loss, and the Texas lease predate the June confirmation email. They cannot have been induced by that later email. The email may be corroborative evidence that the April promise existed, but the factfinder must determine whether Hayes sold and leased because of the alleged April full-remote assurance or because he accepted a written Austin job structured as hybrid under company policy.[1][76]

The claimed loss is also not self-proving:

- **Home-sale loss:** The $40,000 figure identifies a possible economic detriment, but Hayes must prove that the loss resulted from a sale undertaken in reasonable reliance on the April promise and that it is a proper measure of restoring his pre-reliance position. In *MTM Electrical Corporation v. Bechtel*, the Texas appellate court held that the claimant identified no evidence of the amount required to restore it to the position it occupied before the alleged representation; proof of a compensable reliance loss and its restorative amount is therefore required. [17] A sale loss may be affected by market conditions, independent reasons for sale, and whether the sale was necessary to accept the job. [17][18]

- **Texas lease:** Lease obligations and associated housing costs may be reliance expenditures if Hayes incurred them because the company induced relocation or a particular location-based work arrangement. The relevant measure is the net loss caused by reliance, not the gross cost of housing that Hayes would have incurred in any event. [27][97][77]

- **Remote-work expectancy:** Hayes cannot use reliance damages simply to obtain the monetary equivalent of permanent full remote work or future employment income. Texas authority distinguishes restoring a past loss from awarding the expected benefit of a future arrangement. [15][17][18][30]

The company may contend that an at-will employment relationship precludes reliance recovery. Texas authorities described in the record are divided. Roberts allowed a claim where a prospective employee left a current job in reliance on promised employment, reasoning that foreseeable, intentional inducement before the written at-will agreement began operating could support liability.[30] Collins took the opposing position: an at-will promise supplies no assurance of future conduct and cannot support detrimental reliance as a matter of law; it also stated that promissory estoppel cannot recover reliance damages when a valid at-will contract exists.[30] This disagreement makes the governing state law and the precise at-will terms of Hayes’s employment consequential.

Judgment — The better characterization of Hayes’s surviving theory is not that the company promised continued employment, but that it allegedly induced a completed, costly relocation-related change of position through a specific work-location promise. That distinction gives Hayes a stronger basis for reliance damages than for an entitlement to future remote work, though it does not eliminate the company’s written-agreement and at-will defenses.

Washington materials point in a somewhat different direction. They expressly permit a promisee to plead contract and promissory estoppel in the alternative, and an employee may pursue promissory-estoppel damages if a contract cannot be proven. Washington recognizes the five elements identified above, including justifiable reliance and injustice avoidable only by enforcement.[16][14] But the Washington Supreme Court has considered without resolving whether promissory-estoppel awards are limited to reliance damages.[16][14] Thus, Washington law does not support assuming either that the offer letter defeats every estoppel remedy or that Hayes would be entitled to specific performance.

The injustice factor guides both liability and remedy. Under Restatement § 90 comment b, whether enforcement is needed to avoid injustice depends on the reasonableness of reliance, its definite and substantial character relative to the remedy sought, the formality of the promise, whether the commercial setting served the functions of formality, and policies including enforcement of bargains and prevention of unjust enrichment.[14][38] Those considerations cut in different directions here.

A cross-country move, home sale, and lease can be substantial reliance; a signed contrary work-schedule provision and bold merger clause serve the cautionary and evidentiary functions that normally make reliance on prior oral promises less reasonable.[59][1]

For that reason, a court could reject the demand for full-remote enforcement while allowing a factfinder to assess whether a limited monetary award is necessary to avoid injustice. Kansas authority offers a useful remedial distinction: specific enforcement or damages equivalent to promised performance requires sufficient particularity, but a restorative remedy for reliance loss may sometimes be available despite some indefiniteness because it restores the claimant’s earlier position rather than parses and enforces every promised detail.[62] That distinction is consistent with Texas’s Wheeler approach.[15][23]

Possible limited relief includes a defined transition period or a specifically proved temporary remote-work exception.[102][106][15][17][121][104]

Hayes should not assume that the entire $40,000 home-sale loss or all lease expenses are recoverable. He bears the burden to establish amount, reasonable reliance, foreseeability, causal connection, and any mitigation. The requested damages should be limited to the net, proven detriment needed to return him as nearly as possible to the position he occupied before acting on the promise.[17][18][77] The company may contest causation and mitigation without thereby proving that no loss occurred.

The practical litigation recommendation is therefore:

1. **Hayes should not seek summary judgment compelling permanent full remote work;** the integrated hybrid term and remedy limits make that request untenable on this record. [1][23][26]

2. **Hayes should preserve a damages claim in the alternative;** he should tie each claimed May 15 loss to the April assurance, rather than to the June email, and present competent evidence of amount and causation. [76][88]

3. **The company should target the enforcement remedy separately from liability;** it has a stronger basis to defeat specific performance than to eliminate every fact-bound claim for reliance losses. [15][17][23][10]

4. **Both parties should frame the June evidence precisely;** it can corroborate or undermine the claimed April promise, but it cannot be the cause of expenditures made before June. [76][10]

The result is that the failure to prove an enforceable full-remote contract does not necessarily eliminate a separate promissory-estoppel damages claim. It does, however, leave Hayes with a claim measured by actual reliance loss rather than by the value of a continuing full-remote arrangement.[16][14][15][18]

The Texas remedial analysis is not uniform in employment cases. *Wheeler* permits reliance damages measured by detriment actually sustained where a promisee failed to obtain a legally sufficient contract.[98][34] In *Lotito*, the majority affirmed traditional summary judgment without reaching the promissory-estoppel elements; a concurrence reasoned that the claimed oral eight-year employment promise did not meet the employment-context requirement of a promise to execute a presently existing written agreement.[43]

Washington permits contract and promissory-estoppel theories in the alternative while contract formation or enforceability remains disputed, including an employment claim for promissory-estoppel damages if the alleged contract cannot be proved.[16] If a valid contract governing the same subject is ultimately established, the alternative estoppel theory becomes unavailable; if the remote-work term cannot be proved as contractual, *Flower* recognizes the possibility of promissory-estoppel damages.[47][99]

The Restatement approach incorporated in Washington permits full-scale enforcement in appropriate cases but also permits restitution, damages, or specific relief measured by reliance; Washington has not resolved whether promissory-estoppel recovery is confined to reliance damages. This remedial flexibility does not make ongoing remote-work performance the required remedy here.[47][98][100]

Promissory-estoppel relief is not uniform: a court may enforce a promise, compensate reliance expenses, or provide narrower relief. A claimant may plead contract and promissory estoppel in the alternative when permitted, but may not obtain duplicative recovery for the same injury.[101]

The reported pre-employment cases awarded applicants compensation for reliance loss, including loss from leaving a prior job, where an employer withdrew what appeared to be a firm offer before the scheduled start date. The accompanying analysis reasons that the employer can avoid that loss by making its final hiring decision before extending an apparently firm offer and that parties ordinarily expect at least reliance loss to be compensated if the employer changes its mind.[50]

*Bouton* links the promise to the change it was intended to induce—leaving a teaching job—and treats the financial benefit forgone, rather than the value of the promised ranch work, as the relevant reliance measure. In *Bouton*, the alleged promise was made in response to concerns about leaving the job, and the employee resigned shortly afterward; the Kansas Court of Appeals held that those facts permitted a factfinder to conclude that the promisor intended reliance and reversed summary judgment.[62]

The same distinction supports evaluating Hayes's claimed losses as the net financial detriment caused by the relocation-related action the April promise allegedly induced, not as the value of the promised remote-work performance.[62]

In *McDavid*, the Texas court held that an affidavit stating the claimant would not have relocated or incurred more than $350,000 in construction expenses absent the oral relocation permission created a genuine fact issue on reliance damages, despite evidence that the relocation may have benefited the claimant; the court reversed summary judgment on promissory estoppel and remanded that claim for further proceedings.[18]

### Synthesis

The direct result is to deny Hayes’s affirmative summary-judgment motion for continuing full remote work. The May 1 hybrid-schedule term and bold integration clause, signed before the May 15 home sale and Texas lease, strongly oppose treating the prior oral assurance as an enforceable entitlement and strongly disfavor specific performance.[36][37][1][59][23] The company’s separate motion may succeed on the continuing-full-remote remedy if the integrated offer controls work location; however, the April assurance, June confirmation email, unobjected-to remote work through June, and written remote approvals for two simultaneously hired engineers create disputed inferences on promise, authority, inducement, and causation that can require trial on a narrower reliance-damages claim.[36][18][37][76][75][10] Any such monetary relief is limited to proven net losses, including only a $40,000 home-sale loss, lease costs, or other loss reasonably and foreseeably caused by the April promise rather than by the later June email.[15][17][36][18][76]

### Open questions

The governing state law remains open between Texas and Washington. The meaning and temporal scope of hybrid schedule per company policy, including whether it allowed an individual full-remote exception or later policy changes, remain open. The manager’s actual or apparent authority, the exact April words and circumstances, Hayes’s reasons for selling and leasing, and whether the two other written approvals reflected a comparable exception remain open. Those issues would be settled by the parties’ contracting, negotiation, authority, policy, communications, and comparator evidence.
